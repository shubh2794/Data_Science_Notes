Data Sampling & Imbalance

How you select rows decides what the model can learn. Two problems recur: drawing a representative sample with a known error bar, and surviving class imbalance, where the class you care about — fraud, disease, defects — is a rounding error in the data.

Data Engineering Datasets Imbalance Fundamentals

01 Representative sampling

  • A sample is representative when every unit of the target population had a known, non-zero probability of being drawn. Break either condition and no amount of modelling repairs it — you have estimated something, just not the quantity you wanted.
  • Selection bias enters through the sampling frame, not the algorithm: surveying app users about app quality, training a defect detector only on units that reached final inspection, or labelling only the cases a human already flagged.
  • Survivorship and truncation are the same failure in time: rows that churned, crashed, or were rejected never enter the table, so the model learns the physics of the survivors.
  • Distribution shift is the gap between the sampling distribution and production. Covariate shift moves P(x), label shift moves P(y), concept drift moves P(y|x). Sampling design controls the first two; only monitoring catches the third — see Monitoring & Drift.
  • Sample weights are the repair when inclusion probabilities are unequal: weight each row by wᵢ = 1/πᵢ so the weighted sample behaves like the population.
Takeaway: the sampling frame is a modelling assumption. Write down who could not have been sampled before writing down the loss function.

02 Sampling designs

Five designs cover almost every case you meet in a data pipeline, plus one estimator trick for when you cannot sample from the distribution you want.

The classical four

  • Simple random. Every subset of size n equally likely. Unbiased, trivially analysable, and expensive on a distributed store because it touches everything. Rare strata can be missed entirely by luck.
  • Stratified. Partition into strata (class, region, device) and sample within each. Guarantees representation of small groups and reduces variance whenever the strata differ in mean. Always stratify the train/test split by label.
  • Cluster. Sample whole groups — stores, sessions, hospitals — then take everything inside. Cheap to collect, but observations inside a cluster are correlated, so the effective sample size is far below n. Cluster sampling and grouped splits are the same idea used for different reasons.
  • Systematic. Take every k-th row after a random start. One pass, streaming-friendly — and catastrophically wrong if the file has a period matching k (every 7th row is a Sunday).

Reservoir and importance sampling

  • Reservoir sampling draws a uniform sample of size k from a stream of unknown length in one pass and O(k) memory. Fill the reservoir with the first k items; for item i > k pick j uniformly in 1…i and, if j ≤ k, overwrite slot j. Every item ends up in the reservoir with probability exactly k/n.
  • Importance sampling answers a different question: you can only draw from a convenient proposal q but need an expectation under p. Reweight by the likelihood ratio. It is the formal justification for oversampling rare events and correcting later, and it degrades badly when q has thin tails where p does not.

03 Sample size and the error bar

Every estimate from a sample carries a standard error. Knowing it turns "how much data do I need?" from a vibe into arithmetic.

  • The standard error of a mean shrinks as 1/√n. Halving the error costs four times the data — the single most useful fact in sampling, and the reason labelling budgets hit diminishing returns fast.
  • For a proportion the variance is p(1−p), maximal at p = 0.5 and tiny near 0 or 1. That is why estimating a 0.1% fraud rate to within a relative 10% needs a huge n even though the absolute error looks small.
  • Stratification reduces variance because only within-stratum variance survives; the between-stratum component is removed by construction. With proportional allocation you never do worse than simple random sampling.
  • Finite population correction matters when the sample is a large fraction of a bounded population — sampling 80% of a 10,000-row table has far less error than the 1/√n formula suggests.
  • Clustering hurts: the design effect inflates the variance by roughly 1 + (m − 1)·ρ for clusters of size m and intra-cluster correlation ρ. Ten thousand rows from fifty users are not ten thousand independent rows.

Takeaway: before buying more labels, compute the current standard error. If it is already below the decision threshold, spend the budget on better rows — rare classes, hard cases — instead of more rows.

04 The imbalance problem

When 99% of examples are one class, a model scores 99% accuracy by ignoring the rare class entirely. Rebalance below and watch the minority grow.

Class imbalance & resampling Majority vs minority class — and how resampling changes the mix
  • The ratio is not the problem — absolute minority count is. A 1:99 split with 50,000 positives is comfortable; 1:99 with 40 positives is hopeless, because the model has almost no evidence of what the class looks like. Always report the raw minority count alongside the ratio.
  • Why learners collapse: an unweighted loss is dominated by majority terms, so the cheapest gradient direction is to predict the prior. Regularization pushes the same way, and a tree's split criterion sees almost no purity gain from isolating a tiny class.
  • Overlap makes it worse. If the classes are separable, extreme imbalance is survivable; the damage comes from minority points sitting inside majority territory, where the decision boundary has no reason to bend.
Takeaway: diagnose with three numbers — imbalance ratio, absolute minority count, and class overlap. Only the last two tell you whether the problem is fixable with resampling or needs more data.

05 Why accuracy lies, and what to use

  • With 1% positives, "always negative" scores 99% accuracy and catches nothing. Accuracy is a weighted average of per-class recall with weights equal to the priors, so the rare class is invisible in it by construction.
  • ROC-AUC is also optimistic under imbalance. Its x-axis is the false-positive rate, and with a huge negative pool a thousand false positives barely moves it. PR-AUC uses precision, whose denominator is dominated by those same false positives, so it moves.
  • Know the baselines. A random classifier has ROC-AUC 0.5 regardless of prevalence, but PR-AUC equal to the prevalence π. A PR-AUC of 0.15 at π = 0.01 is a 15× lift, not a failure.
  • F-β encodes the asymmetry explicitly: β > 1 weights recall (missing fraud is expensive), β < 1 weights precision (a false alarm wastes an analyst's hour). Balanced accuracy and MCC are prevalence-robust summaries when you must report a single number.
  • Full definitions and the confusion-matrix mechanics live in Model Evaluation.
the metrics that survive imbalanceprecision = TP/(TP + FP) recall = TP/(TP + FN) TNR = TN/(TN + FP) F_β = (1 + β²)·precision·recall / (β²·precision + recall) β = 1 balanced, β = 2 recall-heavy balanced accuracy = (recall + TNR)/2 random baseline PR-AUC = π (the positive prevalence) MCC = (TP·TN − FP·FN) / √((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)) ∈ [−1, 1], 0 = chance
Takeaway: report PR-AUC against the prevalence baseline, plus precision and recall at the operating threshold you will actually deploy. A single threshold-free number never survives a product conversation.

06 Resampling and boundary cleaning

Change the training distribution so the loss stops ignoring the minority. Every method here trades one pathology for another.

Over, under, and synthetic

  • Random oversampling duplicates minority rows. Nothing is lost, but exact duplicates let a flexible model memorise individual points, and duplicates straddling a CV fold boundary leak outright.
  • Random undersampling discards majority rows. Fast and often surprisingly strong on huge datasets, but it throws away information; bagged undersampling (train an ensemble, each member on a different majority subsample) recovers most of it.
  • SMOTE interpolates: pick a minority point, pick one of its k minority neighbours, and place a new point randomly on the segment between them. It fabricates variety instead of duplicates — and it fabricates it inside the convex hull of the minority, so it cannot invent genuinely new regions and will happily interpolate through majority territory when the classes overlap.
  • Variants aim at where the boundary actually is: borderline SMOTE synthesises only near the frontier, ADASYN allocates more synthetic points to minority examples that are hard to classify, and SMOTE-NC handles mixed categorical columns by taking the majority category among neighbours.

Cleaning the overlap

  • Tomek links remove a pair of nearest neighbours from opposite classes, deleting the majority member. This sharpens the boundary rather than balancing the counts.
  • Edited nearest neighbours (ENN) drops any point disagreeing with the majority vote of its neighbours — an aggressive noise filter.
  • The standard combinations are SMOTE + Tomek and SMOTE + ENN: synthesise first, then delete the synthetic points that landed on the wrong side.

Takeaway: resample the training folds only — never validation or test, and never before the split. A test set rebalanced to 50/50 reports precision for a world that does not exist.

07 Cost-sensitive learning and threshold moving

Usually the cleanest fix touches neither the data nor the model: state the cost of each error type and move the decision boundary. Drag the threshold and watch precision and recall trade off at 1% prevalence.

Threshold moving at 1% prevalence per-class score densities · 1,980 negatives vs 20 positives
  • Class weights multiply each example's loss by a per-class factor — mathematically equivalent to duplicating rows, but without materialising them, so no memorisation and no leakage. The usual default is inverse-frequency weighting.
  • Focal loss goes further by down-weighting easy examples of any class, which matters when the imbalance is between easy background and hard foreground rather than between labels.
  • Threshold moving is the most under-used tool on this page. Train a well-calibrated model on the natural distribution, then choose the cut that minimises expected cost. Under symmetric costs that is 0.5; when a miss costs ten times a false alarm, it drops to about 0.09.
  • Pick the threshold on a validation set at production prevalence, then report the test metrics at that fixed threshold. Choosing it on test is just a subtler overfit.
inverse-frequency class weights (K classes, nₖ rows in class k)wₖ = n / (K · nₖ) binary 1:99 ⟹ w₊ = 50, w₋ ≈ 0.505 cost-optimal threshold — predict positive when expected cost is lowerp·C_FN > (1 − p)·C_FP ⟹ t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) C_FN = 10·C_FP ⟹ t* ≈ 0.091 C_FN = C_FP ⟹ t* = 0.5
Takeaway: class weights plus threshold tuning solve most imbalance problems with less machinery and less risk than synthetic resampling. Try them first; reach for SMOTE only when the minority count is genuinely tiny.

08 Calibration after resampling, and reframing

Resampling breaks your probabilities

  • Train on a rebalanced set and the model learns P(y|x) under the resampled prior π′, not the real one. Every predicted probability is inflated — a "0.6" from a 50/50-balanced model trained on 1% data may mean a true risk of about 0.015.
  • That is fine if you only ever rank, and disastrous if the score feeds an expected-value calculation, a cost threshold, or a downstream rule. Anything that multiplies the probability by money needs calibration.
  • Two repairs: an analytic prior correction that maps the score back to the original base rate, or a post-hoc calibrator (Platt scaling, isotonic regression) fitted on a held-out set at natural prevalence. Check the result with a reliability curve and expected calibration error.
undoing the resampled prior — π = true prevalence, π′ = training prevalencelet r = (π/π′) / ((1 − π)/(1 − π′)) p_corrected = r·p / ( r·p + (1 − p) ) π = 0.01, π′ = 0.5, p = 0.6 ⟹ r ≈ 0.0101 ⟹ p_corrected ≈ 0.0150

When to stop rebalancing and reframe

  • Below roughly a few hundred positives, supervised classification runs out of evidence. Anomaly detection reframes the task as one-class: model the majority (isolation forest, one-class SVM, an autoencoder's reconstruction error) and score deviation, using the few positives only to set the threshold.
  • Other reframings: ranking ("score every claim, we will review the top 200 a day") sidesteps thresholds entirely and matches how the output is consumed; weak supervision and heuristic labelling manufacture more positives; active learning spends the labelling budget on the ambiguous region instead of uniformly.
  • Ask what the consumer actually needs — a capacity-constrained review queue wants precision@k, not a probability — before optimising anything.
Takeaway: if you resampled, you owe the downstream consumer a calibration step. If there is almost nothing to resample, change the framing rather than manufacturing minority points that were never observed.

09 Pitfalls & checklist

Pitfalls

  • Resampling before the split — synthetic points derived from a row that lands in validation, so the score is fiction.
  • Rebalancing the test set — precision computed at a prevalence that will never occur in production.
  • Reporting ROC-AUC alone at 0.1% prevalence, where it stays flattering while precision collapses.
  • Comparing PR-AUC across datasets with different prevalence — the baseline moved, so the numbers are not comparable.
  • SMOTE on high-dimensional or one-hot data, where interpolation produces points that are half-way between two categories and physically impossible.
  • Using resampled probabilities in an expected-value rule without prior correction.
  • Ignoring cluster structure: 10,000 rows from 50 users, treated as 10,000 independent observations.

Checklist

  • State the population, the sampling frame, and who could not have been sampled.
  • Stratify the split by label (and by any small subgroup you must not lose); group by entity where rows repeat.
  • Report imbalance ratio and absolute minority count; compute the standard error of the key estimate.
  • Resample inside the CV loop, training folds only; leave validation and test at natural prevalence.
  • Try class weights and threshold moving before synthetic resampling.
  • Choose the threshold from an explicit cost ratio on validation; fix it before touching test.
  • Calibrate if the probability is consumed numerically, and check the reliability curve.
  • Report PR-AUC against the prevalence baseline plus precision/recall at the deployed threshold.
