Dashboards

A dashboard is a standing answer to a recurring question: is the thing we care about healthy, and if not, where should we look? Good ones are designed backwards from a decision — which metric, compared to what, refreshed how often, encoded so the eye reads it accurately, with alerts that fire on signal rather than noise.

01 Start from the decision, not the data

The most common dashboard failure is not a bad chart. It is a page of correct charts that answers no question anyone is asking.

  • Write down, before building: who looks at it, how often, what decision they make from it, and what they do when a number is bad. If the last answer is "nothing", the metric does not belong on the page.
  • Every number needs a comparison to be meaningful: a target, the previous period, the same period last year, a control group, or a peer segment. "Conversion 3.2%" is data; "3.2%, down from 3.6% last week, target 3.5%" is information.
  • Dashboards monitor; analyses explain. A dashboard should tell you that something moved and roughly where; explaining why usually needs an ad hoc analysis or a narrative (see Data Storytelling).
Takeaway: one dashboard, one audience, one primary question. A dashboard trying to serve executives and on-call engineers at once serves neither.

03 KPI design

  • Properties of a good KPI: aligned with an outcome the organization cares about; actionable by the audience; precisely defined; sensitive enough to move within the decision horizon; hard to game.
  • Leading vs lagging. Revenue and churn are lagging — definitive but slow. Activation rate, week-one engagement, and support-ticket volume are leading — noisier, but early. A healthy set pairs them.
  • Primary metric + guardrails. One north-star metric for the goal, plus guardrails that must not regress (latency, cost, complaint rate) — the same structure as experiment metrics in A/B Testing.
  • Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. Average handle time as a target produces agents who hang up on hard calls; pair it with a resolution metric.
  • A definition is a spec. Numerator, denominator, unit, time window, time zone, filters, and treatment of nulls and test accounts. Two dashboards computing "active users" differently is the fastest way to lose trust — centralize definitions in a governed semantic layer or the warehouse model.

04 Metric trees: decomposing a KPI

A metric tree writes the top KPI as an exact function of its drivers. When the top moves, the tree tells you which branch moved it.

multiplicative decomposition and its additive log formrevenue = visitors × conversion rate × orders per buyer × average order value Δ ln(revenue) = Δ ln(visitors) + Δ ln(conversion) + Δ ln(orders/buyer) + Δ ln(AOV)
  • The log form gives each driver an additive share of the change — "revenue fell 8%: traffic −1%, conversion −6%, AOV −1%" — so the investigation starts on the right branch.
  • Additive trees split by segment (revenue = ∑ revenue by region); multiplicative trees split by funnel stage. Mix-shift effects appear when segment weights change: overall conversion can fall even though every segment's conversion rose (Simpson's paradox, see Data Storytelling).
  • Lay dashboards out along the tree: the KPI at the top, its first-level drivers beneath, drill-down to the leaves.

06 Perceptual accuracy of visual encodings

Graphical-perception experiments ask people to judge the ratio between two encoded values. Accuracy depends strongly on the encoding — which is why bar and dot charts beat pies, bubbles and colour scales for quantitative comparison.

  1. Position along a common scale — dot plots, aligned bars, line charts
  2. Position along non-aligned scales — small multiples with identical axes
  3. Length — unaligned bars, stacked segments above the bottom one
  4. Direction / angle / slope — pie slices, slope charts
  5. Area — bubbles, treemaps
  6. Volume and curvature — 3D shapes
  7. Colour saturation / lightness — heatmaps, choropleths
  • Perceived area grows more slowly than true area (an exponent below 1 in psychophysical power-law fits), so bubbles understate large values. Colour is excellent for categories (hue) and for spotting patterns in a heatmap, poor for reading magnitudes.
  • Use the top of the ranking for the comparison that matters most; reserve lower channels for secondary dimensions.
psychophysical power lawperceived magnitude ∝ (physical magnitude)ᵝ β ≈ 1 for length, β < 1 for area
Judge the ratio yourself what percentage of the larger mark A is the smaller mark B?

07 Layout and information hierarchy

  • Inverted pyramid: headline KPIs in a top row, trends and breakdowns in the middle, detail tables at the bottom. A reader who stops after five seconds should still get the answer.
  • Reading order in left-to-right languages starts top-left; put the most important tile there, not the logo.
  • Limit the count. Roughly 5–9 views per screen; more becomes a scavenger hunt. Split into tabs by audience or question, not by data source.
  • Consistency is meaning: one colour per entity across all charts, shared axes in small multiples, identical date ranges, the same number formatting. Inconsistency gets read as a difference.
  • Colour sparingly: grey for context, one accent for what needs attention, red/green reserved for status (with a non-colour cue such as an arrow, for colour-vision deficiency).
  • Group by relationship using proximity and alignment — the Gestalt principles that also underpin decluttering in Data Storytelling.

08 Filters, drill-down, and performance

  • Overview first, then zoom and filter, then details on demand — the classic interaction sequence. Default views must be useful without touching a control.
  • Global vs local filters: a date and region filter applies to every tile; a local filter applies to one chart. Always show the active filter state; a screenshot of a filtered view without its filters is a source of wrong decisions.
  • Drill-down follows the hierarchy of the metric tree or a dimension hierarchy (country → region → store). Drill-through opens a detailed view of one entity.
  • Query cost is a design constraint. Each tile is a query; ten tiles × five filters × fifty viewers is a load test. Pre-aggregate in the warehouse, cache results (see Caching), index filter columns (see Indexing), and budget per-tile latency (≈ 1–3 s feels interactive; beyond ~10 s people stop exploring).
  • Filtering small groups invites noise-chasing: a 40% drop in a segment of 25 users is two people. Show counts or confidence intervals next to rates.

  • Refresh faster than the decision cadence and you mostly display noise. A weekly pricing decision does not need a per-second chart.
  • Show freshness on every dashboard ("data as of 06:00 UTC, complete through yesterday"). A stale dashboard that looks fresh is worse than none.
  • Incomplete periods: today's partial bar always looks like a drop. Mark it, project it, or exclude it.

10 Alerting thresholds and control charts

A threshold is a hypothesis test run on every refresh. Statistical process control turns "is this number weird?" into explicit false-alarm and detection rates.

shewhart control chartcentre = μ̂ , UCL = μ̂ + k·σ̂ , LCL = μ̂ − k·σ̂ (estimated from an in-control baseline period)
false alarms and average run length (independent normal points)α = P(|Z| > k) = 2·(1 − Φ(k)) ARL₀ = 1/α (k = 3 → α ≈ 0.0027, ARL₀ ≈ 370) after a shift of d·σ: p = Φ(−k + d) + Φ(−k − d), ARL₁ = 1/p (k = 3, d = 1 → ARL₁ ≈ 44)
  • Run rules detect small sustained shifts faster than a single 3σ limit. A common set: (1) one point beyond 3σ; (2) two of three consecutive beyond 2σ on the same side; (3) four of five beyond 1σ on the same side; (4) eight consecutive on one side of the centre. Each rule adds detection power and some false alarms.
  • Seasonality breaks naive limits. A weekly cycle inflates σ̂ (limits too wide, real shifts missed) or, if the baseline is short, makes every weekend an alarm. Model the seasonal pattern and chart the residual, or compare to the same weekday.
  • Many metrics multiply false alarms: m independent metrics each alarming at rate α give a per-refresh chance of at least one false alarm of 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ — 100 metrics at 3σ is ≈ 24% per refresh.
  • Require persistence (N consecutive breaches), route alerts by severity, and attach the chart and a runbook link to every alert. Alert fatigue is the failure mode that kills monitoring.

11 ML monitoring dashboards

A model-monitoring dashboard is an operational dashboard with an extra problem: the most important metric — accuracy — arrives late.

  • Slices: show performance by the segments the model is gated on in CI/CD — aggregate stability can hide a collapsing segment.
  • Show label coverage next to delayed performance metrics; an accuracy computed on the 5% of cases labelled so far is biased toward fast-resolving cases.
