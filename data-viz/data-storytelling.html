Data Storytelling

An analysis changes nothing until someone acts on it. Data storytelling is the discipline of turning findings into a decision: knowing who must decide what, choosing the one message that matters, building a narrative around it, and designing charts that make the point honestly — including how uncertain it is.

01 Exploratory vs explanatory

Exploration is you searching a haystack for needles. Explanation is handing someone the needle. Most bad presentations show the haystack.

  • Exploratory work produces dozens of charts, most of them dead ends. It is optimized for the analyst's speed of discovery: defaults are fine, every dimension is plotted.
  • Explanatory work keeps the few charts that support the conclusion and redesigns them for a reader who has seconds, not hours. It is optimized for comprehension and recall.
  • The transition is where analysts most often fail: presenting the process ("first I looked at… then I tried…") instead of the result. The audience wants the needle and enough evidence to trust it.
  • Standing, recurring views belong in a dashboard; one-off findings that must change a decision need a story.

02 Audience and the decision being made

  • Who is the decision-maker (not "the business" — a named person or group)? What do they already know, what vocabulary do they use, and what do they care about — revenue, risk, customers, headcount?
  • What decision is on the table, and by when? Approve, reject, fund, prioritize, stop. If no decision is attached, ask why the analysis is being presented at all.
  • What do you want them to do or believe after the presentation? Write it as a sentence with a verb.
  • What would change their mind? Anticipate the objection a skeptic will raise and have the evidence ready — often a robustness check or a breakdown by segment.
  • Match depth to role: executives want the answer and the stakes; managers want the plan and trade-offs; technical reviewers want the method and the caveats. One deck rarely serves all three — use an appendix.
the one-sentence big idea[your point of view] + [what is at stake] + [what you want the audience to do] "Annual-plan churn doubled after the price change, putting ~$2M of renewals at risk; we recommend restoring the old price for existing customers."

03 Narrative structure: context, conflict, resolution

Stories persuade because they create tension and resolve it. A data story borrows the shape: here is how things were, here is the problem, here is what we should do.

BeatPurposeIn a data story
Context / situationShared starting point the audience agrees with"Renewals had been stable at 85% for two years."
Conflict / complicationWhat changed; why it matters now"Since the March price change, annual-plan renewals fell to 70%."
ResolutionWhat to do and what it achieves"Grandfathering existing customers recovers an estimated 10–13 points."
  • Narrative order vs answer-first. The context → conflict → resolution order builds tension and works for mixed or skeptical audiences. Busy executives often prefer answer-first: lead with the recommendation, then the supporting arguments, then the evidence. Same content, different order.
  • The storyboard test: write one headline per slide on sticky notes, read only the headlines in sequence. If they do not tell the whole story on their own, the deck is not ready.
  • Horizontal and vertical logic: reading the slide titles across tells the story; reading down any slide, the body proves its title.

04 Choosing the one key message

  • So-what test: for every finding, ask "so what?" until the answer touches the audience's goals. "Churn rose" → "so what?" → "renewal revenue drops by $2M" → that is the message.
  • Titles are assertions, not topics. "Churn by plan type" is a label; "Annual-plan churn doubled after March" is a message. The reader should get the point from the title alone; the chart is the proof.
  • Cut ruthlessly. Interesting-but-irrelevant findings go to the appendix. Each extra point dilutes the one that matters.
  • One chart, one point. If a chart supports two messages, make two charts, each emphasizing its own.
  • Quantify in the audience's units: customers, dollars, hours, incidents — not standardized coefficients or log-odds.

05 Declutter, then focus with preattentive attributes

Every element on a chart costs attention. Remove what does not carry information, then spend the attention you saved on the one thing that matters.

  • Declutter: remove chart borders, heavy gridlines, data markers on dense lines, redundant axis labels, 3D effects and background fills. Replace legends with direct labels at the end of each line.
  • Preattentive attributes are processed in well under a second, before conscious attention: colour hue and intensity, size, position, orientation, enclosure, added marks, and motion. Used sparingly, they tell the eye where to look first.
  • Grey is your friend: render context in grey, the focus series in one strong colour. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
  • Gestalt grouping: proximity, similarity, enclosure, and connection make readers see structure without labels — use them deliberately, because readers will infer groupings whether or not you intended them.

06 Annotation: words on the chart

  • Assertion title (the message) plus a smaller subtitle with the metric definition, unit and time range.
  • Event markers: vertical lines for launches, price changes, outages, model deploys. Most "why did it move?" questions are answered by a well-placed event marker.
  • Callouts next to the data point they explain, not in a footnote. Keep them to a sentence.
  • Reference lines and bands for targets, prior-year values, or a normal range, so the reader does not have to remember the comparison.
  • Source and caveats in small text at the bottom: data as-of date, exclusions, known issues. This is where credibility is earned or lost.

07 Misleading charts and how to spot them

Most misleading charts are not lies about numbers; they are true numbers drawn so the visual impression differs from the data. The lie factor quantifies the gap.

08 Simpson's paradox: when the aggregate reverses

A trend that holds in every subgroup can reverse when the subgroups are combined, because the overall rate is a weighted average and the weights differ between the groups being compared.

the aggregate is a mix-weighted averagep_A = ∑ₛ w_A,s · p_A,s p_B = ∑ₛ w_B,s · p_B,s p_A,s > p_B,s for every s does NOT imply p_A > p_B when w_A ≠ w_B
  • Classic shape: treatment A is better for both mild and severe cases, but A is given mostly to severe cases (which have lower success regardless), so A looks worse overall.
  • Which answer is right depends on the causal structure, not the arithmetic. If severity influences both treatment choice and outcome (a confounder), the stratified comparison is the right one. If the stratifying variable is itself caused by the treatment (a mediator), the aggregate may be right.
  • In dashboards it appears as mix shift: every region's conversion improves, the total falls, because traffic moved toward the lowest-converting region.

09 Communicating uncertainty

  • Always show it for estimates and forecasts. A point estimate without an interval invites the audience to over-trust it. The underlying intervals are covered in Estimation & Confidence Intervals.
  • Intervals and bands: error bars for a few estimates; shaded bands for a series; fan charts (nested 50/80/95% bands) for forecasts. Label what the interval means.
  • Frequency framing beats percentages for lay audiences: "about 3 in 10 flagged transactions are fraud" is understood more reliably than "precision 0.3". Quantile dot plots (e.g. 20 dots, each a 5% outcome) make a distribution countable.
  • Hypothetical outcome plots animate draws from the distribution so the viewer experiences the variability instead of reading it.
  • Verbal probability is ambiguous: "likely" is read by different people as anything from about 55% to 90%. Pair words with numbers.
  • No false precision: "conversion up 2.37%" from a noisy test implies certainty you do not have. Round to what the interval supports — "up roughly 1–4%".
  • Say what you do not know and what would reduce the uncertainty (more data, a longer test). Stated limitations raise credibility.
same estimate, three framings"lift = 2.4%" "lift = 2.4% (95% CI 0.8% to 4.0%)" "likely positive; plausibly between about 1% and 4%"

10 Presenting model results to non-technical stakeholders

Stakeholders do not buy an AUC. They buy a change in a business outcome, at a cost, with known risks.

  • Translate metrics into operations. Not "precision 0.82 at recall 0.60" but "of every 100 transactions we flag, about 82 are fraud, and we catch 6 in 10 frauds overall". The metric definitions live in Model Evaluation.
  • Always show the baseline: the current process, a simple rule, or random. "82% precision" means nothing until the audience learns the current rule gets 40%.
  • Put the threshold in business terms. Operating points are trade-offs between missed cases and false alarms; show two or three options with their cost in money or staff hours and let the decision-maker choose.
  • Explain predictions with examples and a small number of drivers ("orders shipped to a new address at night are the strongest signal") — see Model Interpretability. Avoid implying the drivers are causal.
  • Be explicit about limits: where the model was not tested, which populations it may serve worse, how it will be monitored, and what happens when it is wrong.
  • Offline is not online. Present offline results as a forecast and propose the A/B test that will confirm them.

  • Pre-wire key stakeholders before the meeting; surprises in a decision meeting create defensiveness, not decisions.
  • Appendix for methodology, robustness checks and every chart you cut. It answers the skeptic without derailing the main line.
  • Rehearse with someone outside the project. Where they get lost is where the audience will.
  • Checklist: one big idea · assertion titles · each chart decluttered with one highlight · uncertainty shown · baseline and comparison present · limitations stated · explicit ask at the end.

12 Takeaways

  • Start from the decision and the person making it; write the big idea in one sentence.
  • Structure: context → conflict → resolution, or answer-first for executives; titles that are assertions.
  • Declutter, then highlight with one preattentive cue; annotate events and comparisons on the chart.
  • Keep visual integrity: zero-based bars, no dual-axis tricks, one-dimensional scaling, stratify when mix differs — aim for a lie factor near 1.
  • Show uncertainty in the audience's terms, and present models as business trade-offs against a baseline.
