Deep Learning · part 5 · mechanism

Attention

Attention is a learned, differentiable rule for reading from a set of vectors: score every stored vector against a query, turn the scores into non-negative weights that sum to one, and return the weighted sum. It is the mechanism a decoder uses to read a source sentence one step at a time, and the mechanism a Transformer uses to let every token read every other. It exists because of a counting argument. A recurrent encoder compresses a length-L source into one dₕ-dimensional vector, so the average fraction of the input that any decoder can recover is dₕ/(L·dₓ) — a rank, not a training outcome, and therefore not fixable by a better optimiser, a better initialisation or a bigger corpus (the RNN page measures this to six decimal places). Attention removes the cause. Do not summarise the source at all: keep every encoder state, and give the decoder a learned, differentiable rule for deciding which of them to read at each step. The length dependence disappears in one line, and what replaces it is a mechanism with its own arithmetic, its own failure modes and its own hardware economics — which is what the remaining thirty-five sections are about.

The centre of this page is the mechanism, taken apart: the additive alignment score derived from the problem it solves, the dot-product replacement and why it won, the 1/√dₖ scaling derived from the variance of a dot product rather than asserted, the query/key/value abstraction as a differentiable dictionary lookup, and the framing everything else follows from — attention computes its own mixing matrix from the input, where a dense layer's is fixed and a convolution's is fixed and tied. From there: multi-head attention and what a head actually specialises in, masking and the −∞-before-softmax detail that fails quietly, the exact cost in multiply–accumulates and the sequence length at which the quadratic term genuinely starts to matter, what attention cannot do, and the KV cache — the object that decides how much a long-context model costs to run, and the thing MQA, GQA and MLA each attack in a different way.

Every number printed here was recomputed against an independent implementation before it was printed: the variance of the dot product, the backward pass (audited against central differences, worst relative error 1.3e−09), the mask arithmetic in three precisions, the MAC crossovers, the KV-cache sizes, the arithmetic intensities, the rank-collapse curves and the long-context margins. Four widely repeated claims did not survive the recomputation — the standard account of what the missing √dₖ does to the gradient (§06), the standard summary of what stops rank collapse (§22), the headline KV-cache reduction of the low-rank cache design (§28), and “attention dominates the cost” (§19). Each is taught as a discrepancy in place, with the measurement, rather than repeated.

01 What attention is, and the conventions this page never departs from

One sentence first: attention is a learned, differentiable rule for reading from a set of vectors: score each stored vector against a query, turn the scores into non-negative weights that sum to one, and return the weighted sum. The query is the vector doing the reading; the set is what is being read, one vector per member; the weights are a probability distribution over the set; and the output is a convex combination of the set's members — one fixed-size vector, whatever the size of the set.

the mechanism, in the row convention of this page — one vector per ROWquery q a (1 × dₖ) ROW — the state doing the reading the set K = [k₁; …; kₙ] (n × dₖ), one stored vector per row, and V = [v₁; …; vₙ] (n × d_v), what each row hands back if chosen score eⱼ = score(q, kⱼ) one number per stored row normalise αⱼ = exp(eⱼ) / ∑ₖ exp(eₖ) so αⱼ ≥ 0 and ∑ⱼ αⱼ = 1 weighted sum out = ∑ⱼ αⱼ · vⱼ = α·V a (1 × d_v) ROW, for ANY n many queries at once, Q (n_q × dₖ): out = softmax( score(Q, K) )·V (n_q × d_v) with the dot-product score of §04: out = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ )·V
Equation decoder — out = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dₖ )·V
Say it
“out equals softmax of Q K-transpose over root d-k, times V” — dot every query row against every key row, shrink every score by √dₖ, softmax each row of scores into weights, blend the rows of V with those weights.
Shapes
Q (n_q × dₖ) · Kᵀ (dₖ × n_k) → scores (n_q × n_k); weights (n_q × n_k) · V (n_k × d_v) → out (n_q × d_v). The sequence length appears only in the middle matrix — the output is d_v wide no matter how many keys there are, which is the whole point of §01.
Scope
The softmax runs over each row of the (n_q × n_k) score matrix: one query's scores against all keys normalise together, and nothing normalises across queries or across feature dimensions. The √dₖ divides every score; the mask of §15, when present, is added before this softmax.
Plain words
Each query asks every stored entry “how much are you like me?”, converts the answers into percentages that sum to 100%, and returns the percentage-weighted average of the values — a dictionary lookup made differentiable by returning a blend instead of the single best hit.
Tiny example
dₖ = 2, q = [1, 0], keys [1,0] [0,1] [1,1], values [1,0] [0,1] [1,1] scores q·kⱼ/√2 = [0.707, 0, 0.707] weights softmax(scores) = [0.401, 0.198, 0.401] (sum = 1) out ∑ αⱼ·vⱼ = [0.802, 0.599] without the /√dₖ the weights would be [0.422, 0.155, 0.422] — same ranking, sharper; §05–§06 derive why that sharpening becomes saturation as dₖ grows
Why it's here
This is the line every Transformer layer repeats. The softmax keeps the output inside the convex hull of the values (§02); the √dₖ keeps the softmax out of saturation at initialisation (§05–§06); and the (n_q × n_k) score matrix is where the quadratic cost and memory live (§18–§20).

Read it as a soft, differentiable lookup. A hash table takes a key, finds the one entry that matches exactly and returns its value; attention takes a query, scores every entry by how well it matches, and returns the value-weighted blend, with the weights summing to one so the answer lies inside the convex hull of the values. Because each step — the score, the softmax, the sum — is smooth, the gradient of a loss flows back into the query, into the stored vectors and into whatever produced them, and the rule for what to read is learned from the same loss as everything else. In its first home the query is a decoder state, the set is the encoder's hidden states and the read happens once per output token (seq2seq decoding, §02–§03); in its second every token of a sequence is a query against every token of the same sequence (self-attention, §09), which is the sub-layer a Transformer block is built around.

Why it exists: the fixed-length bottleneck

Attention exists because a recurrent encoder's single summary vector loses information in proportion to the source length, and the repair is a counting repair: stop throwing the states away, keep all of them, and let the decoder choose what to read. Everything else on this page is a consequence of taking that seriously.

Restate the bound as the RNN page's bottleneck section derives it. A linear recurrent encoder makes the context vector an explicit linear function of the whole input,

the bottleneck bound (derived on the RNN page)C = h_L = ∑ₖ xₖ · ( U W⁽ᴸ⁻¹⁻ᵏ⁾ ) so C is a linear map from L·dₓ └────┬────┘ numbers into dₕ numbers, and Mₖ rank(map) ≤ min(L·dₓ, dₕ) mean recoverable fraction of the input = min(1, dₕ / (L·dₓ)) ↑ the length is in the DENOMINATOR

Look at where the length sits. L appears once, dividing, because the numerator dₕ is a constant chosen by the architect and the denominator L·dₓ is chosen by the data. Any repair must either put an L in the numerator or take it out of the denominator. Two ways of putting one in the numerator present themselves: grow dₕ with L, which means the model's width depends on its longest input and is therefore not a fixed model; or keep L vectors instead of one. The second is free — the encoder already computed h₁ … h_L and then discarded all but the last.

the repair, and what it does to the boundBEFORE the decoder sees C = h_L dₕ numbers AFTER the decoder sees H = [h₁; h₂; …; h_L] L·dₕ numbers mean recoverable fraction = min(1, L·dₕ / (L·dₓ)) = min(1, dₕ/dₓ) ↑ ↑ the L's CANCEL and dₕ/dₓ does not depend on the source length AT ALL. For any encoder wide enough that dₕ ≥ dₓ, the bound is exactly 1 — every position of the input remains linearly recoverable, at every length.

That cancellation is the motivation for everything on this page, so it is worth being precise about what it does and does not say. It says the information is still there: keeping every encoder state removes the length-dependent loss of information. It does not say the decoder can use it, because H is a variable-length object and a decoder step needs a fixed-size input. The mechanism that turns a variable-length set of vectors into one fixed-size vector, without reintroducing a length-dependent bottleneck, is attention: at each decode step, produce a different convex combination of the L states, with the coefficients computed from the state doing the reading. One fixed-size read per step, but a different read each time, and the choice of read is learned.

The layout law of this page, declared once and never departed from

This is the place a series forks its conventions silently. The feedforward page declared the row convention: a batch X is (N × d), one example per row, and a layer is X·W + b with W of shape (dᵢₙ × dₒᵤₜ). The convolution page extended it to NCHW; the RNN page extended it to time, one time step per row. This page extends it to tokens and heads, in the only way that keeps a row a single vector of features — and the head split is the fork nobody notices, because an interleaved split also runs and never throws — it groups different columns into each head, which is harmless when training from scratch (the two groupings differ only by a fixed permutation of the projection columns) and silently wrong the moment weights trained under the other convention are loaded.

Sources that write q, k, v as COLUMN vectors and z = Wᵠx have the TRANSPOSES of the W above. Both are correct. Mixing them inside one derivation is the standard shape bug, and because W_Q is usually square it does not crash — it runs and computes a different model.
Three more rules, so no figure has to restate them.
  • A superscript ᵀ always means transpose. Exponents are written with an explicit numeral or a named symbol; Wᵈ is a power and Wᵀ is a transpose, and the two never appear ambiguously.
  • n is the sequence length, d is d_model, h is the number of query heads and g the number of key/value heads. g = h is multi-head attention, 1 < g < h is grouped-query, g = 1 is multi-query. Carrying g from the start costs nothing and means §27 is a change of one number rather than a new mechanism.
  • Cost is counted in MACs — multiply–accumulates — matching the convolution page's counting convention and DL.macs from the feedforward page. A source quoting "FLOPs" often means MACs (the convolutional-network literature in particular counts this way); one that means literal floating-point operations — one multiply plus one add — quotes twice these numbers. Every count on this page is a MAC count and every figure says so.

33 Attention as retrieval, and the circuit that made in-context learning legible

§07 framed attention as a differentiable lookup. The strongest evidence that this is what models actually do with it is a two-layer circuit that can be written down exactly, that appears in real trained models, and whose appearance during training coincides with a step change in their behaviour.

The pattern

what an induction head does given a context that contains … A B … … … A predict B "find the previous occurrence of the token I am now looking at, and predict whatever followed it." Note that this requires NO knowledge of what A or B mean, and no weights specific to them. It is a pure copy-with-lookup, and it generalises to tokens the model has never seen together — which is exactly what "learning from the context" means. the circuit, in two layers, and why it cannot be done in one LAYER 1 — a "previous-token" head position s attends to s−1 and COPIES that token's identity into a spare subspace of position s's representation. After layer 1, position s carries two things: "I am token x_s" and "I follow x_{s−1}". LAYER 2 — the induction head query at position t reads "I am token x_t" key at position s reads "I follow x_{s−1}" score(t, s) is large exactly when x_{s−1} = x_t value at position s is "I am token x_s" output = the token that followed the previous occurrence of x_t. WHY TWO LAYERS. Layer 2's key must describe the token BEFORE s, and a single attention layer's key is a function of position s alone. Some earlier layer has to move x_{s−1} into position s first. §14's additivity also matters here: the two heads compose ACROSS layers, through the representation, which is the only way heads can interact at all.

Two facts make this more than a cute construction. First, heads with exactly this behaviour are found in real trained transformers, and they can be identified by a behavioural test — does the head attend from a token to the position after that token's previous occurrence — rather than by inspecting weights. Second, in the small models where this has been tracked step by step, the point in training at which they appear coincides with a visible bump in the loss curve and with a sharp increase in the model's ability to use its context — one of the clearest cases so far of a capability being tied to an identifiable circuit.

A hand-wired induction circuit, and what breaks when you remove half of it the ablation switch is the experiment: delete the previous-token head and the second layer's attention becomes meaningless
What interpretability has and has not established here. Established: that specific, describable algorithms are implemented by identifiable small groups of heads; that the algorithm above is one of them; that §14's factorisation into a "where it reads" matrix W_Q W_Kᵀ and a "what it writes" matrix W_V W_O is the right basis for describing them, because only those products are invariant to the arbitrary basis inside a head. Not established: that most of what a large model does decomposes this way, that a head's role is stable across seeds (§12 measured that it is not), or that reading an attention map is a reliable explanation of a prediction — a head can attend strongly to a token and write almost nothing about it, since where a head reads and what it writes are two different matrices. Model Interpretability owns the general treatment.

34 Convolution, recurrence, attention: three ways to move information between positions

A convolution moves information a fixed distance per layer through a tied, banded matrix; a recurrence moves it one step at a time through repeated application of one matrix; attention moves it any distance in one layer through a weight computed from the content. The whole series has now built all three. Put them side by side on the questions that actually differ — path length, parallelism, cost, what each assumes, how each fails — and the choice stops being a matter of fashion.

The row that changed the field is the one on how the gradient travels. A recurrence connects two positions through a power of one matrix, and the RNN page spends ten sections on the consequences: the gradient decays like ρ^Δ and the entire LSTM exists to make ρ ≈ 1 by construction. Attention connects them through a single weight, so there is no product to decay and the distance does not appear in the gradient path at all. That, and not the parallelism, is the deeper reason attention replaced recurrence — though the parallelism is why it was affordable to find out.

The same information-moving job, three ways the gradient-at-distance panel is measured by perturbation, not by evaluating a formula
The synthesis the feedforward page's inductive-bias section was building towards. Every one of these is a constrained dense layer over positions. A convolution constrains the mixing matrix to be banded and tied. A recurrence constrains it to be generated by powers of a single matrix. Attention removes the constraint on which entries may be non-zero and replaces it with a constraint on how the entries are produced — from the data, by a low-rank bilinear form. The prior gets weaker in that order, and — roughly, since the ordering of convolution against recurrence depends on the task — the sample efficiency falls and the ceiling given enough data rises along with it. That is the same trade the whole domain is organised around, applied to one axis.

35 Pitfalls, in the order they are usually met

Each of these is something that runs, trains, and produces a wrong or worse model without raising an error. That is the selection criterion — things that crash are not on this list.

shapes
A strided head split
Taking every h-th column for head 1 instead of the first dₖ. Both partition the same columns, so nothing throws. Equivalent when trained from scratch (a column permutation apart), silently wrong when loading anyone else's checkpoint. §08.
conventions
Mixing row and column layouts
Half a derivation in xW and half in Wᵀx. Square matrices transpose into square matrices, so it runs. §01 declares one and never leaves it.
masking
Zeroing after the softmax
Rows stop summing to one, so the output is rescaled by however much mass the mask removed — and by more for later positions under a causal mask. A position-dependent shrinkage of the output. §15.
masking
A finite mask constant on an all-masked row
The softmax is shift-invariant, so a constant added to every entry does nothing. The layer attends at full strength to positions that were all supposed to be forbidden. −∞ gives NaN, which is the report you want during development — and the left-padding case has to be handled explicitly. §16.
masking
A padding mask on the query axis
Padding needs silencing as a key, not as a query. Masking the query axis creates all-masked rows and therefore the previous bug. §15.
masking
−1e9 under mixed precision
Not representable in float16, so it silently becomes −∞ on the cast — and a latent all-masked-row bug that was invisible in float32 starts producing NaN, reported as "AMP is unstable". §16.
gradients
Dropping the row-sum term
dS = A ⊙ dA instead of A ⊙ (dA − ⟨dA, A⟩). Adds a component the softmax is blind to; training is slower and nothing indicates why. §17.
gradients
Materialising the softmax Jacobian
Forming the (n × n) Jacobian per row instead of using the two-term identity. Correct, and n times slower on the largest tensor in the model. §17.
scaling
Scaling by √d_model instead of √dₖ
A factor of √h — 2.8× at eight heads, 5.7× at thirty-two. The layer starts more uniform than intended, and on tasks whose answer is a selection it sharpens more slowly. §05.
variants
Expecting GQA to speed up prefill
GQA shrinks the cache and the projections; the quadratic term is untouched. It buys a factor of h/g at decode and close to nothing on a long prompt. §27.
variants
A rolling window that evicts token 0
The first token is the attention sink. Evicting it at the moment the buffer wraps removes the only place a query with no match can put its unavoidable unit of weight, and quality falls off a cliff at exactly that point. Keep a few. §29.
reasoning
"Attention dominates the cost"
At their trained context lengths the quadratic term is 10–24% of a layer's arithmetic for every configuration in §19's table. The binding costs are the n² activation memory at training time and the KV-cache bandwidth at inference. §19, §20, §26.
reasoning
Reading an attention map as an explanation
Where a head reads (W_Q W_Kᵀ) and what it writes (W_V W_O) are different matrices. A head can attend strongly to a token and contribute almost nothing about it. §14, §33.
reasoning
Naming a numbered head's job
Heads are defined up to permutation and their assignment is a property of the run. §12 measured three of six seeds giving a clean split and three not. §12.

36 What to remember, and exactly where the Transformer page begins

The mechanism, in the order it was built

  • Attention exists because a fixed-size summary has a rank. The recurrent encoder's bound was dₕ/(L·dₓ); keeping every encoder state makes it dₕ/dₓ and the length cancels (§01). Everything else is the cost of that cancellation.
  • Score, normalise, blend. Three steps, and the softmax is load-bearing: it puts the weights on the simplex, so the output is a convex combination whose norm cannot grow with n (§02), and it makes a discrete selection differentiable, which is why the gradient has zero variance instead of the sampled estimator's (§03).
  • 1/√dₖ is a z-score. Var[q·kᵀ] = dₖ exactly, for independent zero-mean unit-variance q and k, whatever their distribution (§05). Without it an untrained head at dₖ = 64 already puts 79% of its weight on one arbitrary key, and on a task whose answer is a spread rather than a pick it never recovers (§06).
  • Q, K, V is a differentiable dictionary lookup in which equality has become a learned, asymmetric, rank-dₖ similarity and a hit has become a blend (§07). There is no "not found": the weights sum to one whatever the scores are, which is the origin of attention sinks (§29) and of the long-context dilution of §32.
  • The framing to carry away: attention computes its own mixing matrix from the input (§10). A dense layer's is a fixed parameter and needs n² of them; a convolution's is fixed, banded and tied; attention's has zero free parameters and is a function of X. That is why the same weights run at any length, why the cost is quadratic, and why the prior is weak.
  • Attention is permutation-equivariant, exactly (§11) — measured at 5.6e−16 — so position must be injected. A causal mask breaks the symmetry on its own, which is why the requirement is about quality rather than possibility.
  • Multi-head is a selection bottleneck, not a capacity one (§12): one head is one convex combination per position, so k simultaneous relations need at least k heads, and the loss falls four orders of magnitude between one head and three and not at all between three and four. The head count is free in both budgets; what it costs is the rank d/h of each head's relation (§13).
  • Y = ∑ⱼ Aⱼ X (W_Vʲ W_Oʲ) (§14). Heads are additive and do not interact within a block; each is two low-rank matrices, one for where it reads and one for what it writes. Every later section is stated in this form.
  • Masks are additive and −∞ (§15–§16). A finite constant is a no-op on an all-masked row, by shift invariance; the NaN that −∞ produces is the report you want, and the one legitimate all-masked case (left padding) has to be handled on purpose.
  • The quadratic term is a minority of the arithmetic at every real configuration's trained context — 10–24% (§19) — and overtakes everything else only at n ≈ 2d + nmat·d_ff/2. What binds is memory: h·n² activations at training time (§20) and KV-cache bandwidth at inference (§26), where the arithmetic intensity is h/(g·b) and does not improve with batch.
  • MQA, GQA and MLA are three ways to shrink the same object. Share the key/value heads (h/g less cache, quadratic term untouched), or compress them into a latent ((d_c + dₕᴿ) per token, absorbed into W_Q and W_O so the expansion costs arithmetic and no bytes). §28 checks the headline number and finds it is architecture plus quantisation, not architecture alone.
  • FlashAttention changes the memory traffic and nothing else (§31): same result to round-off, same MAC count, Θ(n²d²/M) HBM accesses instead of Θ(nd + n²), via an online softmax that is exact.
  • What attention cannot do: it supplies no per-token non-linearity (§21 — with one token, any depth of attention is one matrix multiply); stacked alone it collapses every token to the same vector within two or three layers (§22); and its rows fail at both ends of the entropy scale (§23).
The hand-off to the Transformer page, line by line. This page owns the mechanism and stops at the block boundary. The Transformer page owns everything that turns it into a model, and this page has deliberately left each of these open:
  • Positional encoding. §11 proves it is necessary and measures the symmetry breaking. It does not teach sinusoidal, learned, RoPE or ALiBi — those are architectural choices about how to break it, and §32 links to Context-Length Extension for what happens when they are stretched.
  • The residual stream and normalisation. §22 measures that the residual-plus-pre-norm wiring is what stops rank collapse, and §14 says that a head's output is a low-rank write. The Transformer page owns the wiring itself — pre-norm against post-norm, where the LayerNorm goes, the residual scaling.
  • The position-wise FFN. §21 proves it is necessary by showing attention's feature map is exactly linear, and §08 and §19 count its parameters and its MACs because they are needed to say anything about attention's share. The Transformer page owns the FFN as a design object — the width ratio, the activation, gated variants.
  • The block, the stack and the training recipe. Encoder, decoder and encoder–decoder; how many layers; warmup and schedules; dropout placement; weight tying; the whole assembly.
  • What the Transformer page may assume without re-deriving: the layout law of §01, the exact shapes and parameter count of §08, the mask semantics of §15–§16, the backward pass of §17 (audited to 1.3e−09), the MAC and memory accounting of §18–§20, and the KV-cache arithmetic of §24–§28.
