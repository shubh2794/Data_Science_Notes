12 Multi-head attention: why one head is not enough, stated as a limitation

Multi-head attention runs h independent attention operations side by side, each with its own W_Q, W_K, W_V on a dₖ = d/h-wide slice, and concatenates their outputs. It is needed because one head can produce only one weighted average per position — so a position that must read two different things at once needs two heads. The usual justification — "different heads capture different relationships" — is true and unfalsifiable as stated; the sharper version is that capacity argument, and it predicts exactly when adding a head helps and when it does not.

what one head can and cannot produce zᵢ = ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ vⱼ αᵢⱼ ≥ 0, ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ = 1 For output position i, ONE head produces exactly ONE convex combination of the value rows. That is one point in the convex hull of {v₁ … v_n}. So if position i needs to report TWO unrelated facts about two DIFFERENT positions — "the token before me" AND "the first token of the sentence" — one head must place its single weight vector somewhere between them, and the two facts arrive AVERAGED, in the same d_v-dimensional slot, with no way for a downstream position-wise layer to separate them again — unless the two values happen to occupy separate subspaces, which the fourth bullet below returns to. h heads produce h SEPARATE convex combinations, written into h DISJOINT blocks of the concatenated output. Two facts, two slots, no interference.

The reason "just make the single head wider" does not fix it is worth stating explicitly: widening a head widens d_v, giving each value more room, but it does not add a second set of weights. The bottleneck is the single row αᵢ, not the width of what it multiplies. It is a selection bottleneck, not a capacity bottleneck, and more heads is the direct relief; the only indirect one — packing two facts into orthogonal subspaces of a single head's value — is expensive and fragile, as the measurement below shows.

The measurement

A task with a controllable number of relations makes the prediction testable. At every position t of a random symbol sequence, the model must emit three things at once: the token at t−1, the token at position 0, and the token at t itself. Nothing else changes — same width, same optimiser, same seeds, only the head count.

Nothing in the loss asked for that split; it is what minimising the loss produced.
  • The step from one head to three is the whole effect, and the step from three to four is nearly nothing. That is what a selection bottleneck looks like: adding heads helps sharply until there is one per relation the task needs, and then stops. It is not a smooth capacity curve — it is four orders of magnitude from one head to three, and none from three to four.
  • Three heads is the minimum, not always the sufficient number. On four of the six seeds three heads solves the task; on seeds 2 and 6 it stalls at 3.9e−2 and 1.9e−1 and only four heads gets there. The capacity argument gives a lower bound on the head count and says nothing about whether the optimiser will find the assignment — which is a good reason for models to carry more heads than the minimum, and part of why head pruning so often removes a large fraction of them with little loss.
  • Two heads gets part of the way, which is informative rather than sloppy. The position embedding makes the value vector at position t distinguishable from the one at t−1, so a head that splits its weight between two positions can, in principle, write them into orthogonal subspaces of its own output and be decoded again. That is an expensive, fragile solution and the loss shows it: 0.11–0.36 against 4e−5.
  • The clean one-relation-per-head split is common but not guaranteed. Three of the six seeds give one head per relation; the others put two heads on the same relation and pay for it in loss. Heads are interchangeable and the loss does not care which does what — the specialisation is real but its assignment is arbitrary, which is exactly the caveat that interpretability work on real models has to live with (§33).
Three relations, one task: train it live and watch the heads divide the work press train and then move the head slider — the loss falls off a cliff between one head and three, and the attention maps show which head took which relation
The claim to be careful with. "Head k is the syntax head." Heads are only defined up to permutation, and the assignment is a property of the run, not of the architecture. Retrain with a different seed and the same relation appears in a different slot, or split across two. Statements of the form "some head implements X, reproducibly across seeds" are well supported; statements about a numbered head in a particular checkpoint are about that checkpoint.

13 The head-dimension trade: h·dₖ = d, and what it costs each head

Splitting a width-d block into h heads of width d/h keeps the parameter count and the arithmetic almost exactly constant. It is not free — each head gets a strictly weaker relation to express — and the constraint is a rank.

the constraint, and where it bindsStandard choice: dₖ = d_v = d / h so h·dₖ = d parameters d·dₖ·(h+g) + d·d_v·(g+h) = 4d² when g = h — INDEPENDENT of h quadratic MACs 2 n² · h·dₖ = 2 n² d — INDEPENDENT of h So h is FREE in both budgets. What is not free: head j's score matrix is Sⱼ = X (W_Qʲ W_Kʲᵀ) Xᵀ / √dₖ └──────┬──────┘ Mⱼ, a (d × d) matrix with rank(Mⱼ) ≤ dₖ = d/h The relation ONE head can express is a bilinear form of rank at most d/h. Doubling h halves that rank. At h = 32, d = 4096 the rank is 128 — one thirty-second of what an unconstrained (d × d) form could express.

So the trade is: h relations of rank d/h each, against one relation of rank d. The total rank across heads, h · (d/h) = d, is the same either way — which is why the arithmetic does not change — but the partition is different, and it is the partition that matters, because §12 showed the binding constraint is the number of separate weight rows, not the total rank.

Read through the rank argument, that says: as models get wider, they do not want richer individual relations, they want more of them. It is also convenient — a fixed dₖ keeps the √dₖ statistics of §05 and the kernel tiling of §31 unchanged across a whole family.

14 Concat-and-project: what W_O is actually doing

The last step of a multi-head block concatenates the h head outputs into one (n × h·d_v) matrix and multiplies by W_O to get back to width d. Because a concatenation times a matrix is a sum of blocks times sub-matrices, this is the same as adding up h per-head contributions, each with its own (d_v × d) slice of W_O. That looks like plumbing, but cutting W_O into blocks is what makes the block output a sum of per-head contributions, each with its own private output matrix, and every statement about "what a head writes into the residual stream" follows from that one rewriting.

the rewriting, in three lines Y = [ Z₁ | Z₂ | … | Z_h ] · W_O W_O is (h·d_v × d) Cut W_O into h row-blocks of d_v rows each, W_O = [ W_O¹ ; W_O² ; … ; W_Oʰ ]: Y = ∑ⱼ Zⱼ · W_Oʲ each W_Oʲ is (d_v × d) and substituting Zⱼ = Aⱼ Vⱼ = Aⱼ X W_Vʲ: Y = ∑ⱼ Aⱼ · X · ( W_Vʲ W_Oʲ ) ─┬─ └─────┬─────┘ │ the head's OUTPUT–VALUE matrix, (d × d), │ rank ≤ d_v — one object, not two the head's MIXING matrix, (n × n), rank ≤ n what that buys1 · A head is TWO low-rank matrices and nothing else: W_Q W_Kᵀ decides WHERE it reads from rank ≤ dₖ W_V W_O decides WHAT it writes rank ≤ d_v Everything a head does factors through those two, and both are invariant to the arbitrary basis inside the head. Interpretability work reads these two products rather than W_Q and W_K separately, because only the products are well defined (§33). 2 · The heads are ADDITIVE. They do not interact inside the block at all — no head can see another head's output before W_O. Interaction happens only in the NEXT layer, through the FFN and the next attention. "Heads compose across layers, not within one." 3 · Deleting a head is deleting one term of a sum. That is why head pruning is trivial to implement and why measuring a head's causal effect by ablation is meaningful: the arithmetic really is a sum.

The second point is the one people get wrong most often, and it has a testable consequence: within a single attention block, the heads are exactly parallel and non-interacting. A claim like "head 3 uses head 7's output" cannot be about one layer; it is necessarily about a path through at least two. The figure makes the additivity concrete by reconstructing the block output as a running sum of head terms.

The block output as a running sum of per-head terms add heads one at a time and watch the reconstruction converge — the residual reaches exactly zero at the last head, because the identity is exact, not an approximation
Where this is going. The decomposition Y = ∑ⱼ Aⱼ X (W_Vʲ W_Oʲ) is the form in which §22's rank-collapse result is proved, the form in which §33's induction-head circuits are described, and the form in which head pruning and head-level interpretability are defined. It is worth being able to write it from memory. The Transformer page adds the residual stream around it, at which point "what a head writes" becomes literally an addition to a running sum that every later layer reads.

15 Masking: the two kinds, and what each is actually protecting

A mask forbids some query–key pairs. There are two of them in practice, they protect against completely different failures, and they are almost always applied together and combined by addition.

causal / look-ahead
Protects the training objective
Position i may read only j ≤ i. Without it, a language model trained to predict token i+1 can simply read token i+1 — the loss goes to zero and the model has learned nothing. It is not a modelling choice; it is what makes the objective non-trivial.
padding
Protects the batch
Sequences in a batch have different lengths, so short ones are padded to the longest. Padding tokens are an artefact of the batching, not data. Without a mask they contribute to every weighted sum, and a model's output for a sentence would depend on which other sentences were in its batch.
window / sparsity
Protects the budget
A structural mask that forbids far-away pairs to save the n² cost (§29). Mechanically identical to the other two; motivated by arithmetic rather than by correctness.
the implementation, and the two properties that make it work masked scores S′ = S + M M ∈ {0, −∞}, ADDED, never multiplied weights A = softmax(S′) rows still sum to 1 over what survives why ADDITIVE and −∞ rather than multiplying A by a 0/1 mask afterwards: · softmax( … , −∞ , … ) gives EXACTLY 0 at that entry, and the remaining entries are renormalised to sum to 1 automatically. · multiplying AFTER the softmax gives zeros too, but the surviving weights no longer sum to 1 — the output shrinks by the fraction of mass that was removed, which is a different (and length-dependent) operation. It is a real bug and it does not throw. combining two masks: M = M_causal + M_pad, and −∞ + 0 = −∞, −∞ + −∞ = −∞. The masks, drawn, combined, and applied switch to multiply after the softmax to see the bug that does not throw: the rows stop summing to one and the readout says by how much
The causal mask is not only a mask — it is also the reason the KV cache works. Because position i can never read position j > i, the key and value of a token are final the moment it is produced: no future token will change them. Without causality, appending a token would invalidate every previously computed attention weight and generation would cost O(n²) per step instead of O(n). §24 builds on this directly. It is also why a bidirectional encoder cannot be decoded incrementally at all.

16 −∞ against "a large negative number": the detail that fails quietly

The mask is implemented by adding −∞ to the forbidden scores before the softmax; exp(−∞) = 0, so those weights are exactly zero and the rest renormalise among themselves. Every implementation has to turn "forbidden" into a number, and the two common choices are −Infinity and a large finite constant such as −1e9, usually described as equivalent. They are equivalent whenever a row has at least one permitted key, and dangerously different when it has none — and the difference is a one-line property of the softmax.

Where they agree: how negative is negative enough

A masked weight is exp(s + C − max) divided by the row sum. It is exactly zero once the exponential underflows, which happens at a gap that depends only on the floating-point format:

The "large negative constant" is fine — as long as the row has at least one unmasked entry.

Where they disagree: an all-masked row

This is the case, and the reason is one line of algebra that everyone knows and nobody applies here.

softmax is SHIFT-INVARIANT, which is normally a feature softmax(s + c·1) = softmax(s) for any scalar c That identity is why the stable implementation subtracts the row max. It is also why a FINITE mask constant is a NO-OP on a fully masked row: adding the SAME constant to EVERY entry changes NOTHING. measured — the row [−1.0, 0.5, 2.0, −0.3] with every entry masked mask = −∞ softmax → [ nan nan nan nan ] sum = nan mask = −1e9 softmax → [0.036257 0.162492 0.728239 0.073012] sum = 1 mask = −1e4 softmax → [0.036257 0.162492 0.728239 0.073012] sum = 1 and softmax of the UNMASKED row [−1.0, 0.5, 2.0, −0.3] is [0.036257 0.162492 0.728239 0.073012] — the SAME NUMBERS. The finite mask did not weaken the forbidden positions; it did not touch them. The layer attends, at full strength, to positions that were all supposed to be forbidden, and reports a perfectly normal-looking distribution while doing it.
The right conclusion, which is not "always use −∞". With −∞ an all-masked row produces NaN, the loss becomes NaN, and training stops immediately and loudly — pointing straight at the bug. With a finite constant, training continues and quietly optimises a model that attends to padding. During development the NaN is the behaviour you want, because the quantity really is undefined: "the weighted average over an empty set" has no value. This page's DL.softmaxRows returns NaN on an all-masked row on purpose and says so in its comment.

One case is legitimate rather than a bug, and it has to be handled deliberately: left-padded batches. When short prompts are padded on the left so that generation can append on the right, a padding query under a causal-plus-padding mask sees only padding keys — a genuinely all-masked row. Its output is discarded, but a NaN there does not stay put: it enters the next layer's keys and values for that position, and NaN + (−∞) is NaN, so the next layer's softmax poisons every real row too. Production implementations therefore either guarantee that such rows attend to something (letting the padding row attend to itself, or un-masking fully masked rows entirely, both harmless because the output is discarded), or use the finite constant knowingly for exactly those rows. The rule is: no all-masked row may reach the softmax, and if one must exist, decide explicitly what it attends to.

The habit worth breaking. Writing scores.masked_fill(mask, -1e9) because "-inf causes NaN". It does, and that is the point. The NaN is a report that some row has no keys to attend to, which is nearly always a bug in the mask construction — a zero-length sequence, a query-axis padding mask, a sliding window that starts before the sequence does, a chunk boundary that leaves a row empty — and occasionally the left-padding case above, which deserves explicit handling rather than a blanket constant. Replacing −∞ with −1e9 everywhere silences the report and keeps the bug. The correct fix is upstream: never let an all-masked row reach the softmax without deciding what it should attend to.

17 The backward pass, derived and audited against central differences

Attention has one non-obvious gradient in it — the one through the row-wise softmax — and it is the one every hand-written implementation gets subtly wrong. Here it is derived, and then checked the way the convolution page and the RNN page checked theirs.

The three ways to get it wrong

error 1
Forgetting the row-sum term
Writing dS = A ⊙ dA. This is the gradient of a normalisation-free exponential, not of a softmax. It is not orthogonal to the ones vector, so it pushes all scores up or down together — a direction the softmax is blind to — and training becomes mysteriously slow rather than broken.
error 2
Applying the scale once
The 1/√dₖ multiplies the score, so it must appear in ∂L/∂S exactly once, before both ∂L/∂Q and ∂L/∂K. Applying it to only one of them gives a gradient that is correct in direction for Q and wrong by √dₖ for K — an 11× error at dₖ = 128, which Adam will partially hide.
error 3
Reusing the mask
Adding the mask again in the backward pass, or re-masking ∂L/∂S. Unnecessary: A is already exactly zero there, so ∂L/∂S is too. Harmless with −∞; with a finite constant it silently multiplies the leaked gradient by a second copy of the mask.

Every parameter, analytic against numeric — and each of the three errors, injected the fault selector is the useful part: pick an error and watch which parameter's residual jumps and by how much
Why this audit is worth the trouble every time. All three faults above train. Fault 1 gives a gradient with a spurious component along the ones vector, which the softmax ignores in the forward pass, so the model still improves — just more slowly, and no loss curve will tell you why.
