02 Alignment: the original formulation, derived from what it has to do

The decoder needs one fixed-size vector per step, and it has L of them to choose from. Write down the three requirements that choice must satisfy and the 2015 formulation is essentially forced.

At decode step i the decoder has a state sᵢ₋₁ — everything it has produced so far — and it has the encoder states h₁ … h_L. It must produce a single vector cᵢ to condition the next output on. Three requirements:

requirement 1
Fixed size, any L
cᵢ must have a shape the decoder can consume, and that shape cannot depend on L — otherwise the decoder's weights depend on the input length and it is not a model.
requirement 2
Different at every step
If cᵢ were the same for all i we would be back at the recurrent encoder's single summary and the bound would return. The read must depend on sᵢ₋₁.
requirement 3
Differentiable end to end
There is no supervision for which source word to look at. The choice must be learned from the translation loss alone, which means the gradient has to flow through the choosing.

From the requirements to the three steps

Requirement 1 forces a reduction over the L states. The simplest linear reduction that keeps each state intact — each hⱼ enters scaled by a single number rather than being passed through its own matrix — is a weighted sum with scalar weights, and that is the choice the original formulation made. Requirement 2 forces the weights to be a function of sᵢ₋₁. Requirement 3 forces that function to be smooth. Put together:

the mechanism, in the three steps it has always had score eᵢⱼ = a( sᵢ₋₁ , hⱼ ) for every j = 1 … L normalise αᵢⱼ = exp(eᵢⱼ) / ∑ₖ exp(eᵢₖ) so αᵢⱼ ≥ 0, ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ = 1 blend cᵢ = ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ · hⱼ ONE vector, dₕ wide, any L and the alignment function a( · , · ) that the 2015 model useda(s, h) = vᵀ · tanh( s·W_a + h·U_a ) ← ADDITIVE / concat scoring shapes, in the row convention of §01: s (1 × d_s) W_a (d_s × d_a) h (1 × dₕ) U_a (dₕ × d_a) v (d_a) a single vector the score is a SCALAR: a one-hidden-layer network with d_a units, one output, no output bias, applied to the pair (s, h). "vᵀ tanh(·)" is the dot product of v with the d_a hidden activations; in the row convention it is tanh(s·W_a + h·U_a) · v.
The standard vocabulary, since other sources use it. The scores eᵢⱼ are the alignment scores (or energies) and the function a(·,·) is the alignment model or score function; the normalised αᵢⱼ are the attention weights (alignment weights); cᵢ is the context vector. Scoring with a small network, as above, is additive attention (also "concat" or "MLP" attention); scoring with a dot product, as §04 introduces, is multiplicative or dot-product attention. Because the query comes from the decoder and the keys from the encoder, this wiring is encoder–decoder attention, which §09 renames cross-attention; when queries and keys come from the same sequence it is self-attention (originally "intra-attention"). Selecting by similarity of content rather than by index is content-based addressing, the term the differentiable-memory literature uses for the same operation. The original formulation scores against the previous decoder state sᵢ₋₁; a common later variant scores against the current state sᵢ and feeds the context into the output layer instead of the recurrence — a plumbing difference, not a different mechanism.

Three design decisions hiding in the formula

Three things about that formula are worth naming, because each is a design decision and not a detail.

  • It is a small MLP, and it is the smallest one that can work. A linear score (sW + hU)·v would be additively separable, and each of its two terms is useless on its own: the sW·v term is the same for every key j, so it shifts a whole softmax row by a constant and cancels; the hU·v term does not depend on the query at all, so it gives every decode step the same weights — a fixed prior over source positions, which is exactly what requirement 2 forbids. The tanh is what makes the score depend on the interaction between the query and the key rather than on each separately. §04's dot product gets the interaction a different way.
  • The two projections have different input widths on purpose. d_s and dₕ need not match: additive attention can score a query against a key of a completely different dimension, which is why it survives in settings where the two sides are genuinely different objects. The dot product cannot; it requires the same width dₖ on both sides, which is one reason W_Q and W_K exist (§07 gives the other: a learned, asymmetric notion of similarity).
  • The softmax is what makes the choice a choice. It is not there to be probabilistic. It is there because it maps L unconstrained real numbers onto the simplex — non-negative and summing to one — so that cᵢ is a convex combination of the states and therefore lives in their convex hull, at the same scale as any single state, for any L. Replace the softmax with, say, raw scores and cᵢ's norm grows with L; replace it with a sigmoid applied per position and the sum grows with L too. §06 shows what else the softmax quietly guarantees, and §23 shows what it costs.
One decode step, computed: score every source position, normalise, blend step the decoder along and watch c move — the bars are the weights, the arrow is the blend, and the bottom strip is the weight matrix the whole decode builds up

The alignment matrix

The bottom strip of that figure is the object the mechanism is usually judged by: the alignment matrix α, one row per decode step and one column per source position. Nothing in the loss asks for it to be interpretable. It becomes roughly monotone on language pairs with similar word order because that is what minimises the translation loss, and it stops being monotone exactly where the languages disagree about order — which was early evidence that the mechanism was learning something structural rather than memorising. §33 revisits how much weight that kind of evidence can bear.

Where this landed historically. The formulation above was introduced to fix translation quality on long sentences, and the effect it fixed is measurable: without it, quality falls off sharply once the source is longer than the sentences the model was trained on, because the fixed summary is being asked to hold more than §01's bound permits; with it, the fall-off largely disappears. Machine Translation owns that story and the evaluation metrics that measured it.

03 What "soft" alignment means, and why the hard version is a different problem

Soft attention reads every source position and weights each one; hard attention picks a single position. The soft version is differentiable end to end, because a weighted sum has a gradient with respect to its weights and the softmax has a gradient with respect to its scores; the hard version is not, because an argmax has zero gradient almost everywhere. That is why "soft" is not a hedge or an approximation of something better: it is the choice that makes the selection trainable by gradient descent at all, and the alternative is not a slightly worse attention — it is a reinforcement-learning problem.

The honest way to see this is to write down the hard version and try to differentiate it. Hard selection picks one source position:

hard selection, and the wall it hitsSOFT cᵢ = ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ hⱼ α = softmax(e) ∂cᵢ/∂e exists everywhere HARD cᵢ = h_{j*} where j* = argmaxⱼ eᵢⱼ ∂ j* / ∂ e = 0 almost everywhere, and UNDEFINED on the ties. The argmax is piecewise constant. Its gradient carries no information about how to change the scores, because a small change in e leaves j* alone — until it does not, and then it jumps. There is nothing for the chain rule to propagate. the two standard escapes from the hard version, what each costs, and the third option — which is soft attention1 · SAMPLE and use a score-function estimator (REINFORCE): j ∼ α ∇ E[loss] = E[ loss · ∇ log α_j ] unbiased, and its VARIANCE grows with L and with the loss scale. It needs a baseline, many samples, or both. 2 · RELAX: keep the sample but reparametrise it (Gumbel-softmax / concrete), giving a biased but low-variance gradient, with a temperature that has to be annealed. 3 · DO NOT CHOOSE. Take the convex combination and let the WEIGHTS carry the choice. Gradient is exact, variance is zero, no extra hyper-parameter. This is soft attention, and it is the reason the mechanism was usable immediately.

The figure measures the difference rather than asserting it. It takes one tiny selection problem and computes the gradient two ways: exactly, by differentiating the soft mechanism, and by sampling, as the hard mechanism with a score-function estimator must. The right panel plots the spread of the sampled estimate of the whole gradient vector, relative to the norm of the exact gradient, against the number of candidates — and that relative spread is the quantity that grows like √L.

The trade being made, stated plainly. Soft attention reads everything and weights it. That is more expensive than reading one position — O(L) work instead of O(1) — and it is the origin of the quadratic cost in §18. Hard attention would be cheaper at inference, and the analogous discrete choice is what a retrieval system makes when the candidate set is enormous (millions of documents, where reading everything is not an option). The reason soft won inside the model is not that it is cheaper; it is that it turns a discrete decision into a smooth one, and gradient descent can only optimise the second kind.
The common phrasing. "Soft attention is a differentiable approximation to hard attention." A legitimate reading, but the emphasis is the wrong way round for learning the mechanism. Hard attention is the special case that soft attention reaches in the limit of infinitely sharp scores, and it is the one that loses the property being relied on. Calling the smooth thing an approximation of the non-smooth thing makes the softmax look like a compromise instead of the load-bearing part.

04 Score functions: additive, dot, general — and one that cannot do what the others do

A score function takes a query and a key and returns one number. The two families are additive — a small network on the pair, vᵀtanh(qW + kU) — and multiplicative — a dot product q·kᵀ, optionally through a learned matrix or divided by √dₖ. The dot product won because a whole matrix of scores is one matrix multiplication. Four candidates, all in use, are tabulated below. They differ in parameter count, in cost, and — this is the part usually skipped — in what they can represent at all: the bias-free additive scorer has an exact symmetry that makes it incapable of expressing a similarity, and it is the one everybody writes first.

The cost column is the reason the dot product won, and it is a bigger reason than the arithmetic suggests. An n×n matrix of additive scores is n² independent little MLPs; an n×n matrix of dot-product scores is one matrix multiplication, which is the operation modern accelerators are built around. The additive form does more arithmetic and does it in the shape hardware is worst at. §26 makes the general version of that argument.

The part that is not about cost: an odd scorer cannot express a similarity

So the bias-free additive scorer cannot represent the dot product — not approximately, not with more units, not with better training. Its whole even component is identically zero. Why this matters in practice and not only on paper. Frameworks differ on whether the additive alignment layer carries an internal bias, and the difference is usually presented as a nicety. It is not: without it, the scorer is constrained to a function class that excludes the most obvious notion of "these two vectors are similar". It can still solve alignment tasks — the figure in §02 shows a bias-carrying version doing exactly that — but it is solving them with a strictly odd function, and if your task's true score is even, the model must build it out of interactions with whatever asymmetry the inputs happen to have. The fix is one bias vector.
Four score functions on the same pairs: what each can represent, and what each costs the internal-bias slider is the whole point — take it to zero and the additive curve sits flat at 1.0 for every width; raise it and the same architecture starts working
The usual summary. "Additive and multiplicative attention perform similarly; multiplicative is faster, so we use it." The first half is true only once both are trained, and it hides the fact that they are different function classes. The original comparison found that the additive form actually outperformed the unscaled dot product at large dₖ — which is what motivated the scale in the first place (§05), and is the opposite of "similar". Once the scale is added they become comparable in quality, and then speed decides. The honest ordering is: representational difference first, scale second, speed third.

05 Why the 1/√dₖ is there, derived rather than asserted

A dot product of two dₖ-dimensional vectors with independent unit-variance components has variance dₖ; dividing by √dₖ makes every score have variance 1 regardless of the head width, so the softmax starts at the same, gentle sharpness for any dₖ. That is the whole reason the factor is there. It is therefore not a tuning constant and not "for numerical stability": it is the exact standardisation of a sum of dₖ independent products, and the derivation takes three lines.

Take the assumption the scale is derived under and state it plainly: the components of q and k are independent, mean 0, variance 1. That is not true of a trained model, but it is close to true at initialisation, which is when the decision has to be made — a layer that saturates at step zero never gets to step one.

the variance of a dot product, exactlyq·kᵀ = ∑_{c=1..dₖ} q_c k_c with q_c, k_c independent, E = 0, Var = 1 E[ q_c k_c ] = E[q_c]·E[k_c] = 0 independence Var[ q_c k_c ] = E[q_c²k_c²] − 0 = E[q_c²]·E[k_c²] = 1 independence again The dₖ terms are independent, so the variances ADD: E[ q·kᵀ ] = 0 Var[ q·kᵀ ] = dₖ sd[ q·kᵀ ] = √dₖ so the scale is a z-score and nothing more q·kᵀ / √dₖ has mean 0 and variance 1, for EVERY dₖ. That is the whole derivation. The factor is √dₖ because a sum of dₖ independent unit-variance terms has standard deviation √dₖ, the same reason a random walk of dₖ steps travels √dₖ. Not dₖ, not √d_model, not √(dₖ·h) — √dₖ, the width of ONE head's key.

What the derivation assumes, and what it is worth when the assumption fails. It needs three things: q and k independent of each other, every component with mean 0 and second moment 1, and the components of at least one of the two vectors uncorrelated with one another (then Var[∑ q_c k_c] = ∑_{a,b} E[q_a q_b]·E[k_a k_b] = ∑_c 1·1 = dₖ). It does not need Gaussian, identically distributed or even continuous components — the figure checks that by swapping in a uniform, a two-point and a heavy-tailed distribution and getting the same law. What it genuinely needs is the independence across components, and that is what training typically breaks: q and k come to live in a lower-dimensional, correlated subspace, and the effective number of independent directions falls below dₖ. The scale is therefore right at initialisation and approximate afterwards — which is exactly why §06's failure mode is a training-time phenomenon rather than an initialisation one, and why some models add an explicit normalisation of q and k on top of the fixed scale.

06 What actually goes wrong without the scale — and what does not

Without the scale, an untrained head's scores have standard deviation √dₖ instead of 1, so at the usual head widths the softmax row is already nearly one-hot on an arbitrary key before any learning, and most query–key pairs receive almost no gradient. With the scale, every row starts near uniform and every pair is trainable. That is what to take away. The standard sentence — "for large dₖ the dot products grow, pushing the softmax into regions where it has extremely small gradients" — is the same fact, but every clause of it is worth checking, and the check sharpens the conclusion: the typical gradient really does collapse, the aggregate gradient does not, and the damage that actually shows up in training depends on what the task wants the attention to look like.

Read the first column before anything else. At dₖ = 64 — the head width of the original design — an untrained unscaled head already puts 79% of its weight on one randomly chosen key, and one row in five is more than 99% committed. The layer is not "slightly sharp"; it is a hard, arbitrary selector that has learned nothing. That is the first-order damage, and it is a statement about the forward pass. The scaled column is at 0.107 at every dₖ — what a softmax of 64 unit-variance scores gives: the largest of 64 standard normals is about 2.4, so the top weight is roughly e^2.4 / (64·e^0.5) ≈ 0.10, seven times the uniform 1/64 = 0.0156 but nowhere near a decision.

Now the gradient claim, checked

The softmax Jacobian is the object the claim is about:

the softmax Jacobian, and why it dies at BOTH ends ∂αᵢ/∂sⱼ = αᵢ(δᵢⱼ − αⱼ) J = diag(α) − ααᵀ α uniform (αᵢ = 1/n): ‖J‖_F = √(1/n − 1/n²) → 0 as n grows α one-hot: J = 0 EXACTLY in between: ‖J‖_F is largest at MODERATE concentration — for α = (½, ½, 0, …) it is exactly ½. So ‖J‖_F is not monotone in the sharpness, and therefore not monotone in dₖ.

That non-monotonicity is visible in the table and it is where the standard sentence over-reaches. The mean ‖J‖_F rises from 0.184 at dₖ = 1 to 0.295 at dₖ = 16, and at dₖ = 64 it is still 0.210 — larger than the scaled value of 0.184. On the mean, the unscaled head at the original design point has a bigger softmax gradient, not a smaller one.

The claim is nonetheless true, and the table shows in which sense. Look at the median and the 5th percentile. At dₖ = 1024 the median ‖J‖_F is 3e−04 against the scaled 0.180 — a factor of 600 — and the 5th percentile is 1.3e−15, which is not small but at the level of double-precision round-off. The mean is being held up by the minority of rows that happen not to have saturated. So:

The precise version of the claim. Without the scale, the typical query–key pair receives essentially no gradient — the median softmax Jacobian norm falls by a factor of 600 at dₖ = 1024 and the bottom 5% by fourteen orders of magnitude — while the total gradient norm stays large, concentrated in a few unsaturated rows. Training does not stall silently. It becomes a high-variance, nearly discrete search, in which most of the attention map is frozen and a handful of rows carry all the signal. "Gradients vanish" describes the distribution's body and mis-describes its total.

The experiment that decides it, on one task family: train with and without, on tasks that need different answers

Statistics of an untrained layer are suggestive, not decisive. The decisive test is to train the same head twice, changing nothing but the scale — and the result depends on what the task wants the attention to look like, which is why single-benchmark comparisons of this question disagree with each other.

What a single benchmark would have told you. Run the comparison on retrieval and the scale looks useless or harmful. Run it on averaging and it looks essential. Both results are real and they are the same experiment. The scale's function is to make the layer start undecided. That is worthless when the answer is a decision and decisive when it is not — and a deep model does both in different heads (§12), which is why the scale is worth keeping even though many heads would be fine without it.
What is done instead, when the scale is not enough. The √dₖ factor is fixed at initialisation-time statistics and §05 showed why it drifts: training correlates the components of q and k, and the effective dimension of the dot product falls below dₖ. Two responses are common. A learned scalar multiplying the scores, so the model can pick its own sharpness — cheap, and it can be learned into saturation too. Normalising q and k before the dot product. In its strict form — projecting each onto the unit sphere ("cosine attention") — every score is bounded in [−1, +1] and saturation is impossible without an explicit learned temperature; the more common "QK-norm" applies a LayerNorm or RMSNorm with learned gains to q and k, which bounds the growth of the scores rather than the scores themselves. Either is a genuine architectural change and belongs to the Transformer page's account of what goes into a block. §23 measures the failure mode both are aimed at.
