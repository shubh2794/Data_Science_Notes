Classical Neural Networks · part 3 of 10

Boltzmann Machines & RBMs

A Hopfield network (part 2) rolls downhill on an energy function and stops. Add noise, and let each unit turn on with a probability that depends on how much it would lower the energy. The network then never stops. It keeps wandering, and it spends time in each state in proportion to e−E. That change turns a memory into a probability model: a Boltzmann machine defines a distribution p(x) = e^(−E(x))/Z over binary vectors, and learning means reshaping the energy so that the data are the likely states. The difficulty is Z, a sum over exponentially many states, which hides inside every gradient. This page follows the chain of fixes that made these models trainable. The restricted Boltzmann machine drops the connections within each layer so its conditionals factorise. Block Gibbs sampling exploits that. Contrastive divergence replaces an intractable expectation with a few sampling steps. Stacking RBMs gave the deep belief network of 2006, the result that restarted deep learning. The page ends with where these ideas live now: score functions, diffusion models and contrastive losses all avoid Z in their own way.

01 From deterministic to stochastic units

A stochastic binary unit does not take the sign of its input. It switches on with a probability given by a sigmoid of that input, divided by a temperature T. At T → 0 the sigmoid becomes a step, and the unit is a Hopfield unit again. At large T it is a fair coin. In between, the unit usually moves downhill in energy and sometimes moves uphill, and those uphill moves are what let the network escape shallow minima.

units and energy (0/1 coding; symmetric W, zero diagonal)sᵢ ∈ {0, 1}, E(s) = −∑ᵢ<ⱼ wᵢⱼ sᵢ sⱼ − ∑ᵢ bᵢ sᵢ energy gap of unit i: how much lower the energy is with it onΔᵢ = E(sᵢ = 0) − E(sᵢ = 1) = ∑ⱼ wᵢⱼ sⱼ + bᵢ (exactly the Hopfield local field) the stochastic updatep(sᵢ = 1 | s₋ᵢ) = σ(Δᵢ / T) = 1 / (1 + e^(−Δᵢ/T)) limitsT → 0: sᵢ = 1 iff Δᵢ > 0 — the deterministic Hopfield rule T → ∞: p = ½ regardless of the input the ±1 coding gives the same thing with a factor of 2p(sᵢ = +1) = σ(2hᵢ / T), because flipping a ±1 unit changes sᵢhᵢ by 2hᵢ

Run these updates one unit at a time, forever, and the network is a Markov chain on the 2ᴺ states. The next section shows that this chain has a precise long-run distribution, the Boltzmann distribution, and that the sigmoid is exactly the conditional distribution of a unit under it, so updating by the sigmoid is Gibbs sampling. Lowering T slowly while the chain runs is simulated annealing (Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi, 1983). It is a way to find low-energy states, the global minimum in the limit of infinitely slow cooling, and it was the original motivation for putting noise into Hopfield networks.

02 The Boltzmann distribution: energy → probability

The stochastic updates above, applied to any unit in turn, leave one distribution invariant: p(s) ∝ e^(−E(s)/T). Low energy means high probability. Energy differences, not absolute energies, set probability ratios, and the temperature sets how strongly those differences matter.

the Boltzmann (Gibbs) distributionp(s) = e^(−E(s)/T) / Z, Z = ∑ₛ′ e^(−E(s′)/T) (Z is the partition function) ratios need no Zp(s) / p(s′) = e^(−(E(s) − E(s′))/T) why the sigmoid is the right rule — the conditional of the Boltzmann distributionp(sᵢ=1 | s₋ᵢ) = e^(−E₁/T) / (e^(−E₁/T) + e^(−E₀/T)) = 1 / (1 + e^(−(E₀−E₁)/T)) = σ(Δᵢ / T) so updating a unit by sampling its conditional is Gibbs sampling, whose stationary distribution is p(s)and the chain is ergodic for T > 0 (every state reachable), so it converges to p(s) from any start convention used from here onT = 1 (absorb it into the weights): p(s) = e^(−E(s)) / Z

Worked numbers. Two units, w₁₂ = 2, b₁ = b₂ = −1. The energies are E(00) = 0, E(10) = E(01) = 1, and E(11) = −2 + 1 + 1 = 0. At T = 1 the unnormalised weights are 1, e⁻¹ = 0.368, 0.368 and 1, so Z = 2.736 and p(00) = p(11) = 0.366, p(10) = p(01) = 0.134. The positive weight has made the units agree: the probability they match is 0.73, although each unit alone is on only half the time. At T = 0.5 the weights become 1, e⁻² = 0.135, 0.135, 1, and p(00) = p(11) = 0.440. At T → ∞ all four states tend to ¼.

Energy → probability for a 3-unit Boltzmann machine, and the temperature knob slide T down to 0.1 and all mass piles onto the lowest energies; slide it up and the distribution flattens to 1/8; press Gibbs to check a sampler agrees

The frustrated triangle is worth a look. Every pair wants to disagree, but three binary units cannot all disagree pairwise. There is no configuration that satisfies every weight, so several states tie for lowest energy. At low temperature the distribution splits evenly between them. This is the spin-glass frustration that §08 of part 2 blamed for spurious states, now visible as a probability distribution.

03 The partition function problem

Z sums over every configuration. For 100 units that is 2¹⁰⁰ ≈ 10³⁰ terms. Probability ratios never need it, but any absolute probability does, and so does the gradient of the log-likelihood. That makes Z the central obstacle for every undirected model on this page.

log-likelihood of one data vector, for any energy-based model with parameters θlog p(x) = −E(x; θ) − log Z(θ) its gradient splits into two expectations∂ log p(x)/∂θ = −∂E(x)/∂θ − ∂ log Z/∂θ and the second term is an expectation under the model itself∂ log Z/∂θ = (1/Z) ∑ₓ′ e^(−E(x′)) · (−∂E(x′)/∂θ) = E_{x′∼p_model}[ −∂E(x′)/∂θ ] so, averaged over the data∇θ ⟨log p⟩ = E_data[ −∂E/∂θ ] − E_model[ −∂E/∂θ ] └── positive phase ──┘ └── negative phase ──┘

This is the shape of every learning rule on this page. The positive phase lowers the energy of the data, and it is cheap because the data are given. The negative phase raises the energy of whatever the model currently finds likely. That requires samples from the model, which requires running a Markov chain to equilibrium. When the model's samples look like the data, the two phases cancel and learning stops. The approximations that follow (CD, PCD, mean-field) are all different ways of getting cheaper negative-phase samples. Evaluating the trained model has the same problem. To report a log-likelihood you need log Z, and the standard estimator is annealed importance sampling (Neal, 2001; applied to RBMs by Salakhutdinov and Murray, 2008). It interpolates from a tractable distribution to the model through a sequence of temperatures.

04 The general Boltzmann machine learning rule

Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski (1985) split the units into visible ones, which are clamped to data, and hidden ones, which are free latent variables. Applying §03 to the energy −∑wᵢⱼsᵢsⱼ gives a strikingly local rule. Each weight changes by the difference between how often its two units are on together with the data clamped, and how often they are on together when the network runs freely.

model over visible v and hidden h; s = (v, h)p(v) = ∑ₕ e^(−E(v, h)) / Z the gradient (with −∂E/∂wᵢⱼ = sᵢ sⱼ)∂ log p(v)/∂wᵢⱼ = ⟨sᵢ sⱼ⟩_{h∼p(h|v)} − ⟨sᵢ sⱼ⟩_{(v,h)∼p(v,h)} the learning rule, averaged over the training setΔwᵢⱼ = ε ( ⟨sᵢ sⱼ⟩_clamped − ⟨sᵢ sⱼ⟩_free ), Δbᵢ = ε ( ⟨sᵢ⟩_clamped − ⟨sᵢ⟩_free ) each term is a correlation between the two units the weight connects — Hebbian minus anti-Hebbianclamped phase: hold v at a data vector, let h equilibrate, record co-activations (Hebbian learning) free phase: let everything run, let it equilibrate, record co-activations (unlearning, as in part 2 §08)

The rule is beautiful and nearly unusable. Both phases need the chain to reach equilibrium. In the clamped phase that must happen once per training vector, and in the free phase once per update. With lateral connections among hidden units, and among visible units, the chains mix slowly, especially as the weights grow and the energy landscape becomes rugged. The estimates are also a difference of two noisy numbers. In 1985 this confined Boltzmann machines to toy problems with a few dozen units (encoders, shifters, parity). The next section removes the lateral connections, and with them most of the cost.

A useful reading of the rule. The gradient is zero exactly when the model's pairwise statistics match the data's: ⟨sᵢsⱼ⟩_model = ⟨sᵢsⱼ⟩_data for every connected pair. A fully visible Boltzmann machine is therefore the maximum-entropy distribution with given means and pairwise correlations, the binary analogue of a multivariate Gaussian. Hidden units are what let it capture higher-order structure.

05 The restricted Boltzmann machine: bipartite, factorised

An RBM keeps only the connections between the visible layer and the hidden layer, with none inside either layer. This single restriction (Smolensky's "harmonium", 1986) makes the hidden units conditionally independent given the visibles, and the visibles conditionally independent given the hiddens. Every conditional becomes a vector of independent sigmoids, computed with one matrix multiply.

energy — v ∈ {0,1}ⁿ (visible), h ∈ {0,1}ᵐ (hidden), W is n × mE(v, h) = −aᵀv − bᵀh − vᵀW h = −∑ᵢ aᵢvᵢ − ∑ⱼ bⱼhⱼ − ∑ᵢⱼ vᵢ Wᵢⱼ hⱼ conditional of h given v — the energy is a sum of separate terms in each hⱼp(h | v) ∝ exp(∑ⱼ hⱼ (bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)) = ∏ⱼ exp(hⱼ (bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)) ⇒ p(h | v) = ∏ⱼ p(hⱼ | v), p(hⱼ = 1 | v) = σ(bⱼ + ∑ᵢ vᵢ Wᵢⱼ) and symmetricallyp(v | h) = ∏ᵢ p(vᵢ | h), p(vᵢ = 1 | h) = σ(aᵢ + ∑ⱼ Wᵢⱼ hⱼ) in matrix form, for a mini-batch V (rows = examples)P(H=1 | V) = σ(V W + b), P(V=1 | H) = σ(H Wᵀ + a) what is still intractableZ = ∑ᵥ ∑ₕ e^(−E(v, h)) — the restriction simplifies the conditionals, not the normaliser
  • The forward pass is an ordinary layer. p(h|v) = σ(vW + b) is exactly a sigmoid layer of an MLP. An RBM is a generative model whose inference network happens to be a feed-forward layer. That is why its weights could be dropped straight into an MLP as an initialisation (§13).
  • The backward pass uses the same weights, transposed. This is the BAM of part 2 §11 made stochastic. The tied weights are what the energy function requires.
  • Hidden units are feature detectors. Each column W₍:ⱼ₎ is a template. Unit j turns on when the visible vector matches it, and when on it adds W₍:ⱼ₎ to the visibles' input. The model's distribution over v is a product of experts (Hinton, 2002): each hidden unit contributes a factor (1 + e^(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)), as §06 shows.
  • Universal, in principle. With enough hidden units an RBM can represent any distribution over binary vectors (Le Roux and Bengio, 2008). In the worst case that takes one hidden unit per configuration with non-zero probability.

06 Free energy: summing the hidden units out exactly

Because the hidden units are independent given v, the sum over all 2ᵐ hidden configurations factorises into m two-term sums. The marginal p(v) then has a closed form up to Z: p(v) = e^(−F(v))/Z, where F is the free energy. It can be evaluated for any v in O(nm).

sum out h∑ₕ e^(−E(v,h)) = e^(aᵀv) ∏ⱼ ∑_{hⱼ∈{0,1}} e^(hⱼ (bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)) = e^(aᵀv) ∏ⱼ (1 + e^(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)) free energyF(v) = −aᵀv − ∑ⱼ log(1 + e^(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)) = −aᵀv − ∑ⱼ softplus(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎) p(v) = e^(−F(v)) / Z, Z = ∑ᵥ e^(−F(v)) (still 2ⁿ terms) the exact log-likelihood gradient for the weights∂ log p(v)/∂Wᵢⱼ = vᵢ σ(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎) − E_{v′∼p}[ v′ᵢ σ(bⱼ + v′ᵀW₍:ⱼ₎) ] and for the biases∂/∂aᵢ = vᵢ − E_p[v′ᵢ], ∂/∂bⱼ = σ(bⱼ + vᵀW₍:ⱼ₎) − E_p[σ(bⱼ + v′ᵀW₍:ⱼ₎)]

Z = 3·1.368 + 2.854 = 6.957. Each visible unit is on with probability 0.197 + 0.410 = 0.607, so under independence p(11) would be 0.607² = 0.368. The model gives 0.410. The single hidden unit, excited by both visibles, has created a positive correlation between them, and this is all a hidden unit does: it couples the visibles it connects to. The free energy of 11 is lowest (F = −1.049) because the hidden unit can "explain" both bits at once.

The free energy is also the practical monitoring tool. The difference in average F between training data and held-out data needs no Z, and a widening gap signals overfitting (§11). And ratios p(v)/p(v′) = e^(F(v′)−F(v)) are exact. The pseudo-likelihood used in §10 is built from such ratios.

07 Block Gibbs sampling

A general Boltzmann machine must be sampled one unit at a time. An RBM can update a whole layer at once, because the units in a layer do not interact. One step of the chain is two matrix multiplies and two rounds of coin flips: v → h → v′.

one Gibbs steph ∼ p(h | v) = Bernoulli(σ(b + Wᵀv)) v′ ∼ p(v | h) = Bernoulli(σ(a + W h)) the chain v⁰ → h⁰ → v¹ → h¹ → … has stationary distribution p(v, h)and the visible samples alone are distributed as p(v) once the chain has mixed why mixing can be slowlarge |W| ⇒ conditionals near 0 or 1 ⇒ the chain rarely leaves a mode; time between modes grows ~ exponentially in the barrier height
A tiny RBM (4 visible, 3 hidden) with two modes: the Gibbs chain against the exact p(v) at weight scale 1 the chain hops freely; at 3 it sticks in one mode for hundreds of steps and the histogram is badly wrong for a long time

This is the practical problem with every sampling-based method on this page. The chain is correct in the limit, but "the limit" can be far away. As training sharpens the modes (larger weights), the chain needed for the negative phase gets slower just as the model gets better. The next two sections work around it. CD never lets the chain run long enough to matter. PCD keeps one chain alive across updates, so it can take the long walk in small instalments.

08 Contrastive divergence (CD-k)

Hinton (2002) suggested starting the negative-phase chain at the data vector and running it for only k steps, often k = 1. The update then compares the data with a one-step "reconstruction". If the model has learned the data, reconstructions look like the data and the update is zero. If the model's chain drifts away from the data even in one step, CD pushes the energy up where it drifts.

CD-k for one training vector v⁰ (mini-batch: average)h⁰ ∼ p(h | v⁰) (positive statistics use p(h | v⁰) directly) for t = 1 … k: vᵗ ∼ p(v | hᵗ⁻¹), hᵗ ∼ p(h | vᵗ) ΔW = ε ( v⁰ p(h | v⁰)ᵀ − vᵏ p(h | vᵏ)ᵀ ) Δa = ε ( v⁰ − vᵏ ), Δb = ε ( p(h | v⁰) − p(h | vᵏ) ) what it approximatesthe true gradient replaces vᵏ by a sample from p(v) (k → ∞); CD-k truncates the chain what it actually follows (named for this)approximately −∇ [ KL(p_data ‖ p_model) − KL(pₖ ‖ p_model) ], pₖ = distribution after k steps from the data
  • Why it works at all. The positive phase is exact, so the data's energy is always pushed down correctly. The negative phase is wrong far from the data, but near the data it is roughly right, and near the data is where the likelihood is decided early in training.
  • Where it fails. CD's chains never leave the neighbourhood of the data, so it cannot see, and cannot remove, spurious modes far from any training example. Probability mass sits there unpunished, and samples from a long chain can look nothing like the data. The CD fixed points are also biased away from maximum likelihood (Carreira-Perpiñán and Hinton, 2005), and the CD update is not the gradient of any function (Sutskever and Tieleman, 2010). In practice the bias is small for generating features and large for density estimation.
  • A useful side effect. Because CD penalises the model whenever the chain moves the data quickly, it rewards hidden units that copy information about v, a little like an autoencoder. That is a feature when RBMs are stacked (§13), because the next layer receives more information.

09 Persistent CD, and other ways around Z

Persistent contrastive divergence keeps a set of "fantasy particles", Markov chains that are never reset to the data. After each parameter update the chains continue from where they were. Because the parameters change only slightly per step, a chain that was near equilibrium stays near equilibrium. The chains can wander to far-away modes and suppress them. This idea was first known as stochastic maximum likelihood (Younes, 1998) and was rediscovered as PCD by Tieleman (2008).

10 Training an RBM, watched in full

A 4 × 4 bars-and-stripes dataset has 30 images: every subset of rows switched on, and every subset of columns, with the all-on and all-off images counted once. With 16 visible units the partition function has only 65 536 terms, so the figure computes the exact log-likelihood as it trains. That lets you see the gap between what the cheap monitor (reconstruction error) says and what is actually happening. The best possible model puts 1/30 of the probability on each image, a log-likelihood of −ln 30 = −3.40.

CD-1, CD-5 and PCD-1 on 4 × 4 bars-and-stripes, with the exact log-likelihood reconstruction error heads to ≈ 0 while the likelihood is still far from the optimum; PCD without a decaying learning rate oscillates

Typical runs, with 12 hidden units, a learning rate of 0.03 decaying, momentum 0.5 and 4000 epochs, end at a log-likelihood of about −5.0 for CD-1, about −4.0 for CD-5, and about −4.3 to −4.7 for PCD-1. The optimum is −3.40. Now switch off the decay for PCD: the likelihood rises, then collapses and oscillates. The persistent chains fall behind the moving model, and the negative phase starts punishing whichever mode the chains happen to be in. This is the documented failure mode of stochastic maximum likelihood with a learning rate too large for its mixing rate. Meanwhile the reconstruction error of every method is near zero within a few hundred epochs, which is why it is a poor thing to stop on.

11 A practical training recipe, and what to monitor

These are the settings that emerged from a decade of RBM practice, collected in Hinton's 2010 practical guide. They are starting points, not laws.

  • Reconstruction error is easy to compute and is useful as a crash detector, but it is not what the model optimises. CD's own negative phase makes it small, and it says nothing about far-away modes. §10 shows it at ≈ 0 while the likelihood is still poor.
  • Pseudo-likelihood, estimated by flipping one random bit per example, n · log σ(F(ṽ) − F(v)), is cheap and tracks the likelihood's trend much better.
  • AIS estimates of log Z give real log-likelihoods for model comparison. They are the standard for papers.
  • The free-energy gap is the mean F on a held-out set minus the mean F on the training set. It needs no Z. When it starts growing, the model is overfitting.
  • Look at things. Histograms of weights and of hidden activations (neither all-off nor all-on), the filters as images, and samples from long chains. Almost every failure is visible there before it is visible in a number.

12 Gaussian–Bernoulli RBMs and other unit types

Real-valued data such as pixel intensities, audio features or measurements need real-valued visible units. The Gaussian–Bernoulli RBM gives each visible unit a Gaussian conditional, and keeps binary hidden units. The resulting marginal is a mixture of 2ᵐ Gaussians whose means and weights are all tied through the same W.

energy (σᵢ is the standard deviation of visible unit i)E(v, h) = ∑ᵢ (vᵢ − aᵢ)² / (2σᵢ²) − ∑ⱼ bⱼ hⱼ − ∑ᵢⱼ (vᵢ / σᵢ) Wᵢⱼ hⱼ conditionalsp(vᵢ | h) = 𝒩( aᵢ + σᵢ ∑ⱼ Wᵢⱼ hⱼ , σᵢ² ) p(hⱼ = 1 | v) = σ( bⱼ + ∑ᵢ Wᵢⱼ vᵢ / σᵢ ) marginal over v (equal σ for readability)p(v) ∝ exp(−‖v − a‖² / 2σ²) · ∏ⱼ (1 + exp(bⱼ + Wⱼᵀv / σ)) equivalently a mixture, one component per hidden configuration hp(v) = ∑ₕ πₕ 𝒩(v; a + σWh, σ²I), πₕ ∝ exp(bᵀh + aᵀWh/σ + ½‖Wh‖²)
A Gaussian–Bernoulli RBM on 2-D data: 2ᵐ tied Gaussian components, trained with CD-1 three hidden units fit four blobs using combinations of three "offset vectors"; with two hidden units the fit can get stuck — reset to try another start
  • The tying is the point. A mixture of 2ᵐ free Gaussians would need 2ᵐ mean vectors. The RBM has m offset vectors, and every component mean is a sum of a subset of them. That is a distributed, combinatorial code: four corners of a square need only two offsets, and the "L" needs the fourth combination suppressed by the weights πₕ. The same exponential-mixture view explains why RBMs can model data with many independent factors of variation.
  • In practice. Standardise every input dimension, fix σᵢ = 1 (learning σ is unstable), and use a learning rate one to two orders of magnitude smaller than for binary units. When training on natural images, remember that a single Gaussian conditional with a shared σ models pixel noise poorly. The mean-and-covariance RBM and spike-and-slab RBM variants exist for that reason.
  • Other units. Softmax (one-of-K) visible units for categorical data, used in the collaborative-filtering RBM of the Netflix Prize (Salakhutdinov, Mnih and Hinton, 2007). Noisy rectified linear hidden units, max(0, x + 𝒩(0, σ(x))) (Nair and Hinton, 2010). This was one of the first uses of ReLUs in deep learning. Conditional RBMs, whose biases depend on past frames, for time series.

13 Deep belief networks and greedy layer-wise pretraining

Hinton, Osindero and Teh (2006) showed how to train a deep generative model one layer at a time. Train an RBM on the data. Freeze it. Treat its hidden activations as data for a second RBM, and repeat. The stack is a deep belief network. Its top two layers form an undirected RBM, and every layer below is a directed, top-down sigmoid belief net. Each added layer, trained this way, improves a variational lower bound on the log-likelihood of the data.

the DBN's joint distribution (three hidden layers)p(v, h¹, h², h³) = p(v | h¹) · p(h¹ | h²) · p(h², h³) p(v | h¹) = ∏ᵢ σ(aᵢ + W¹ᵢ h¹) p(h¹ | h²) = ∏ⱼ σ(bⱼ¹ + W²ⱼ h²) p(h², h³) = RBM with W³ the variational bound that justifies stacking, with q(h¹ | v) = σ(b¹ + W¹ᵀv) from the first RBMlog p(v) ≥ ∑ₕ₁ q(h¹ | v) [ log p(h¹) + log p(v | h¹) ] + H(q(h¹ | v)) the argumentif W² is initialised to W¹ᵀ, the two-layer model defines exactly the same p(v) as RBM₁ and the bound is tight; training layer 2 on samples from q(h¹ | v) improves p(h¹) and so raises the bound (not necessarily log p(v) itself)
Greedy layer-wise construction of a DBN, step by step step through: train, freeze, push up, repeat; then see how the finished model generates and how it is fine-tuned
  • Why it mattered in 2006. Deep sigmoid networks trained from random initialisation with backprop were then widely found to get stuck in poor solutions. Pretraining each layer as an RBM, then fine-tuning, trained deep networks that beat shallow ones: MNIST classification, the deep autoencoders of Hinton and Salakhutdinov (Science, 2006) that beat PCA at dimensionality reduction, and the first deep acoustic models for speech (around 2009–2012). Bengio et al. (2007) showed that greedy pretraining with autoencoders works similarly. See Autoencoders & VAEs.
  • Why it is rarely used now. ReLUs, good initialisation, normalisation, residual connections and far more data made plain backprop work for deep nets from scratch (Neural Network Training). Pretraining survived in a new form. Self-supervised pretraining of a whole network, such as masked language models and contrastive learning, is the direct descendant of "learn p(x) first, then fine-tune".
  • Generation is two-stage. Mix in the top-level RBM with a long Gibbs chain, then do one ancestral pass downward. The top RBM acts as an associative memory over abstract features, which brings back the Hopfield picture.

14 Deep Boltzmann machines

A deep Boltzmann machine (Salakhutdinov and Hinton, 2009) is a stack of layers in which every connection stays undirected. It is one big Boltzmann machine with a layered graph. Its advantage over a DBN is that inference combines bottom-up and top-down signals, so each layer's posterior hears from both neighbours. The cost is that the posterior no longer factorises.

energy of a two-hidden-layer DBM (biases omitted)E(v, h¹, h²) = −vᵀW¹h¹ − h¹ᵀW²h² conditionals — each layer depends on BOTH neighboursp(h¹ⱼ = 1 | v, h²) = σ( ∑ᵢ vᵢ W¹ᵢⱼ + ∑ₖ W²ⱼₖ h²ₖ ) the even and odd layers are conditionally independent of each other: block Gibbs alternates {v, h²} and {h¹}posterior p(h¹, h² | v) is NOT factorial ⇒ approximate it with mean-field q(h) = ∏ q(hⱼ) mean-field fixed-point equations (iterate to convergence)μ¹ ← σ(W¹ᵀv + W²μ²), μ² ← σ(W²ᵀμ¹) trainingpositive phase: mean-field statistics; negative phase: persistent chains (PCD); initialise by greedy RBM pretraining, doubling the bottom-up input to the first layer and the top-down input to the last, to compensate for the missing neighbour

DBMs gave the best generative log-likelihoods on MNIST of their era, and they were used for multimodal models such as joint image–text DBMs (Srivastava and Salakhutdinov, 2012). They are slow to train and to sample from, and the mean-field positive phase combined with the PCD negative phase is fragile. Once VAEs (Autoencoders & VAEs) and GANs (GANs) arrived around 2014, most generative modelling moved to directed, reparameterised or adversarial models that avoid Z altogether.

15 Where energy-based ideas live today

RBMs are no longer the tool of choice, but their core idea, learn an energy (or unnormalised log-density) and sample by following it downhill with noise, is alive. The modern methods mostly differ in how they escape the partition function.

the key identity — the score does not depend on Z∇ₓ log p(x) = ∇ₓ ( −E(x) − log Z ) = −∇ₓ E(x) Langevin dynamics samples p ∝ e^(−E/T) using only that gradientx ← x − (ε/2) ∇ₓE(x)/T + √ε · z, z ∼ 𝒩(0, I) score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005): fit s_θ(x) ≈ ∇log p without ZJ(θ) = E_data[ ½ ‖s_θ(x)‖² + tr ∇ₓ s_θ(x) ] denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011) — the diffusion-model lossE ‖ s_θ(x + σz) + z/σ ‖² ⇒ s_θ ≈ ∇ log p_σ, the score of the noised data noise-contrastive estimation (Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010)treat log Z as a parameter; learn E by classifying data vs samples from a known noise distribution
Langevin dynamics: sampling from e^(−E/T) using only ∇E (no partition function) with noise the shares drift towards the target over a few thousand steps (crossings between wells are rare, which is the mixing problem again); switch noise off and every particle just falls into its nearest well — Hopfield descent
  • Diffusion and score-based models (Song and Ermon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020) learn the score at many noise levels and run annealed Langevin, or its SDE and ODE generalisations, from pure noise down to data. Tempering, annealing and "follow the energy with noise" are the same Boltzmann-machine ideas, now with a network that outputs ∇log p directly. See Diffusion Models.
  • Contrastive learning (InfoNCE, CLIP) is NCE's descendant. It scores a positive pair against negatives, which is a Boltzmann distribution over candidates whose Z is summed over the batch. See Contrastive Learning.
  • Energy-based models with neural energies are trained with short-run Langevin chains and persistent replay buffers (e.g. Du and Mordatch, 2019). That is PCD with a CNN as the energy. JEPA-style world models are framed explicitly as energy-based (World Models & JEPA).
  • VAEs replace the intractable posterior of a DBN/DBM with a learned encoder and the intractable Z with a directed decoder (Autoencoders & VAEs). The RBM's p(h|v) = σ(vW + b) was already an encoder.
  • Attention is a modern Hopfield network, as shown at the end of part 2, and softmax is a Boltzmann distribution over keys at temperature √dₖ.

Takeaways. (1) Noise turns a Hopfield network into a probability model: p(x) = e^(−E(x))/Z, sampled by Gibbs updates with sigmoid units. (2) Every likelihood gradient is data statistics minus model statistics. The model statistics need samples, and Z is the reason they are hard to get. (3) Making the graph bipartite (RBM) gives exact factorised conditionals, a closed-form free energy and block Gibbs sampling. CD-k and PCD make the negative phase cheap, with a bias or a learning-rate constraint respectively. (4) Reconstruction error is not the objective. Monitor pseudo-likelihood, AIS or the free-energy gap. (5) Stacking RBMs (the DBN) started the deep-learning revival. The lasting ideas are pretraining, and "learn an energy and follow its gradient with noise", which now powers diffusion models, contrastive learning and modern EBMs.
