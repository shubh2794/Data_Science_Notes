Classical Neural Networks

Before "deep learning" had a name, neural networks were a crowded field of different machines built on different ideas: units that learn from their mistakes, memories that are the minima of an energy, maps that organise themselves, networks of local bumps, networks that are fuzzy rule bases, networks trained by a Kalman filter, networks found by evolution, and networks cast directly into silicon. Most of them faded from everyday practice once backpropagation, GPUs and large datasets made deep feedforward and recurrent models dominant — but almost none of the ideas disappeared. They reappear, sometimes under new names, inside attention, diffusion, mixture-of-experts routing, VQ codebooks, second-order optimizers, world models, architecture search and low-precision accelerators. This is the overview of a ten-part series: the map, a timeline from 1943 to 2006, a one-screen comparison of the families, and where each idea lives now.

The order is roughly historical and roughly dependency-driven. Part 1 builds the unit and the first learning rules every later part assumes. Parts 2–4 are the unsupervised and energy-based families that grew from Hebb's rule. Parts 5–6 are shallow, trainable function approximators with strong structure. Parts 7–8 treat networks as dynamical systems — trained by estimation theory, used to model and control plants. Parts 9–10 leave the gradient behind: search over architectures by evolution, and the arithmetic of the chips that run it all.

Part 1 · the unit and its rules
Perceptrons & Early Learning Rules
McCulloch–Pitts · Hebb · perceptron · Adaline/LMS · XOR
Threshold logic, Hebbian correlation learning, the perceptron rule and its (R/γ)² mistake bound, the pocket algorithm, Adaline and the delta rule as gradient descent on squared error, Madaline, linear separability, and the XOR wall that forced hidden layers.
Part 2 · memory as an energy minimum
Hopfield Networks & Associative Memory
Outer-product storage · energy · capacity · BAM
Content-addressable memory: store patterns with a Hebbian outer product, recall by descending an energy function, the 0.138·N capacity limit and spurious states, bidirectional associative memory — and the modern Hopfield network whose update rule is attention.
Part 3 · stochastic and generative
Boltzmann Machines & RBMs
Boltzmann distribution · Gibbs sampling · CD-k · DBN
Energy-based probability models over binary units, the intractable partition function, the bipartite restricted Boltzmann machine and block Gibbs sampling, contrastive divergence, and the deep belief network pretraining of 2006 that restarted deep learning.
Part 4 · competitive and unsupervised
Self-Organizing Maps & Competitive Learning
Winner-take-all · Kohonen map · U-matrix · LVQ · ART
Units compete for each input and only the winner (and its lattice neighbours) learns: topology-preserving maps with a shrinking neighbourhood, the U-matrix, learning vector quantization, and adaptive resonance theory's answer to the stability–plasticity dilemma.
Part 5 · local function approximation
Radial Basis Function Networks
Gaussian bumps · k-means centres · least-squares readout
One hidden layer of localized Gaussian units and a linear readout: centre selection by clustering, width heuristics, closed-form output weights, exact interpolation vs regularization networks, and the link to kernels, SVMs and general regression networks.
Part 6 · rules you can read
Neuro-Fuzzy Systems
Membership functions · Mamdani vs Sugeno · ANFIS
Fuzzy sets and if–then rules, the two classic inference schemes, and ANFIS — a fuzzy inference system laid out as a five-layer network whose premise and consequent parameters are trained by a hybrid of gradient descent and least squares.
Part 7 · training as estimation
Kalman Filter Training
Weights as state · EKF · decoupled EKF · UKF
Treat the weights as the hidden state of a dynamical system and the targets as noisy measurements: the extended Kalman filter as a fast second-order trainer, global vs decoupled variants, training recurrent networks, the unscented filter, and the quadratic memory cost.
Part 8 · networks as dynamical models
Neural System Identification & Control
NARX · series-parallel · MRAC · NARMA-L2 · MPC
Learning a model of a plant from input–output records, tapped-delay-line time-series prediction, series-parallel vs parallel identification, and the classic neural control architectures — model-reference, NARMA-L2, model predictive and inverse control.
Part 9 · search instead of gradients
Neuroevolution & Growing Networks
Encodings · NEAT · HyperNEAT · cascade-correlation · pruning
Evolving weights and topologies: direct vs developmental encodings, NEAT's historical markings and speciation, HyperNEAT and CPPNs, networks that grow (cascade-correlation) or shrink (pruning), and the road to modern architecture search.
Part 10 · the silicon
Neural Network Hardware
Fixed point · activation approximation · systolic arrays · FPGA
Running networks on chips: fixed-point and low-precision arithmetic, cheap sigmoid/tanh approximations, MAC arrays and systolic dataflows, FPGA trade-offs between parallelism, area and precision, on-chip learning, and why GPUs and TPUs won the general case.
How the series fits the rest of the notes. The mainline — multilayer networks trained by backpropagation — has its own pages: Feedforward Networks for the model and Neural Network Training for the optimisation. This series is everything beside that line, and the ideas it passed down.

why What "classical" means here, and why it still matters

"Classical" is not "obsolete". It means the models were designed in an era of small data, slow machines and no automatic differentiation — so each one had to earn its keep with structure: a convex loss, a closed-form solution, an energy that guarantees convergence, a local learning rule, an interpretable rule base, or hardware simple enough to build. That constraint is exactly what makes them worth studying now.

a unit: weighted sum + nonlinearity→a learning rule (local or global)→an objective it provably descends→an architecture that makes the rule tractable→a guarantee, and a failure mode
  • Each family is a clean instance of one idea. A Hopfield network is "memory = attractor" with nothing else in the way; a self-organizing map is "competition + neighbourhood"; an RBF network is "local basis + linear readout". Modern architectures mix these ideas together, and they are much easier to recognise once you have seen them alone.
  • Many have exact analyses. The perceptron has a mistake bound, LMS has a closed-form stability limit, Hopfield nets have a Lyapunov function and a capacity, RBMs have a tractable conditional, RBF output weights solve a least-squares problem. Those analyses are the vocabulary in which modern results are still argued.
  • The ideas came back. The modern Hopfield update is softmax attention; energy-based modelling returned as score-based diffusion; winner-take-all competition returned as vector-quantized codebooks and expert routing; Kalman-style preconditioning returned in second-order optimizers; neuroevolution returned as architecture search and evolution strategies; fixed-point neural hardware returned as int8 and fp8 accelerators. The last section maps each one.
  • They still win in their niches. Small data with known structure, strict latency or power budgets, a need for readable rules, online adaptation to a drifting system, or objectives with no gradient — each of these favours one of the families here over a large network.
  • Hebb, the perceptron and LMS are the roots of everything. Nearly every learning rule in this series is either a Hebbian correlation term, an error-correction term, or the two combined with a decay. Part 1 is the prerequisite for all the others.

Sixty-three years, from the first logical neuron to the pretraining result that opened the deep-learning era. The shaded bands are the conventional eras: a first wave of optimism, the long quiet after 1969, the 1980s revival driven by Hopfield, Boltzmann machines and backpropagation, and the decade when kernel methods overtook neural networks in mainstream machine learning.

Events by family — click a dot, or step through in order each lane is a family; dots link to the part that covers them

Three patterns stand out. Ideas arrive in clusters — 1982–1989 alone produced Hopfield nets, Kohonen maps, Boltzmann machines, backpropagation, RBMs, RBF networks, BAM, EKF training and universal approximation. The mainline (bottom lane) was quiet for a long time before it was loud: backpropagation was derived in 1974 and ignored until 1986. And the end of this timeline is the start of another: the 2006 deep belief network was a classical energy-based model used to initialise a deep feedforward one, and the pages beyond this series pick up from there.

Three axes to read the table by. Where the error comes from: a target (parts 1, 5–8), the data's own statistics (2–4), or a fitness score (9). How local the rule is: Hebbian and competitive rules use only the two ends of a connection; LMS and the delta rule add one error signal; backprop and EKF need information from the whole network. What is guaranteed: convergence (perceptron, LMS, Hopfield), an exact optimum (RBF output layer, ANFIS consequents), or nothing but empirical progress (evolution, CD-k).

now Where each idea lives in modern deep learning

None of these families is a dead end. Each contributed at least one mechanism that a current architecture or training method still uses, often without the name. The map below draws those lines; the table after it states each one in a sentence.
