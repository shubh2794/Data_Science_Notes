Kalman Filter Training

Backpropagation tells you which way is downhill; it does not tell you how far, and it forgets everything about the curvature it has seen as soon as the step is taken. Kalman filter training fixes both at once by a change of viewpoint: treat the network's weights as the hidden state of a dynamical system that barely moves, treat every training target as a noisy measurement of that state through the network, and run a filter. The extended Kalman filter (EKF) then does, for each sample, what a Bayesian would do — it updates a Gaussian belief about the weights, with a mean (the weights you use) and a covariance matrix P that accumulates second-order information from every gradient it has seen. The result is a recursive Gauss–Newton method with an automatic, per-direction learning rate, which trained small feedforward and recurrent networks in tens of epochs where backpropagation needed thousands. The price is written in P's size: M² numbers for M weights. This page derives the filter, the network form (global and decoupled EKF), its reading as online Gauss–Newton, natural gradient and recursive least squares, the recurrent-network version, the unscented alternative, and the practical knobs — and says plainly where it still belongs.

01 The idea: training as state estimation

Minimizing a sum of squared errors over a stream of samples is the same problem as estimating a constant vector from a stream of noisy nonlinear measurements of it. The second problem has had an optimal recursive solution since 1960 (the Kalman filter) and a practical nonlinear one since the late 1960s (the extended Kalman filter). Singhal & Wu (1989) applied it to multilayer perceptrons.

Stochastic gradient descent processes one sample, takes a step of fixed size along the gradient, and moves on. Nothing it learns about the shape of the error surface survives the step. A Kalman filter keeps, alongside its estimate, a covariance matrix P that says how uncertain each direction of the estimate still is. A new sample that is informative about a direction the filter is already sure of barely moves it; one that probes a direction still uncertain moves it a lot. For a network, the "directions" are combinations of weights, the "informativeness" of a sample in a direction is the network's output Jacobian, and the resulting update is a second-order step — it rescales and rotates the gradient using everything seen so far — computed without ever forming a Hessian.

the translation tablestate xₖ ↔ weight vector wₖ (M numbers) state dynamics ↔ wₖ₊₁ = wₖ + ωₖ the weights "should" be constant; ω is artificial process noise measurement zₖ ↔ training target yₖ = h(wₖ, uₖ) + νₖ h is the network, uₖ the input estimate x̂ₖ ↔ the weights you actually use error covariance Pₖ ↔ M × M matrix of weight uncertainty = accumulated curvature (§06) Kalman gain Kₖ ↔ a per-sample, per-direction learning rate matrix
  • Where it came from. Kalman (1960) for linear-Gaussian systems; the EKF from the Apollo-era navigation work of the 1960s; parameter estimation with the EKF is classical system identification. Singhal & Wu (1989) reported MLPs trained with a global EKF in orders of magnitude fewer epochs than backprop; Puskorius & Feldkamp (1991) introduced the decoupled variants that made it affordable, and through the 1990s applied them to recurrent networks for automotive engine control and diagnosis.
  • Why it is in a "classical" series. The M² covariance puts it out of reach for networks with millions of weights, so it did not survive the scale-up of deep learning as a general optimizer. Its ideas did: curvature-preconditioned steps (Gauss–Newton, natural gradient, K-FAC), block-diagonal curvature approximations, and online Bayesian learning all have EKF training as an ancestor or a special case.

02 Kalman filter recap: predict, update, gain

For a linear system with Gaussian noise, the Kalman filter computes the exact posterior mean and covariance of the state, recursively, in two steps per measurement: predict (push the belief through the dynamics, which adds uncertainty) and update (fold in the measurement, which removes it).

  • Q/R is the only thing that matters in steady state. Large Q/R: "the state moves a lot, trust the sensor" — K near 1, the estimate chases the noise. Small Q/R: "the state is nearly constant, average many readings" — K small, the estimate is smooth but lags a moving truth. Setting the filter's Q and R to the true values minimizes the estimation error; try detuning either one and watch the RMSE rise.
  • The gain starts large and settles. With an uncertain initial P, the first measurements are trusted almost fully; the gain then decays to K∞. That transient is exactly the automatic learning-rate schedule that EKF training inherits (§06). With Q = 0 there is no floor at all: K → 0 like 1/k and the filter becomes a running mean — which is correct for a truly constant state and fatal for a drifting one (§09).

03 Weights as state: the linear case is recursive least squares

Put the weights in the state, make the dynamics the identity, and make the network the measurement function. When the network is linear in its weights, the Kalman filter is exact, and it is precisely recursive least squares (RLS).

state-space model of trainingwₖ₊₁ = wₖ + ωₖ, ωₖ ~ N(0, Qₖ) (process equation — F = I) yₖ = h(wₖ, uₖ) + νₖ, νₖ ~ N(0, Rₖ) (measurement equation — the network) linear-in-weights modelh = Hₖ w, Hₖ = the regressor row (e.g. uₖᵀ): Kₖ = Pₖ Hₖᵀ (Hₖ Pₖ Hₖᵀ + Rₖ)⁻¹ ŵₖ₊₁ = ŵₖ + Kₖ (yₖ − Hₖ ŵₖ) Pₖ₊₁ = Pₖ − Kₖ Hₖ Pₖ + Qₖ with no process noise this is exactly RLSQ = 0, R = r·I, P₀ = p₀·I ⇒ after k samples: ŵₖ = argmin_w ( ‖w − ŵ₀‖² / p₀ + (1/r) ∑ⱼ₌₀ᵏ⁻¹ ‖yⱼ − Hⱼ w‖² ) — ridge regression, solved recursively Pₖ = ( I/p₀ + (1/r) ∑ⱼ Hⱼᵀ Hⱼ )⁻¹ — the inverse regularized Gram matrix
  • The prior is the regularizer. P₀ = p₀·I says "before any data, each weight is within about √p₀ of its initial value". That is a Gaussian prior, and it enters the cost as weight decay with strength 1/p₀. A large P₀ (100–1000 is typical) is a weak prior: the first samples move the weights almost as far as least squares would.
  • Only the ratio of R to P matters for the weights. Scale R, P₀ and Q all by the same μ > 0 and the gain K = PHᵀ(HPHᵀ + R)⁻¹ is unchanged, so the weight trajectory is identical. EKF training therefore has only two effective knobs: the ratio of R to P₀ (a learning rate) and Q relative to both (§09).
  • The ADALINE contrast. The LMS rule of part 1 solves the same linear problem with Δw = η ξ uₖ: a fixed scalar gain in place of the matrix Kₖ. RLS converges in roughly M samples regardless of how correlated the inputs are; LMS converges at a rate set by the input covariance's condition number. EKF training is the attempt to get RLS behaviour for a nonlinear network.

04 The extended Kalman filter: linearize through the network Jacobian

A network is not linear in its weights, so the EKF linearizes it around the current estimate: the measurement matrix Hₖ becomes the Jacobian of the network's outputs with respect to all its weights, computed by backpropagation.

first-order expansion about the current weightsh(w, uₖ) ≈ h(ŵₖ, uₖ) + Hₖ (w − ŵₖ), Hₖ = ∂h/∂w |_(ŵₖ, uₖ) size Nₒ × M how Hₖ is computedone backward pass per network OUTPUT, each seeded with a 1 at that output (not with the error) row o of Hₖ = ∂ŷₒ/∂w — the ordinary backprop gradient of ŷₒ, before multiplying by the error per-node structure (feedforward net)the block of Hₖ for node i's incoming weights is an outer product: (cᵢ)(uᵢ)ᵀ uᵢ = the node's input vector, cᵢ = ∂ŷ/∂(node i's net input) (an Nₒ-vector) convention notemuch of the EKF-training literature writes Hₖ as the M × Nₒ transpose of this; the equations below use Nₒ × M, the usual Jacobian
  • The Jacobian, not the gradient. Backprop for SGD seeds the backward pass with the error and collapses all outputs into one gradient vector. The EKF needs each output's sensitivity separately, because the filter weighs output errors against each other through R and through the covariance of the innovations. For a single-output network the two coincide up to the scalar error: gradient of ½ξ² = −ξ·Hₖᵀ.
  • What linearization costs. The EKF's covariance is propagated through the tangent plane only. Where the network is strongly curved over the region of weight space that P still allows, the filter's belief is miscalibrated — typically overconfident. This is the gap the unscented filter of §11 targets.

05 The global EKF training algorithm

GEKF keeps one full M × M covariance over all weights and, for each training sample, does one forward pass, Nₒ backward passes, and one Kalman update. The Kalman "predict" step is trivial because F = I: it only adds Q.

one GEKF step per training sample1. forward: ŷₖ = h(ŵₖ, uₖ), ξₖ = yₖ − ŷₖ 2. backward: Hₖ = ∂ŷ/∂w (Nₒ × M), one pass per output 3. scaling: Aₖ = (Rₖ + Hₖ Pₖ Hₖᵀ)⁻¹ Nₒ × Nₒ — a scalar for one output 4. gain: Kₖ = Pₖ Hₖᵀ Aₖ M × Nₒ 5. weights: ŵₖ₊₁ = ŵₖ + Kₖ ξₖ 6. covariance: Pₖ₊₁ = Pₖ − Kₖ Hₖ Pₖ + Qₖ cost per sampletime O(Nₒ M²) for steps 3–6 (plus Nₒ backward passes) memory O(M²) for P worked: one tanh neuronŷ = tanh(w u), w = 0.5, u = 2, target 0.9, P = 100, R = 1 ŷ = tanh(1) = 0.7616 ξ = 0.1384 H = u(1 − ŷ²) = 0.8399 A = 1/(1 + 0.8399²·100) = 0.01398 K = 100·0.8399·0.01398 = 1.174 Δw = K ξ = 0.1625 → w = 0.6625, new output tanh(1.325) = 0.868 P: 100 → 1.398 compare: Gauss–Newton step ξ/H = 0.1648; SGD with η = 0.1: η ξ H = 0.0116 — fourteen times smaller

The worked step shows the character of the method. With a vague prior (P = 100) the EKF takes almost the full Gauss–Newton step to fit the new sample — Δw = (ξ/H)·H²P/(R + H²P), i.e. the Newton step shrunk by the factor 0.986 — and in the same update it slashes its uncertainty in that direction from 100 to 1.4, so the next sample that pulls this weight will move it far less. SGD knows none of this: its step is proportional to the gradient's size, which is small exactly where the neuron saturates.

The decoupled variants fall in between, ordered by how much of P they keep — which is exactly the trade §08 makes. The measured time per epoch in the readout shows the other side of the ledger.
  • Per-sample updates. Everything here is fully online: one sample, one update, which is why the EKF lives naturally in adaptive and streaming settings. The q annealing (×0.9 per epoch down to 10⁻⁶) follows common practice (§09).

06 The quadratic cost in the weights

The EKF is the exact minimizer of a growing quadratic cost — a prior term plus a sum of squared errors, each error linearized at the weights in use when its sample arrived. P⁻¹ is the Hessian of that cost.

The covariance ellipse shrinking over updatesa 2-weight model · contours = total squared error over the dataset · ellipses = 2-standard-deviation contour of P
  • Each sample shrinks P in one direction only. With one output, HₖᵀRₖ⁻¹Hₖ is rank one: a sample tells you about the weight combination along Hₖ and nothing about the orthogonal ones. Step through a few samples: each ellipse is squeezed along the current sample's Jacobian direction. Inputs u with a non-zero mean make w₁ and w₂ correlated — the ellipses tilt — and the EKF moves along the valley instead of across it, which is exactly what plain SGD fails to do.
  • 1/√k. With Q = 0 the ellipse axes shrink roughly like 1/√k — the familiar standard-error scaling — so the effective learning rate decays like 1/k: a stochastic-approximation schedule chosen automatically. Raise q and the ellipses stop shrinking at a floor where the information gained per sample balances the uncertainty injected.
  • The tanh case. The contours are no longer ellipses and the Jacobian changes with w. The filter's P is only a local (Laplace-like) approximation, and early large steps can overshoot — the motivation both for a moderate P₀ and for the iterated and unscented variants.

07 What the EKF is: online Gauss–Newton, natural gradient, RLS

Three different communities arrived at the same recursion. Read through the information form, the EKF is an incremental Gauss–Newton method; for probabilistic outputs it is online natural gradient; for linear models it is recursive least squares.

  • Why the EKF is not "just Gauss–Newton". Batch Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt recompute curvature over the whole dataset at every iterate; the EKF accumulates curvature evaluated at different weights as training moves, discounting old information through Q. It is cheaper per sample, fully online, and — as its authors emphasised — its sequential, noisy updates help it escape the poor local optima batch second-order methods get stuck in.
  • Only squared error, natively. Everything here assumes a Gaussian measurement model, i.e. a sum-of-squares loss. Cross-entropy and other losses can be accommodated (by transforming them into an equivalent squared-error form, or via the exponential-family natural-gradient view), but not as simply as swapping a loss function in an SGD loop.

08 Decoupled EKF: DEKF, node-decoupled, fully decoupled

Throw away the covariance between chosen groups of weights, so P becomes block-diagonal, and run one small filter per group that shares a single global scaling matrix. Node decoupling — one group per neuron's incoming weights — is the classic compromise.

Which parts of P each variant keepsa 4-H-H-2 network with biases, weights ordered node by node
  • Why nodes are the natural groups. The Jacobian block for a node is the outer product cᵢuᵢᵀ (§04), so NDEKF's per-node quantities reduce to vector operations, and weights feeding the same neuron are the ones most strongly coupled in the curvature (they share the same backpropagated signal). In practice NDEKF typically loses little accuracy against GEKF while costing a fraction of it — what the training comparison above shows.
  • Fully decoupled keeps only part of the benefit. A diagonal P is a per-weight step size that decays as that weight gathers evidence — roughly an AdaGrad-like scaling with a Kalman flavour. Useful, but it has discarded the cross-weight curvature that was the point.
  • The shared A is what keeps decoupling consistent. Every group's gain is normalized by the same total predicted output variance ∑ HʲPʲHʲᵀ + R, so the groups jointly explain the innovation instead of each trying to fix the whole error alone.

09 Process noise Q: learning-rate floor, annealing and tracking

Q is artificial — the ideal weights do not really wander — but it is the most important knob. It stops P from collapsing, which keeps the gain (the learning rate) from decaying to zero, lets the filter track targets that change, and injects exploration that helps escape poor local minima.

typical settings (inputs and outputs scaled to unit variance)P₀ = ε⁻¹ I with ε ≈ 0.01 (sigmoidal nodes) … 0.001 (linear nodes) → P₀ diagonal 100 … 1000 R = η⁻¹ I with η ∈ [0.001, 1] smaller η for noisier targets Q = q I, q annealed from ≈ 0.01–0.1 down to ≈ 10⁻⁶ during training — never exactly 0 what Q does to the steady-state gain (scalar intuition from §02)Q = 0: P ∝ 1/k, K → 0 Q > 0: P → P∞ > 0, K → K∞ ≈ √(Q/R) for small Q/R RLS forgetting factor as process noisePₖ₊₁ = λ⁻¹ (Pₖ − KₖHₖPₖ) ⇔ Q = (λ⁻¹ − 1)(Pₖ − KₖHₖPₖ), effective memory ≈ 1/(1 − λ) samples
Tracking a target that changes: q = 0 versus q > 0a 2-weight linear neuron; the true weights jump at sample 200 and then drift
  • The q = 0 filter learns and then stops. Its covariance falls like 1/k; by the time the target jumps, P is tiny, the gain is tiny, and the filter is confidently wrong — it treats the new data as noise. With q > 0 the covariance levels off and the gain stays large enough to re-converge. Too much q and the estimate becomes noisy in the stationary phase: the same bias/variance dial as the forgetting factor of RLS or the Q/R ratio of §02.
  • Annealing q in offline training is a learning-rate schedule: start with enough process noise that early, badly linearized updates do not freeze P prematurely, then shrink it so the final weights settle. Keeping a small non-zero floor also guards against P losing positive definiteness through round-off.
  • Q and exploration. Injecting q into P is not the same as adding noise to the weights, but it has a similar effect on the search: directions that had been "settled" become movable again, so the next samples can pull the weights out of a shallow basin.

10 Training recurrent networks: EKF with truncated BPTT derivatives

For a recurrent network the measurement function depends on the whole input history through the hidden state, so Hₖ must be a dynamic derivative: computed by truncated backpropagation through time or by real-time recurrent learning. The Kalman equations themselves do not change.

recurrent measurement modelŷₖ = h(ŵₖ, uₖ, vₖ₋₁), vₖ = recurrent node activations — which themselves depend on w dynamic derivative by truncated BPTT: a sum of per-step outer productsHₖⁱ = ∑ⱼ₌₁ʰ cᵢ⁽ʲ⁾ (uᵢ⁽ʲ⁾)ᵀ node i's input at step k−j+1 times the output sensitivity to its net input there alternativesRTRL: carry ∂v/∂w forward exactly — O(n⁴) per step for n recurrent units, no truncation BPTT(h): O(h·M) per output per step — the practical choice, truncation depth h ≈ 10–40 steps multistream EKF — several sequence positions in ONE updatestack errors and Jacobians of Nₛ streams: ξ ∈ ℝ^(Nₒ Nₛ), H ∈ ℝ^(Nₒ Nₛ × M), A is (NₒNₛ) × (NₒNₛ)
  • Why the EKF was prized for RNNs. In the 1990s, gradient descent on recurrent nets was notoriously slow and unstable (the vanishing/exploding gradient problem of RNNs & LSTMs). Second-order, per-sample EKF updates trained small recurrent controllers and models that plain BPTT struggled with — Puskorius & Feldkamp (1994) used node-decoupled EKF with BPTT(h) derivatives for engine idle-speed control, and Williams (1992) had formulated EKF training for fully recurrent nets.
  • The recency effect, and multistreaming. A sequential filter drifts towards whatever region of the data it saw last — for time series that must be presented in order, the net may simply retune an output bias for each new regime. Multistream training runs Nₛ copies of the network on Nₛ different positions in the data (or different sequences) and performs one coordinated update from all of them, which is the Kalman-consistent version of a mini-batch.
  • Estimating states and weights together. The same machinery can estimate the network's hidden activations as well: the joint EKF augments the state with the weights; the dual EKF runs two coupled filters, one for states and one for weights. This is the route to learning models of noisy dynamical systems, and to part 8's system identification.

11 The unscented Kalman filter alternative

Instead of linearizing the network, push a small deterministic set of weight vectors — sigma points — through it and measure the spread of the outputs. No Jacobians are needed, and the mean and covariance are captured correctly to second order, where the EKF is only first-order.

unscented transformx ~ N(m, P), dimension L sigma points: X₀ = m, Xᵢ = m ± (√((L + λ) P))ᵢ, i = 1…L (2L + 1 points; √ = Cholesky column) weights: W₀ = λ/(L + λ), Wᵢ = 1/(2(L + λ)) λ = α²(L + κ) − L propagate: Yᵢ = f(Xᵢ) ȳ = ∑ Wᵢ Yᵢ Pᵧ = ∑ Wᵢ (Yᵢ − ȳ)(Yᵢ − ȳ)ᵀ (+ (1 − α² + β) on the i = 0 term) for parameter estimationsigma points are 2M + 1 whole weight vectors → 2M + 1 forward passes per sample, no backward pass cross-covariance Pwy replaces PHᵀ; K = Pwy (Pyy + R)⁻¹ worked: a unit Gaussian centred at 1, through tanhtrue (quadrature) mean 0.550 std 0.497 linearized (EKF) mean 0.762 std 0.420 unscented, κ = 2 mean 0.569 std 0.540
Propagating a Gaussian through a nonlinearity: linearization versus sigma pointsthe true output density is from numerical quadrature
  • Where each method fails. Put the mean on the flat part of tanh with a wide spread: the tangent is almost horizontal, so the linearized variance collapses towards zero while the true output is spread across ±1. Try x²/2 at mean 0: the EKF predicts a point mass at 0 (zero slope, zero variance) and a mean of 0; the true mean is σ²/2, which the unscented transform gets exactly.
  • For network training the gain is smaller than the demo suggests. In parameter estimation the dynamics are the identity, so the only nonlinearity is the network itself, and as P shrinks the region the sigma points probe becomes nearly linear. The UKF's lasting advantages there are (1) no derivatives, so non-differentiable architectures or losses are fine; (2) better behaviour early in training when P is large. Its cost per sample, 2M + 1 forward passes plus an O(M³) Cholesky factor (O(M²) in square-root forms with rank-one updates), is comparable to or larger than GEKF's (Julier & Uhlmann 1997; Wan & van der Merwe 2000).

13 When to use it today

EKF training is the right tool when the network is small, the data arrive as a stream, and every sample must count — and the wrong one for anything with millions of weights.

  • Good fits. Online adaptation of small models in the loop: adaptive control and system identification (part 8), sensor calibration and soft sensors, tracking time-varying relationships, embedded settings where there is no second pass over the data. Also any case where a calibrated uncertainty over the weights is useful — P gives you an approximate posterior for free, so predictions can come with error bars (ŷ variance ≈ HPHᵀ + R).
  • Also good: the last layer only. Freeze a large pretrained feature extractor and run an exact Kalman filter/RLS on the final linear layer: O(d²) for d features, fully online, with uncertainty. This "Bayesian last layer" pattern is a common, practical descendant.
  • Poor fits. Large networks (the M² wall), heavily non-Gaussian losses, and offline training on big datasets, where mini-batch SGD/Adam on GPUs is simpler, better supported and fast enough.
  • The ideas that live on. Curvature preconditioning with Kronecker-factored or low-rank approximations (K-FAC, Shampoo); natural gradient; and online Bayesian deep learning, where diagonal and low-rank-plus-diagonal EKF variants have been revisited for streaming and continual learning — e.g. the low-rank EKF of Chang et al. (2023).

Takeaways

  • Training = estimating a constant state (the weights) from noisy measurements (the targets) through the network; the EKF linearizes the network with its Jacobian.
  • GEKF: A = (R + HPHᵀ)⁻¹, K = PHᵀA, w ← w + Kξ, P ← P − KHP + Q. Cost O(NₒM²) time, O(M²) memory.
  • P⁻¹ accumulates the Gauss–Newton Hessian, so the EKF is an online Gauss–Newton/natural-gradient method with an automatic, per-direction, decaying learning rate; for linear models it is exactly RLS.
  • Decoupling (node-level especially) keeps most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost; fully decoupled keeps much less.
  • Q is the learning-rate floor: anneal it, never set it exactly to zero, and raise it to track drifting targets.
  • For recurrent nets use BPTT(h) or RTRL derivatives and multistreaming; the UKF replaces Jacobians with sigma points.
  • Use it for small, online, uncertainty-aware models; use its descendants for large ones.
