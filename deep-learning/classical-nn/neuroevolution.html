Classical Neural Networks · part 9 of 10

Neuroevolution & Growing Networks

Every other page in this series fixes a network's shape by hand and then adjusts its weights by following a gradient. This page drops both assumptions. Neuroevolution searches with a population instead of a gradient: generate many networks, keep the ones that work, and recombine and mutate them. That makes it possible to search over things a gradient cannot reach, such as the number of neurons, the wiring, the activation functions, and the learning rule itself. Growing networks take the opposite route to the same end: start small and add neurons while training (constructive methods), or start large and remove what is not needed (pruning). Both families run into the same questions. How do you encode a network so that recombining two good ones gives a good one? How do you protect a new structure before its weights have been tuned? How do you describe a network with millions of connections using a genome of a few hundred numbers, the way a genome describes a brain? The answers run from the genetic algorithms of the late 1980s through NEAT and HyperNEAT to evolution strategies on thousands of CPU cores and the evolutionary search that designed image classifiers competitive with the best hand-built architectures.

01 Why evolve networks at all?

Backpropagation needs a differentiable loss, a fixed architecture and a supervised signal at every step. Evolution needs only a number that says how good a network is. Where the first set of requirements holds, gradients win by orders of magnitude in sample efficiency. Neuroevolution exists for the cases where they do not hold, and for the questions that sit one level above weight fitting.

what gradient training assumesθ ← θ − η ∇_θ L(θ) needs: L differentiable in θ · the architecture fixed so that θ has a fixed shape · a per-example target what evolution assumesa population {θ₁, …, θ_P}, a fitness F(θ) ∈ ℝ, variation operators (mutation, crossover), selection F may be non-differentiable, noisy, delayed (one number per whole episode), or computed by a simulator you cannot differentiate the priceeach fitness evaluation is a full run of the network; a gradient step extracts |θ| numbers of information per backward pass, a fitness call extracts one
  • Reinforcement-style problems with sparse or delayed reward. A pole-balancing controller, a walking gait, a game policy: the fitness is "how long did it survive" or "what was the score", and there is no target for each action. Neuroevolution was competitive on such control benchmarks through the 1990s and 2000s and, at scale, again in 2017 (§13).
  • Searching over structure. The number of hidden units, the connectivity, which inputs matter, recurrent or not: these are discrete choices with no gradient. Evolving topologies (§05–§06) and architecture search (§14) handle them directly.
  • Searching over the learning process itself. Plasticity rules, learning rates and initialisations. Evolution can optimise how a network learns during its "lifetime", which is where evolution and learning interact (§09).
  • Exploration and parallelism. A population explores many regions at once and can escape local optima that trap a single gradient trajectory. Evaluations are independent, so the work spreads across as many machines as you have, with almost no communication (§13).
  • Hardware and non-differentiable components. Networks with step activations, integer weights, spiking neurons, or analogue hardware in the loop (see Neural Network Hardware) can be evolved as they are.
The one-sentence summary to carry through the page. Evolution trades sample efficiency for generality. It needs only a fitness score, so it can search over weights, structure and learning rules alike, and the recurring engineering problem is choosing a representation in which small genetic changes make small, meaningful changes to the network.

02 Genetic algorithms in one page

A genetic algorithm (Holland 1975) keeps a population of candidate solutions, each encoded as a genome, and repeats a loop: evaluate fitness, select parents in proportion to how good they are, recombine them, mutate the offspring, and replace the population. Evolution strategies (Rechenberg, Schwefel, 1960s–70s) are the real-valued branch of the same family, with mutation as the main operator and the mutation size itself often evolved.

The classical justification for crossover is the building-block hypothesis: short, fit fragments of genome (schemata) combine into fitter wholes, and Holland's schema theorem says that such fragments grow in frequency under selection when crossover rarely breaks them. For neural networks that hypothesis runs straight into a problem, because in a network genome "fragments" do not have a fixed meaning. §04 shows why. CMA-ES (Hansen & Ostermeier 2001) is the modern standard for real-valued evolution of up to a few thousand parameters. It adapts a full covariance matrix for its Gaussian mutations and so learns the local shape of the fitness landscape, a gradient-free relative of second-order optimisation.

03 Evolving the weights of a fixed network

The simplest neuroevolution fixes the architecture and treats the flattened weight vector as a real-valued genome. Early work (Whitley and others, 1988–90; Montana & Davis 1989) showed that this can train small feedforward nets and, on some deceptive problems, escape local minima that trapped backpropagation. In the supervised setting, though, it is almost always slower than gradient descent.

With the defaults (population 50, mutation σ = 1, tournament of 2, uniform crossover, elitism) the run shown solves XOR at generation 33, after about 1 700 network evaluations. The best-fitness curve climbs in steps, and the mean catches up as the population converges on the solution. One run proves little, though, so use "20 runs", which repeats the experiment from 20 seeds and reports how often it succeeds within 200 generations. With the defaults, 17 of 20 runs succeed, with a median of 31 generations. Selection pressure is the dominant knob. A tournament of 8 solves quickly when it works (median 11.5 generations) but succeeds in only 8 of 20 runs, because the population converges on a network that gets three of the four patterns right and has nowhere to go. A tournament of 1, which is no selection beyond the elite copy, succeeds in 20 of 20 but takes a median of 67.5 generations: close to a random walk anchored at the best-so-far. Switching elitism off drops success to 10 of 20, because the best solution can be lost to an unlucky generation. A larger population is more reliable (200: 20 of 20) but costs 4× the evaluations per generation. And crossover? Mutation alone solves 14 of 20 with a median of 17 generations, one-point crossover 16 of 20, and uniform 17 of 20. On a 9-weight network, crossover buys little, which is a first hint of the problem in §04.

Neuroevolution of weights has two recurring weaknesses. The first is scaling with the number of weights: a mutation perturbs all directions at random, and the chance that a random perturbation improves the fitness falls as the dimension grows, which is why pure GA weight search was long considered hopeless beyond a few thousand weights (§13 shows how that changed). The second is that crossover of weight vectors is often destructive, for the reason in the next section.

04 The competing-conventions problem

A network's hidden units can be listed in any order without changing what the network computes. So many different genomes encode the same function, and two good parents that happen to use different orderings, or different "conventions", produce children that copy some units twice and lose others. This is the competing-conventions or permutation problem (Schaffer, Whitley & Eshelman 1992; Radcliffe 1993), and it is the main reason naive crossover of neural genomes tends to hurt more than it helps.

the symmetry, countedH hidden units in one layer: H! orderings compute the same function with odd activations (tanh), flipping all of one unit's weights and its outgoing weight also preserves the function: H! · 2ᴴ H = 2: 8 equivalent genomes; H = 10: 3 715 891 200; a real network: astronomically many, per layer why crossover breaksparent A = [ OR-unit | AND-unit | out ], parent B = [ AND-unit | OR-unit | out′ ] (the same network, listed the other way) one-point child = [ OR-unit | OR-unit | out′ ]: the AND detector is lost, one feature is duplicated, the output weights no longer match
Two parents that solve XOR, one child that does not move the crossover point; switch parent B between a relabelled copy and a genuinely different solution

With parent B set to parent A with its hidden units swapped, the two parents compute exactly the same function (MSE 0.001 each), yet a cut after gene 3 produces a child whose hidden units are OR and OR, with MSE 0.482, nearly twice as bad as a network that always outputs 0.5 (MSE 0.25). A cut after gene 6 keeps both hidden units from A, in A's order, but takes the output weights from B, which expect them the other way round: MSE 0.500. Only the trivial cuts (after gene 1 or 8, where the child is essentially one parent) survive. Now switch B to a genuinely different XOR solution, OR-and-NAND. Cut after gene 3 and the child happens to be B again (OR is the shared first unit), but cut after gene 6 and the child combines A's OR and AND with B's output layer, which was built for OR and NAND: MSE 0.719, worse than guessing. Crossover between good parents is only useful when their genes play the same roles, and with neural networks that is the exception, not the rule.

There are three kinds of cure. Avoid crossover: many successful weight-evolution systems, and the large-scale ES of §13, use mutation only. Align before recombining: match hidden units by what they compute, for example by correlating their activations, before swapping genes. Align by history: record where each gene came from, so that genes with a common origin line up automatically. NEAT's historical markings (§06) are the third cure, and they solve the problem cheaply for topologies as well as weights.

05 Evolving topologies: direct encodings and their pitfalls

Once the structure is also evolved (topology and weights evolving networks, or TWEANNs), the genome has to describe which connections exist. A direct encoding writes every neuron and connection into the genome explicitly. An indirect encoding writes a program that builds the network (§08).

connectivity-matrix encoding (Miller, Todd & Hegde 1989)genome = the N×N binary matrix Cᵢⱼ ∈ {0, 1} flattened to N² bits; weights then trained by backprop genome size grows as N²; the maximum network size is fixed in advance graph / list encodinggenome = a list of node genes and connection genes (from, to, weight, enabled): variable length, can grow structural mutationsadd connection: a new gene (i → j, random w) add node: split an existing connection i → j into i → new → j remove connection / node; enable / disable a gene

Three problems made topology evolution hard before NEAT. Competing conventions again, worse: two networks with different topologies have no obvious gene-to-gene correspondence at all, so crossover is ill-defined. Systems such as GNARL (Angeline, Saunders & Pollack 1994) gave up on crossover entirely. Innovation gets killed: adding a node or connection usually lowers fitness at first, because the new structure's weights are untuned, so selection removes it before it can prove useful. Bloat: starting from random, large topologies wastes evaluations in a huge search space and produces networks much bigger than needed. A different line of work, neuron-level evolution, evolved populations of individual hidden neurons that cooperate to form networks (SANE, Moriarty & Miikkulainen 1996; ESP; CoSyNE, Gomez et al. 2008). It sidesteps the permutation problem by evolving the parts in separate subpopulations, and it held pole-balancing records for years.

06 NEAT: historical markings, speciation, complexification

NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (Stanley & Miikkulainen 2002) answers the three problems of §05 with three mechanisms. Historical markings let genomes line up for crossover. Speciation protects new structure while its weights are tuned. Complexification, starting from the smallest possible networks, keeps the search space small and the solutions compact.

NEAT genomes: structural mutations, historical markings, crossover and δ mutate either parent; watch innovation numbers line the genes up and δ decide the species

The figure opens after a short, scripted history. Both parents split the same connection 1→4 independently, and because a mutation is identified by what it does, not by who did it, both receive node 5 and innovations 4 and 5, so those genes line up as matching without any analysis of the graphs. Each parent then added a different connection, which gives one disjoint and one excess gene. Add a few nodes and connections to one parent and watch the excess and disjoint counts rise, and δ with them. At the start the parents share five genes, differ by one disjoint and one excess gene, and have a mean weight difference of 0.100 on the matching genes, so δ = 1 + 1 + 0.4·0.100 = 2.04 < 3.0: the same species. Give parent 1 an add-node, an add-connection and another add-node, and it grows to 11 genes, five of them excess, so δ = 5.06 and the two parents now belong to different species. A structural innovation has started a new niche, in which it competes only with its own kind until its weights are tuned. Note also that the child's structure comes from parent 1, the fitter one, because disjoint and excess genes are taken only from the fitter parent. Crossover in NEAT refines weights within a shared topology and does not merge two topologies.

Why each mechanism is needed

  • Historical markings make crossover meaningful at the cost of a counter. Aligning two arbitrary graphs is an expensive graph-matching problem. Aligning two lists sorted by innovation number is linear. Genes with the same number descend from the same ancestral mutation, so they play the same structural role, which solves the competing-conventions problem for everything evolved after the common ancestor.
  • Speciation turns "innovation gets killed" into "innovation gets a grace period". A genome with a new node is structurally distant from the rest, so it forms or joins a small species and competes only within it. Fitness sharing divides each individual's fitness by the size of its species, so a new species' few members are not swamped by one large, established species. A species that fails to improve for about 15 generations is removed.
  • Complexification keeps the search in a small space. Every genome starts as inputs wired directly to outputs, with no hidden units and only random weights. Structure is added only when mutations that add it survive selection, so the search passes through small networks first and the solutions stay compact. On XOR, NEAT found solutions in an average of 32 generations (about 4 755 networks evaluated, population 150), usually with 2 or 3 hidden nodes, where the minimal solution needs 1. The ablations in the original paper showed that removing any one of the three mechanisms made performance much worse.

NEAT and its descendants (rtNEAT for real-time evolution in a video game, odNEAT for online evolution in robots, and the HyperNEAT family of §07) remain a standard baseline in evolutionary robotics and artificial life, and it is the reference design for any evolutionary method that has to recombine variable-size graphs.

07 CPPNs and HyperNEAT: networks that paint networks

A direct encoding needs one gene per connection, so a network with a million connections needs a million genes, with no way to say "make the left side mirror the right" or "repeat this motif across the retina". A compositional pattern-producing network (CPPN; Stanley 2007) is a small network of mixed activation functions (Gaussian, sine, sigmoid, absolute value, linear) that maps coordinates to values. HyperNEAT (Stanley, D'Ambrosio & Gauci 2009) evolves a CPPN with NEAT and then queries it for the weight of every connection in a large network whose neurons have been placed in space, called the substrate.

One CPPN, any resolution: querying a substrate's connectivity pick a CPPN, then raise the substrate resolution: the genome stays the same size, the network grows

The "locality" CPPN is a single Gaussian of x₂ − x₁, so each target neuron connects to the sources directly below it: a receptive field, the connectivity principle of a convolutional layer, found here as a two-node function of geometry. "Mirror symmetry" connects each position to its mirror image. "Repetition" gives periodic bands. Move the resolution slider from 9 to 41 and the connection count grows from 81 to 1 681 potential links, while the genome that describes them stays at a handful of numbers and the pattern keeps its character. That is the post-training scalability HyperNEAT was built for: CPPNs with a few dozen connections have specified networks with millions. The right-hand heat map is the key insight in miniature. A layer's connectivity is a two-dimensional image over (source, target) space, and evolving CPPNs that paint images is a problem NEAT already solved. The published method evolves the CPPN's weights and topology with NEAT. Here the function is fixed and only one parameter moves, so that the geometry–connectivity mapping stays readable.

HyperNEAT's weakness is the other side of its strength. It is excellent at regular connectivity and poor at the irregular exceptions that many tasks also need, a known finding that led to hybrids (HybrID) that switch to a direct encoding for fine-tuning. Later variants learned where to place the hidden neurons (ES-HyperNEAT, Risi & Stanley 2012) and added a separate output that decides whether a link exists (LEO). CPPNs outlived HyperNEAT as a generative tool: interactive evolution of CPPN images (Picbreeder) and, in modern deep learning, coordinate-based networks such as NeRF and SIREN, which also map positions to values through periodic activations.

08 Indirect and developmental encodings: growing a network from a genome

A human genome has roughly 20 000 protein-coding genes, and the brain it builds has about 10¹⁴ synapses. There is no way the genome lists them. It encodes a developmental process (cell division, migration, axon guidance, activity-dependent pruning) that builds the brain by interacting with itself and its environment. Artificial neurogenesis tries to give evolved networks the same compression and the same biases toward modularity, repetition and hierarchy.

grammatical encoding (Kitano 1990): evolve rewriting rules, expand them into a connectivity matrixS → [A B; C D], A → [c p; a c], …, terminal symbols → 2×2 blocks of 0/1 — k rewriting steps give a 2ᵏ × 2ᵏ matrix cellular encoding (Gruau 1992–94): a program tree read by dividing cellsstart: input → [mother cell] → output; each cell reads a node of the genome tree: SEQ (divide in series), PAR (divide in parallel), weight and bias edits, recursion (repeat a subtree n times) ⇒ modular, repeated structure from a short genome genetic regulatory networks (e.g. GReaNs)genes produce products that switch other genes on and off; cells read the concentrations and divide, differentiate and connect the general patternphenotype = develop( genome, environment ) search happens over genomes; fitness is measured on phenotypes
  • Grammar-based encodings. Kitano (1990) evolved matrix-rewriting grammars in the style of Lindenmayer systems and reported better scaling than direct encodings on encoder–decoder tasks. Later work in a different setting disputed that advantage, and the debate about whether developmental encodings really scale better has never fully settled. Grammar and graph-rewriting encodings were also used to evolve the bodies and brains of Sims' "virtual creatures" (1994) and the robots of Hornby's GenRe.
  • Cellular encoding produced modular networks for parity, symmetry and decoder problems, and evolved neural controllers for hexapod walking. Its genomic recursion let one subtree describe a module used many times, which is the property direct encodings lack.
  • Gene-regulatory and cell-chemistry models go closest to biology. Cells, morphogen gradients and regulatory networks develop into the neural structure. They are the most expressive and the most expensive to evaluate, and they are mostly research tools for studying which developmental biases help.
  • Activity-dependent self-organisation. Development need not be purely genetic. Networks can wire themselves through Hebbian learning on internally generated activity, the way retinal waves help organise the visual system before birth. The self-organising maps of part 4 are the simplest instance of this.

The payoff that developmental encodings aim for is generalisation through regularity. A genome that says "repeat this module 8 times" can be improved once and benefits in 8 places, a mutation that changes a module changes all its copies coherently, and the result tends to be modular. The cost is locality: a small change to a developmental genome can make a huge change to the phenotype, which makes the fitness landscape rugged. The same tension between compact, regular encodings and smooth, local search reappears in the choice of search space for architecture search (§14).

09 Combining development and learning: the Baldwin effect

If individuals can learn during their lifetime, does that help evolution, even though what is learned is not inherited? The Baldwin effect (Baldwin 1896) says yes: learning lets an individual that is genetically close to a good solution reach it, which gives it a fitness advantage, which lets selection favour genomes that are close, until later generations need less learning to get there. Hinton and Nowlan (1987) built the classic minimal simulation, reproduced below.

How learning guides evolution: Hinton & Nowlan's 20-locus needle in a haystack population 1 000, fitness-proportional selection, one-point crossover, 50 generations; compare with and without learning

With learning, the incorrect alleles disappear within a few generations: any fixed 0 makes the target unreachable, so those individuals get the minimum fitness. After 50 generations the population holds 76.5% correct, 0.0% incorrect and 23.5% learnable alleles, and 100% of individuals find the target within their lifetime (mean fitness 19.35 of a possible 20). Switch learning off and nothing happens: after 50 generations 0% of individuals have ever hit the one fit genotype, the fitness is flat at 1, and the allele frequencies drift randomly around 50/50 (51.5% correct). Learning turned an impossible search into an easy one without anything learned being inherited. The trials slider shows how the effect depends on the learning budget. With only T = 200 trials, the typical starting individual (about 10 question marks, so p = 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 1/1 000 per trial) almost never succeeds, the landscape is flat again, and 50 generations produce nothing (0% succeed). With T = 3 000 the outcome resembles T = 1 000 (73.9% correct). Note also that the question marks do not go to zero. Once only a few remain, learning finds the answer within a handful of trials, and the fitness difference between four ?s and none is too small for selection to act on. That plateau appears in the original paper too, and it is the Baldwin effect's built-in limit: evolution fixes what learning makes cheap to fix, and leaves the rest to learning.

The lesson carries over to neural networks directly: evolve what a network starts from and how it learns, and let learning do the fine-tuning. Evolving initial weights that backprop then trains, evolving Hebbian plasticity rules so that networks adapt during their lifetime (Floreano & Mondada 1996; later neuromodulated plasticity), and evolving learning rates are all Baldwinian designs. Work on artificial neurogenesis argues that development and learning are partly interchangeable: structure that a developmental program builds and structure that activity-dependent learning builds can substitute for each other, and the best systems use both. Modern meta-learning, which optimises initialisations so that a few gradient steps suffice (MAML, Finn et al. 2017), is a gradient-based Baldwin effect. Evolution-based versions, such as evolving learning rules or entire learning algorithms from primitives (AutoML-Zero, Real et al. 2020), are the direct descendants.

10 Constructive algorithms: growing a network during training

Growing a network does not require a population. A constructive algorithm starts with a minimal network, trains it, and adds units when training stalls. The most influential is cascade-correlation (Fahlman & Lebiere 1990). Each new hidden unit is trained to explain as much as possible of the network's remaining error, then frozen and installed as a new layer that receives input from every earlier unit.

On the XOR quadrants, a linear output unit alone misclassifies 40% of the points, and cascade-correlation reaches 0% training error with 5 hidden units (25 frozen weights and 8 output weights). The concentric rings need only 3 units. The two spirals, the benchmark cascade-correlation was designed to show off, need 13 units, each one layer deeper than the last, with 117 frozen and 16 trainable weights. Fahlman and Lebiere reported 12 to 19 units on the same problem, where plain backpropagation needed a hand-designed multi-layer network and far more epochs. Watch the error curve: every green marker is a new unit, and the output-only training that follows it drops the error in a step. That staircase is the signature of constructive learning. Look also at the spiral decision surface after it is solved. It weaves between individual training points, a reminder that "grow until the training error is zero" is a recipe for overfitting (§15).

  • Why the correlation objective. A candidate that correlates strongly with the residual error is a feature the output layer can use to cancel that error. Maximising covariance, not minimising error through the unit, keeps each step a single-layer optimisation. The candidate pool (8 here, typically 4–16) is cheap insurance against bad random starts.
  • Why freezing matters. In backprop all hidden units chase a moving target, because each one's best role changes as the others change, and this herding slows learning. Cascade-correlation freezes each feature once learned, so later units build on fixed ground. The price is that early features are never refined.
  • Weaknesses. The cascade makes deep, narrow networks whose depth equals the number of units, which is slow at inference and prone to overfitting on noisy regression. It also greedily adds units that fit the training set, with no built-in regularisation. Later variants limited the depth, grouped units into layers, or combined growth with pruning (AMGA; Islam et al. 2009).
  • Relatives. The tiling algorithm (Mézard & Nadal 1989) and the upstart algorithm (Frean 1990) grow networks of threshold units for Boolean problems; the perceptron is their building block. Resource-allocating networks (Platt 1991) add a Gaussian unit whenever a novel, badly predicted input arrives, a constructive RBF network. Modern echoes include progressive networks (Rusu et al. 2016), which add columns for new tasks and freeze the old ones, and net-to-net growth (Chen et al. 2016), which widens or deepens a trained network while preserving its function, the same move as NEAT's function-preserving add-node.

11 Destructive algorithms: pruning, Optimal Brain Damage and Surgeon

The opposite route: train a network larger than necessary, then remove weights or units that contribute least, and retrain. The question is which weight contributes least. The obvious answer, the smallest, is often wrong, because a small weight on a high-curvature direction can matter more than a large one on a flat direction. The classical answers use the Hessian of the loss.

The default has trained weights w* = (1.2, −0.8) on a loss that is 8 times steeper in one direction than the other, rotated 15°. Magnitude pruning removes w₂, the smaller weight, but w₂ sits on the steep direction: even after retraining w₁, removing w₂ raises the loss by 1.743. OBD and OBS both remove w₁, which costs 1.058 with w₂ held fixed and only 0.765 once OBS's correction lets w₂ absorb part of the loss. That gap between 1.058 and 0.765 is the value of OBS's extra step: it predicts the loss after the survivors adjust, and it gives the adjustment without retraining. Set the rotation to 0° and the Hessian becomes diagonal, OBD and OBS coincide exactly, and only the magnitude rule is still fooled by the curvature. A subtle point the figure makes visible: with only two weights OBD and OBS always choose the same weight, because both reduce to comparing H₁₁w₁² with H₂₂w₂². They can disagree only when three or more weights are coupled, which is exactly the regime of real networks.

OBD's original demonstration pruned a digit-recognition network by a factor of four with no loss in accuracy. OBS pruned further on small benchmarks, at the price of an inverse Hessian that is quadratic in the number of weights. Modern pruning of large networks mostly returns to magnitude with iterative retraining, because at scale the Hessian is unaffordable and many weights are redundant. But the second-order idea came back in approximations such as layer-wise OBS and Kronecker-factored curvature, and it underlies recent one-shot pruning of large language models (SparseGPT, Frantar & Alistarh 2023, is layer-wise OBS). The lottery-ticket hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin 2019) gives pruning a constructive reading: a large random network contains small subnetworks that, trained from their original initial weights, match the full network. The large network is then a search space, and pruning is the search. See Model Acceleration for pruning as a deployment tool and Knowledge Distillation for the other way to shrink a trained network.

Both constructive and pruning algorithms are greedy in their simplest forms, and both can get stuck in "architectural local optima". Hybrids that grow and prune (split a unit that is doing too much, merge two that are highly correlated) handle that better than either alone. Brains appear to do both: early development overproduces synapses and later prunes them by activity, while neurogenesis continues in a few regions.

13 Evolution strategies at scale

In 2017 a result revived weight neuroevolution for deep networks. Natural evolution strategies, run on up to 1 440 CPU cores, trained deep policy networks on MuJoCo locomotion and Atari about as well as the reinforcement-learning algorithms of the day, and much faster in wall-clock time (Salimans, Ho, Chen, Sidor & Sutskever 2017). The trick is to read the population as a Monte-Carlo estimate of a gradient.

The landscape is a broad bowl with its peak at (1.2, 0.8) (F = 0.90), covered by an egg-crate of local optima. At the default σ = 0.5 the bumps in the smoothed objective shrink by a factor e^(−K²σ²) = 0.018 (K = 4 is the bumps' spatial frequency), so ES effectively sees only the bowl: 50 steps (two presses of "25 steps") take it to F = 0.895, next to the global peak. With σ = 0.1 the smoothing is too weak (the factor is 0.85), the estimate follows the local gradient faithfully (mean cosine 0.96 with the true gradient), and the same 50 steps end in a local optimum at F = −0.91. σ = 0.3 reaches 0.76, and σ = 1.0 reaches 0.88. σ is therefore a resolution knob, not just a step size: large σ ignores structure finer than itself, for better and worse. The population size controls the estimate's accuracy. With 20 samples (10 antithetic pairs) the estimate's mean cosine with the smoothed gradient is 0.95; with 2 samples it is 0.64; with 200 it is 0.99. That is the familiar 1/√n Monte-Carlo trade, and in a million-dimensional network it is why ES needs populations in the hundreds or thousands and the seed-sharing trick to make them affordable.

  • ES is a gradient method on a blurred objective. It is not a mysterious alternative to gradient descent. It is finite differences in random directions, and it optimises J, not F. That explains both its robustness to rugged or noisy fitness and its weakness when the good region is narrower than σ.
  • Why it competes in RL at all. Policy-gradient RL estimates gradients from noise injected into actions at every timestep, and its variance grows with the episode length. ES injects noise into parameters once per episode, so its variance does not depend on the horizon, and it is indifferent to sparse or delayed reward and to frame-skip. It needs no backward pass and no value function.
  • A deep GA also works. A plain genetic algorithm with mutation only, truncation selection and no gradient estimate trained networks with more than four million weights on Atari competitively (Such et al. 2017), a result that surprised the field and counters the scaling argument of §03. Novelty search (Lehman & Stanley 2011) replaces fitness with behavioural novelty and solves deceptive mazes that fitness-driven search cannot.
  • Where it loses. Supervised learning with a differentiable loss: backprop gets |θ| numbers of gradient information per example, while ES gets one scalar per evaluation, and no amount of parallelism closes that gap for large supervised models.

14 From neuroevolution to neural architecture search

Neural architecture search (NAS) is topology evolution with modern building blocks. The genome describes layers or cells (convolutions, attention, skip connections), not individual neurons, and each candidate's weights are trained by gradient descent, so the search is Baldwinian by construction. Evolution is one of the three main NAS search strategies, alongside reinforcement learning and gradient-based relaxation.

large-scale evolution of image classifiers (Real et al. 2017)start from trivial networks; mutations: insert/remove a convolution, change a filter size, add a skip, alter the learning rate, … tournament selection over a population of about 1 000 with weight inheritance; competitive CIFAR-10 accuracy from scratch regularised (aging) evolution (Real, Aggarwal, Huang & Le 2019) → AmoebaNeteach cycle: sample S individuals, the best is mutated into a child, the OLDEST individual (not the worst) is removed aging forces the population to keep re-earning its place ⇒ robust to noisy evaluations; matched RL-based NAS on ImageNet the other strategiesRL controller that emits architectures (Zoph & Le 2017) · differentiable relaxation of the choice (DARTS, Liu et al. 2019) · weight sharing (ENAS) · Bayesian optimisation

Every classical theme resurfaces in NAS. Encoding: searching over repeated cells, not whole networks, is the modularity argument of developmental encodings (§08). A cell found on small images transfers to large ones, the post-training scalability HyperNEAT promised. Protecting innovation: aging evolution plays the role speciation plays in NEAT, keeping the population from locking onto early winners. Cost: early NAS used thousands of GPU-days, and weight sharing, low-fidelity proxies and early stopping of bad candidates are the modern answers to "each fitness evaluation is a full training run". Evaluating many candidates on small budgets and promoting the best is the successive-halving idea covered in Hyperparameter Tuning, which also compares random, grid and Bayesian search. NAS is hyperparameter tuning in which the hyperparameters form a graph. A notable finding: random search over a well-designed space is a strong NAS baseline (Li & Talwalkar 2019), so much of the gain comes from the design of the search space, the encoding question again.

Two further directions extend the ambition. Weight-agnostic networks (Gaier & Ha 2019) evolved topologies that perform tasks with a single shared weight value, showing how much a structure alone can encode. AutoML-Zero (Real et al. 2020) evolved entire learning algorithms from basic mathematical operations and rediscovered backpropagation-trained two-layer networks, normalised gradients and weight averaging. That completes the arc from evolving weights to evolving the learning process.

15 Practice and pitfalls

  • Use gradients when you have them. For supervised learning with a differentiable loss, evolution is almost never competitive on weights. Use it for the structure, the hyperparameters, or the non-differentiable parts, and let gradient descent train the weights inside each candidate.
  • Budget the evaluations, not the generations. Report and compare methods in fitness evaluations (or compute), not generations. A population of 1 000 for 50 generations is not "faster" than 100 for 300.
  • Noisy fitness is the norm. RL episodes and short training runs give noisy scores. Elitism then preserves lucky individuals, not good ones, so re-evaluate survivors, average several episodes, or use aging (§14) or rank-based shaping (§13).
  • Watch diversity. Premature convergence is the classic failure: the whole population becomes one genotype and the search stops. Speciation, fitness sharing, novelty objectives, larger populations and lower selection pressure all counter it.
  • Deceptive fitness landscapes. When the path to the goal first leads away from it, as in a maze with a dead end facing the exit, fitness-driven search stalls. Stepping-stone approaches such as novelty search, quality-diversity (MAP-Elites) and open-ended coevolution (POET) were built for this.
  • Choose the encoding deliberately. Direct encodings search locally and scale poorly; indirect ones scale and generalise but make mutations less predictable. Whatever the choice, a small mutation should usually make a small change in behaviour, or evolution becomes random search.
  • Growing and pruning need a stopping rule. Constructive methods overfit if allowed to add units until the training error hits zero (try the two-spirals data above and look at how the decision surface bends to single points). Use a validation set or a complexity penalty, as with early stopping.
