Contrastive Learning

Learn useful image features with no labels at all. Take two random augmentations of the same image as a positive pair, treat every other image in the batch as a negative, and train an encoder so positives land close together and negatives spread apart. Throw away the contrastive head afterwards and the encoder's features rival supervised pretraining on downstream tasks.

Self-supervised Representation learning InfoNCE Augmentation

01 The core idea: instance discrimination

No classes, no captions — just the rule that an image is similar to itself and different from everything else.

Supervised learning needs a label for every image. Contrastive learning needs none. Its pretext task is instance discrimination: each image is implicitly its own class. The model never sees the word "cat", yet to push a cat's two crops together while keeping them apart from a thousand other images, it has no choice but to learn what makes a cat a cat — shape, texture, parts — features that transfer to real downstream tasks.

The pipeline in one breath

  1. Sample a minibatch of N images.
  2. Apply two independent random augmentations to each → 2N views.
  3. Encode every view with a shared backbone f (e.g. a ResNet) → features h.
  4. Map h through a small projection head g → embeddings z.
  5. Apply a contrastive loss in z-space: each view's positive is the other view of the same image; the rest are negatives.
  6. Discard g; keep f and use h for downstream tasks.
One sentence: contrastive learning turns "is this the same image as that one?" into a self-labelled classification problem, and the encoder learns transferable features as a side effect.

Where it sits among the paradigms

  • vs supervised: no human labels — the supervision signal is manufactured from the data itself via augmentation. See Learning Paradigms for the broader self-supervised family.
  • vs generative / reconstruction SSL: autoencoders and masked-image models (MAE) learn by reconstructing pixels; contrastive methods never reconstruct — they only compare embeddings. Discriminating in feature space avoids wasting capacity on pixel-perfect detail.
  • The payoff is reuse: the pretrained encoder is a starting point for fine-tuning & transfer learning, often with far fewer labels than training from scratch.

02 Building pairs: augmentation is the pretext task

The augmentation pipeline matters more than the architecture — it defines what "the same image" is allowed to mean.

A positive pair is just two random augmentations t, t' of one image x: (t(x), t'(x)). The encoder is being told "these two distorted views are the same thing — find a representation invariant to that distortion." The set of transforms therefore decides exactly which invariances the model acquires.

The standard augmentation stack

  • Random resized crop — sample a sub-region and resize to a fixed size. Forces invariance to scale and position and is the single most important transform.
  • Color jitter / distortion — randomly perturb brightness, contrast, saturation, hue.
  • Random grayscale — occasionally drop color entirely.
  • Gaussian blur — soften high-frequency detail.
  • Random horizontal flip.
Crucial finding: random crop and color distortion only work when applied together. With cropping alone the network cheats — two crops of one image share the same color histogram, so it can "solve" the task by matching average color without learning anything about content. Color distortion breaks that shortcut and forces real feature learning. Either transform alone is weak; the composition is what works.

Stronger augmentation = a harder pretext task = (up to a point) better representations. This is the contrastive analogue of how heavy masking makes masked-image pretraining hard enough to be useful.

03 The NT-Xent / InfoNCE objective

A normalized-temperature cross-entropy loss: a softmax that asks "out of everyone in the batch, which one is my positive?"

Work in z-space. Similarity is cosine similarity on the normalized embeddings:

sim(u, v) = uᵀv / ( ‖u‖ · ‖v‖ )

Take a batch of N images, augment each twice → 2N embeddings z₁ … z₂ₙ. For a positive pair (i, j) (the two views of one image), the loss for that directed pair is

ℓ(i, j) = −log [ exp( sim(zᵢ, zⱼ) / τ ) / ∑ₖ₌₁²ᴺ 𝟙[k ≠ i] · exp( sim(zᵢ, zₖ) / τ ) ]

where τ is the temperature and 𝟙[k ≠ i] excludes the anchor from being compared with itself. The denominator runs over all 2N − 1 other views: the one positive plus 2N − 2 negatives. Read it as a (2N − 1)-way softmax classification — the logits are the scaled similarities, and the correct "class" is the positive.

The total loss averages ℓ over every positive pair in both directions, since ℓ(i, j) ≠ ℓ(j, i) (each has its own anchor and denominator):

L = (1 / 2N) · ∑ₖ₌₁ᴺ [ ℓ(2k−1, 2k) + ℓ(2k, 2k−1) ]
  • Numerator pulls the positive in: maximizing sim(zᵢ, zⱼ) shrinks the loss.
  • Denominator pushes negatives away: every negative that is too similar inflates the sum and the loss.
  • This is exactly the InfoNCE objective (the "NCE" of contrastive predictive coding): a noise-contrastive estimator that lower-bounds the mutual information between the two views. The same loss shape powers cross-modal alignment in CLIP and metric learning over embeddings.

04 Temperature τ: the hardness knob

τ rescales similarities before the softmax, controlling how sharply the loss focuses on the hardest negatives.

Because logits are sim / τ, temperature stretches or compresses the gap between negatives:

  • Low τ (sharp). Dividing by a small number magnifies differences. The softmax concentrates almost all its weight — and gradient — on the nearest (hardest) negatives. The model is harshly penalized for any negative that creeps close to the anchor.
  • High τ (soft). The distribution flattens; all negatives are weighted roughly uniformly, easy and hard alike. Gradients are gentler and the model tolerates nearby negatives.

τ is one of the most sensitive hyperparameters in the whole recipe. The tuned default in the canonical image setup is small — around τ ≈ 0.1 — with related methods using values roughly in the 0.07 – 0.5 range depending on batch size, backbone, and augmentation strength. The exact best value is configuration-dependent; treat it as a knob to tune, not a constant.

Intuition: τ trades off uniformity against hard-negative emphasis. Too low and training fixates on a few hard cases and can destabilize; too high and it stops discriminating finely. The viz below makes the softmax-over-negatives behavior tangible.

05 The projection head — and why you throw it away

A tiny MLP maps features to the space where the loss lives. Counterintuitively, you keep the layer before it for downstream use.

The backbone produces a feature vector h = f(x). A small projection head — typically one hidden layer with ReLU — maps it to the contrastive space:

z = g(h) = W₂ · σ( W₁ · h )

The NT-Xent loss is computed on z. But for the actual downstream task — linear probing, fine-tuning — you discard g and use h instead.

Slightly counterintuitive, and a key result: the representation before the head (h) is substantially better for downstream tasks than the one the loss is applied to (z) — on the order of 10%+ higher on linear evaluation. The head absorbs invariances the loss demands (e.g. throwing away color or orientation) that you do not want baked into your general-purpose encoder. By letting g soak up that augmentation-specific information, h stays richer.

So the head is a buffer: it specializes to the pretext task and is sacrificed, leaving the encoder general.

06 Negatives & batch size: more is better (and costly)

The contrast is only as strong as the negatives you contrast against — and in-batch negatives tie that strength to batch size.

In the in-batch scheme, the negatives for any anchor are simply the other 2N − 2 views in the same minibatch. More negatives means a harder, more informative discrimination problem: the encoder must distinguish its positive from many more distractors, which sharpens the features.

  • Large batches help. A batch of 4096 images supplies thousands of in-batch negatives per anchor, and accuracy keeps climbing with batch size well past where ordinary supervised training plateaus.
  • The cost. Such batches need the LARS optimizer (layer-wise adaptive rates keep huge-batch SGD stable), long training schedules, and a lot of accelerator memory. This is the practical price of in-batch negatives.
  • See Neural-Network Training for why batch size, learning-rate scaling, and optimizer choice interact this way.

07 MoCo: decoupling negatives from batch size

If big batches are the bottleneck, store negatives instead of recomputing them — a dynamic dictionary with a slowly-moving key encoder.

Momentum Contrast (MoCo) keeps a large pool of negatives without a large batch by maintaining a queue — a FIFO dictionary of recently encoded "keys". Each step enqueues the current batch's keys and dequeues the oldest, so a small batch still contrasts against thousands of stored negatives.

Two encoders, one moving slowly

The anchor ("query") is encoded by f_q with parameters θ_q, trained normally by gradient descent. The keys are encoded by a separate momentum encoder f_k whose weights are not updated by gradients but by an exponential moving average of the query encoder:

θ_k ← m · θ_k + (1 − m) · θ_q , m ≈ 0.999

The large momentum makes f_k drift slowly, so keys enqueued many steps apart stay consistent — they were produced by nearly the same encoder. Without this, the queued negatives would be stale and inconsistent, and the contrast would break down.

08 Downstream evaluation: the linear probe

How do you measure a label-free encoder? Freeze it and let a single linear layer do the talking.

The standard protocol is the linear probe:

  1. Freeze the pretrained encoder f — its weights do not change.
  2. Train only a single linear classifier on top of the frozen features h, using labels.
  3. Report top-1 accuracy. Because the encoder is frozen, the number reflects the quality of the features, not the classifier.

On the canonical image setup, a contrastively pretrained ResNet-50 reaches roughly 69.3% top-1 under linear evaluation — closing much of the gap to fully supervised training of the same backbone, with no labels used during pretraining.

  • Fine-tuning. Unfreeze the encoder and adapt end-to-end on the target task — usually beats the linear probe when labels are plentiful. See Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning.
  • Semi-supervised / few-label. Contrastive features shine when labels are scarce: fine-tuning on just 1% or 10% of labels recovers most of the fully-supervised accuracy — the headline practical win.

09 The landscape: SimCLR, MoCo, supervised & beyond

Same core loss, different answers to "where do the negatives come from?" — and a branch that drops negatives entirely.

Supervised contrastive learning is a telling hybrid: instead of a single positive per anchor, it treats every image of the same label as a positive and contrasts against other classes. It uses labels, yet keeps the InfoNCE geometry — a clean bridge between this page and classic supervised training.

Where the field went next: drop the negatives

  • BYOL / SimSiam. Non-contrastive: no negatives at all. A predictor network plus a stop-gradient on the target branch prevents the trivial collapse (all embeddings identical) that negatives normally guard against.
  • DINO. Self-distillation with a momentum teacher, no negatives — yields ViT features with striking unsupervised segmentation properties; ties back to Knowledge Distillation.
  • Cross-modal contrast. CLIP applies the same InfoNCE loss across modalities — matching an image to its caption against a batch of mismatched captions — which powers zero-shot recognition in vision-language models.

10 Takeaways

  • Labels not required. Instance discrimination manufactures supervision: two augmentations of one image are a positive pair; everything else is a negative.
  • Augmentation is the task. Random crop + color distortion together are essential — crop alone lets the network cheat on color statistics.
  • NT-Xent / InfoNCE is a (2N − 1)-way softmax over cosine similarities: "pick my positive out of all the negatives."
  • Temperature τ tunes hardness — low τ sharpens focus onto the hardest negatives; high τ spreads weight uniformly. ≈0.1 is a common default.
  • Keep h, drop z. Apply the loss after the projection head but use the pre-head features downstream — they're 10%+ better.
  • Negatives = signal. SimCLR buys them with huge batches; MoCo buys them with a queue + momentum encoder.
  • Evaluate by linear probe on the frozen encoder (≈69.3% top-1 ResNet-50), then fine-tune — especially powerful with few labels.
  • The idea generalizes: non-contrastive cousins (BYOL, SimSiam, DINO) drop negatives via stop-gradient, and CLIP carries InfoNCE across modalities.
