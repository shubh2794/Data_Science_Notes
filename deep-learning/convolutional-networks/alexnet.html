AlexNet: Deep Convolutional Networks on ImageNet

The 2012 study that trained an eight-layer convolutional network on 1.2 million ImageNet images and cut the competition's top-5 error from 26.2% to 15.3%. The lineage table gives it one row and the image-classification arc gives it one headline number. This page reads the paper itself: the exact layer specification and where the two GPUs talk to each other, why the non-saturating unit mattered, the normalisation and pooling choices with their measured gains, the two augmentations and dropout, the precise optimiser update, every results table, what the network's features turned out to encode, and which of its choices survived.

01 The setting: a dataset big enough, and a model with the right priors

The argument of the paper is that two things had just become available at the same time: labelled image sets in the millions, and GPUs fast enough to train a convolutional network big enough to use them. Earlier labelled sets held tens of thousands of images, which is enough for digits but not for objects in realistic settings.

  • Why a convnet, not a bigger generic network. The paper's reason is that even ImageNet cannot fully specify the problem, so the model needs strong prior knowledge. A convnet assumes stationary statistics and local pixel dependencies, which are "strong and mostly correct" for images. Compared with a fully connected network of similar layer sizes it has far fewer connections and parameters, so it is easier to train, and its best possible performance is likely to be only slightly worse. The full case for those two priors is on the why convolution page.
  • The data. ImageNet held over 15 million labelled images in about 22,000 categories. The competition (ILSVRC) uses a subset with roughly 1000 images in each of 1000 categories: about 1.2 million training, 50,000 validation and 150,000 test images. The 2010 edition is the only one whose test labels are public, so most experiments use it. The 2012 results (§11) are reported on validation, plus the official test number.
  • Preprocessing is almost nothing. The images vary in size, so each is rescaled so that its shorter side is 256 and then centre-cropped to 256×256. The only other step is subtracting the mean activity over the training set from each pixel, so the network trains on centred raw RGB values. There is no whitening, no contrast normalisation and no hand-crafted features.
the two error rates the competition reportstop-1 error = fraction of test images whose most probable label is wrong top-5 error = fraction of test images whose correct label is not among the five labels the model considers most probable

The benchmark itself, and why top-5 is the headline metric with 1000 fine-grained classes, is covered under datasets & benchmarks.

02 The eight layers, counted, and where the GPUs talk

Walk the network: shapes, parameters, arithmetic and cross-GPU traffic, layer by layer click a layer, or switch to the one-GPU-equivalent to see what the split saved; try a 224 input with no padding
  • Parameters live in the head; arithmetic lives in the convolutions. By our count the three fully connected layers hold about 96% of the parameters, and fc6 alone holds about 62%. The convolutions perform about 92% of the multiply-accumulates. That split is why dropout went into the head (§09), and why later designs deleted the head first (§13).
  • The crossings sit on small tensors. The GPUs exchange activations into conv3, after two poolings, and into the fully connected layers. There each GPU needs the other's 13×13×128 = 21,632 conv2 outputs per image, and then 4,608, 2,048 and 2,048 (our arithmetic). The two boundaries that are not crossed, into conv2 and conv4, would each have needed more than 32,000 per image. The paper's own phrasing is that the connectivity pattern was chosen by cross-validation to keep communication "an acceptable fraction" of the computation.
  • The 224 / 227 inconsistency. The paper's text and figure say the input is 224×224×3 and conv1 has an 11×11 stride-4 kernel. With no padding that gives a 54×54 map, not 55×55. The size that reproduces 55 is 227, or 224 with two pixels of padding. The arithmetic is worked on the shape arithmetic page. Set the walker to 224 without padding to see the knock-on effect: a 5×5 final map, and an fc6 with 26.2 M parameters instead of 37.8 M.
  • The paper's neuron counts. Its Figure 2 caption lists 253,440 – 186,624 – 64,896 – 64,896 – 43,264 – 4096 – 4096 – 1000 neurons. Every entry except the first matches the shapes above. The first does not: 55×55×96 is 290,400. With 290,400 the total is about 659,000, which agrees with the abstract's "650,000 neurons" (our arithmetic). The input is 150,528-dimensional (224×224×3).
  • Grouping saved parameters as well as memory. In the one-GPU-equivalent view, conv2, conv4 and conv5 see all 96, 384 and 384 maps below them. The total rises to 62.4 M parameters and 1.14 G MACs, which are the figures the lineage table uses. The split cuts the convolutional arithmetic by about 38% (our arithmetic). This is grouped convolution with two groups, years before it had that name.

03 ReLU: an argument about training speed, not accuracy

The paper orders its architectural novelties (its §3.1–3.4) by their estimated importance, and the non-saturating unit comes first. The claim is about how fast the training set can be fitted. It does not claim the unit generalises better.

saturating vs non-saturating unitstanh: f(x) = tanh(x) f′(x) = 1 − tanh²(x) → 0 as |x| grows logistic: f(x) = (1 + e⁻ˣ)⁻¹ f′(x) = f(x)(1 − f(x)) ≤ 1/4, → 0 as |x| grows ReLU: f(x) = max(0, x) f′(x) = 1 for x > 0, 0 for x < 0
  • The measured evidence is one figure. A four-layer convnet with ReLUs reached 25% training error on CIFAR-10 six times faster than an equivalent network with tanh units. Each network's learning rate was chosen independently to make its training as fast as possible, and neither used any regularisation. The paper says the size of the effect varies with the architecture, but that ReLU networks consistently learned several times faster. Its conclusion is that the large experiments in the paper would not have been feasible with saturating units.
  • Why it is not the same as an earlier result. Prior work had found that |tanh(x)| worked well with contrast normalisation and average pooling on a small dataset, Caltech-101. The paper points out that on so small a dataset the main concern is overfitting, so that is a different effect from the faster fitting it reports. Its view is that faster learning matters most for large models on large datasets.
  • The mechanism, in this site's terms. In the positive regime a ReLU passes gradient through with slope exactly 1. A tanh unit away from zero multiplies the gradient by a number much smaller than 1. The per-unit details are on hidden units: the rectifiers, and what this does to gradients compounded over depth is on gradient flow through g. The cost is the zero slope for negative inputs (the dying ReLU). The paper's bias initialisation (§10) partly targets that cost.
Why there is no simulated curve here. We tried a small fully connected toy and the six-fold gap did not reproduce: tanh and ReLU reached the same error in about the same number of steps. The paper's effect is for a convnet at a particular initialisation and depth. A toy that happened to show a gap would be showing our choice of toy, not the paper's result, so this page quotes the figure and leaves it at that.

04 Two GPUs: splitting the model, not the batch

A single GTX 580 had 3 GB of memory, and the paper found that 1.2 million examples could train networks too big to fit on one. So it puts half the kernels (or neurons) of every layer on each GPU, with one extra trick: the GPUs communicate only in certain layers.

  • The connectivity is a hyperparameter. In the paper's example, conv3 takes input from all of conv2's maps, while conv4 takes input only from the conv3 maps on its own GPU. The paper calls choosing this pattern "a problem for cross-validation", because it lets the amount of communication be tuned against the amount of computation. The GPUs read and write each other's memory directly, without going through host memory.
  • Columns that are not independent. The paper compares the design to an earlier "columnar" convnet whose columns were trained independently. Here the columns interact at conv3 and throughout the head.
  • What it bought. Compared with a network that has half as many kernels in each convolutional layer and is trained on one GPU, the split network cuts top-1 error by 1.7% and top-5 error by 1.2%, and it trains in slightly less time.
  • The comparison is biased toward the baseline, and the paper says so. Most parameters are in fc6, which reads conv5. So that the two networks have roughly the same parameter count, the one-GPU baseline keeps the full conv5 and the full fully connected layers, without halving them. It is therefore bigger than "half the two-GPU net".
In current vocabulary (later general knowledge), this is model parallelism, or more precisely a hand-designed form of tensor parallelism in which each device holds a slice of every layer. The alternative, data parallelism, copies the whole model onto each device and splits the batch. The two are compared on tensor & model parallelism and the distributed training hub. The second idea in the design, restricting which groups of channels connect to which, outlived the hardware constraint as grouped convolution.

05 Local response normalisation: competition across channels

ReLUs do not need normalised inputs to avoid saturating: if some training examples give a positive input, the unit learns. The paper still found that a local normalisation across neighbouring channels at the same pixel helped generalisation.

response-normalised activity, constants k, n, α, βb^i_(x,y) = a^i_(x,y) / ( k + α · ∑_j (a^j_(x,y))² )^β the sum runs over j = max(0, i − n/2) … min(N − 1, i + n/2) a^i_(x,y) activity of kernel i at position (x, y), after the ReLU N number of kernels in the layer reported: k = 2, n = 5, α = 10⁻⁴, β = 0.75 (chosen on a validation set)
  • What it is. Each unit is divided by a power of the summed squared activity of the n "adjacent" kernel maps at the same position. The paper describes this as a form of lateral inhibition, inspired by real neurons, that makes large activities from different kernels compete. "Adjacent" refers to an ordering of the kernel maps that is arbitrary and fixed before training.
  • Brightness, not contrast. It resembles the local contrast normalisation of earlier work, but the mean is not subtracted. The paper suggests "brightness normalization" as the more correct name.
  • Where. After the ReLU, in conv1 and conv2 only, each followed by max-pooling.
  • The measured gain. 1.4% top-1 and 1.2% top-5 on ImageNet. On CIFAR-10 a four-layer convnet went from 13% to 11% test error with the normalisation.
what the constants do (our arithmetic): all five neighbours at activity aa = 0 … 1 : denominator ≈ 2^0.75 = 1.682 b ≈ 0.595 · a a = 10 : (2 + 10⁻⁴·5·100)^0.75 = 2.05^0.75 = 1.713 b = 5.84 a = 100 : (2 + 10⁻⁴·5·10⁴)^0.75 = 7^0.75 = 4.30 b = 23.2

With these constants the layer is almost a fixed rescaling by about 0.6. It only starts to suppress activity when the neighbouring activations reach the tens, which is plausible here because the input is raw mean-subtracted RGB on a 0–255 scale. The same constants on a network with differently scaled activations would do something different.

Not batch normalisation. LRN has no learned scale or shift, no mean subtraction and no batch statistics. It normalises over channels at a single pixel of a single image, with fixed constants. Batch normalisation, which displaced it (§13), normalises each channel over the batch and space, and learns how to undo the normalisation if needed. Also note (later general knowledge) that some library implementations divide α by n inside the sum. The same nominal constants then define a weaker layer, so check the convention before copying the numbers.

06 Overlapping pooling: windows wider than their stride

The paper describes a pooling layer as a grid of units spaced s pixels apart, each summarising a z×z neighbourhood. The usual choice was s = z. This network uses s = 2, z = 3 throughout, so neighbouring windows overlap by one row or column.

  • The measured gain over the non-overlapping s = 2, z = 2, which produces outputs of the same size: 0.4% top-1 and 0.3% top-5. This is the smallest of the paper's architectural gains.
  • The observation offered with it. During training, models with overlapping pooling were "slightly more difficult to overfit". The paper gives no mechanism.
  • Same output size, by construction. At each of the three pooling stages, ⌊(n − 3)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊(n − 2)/2⌋ + 1 for odd n. Both give 55 → 27 → 13 → 6. So the comparison changes only the window, not the downstream shapes.
Overlapping vs non-overlapping max-pooling on a toy activation map turn on the one-pixel shift and count the red cells under each scheme; show coverage to see which input cells a 2×2 grid never looks at
  • What the toy does and does not show. On maps with independent pixels, a one-pixel shift leaves about half of the overlapping-pooled values unchanged and about a third of the non-overlapping ones. A window and its shifted copy share 2 of the 4 columns they span with z = 3, and 1 of 3 with z = 2. Overlapping pooling is therefore somewhat more stable to small translations. That is one plausible reading of the "harder to overfit" remark, but it is our illustration, not the paper's explanation.
  • A side effect worth knowing. With z = s = 2 on an odd-sized map, the last row and column are never pooled. With z = 3, s = 2 they are. The general treatment of pooling, its invariance, and its replacement by strided convolution is on pooling and strided convolution instead of pooling.

07 The objective, and the claim that depth matters

The network ends in a 1000-way softmax and maximises the multinomial logistic regression objective. The paper notes that this is the same as maximising the average log-probability of the correct label across training cases.

the training objective, written as a loss to minimisep(c | x) = exp(z_c) / ∑_(c′=1..1000) exp(z_c′) z = fc8 output L = − (1/N) · ∑ₙ log p(yₙ | xₙ) (cross-entropy)

Nothing about the objective is unusual; softmax & cross-entropy derives it. The architecture's arrangement is in §02. The ReLU follows every convolutional and fully connected layer, LRN follows conv1 and conv2, and max-pooling follows both LRN layers and conv5.

The depth claim, in the paper's words and numbers

  • The claim. Removing any convolutional layer gave worse performance. Removing any of the middle layers cost about 2% top-1. The paper concludes that "the depth really is important" for its results.
  • Why that is a non-trivial claim. The paper notes that each convolutional layer holds no more than 1% of the parameters. So the 2% is not lost capacity in the parameter-count sense: a layer that is a small fraction of the weights is still a large fraction of the function. One small check (our arithmetic): conv3 holds 885,120 of about 61 M parameters, which is 1.45%, slightly above the paper's "no more than 1%". The other four are each below 1%.
  • What it does not establish. The ablation removes layers from a fixed design. It does not show that adding more layers to this design would help. Plain networks much deeper than this later trained worse, not better, until residual connections fixed the degradation problem.

08 Data augmentation: random crops, flips, and PCA colour noise

At 60 million parameters, overfitting was the main problem even with 1.2 million images. The paper notes that with 1000 classes each training example imposes only about 10 bits of constraint on the mapping from image to label (log₂ 1000 ≈ 10). Two label-preserving augmentations are generated on the fly, in Python on the CPU, while the GPU trains on the previous batch. Nothing is stored on disk, so the paper calls them "computationally free".

1 · Translations and horizontal reflections

  • Training: random 224×224 patches, and their horizontal mirror images, are taken from the 256×256 images. The paper says this enlarges the training set by a factor of 2048, though the resulting examples are highly inter-dependent. Without it the network overfits substantially, which would have forced much smaller networks.
  • Where 2048 comes from (our arithmetic): 32 × 32 offsets × 2 reflections. Counted exactly, a 224 crop has 256 − 224 + 1 = 33 positions per axis, which gives 33² × 2 = 2178. Either way the network almost never sees the same pixels twice.
  • Testing: the prediction averages the softmax outputs over ten patches: the four corners and the centre, each with its reflection. This is worth a measurable amount. On ILSVRC-2010 the ten-patch average gives 37.5% / 17.0% top-1 / top-5. Without it the errors are 39.0% / 18.3%.

2 · Altering RGB intensities along the principal components

PCA colour augmentation: per training image, added to every pixelI_xy = [ I^R_xy, I^G_xy, I^B_xy ]ᵀ I_xy ← I_xy + [ p₁, p₂, p₃ ] · [ α₁λ₁, α₂λ₂, α₃λ₃ ]ᵀ pᵢ, λᵢ i-th eigenvector and eigenvalue of the 3×3 covariance of RGB pixel values over the whole ImageNet training set αᵢ ~ Gaussian(mean 0, standard deviation 0.1), drawn once per image per presentation, the same for all its pixels, redrawn next time
  • What it models. The paper's reasoning is that object identity is invariant to changes in the intensity and colour of the illumination. Shifting the whole image along its dominant colour axes is a cheap approximation to a change of lighting. As general knowledge rather than a claim of the paper, the first principal component of natural-image RGB is roughly overall brightness, so the noise moves images along directions in which real photographs already vary.
  • The measured gain: top-1 error falls by over 1%.

Augmentation as a regulariser in general is on augmentation, noise & smoothing. The modern recipe for classification (random-resized crops, mixing methods, label smoothing) is on augmentation & label smoothing.

09 Dropout: a cheap ensemble, in the first two fully connected layers

Averaging many models reduces test error but is too expensive when one model takes days to train. Dropout, which the paper calls "recently-introduced", gives a form of model combination that costs about a factor of two in training time.

dropout as used here (the original, non-inverted form)training: hᵢ ← mᵢ · hᵢ, mᵢ ~ Bernoulli(0.5) (zeroed with probability 0.5) a dropped unit does not contribute to the forward pass and does not participate in backpropagation test: hᵢ ← 0.5 · hᵢ (all units, outputs halved)
  • The ensemble reading. Every presentation samples a different architecture, and all of them share weights. Halving outputs at test time is a reasonable approximation to the geometric mean of the predictive distributions of the exponentially many sub-networks.
  • The co-adaptation reading. A unit cannot rely on any particular other unit being present, so it has to learn features that are useful together with many random subsets of the others.
  • Where, and what it cost. It is applied in fc6 and fc7, the first two fully connected layers, which hold about 89% of the parameters by our count. Without it the network overfits substantially. With it, the number of iterations needed to converge roughly doubles.

The full treatment is on dropout. It covers the later "inverted" form, which scales by 1/(1 − p) during training so that inference needs no change, and the train/test mismatch it creates. A summary is in regularisation §dropout.

10 The training recipe, exactly

Stochastic gradient descent with batch 128, momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.0005. The paper adds an unusual remark: this small weight decay was important for the model to learn at all. It is not merely a regulariser, because it reduced the model's training error.

the update rule for a weight w, as stated (ε = learning rate)v_(i+1) := 0.9 · v_i − 0.0005 · ε · w_i − ε · ⟨ ∂L/∂w |_(w_i) ⟩_(D_i) w_(i+1) := w_i + v_(i+1) i iteration index v momentum variable ⟨ … ⟩_(D_i) average over the i-th batch D_i of the derivative of the objective with respect to w, evaluated at w_i
  • Reading the rule. This is heavy-ball momentum. The decay term is multiplied by the learning rate, so it is L2 regularisation applied through the gradient step. For plain SGD that is the same thing as weight decay up to how the coefficient is quoted. With adaptive optimisers the two separate, which is the point of decoupled decay. Each time the learning rate is divided by 10, the effective per-step shrinkage 0.0005·ε falls by 10 as well.
  • Initialisation. Every layer's weights are drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian with standard deviation 0.01. Biases are set to 1 in conv2, conv4, conv5 and the fully connected hidden layers, and to 0 in the remaining layers (conv1, conv3 and the output layer). The stated reason is that a positive bias gives the ReLUs positive inputs and so speeds up the early stages of learning.
  • Learning-rate schedule. One learning rate for all layers, starting at 0.01 and adjusted by hand. The heuristic was to divide it by 10 when validation error stopped improving, and this happened three times before training stopped.
  • Budget. Roughly 90 passes through the 1.2 million training images, which took five to six days on two GTX 580 3 GB GPUs. By our arithmetic that is 1.2 M / 128 ≈ 9,375 iterations per epoch, or about 840,000 iterations.
A fixed 0.01 against fan-in scaling (our arithmetic, later general knowledge). The fan-in-scaled standard deviation √(2 / fan_in), which later analysis recommends for ReLU layers, is 0.074 for conv1 (fan-in 363), 0.041 for conv2 (1200), and 0.029–0.034 for conv3–5. That is about 3–7× larger than 0.01 in every convolutional layer. With zero biases, a forward signal would shrink by that ratio at each layer, compounding through depth. Reading the constant-1 biases as partly compensating for this is our interpretation, not the paper's. The variance argument is on Xavier, He, and what each assumes.

12 What the network learned: specialised GPUs and semantic neighbours

Two qualitative probes did more for the paper's influence than any ablation. The first shows that the restricted connectivity made the two halves of conv1 specialise. The second shows that the last hidden layer is a semantic embedding, not a pixel-level one.

The 96 first-layer kernels

  • The 11×11×3 kernels of conv1 include frequency- and orientation-selective filters and various coloured blobs. These are the oriented edge and colour detectors described on what a convnet learns.
  • The two GPUs specialise. The 48 kernels on GPU 1 are largely colour-agnostic and those on GPU 2 largely colour-specific. The paper attributes this to the restricted connectivity: conv2 on each GPU sees only its own GPU's conv1 maps. It reports that the specialisation appears in every run, independent of the random initialisation, apart from which GPU ends up with which role.

Top-5 predictions and nearest neighbours

  • Predictions. For eight test images, even off-centre objects (a mite in one corner) are recognised, and most of the top-5 labels are reasonable. For a leopard, only other kinds of cat appear. In some failures (a grille, a cherry) the photograph is genuinely ambiguous about its subject.
  • Neighbours in feature space. For a test image, the paper retrieves the six training images whose 4096-dimensional last-hidden-layer activation vectors are closest in Euclidean distance. The retrieved images are generally not close to the query in pixel-space L2: the dogs and elephants appear in a variety of poses. Two images that are close in this space are ones the network's higher layers consider similar.
  • The retrieval proposal. Euclidean search over 4096-dimensional real vectors is inefficient, so the paper suggests training an autoencoder to compress them into short binary codes. It expects this to retrieve far better than an autoencoder on raw pixels, which ignores labels and tends to return images with similar edge patterns whether or not they are semantically similar.
Why this mattered later (later general knowledge). The observation that a supervised network's penultimate layer is a general-purpose image descriptor is the seed of the transfer-learning paradigm: reuse the features, retrain the head. Combined with the 1 CNN* row of §11 (pretrain big, fine-tune on the target), it describes the workflow of the following decade. See fine-tuning & transfer learning.

14 Common misconceptions

"The 15.3% winning entry was one network." It was an ensemble of seven. Five networks trained on ILSVRC-2012, plus two with an extra convolutional layer pretrained on 15 M images in 22 K categories. A single network scored 18.2% top-5 on validation, which was still far ahead of the 26.2% runner-up (§11).
"This paper invented ReLU and dropout." It adopted both from earlier work. The rectified unit came from earlier work on Boltzmann machines, and the paper calls dropout "recently-introduced". Its contribution was showing at scale that they made a large convnet trainable and kept it from overfitting (§03, §09).
"The two GPUs were used for data parallelism." They split the model. Each GPU held half the kernels of every layer and processed the same images, exchanging activations only at conv3 and the fully connected layers (§04).
"A convnet's parameters are in its convolutions." Not in this one. About 96% of the parameters are in the three fully connected layers, and fc6 alone holds about 62%. The convolutions, with under 4% of the parameters, do about 92% of the arithmetic (§02).
"LRN is an early form of batch normalisation." They normalise over different axes and have different parts. LRN divides by summed squares across neighbouring channels at one pixel of one image. It uses fixed constants, no mean subtraction and nothing learned (§05).
"A 224×224 input gives the 55×55 first map." It gives 54×54 unless padded. 227, or 224 with padding 2, gives 55. The paper's figure says 224 (§02; worked on the off-by-one traps).
"Overlapping pooling downsamples more aggressively." It gives exactly the same output size. With stride 2, windows of 3 and 2 both give 55 → 27 → 13 → 6. Only the window overlap differs (§06).

15 Takeaways

  • Eight weight layers (five conv, three fully connected) and about 61 M parameters, 96% of them in the head. Each image costs about 0.72 G multiply-accumulates, 92% of them in the convolutions.
  • ReLU first: the paper's most important change, justified by training speed (six times faster to 25% CIFAR-10 training error in its four-layer test), not by accuracy.
  • Two GPUs, model-parallel: half of every layer on each GPU, crossings only into conv3 and the fully connected layers. This gave −1.7% top-1 against a smaller one-GPU net, and it became grouped convolution.
  • The measured gains of the small choices: LRN (k = 2, n = 5, α = 10⁻⁴, β = 0.75) −1.4% / −1.2%; overlapping pooling (z = 3, s = 2) −0.4% / −0.3%; PCA colour noise over 1% top-1; ten-crop testing 39.0 → 37.5% top-1.
  • Against overfitting: 224 crops and flips (a factor of 2048), and dropout p = 0.5 in fc6 and fc7 (about twice as many iterations to converge).
  • The recipe: SGD with batch 128, momentum 0.9, decay 0.0005 inside the step, N(0, 0.01²) initialisation with biases of 1 in some layers, learning rate 0.01 ÷ 10 three times, about 90 epochs, five to six days.
  • The results: 37.5% / 17.0% on ILSVRC-2010 test; 15.3% top-5 on ILSVRC-2012 test with seven networks, against 26.2%. Removing a middle convolutional layer cost about 2% top-1.
  • The lasting idea beyond the numbers: the last hidden layer is a semantic embedding that can be reused for other tasks.
