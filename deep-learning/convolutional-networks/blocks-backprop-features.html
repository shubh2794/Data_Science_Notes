24 The canonical block: conv → norm → activation, and where the norm goes

Nobody writes a bare convolution. The unit of architecture is a three-part block, and the two decisions inside it — where the normalisation goes and whether the convolution keeps its bias — are settled questions with reasons.

the block, and the two orderingsPOST-ACTIVATION NORM x → conv → g → norm → out (rare, and worse) PRE-ACTIVATION NORM x → conv → norm → g → out (the standard) with a residual connection (see Residual & Skip Connections): POST-NORM out = g(x + norm(conv(x))) PRE-NORM out = x + conv(g(norm(x))) — the modern default the two rules that follow immediately1 DROP THE CONVOLUTION'S BIAS when a normalisation layer follows it. Normalisation subtracts the mean, so any constant the bias added is removed on the next line. The bias is not harmful — it is INERT, and its gradient is (almost) zero. Its job is done by the norm's own β shift. On a 3×3×512→512 layer that is 512 dead parameters out of 2 359 808 — negligible in size and a real source of confusion when reading a parameter count that does not match the reference implementation. 2 THE NORM GOES BEFORE THE NON-LINEARITY, so that the non-linearity sees a distribution it was designed for. A ReLU applied to something with mean −5 kills nearly everything; the norm is what guarantees it does not.

The one conv-specific thing about normalisation

Everything else about normalisation layers is part 2 §29 and §30 — the train/eval asymmetry, the running statistics, the batch-size dependence, why LayerNorm exists. This page owns exactly one point, and it is the one that is usually stated wrongly:

BatchNorm in a convolutional layer reduces over THREE axes, not oneOn an activation of shape (N, C, H, W), BatchNorm computes ONE mean and ONE variance PER CHANNEL, reducing over N, H and W together: μ[c] = mean over n, i, j of x[n][c][i][j] N·H·W samples σ²[c] = var over n, i, j per statistic so there are 2C statistics and 2C learned parameters (γ[c], β[c]), not 2·C·H·W of them. and this is exactly the parameter-sharing argument againWhy reduce over H and W? Because the layer is TRANSLATION EQUIVARIANT and a per-position statistic would break that: it would normalise the top-left of the image differently from the bottom-right, and the same feature would come out differently depending on where it appeared. Normalising per channel is the ONLY choice consistent with §07. Consequence: the effective batch for BatchNorm's statistics is N·H·W, not N. At N = 2 on a 56×56 map that is 6 272 samples per statistic — which is why convolutional BatchNorm survives batch sizes that would break it on an MLP.
The asymmetry that causes most BatchNorm bugs is part 2 §29's, and it bites hardest here. In training the layer uses the current batch's statistics; at evaluation it uses a running estimate. A convnet forgotten in training mode at evaluation time produces predictions that depend on which other images happened to be in the batch — and the failure looks like a data problem, not a mode problem. The conv-specific aggravation is that the reduction over H and W makes the training-mode statistics look stable, so the discrepancy is smaller and takes longer to notice.

25 Backpropagation through a convolution, derived — and audited

This is the one place where this page must do its own calculus. Part 2 §05 derived backpropagation in general and this section specialises it to a convolution, where two facts emerge that are not obvious: the gradient with respect to the input is a convolution with the flipped kernel, and the gradient with respect to the kernel is a correlation of the input with the output gradient. Neither is asserted below; both are derived and then checked against central differences.

Set up

forward, in the index form of §11 (correlation, as every framework computes it)Z[cₒ][p][q] = b[cₒ] + ∑ᶜ ∑ₘ ∑ₙ V[c][i(p,m)][j(q,n)] · K[cₒ][c][m][n] with the index map i(p, m) = p·s + m·d − p₀ j(q, n) = q·s + n·d − p₀ and the convention that any V index outside the input reads 0. given, from the layer aboveG[cₒ][p][q] = ∂L / ∂Z[cₒ][p][q]

The kernel gradient

A single kernel entry K[cₒ][c][m][n] appears in every output position (p, q) of channel cₒ — that is what parameter sharing means. The multivariable chain rule therefore sums over all of them:

∂L/∂K — sum over every position that used the weight∂L ∑ ∂L ∂Z[cₒ][p][q] ──────── = ─────────── · ──────────────── ∂K[cₒ][c][m][n] p,q ∂Z[cₒ][p][q] ∂K[cₒ][c][m][n] and from the forward equation the second factor is simply V[c][i(p,m)][j(q,n)], because Z is LINEAR in K. So ∂L/∂K[cₒ][c][m][n] = ∑ₚ ∑ᵩ G[cₒ][p][q] · V[c][p·s + m·d − p₀][q·s + n·d − p₀] what that IS, for s = 1, d = 1, p₀ = 0∂L/∂K[cₒ][c][m][n] = ∑ₚ ∑ᵩ V[c][p + m][q + n] · G[cₒ][p][q] Compare with the correlation of §04: (I ⋆ K)(i,j) = ∑ I(i+m, j+n)·K(m,n). This is EXACTLY that, with V as the image and G as the kernel — a VALID CORRELATION of the input with the output gradient, producing an f × f result. NO FLIP.

The input gradient

Now fix an input position V[c][i][j] and ask which outputs it reached. It contributed to output (p, q) through tap (m, n) whenever i = p·s + m·d − p₀. The chain rule sums over every such (p, m) and (q, n) pair:

∂L/∂V — sum over every (output, tap) pair that read this position∂L ∑ ∑ ──────────── = G[cₒ][p][q] · K[cₒ][c][m][n] ∂V[c][i][j] cₒ (p,m) with p·s + m·d − p₀ = i (q,n) with q·s + n·d − p₀ = j what that IS, for s = 1, d = 1, p₀ = 0The constraint becomes p = i − m and q = j − n, so ∂L/∂V[c][i][j] = ∑ᶜₒ ∑ₘ ∑ₙ G[cₒ][i − m][j − n] · K[cₒ][c][m][n] and THAT is the definition of a TRUE CONVOLUTION from §04 — (I ∗ K)(i,j) = ∑ I(i−m, j−n)·K(m,n) with G as the image and K as the kernel. Indices of G outside its range contribute nothing, which is exactly FULL padding of G by f − 1 on each side. The forward pass is a CORRELATION. The backward pass to the input is a FULL CONVOLUTION of the output gradient with the SAME kernel — equivalently a full CORRELATION with the FLIPPED kernel. The flip is forced.

And the bias, for completeness: Z contains b[cₒ] once per output position, so ∂L/∂b[cₒ] = ∑ₚ ∑_q G[cₒ][p][q] — the output gradient summed over both spatial axes.

Three consequences that shape how convnets train

consequence 1
Shared weights get summed gradients
A kernel entry's gradient is a sum over H′W′ terms. Its magnitude therefore scales with the map area, which is why the same learning rate behaves differently at different resolutions and why part 2 §12's variance argument needs the fan-in Cᵢₙ·f², not the parameter count.
consequence 2
Padding costs a gradient
Gradient that lands on a padded position is discarded — it flows to a zero that no parameter owns. Border input positions therefore receive systematically less gradient than interior ones, which is the backward-pass shadow of §08's border argument.
consequence 3
The backward pass costs the same twice
Part 2 §01's 1 : 2 : 3 rule holds exactly: the two backward operations each have the same MAC count as the forward one. A training step on a convolution layer costs three forward passes, and the first layer's ∂L/∂V is the only one you can skip.

26 Convolution as one matrix product: im2col, and the Toeplitz view

§03 said a convolution is a matrix. That was a statement about its structure. This section is the practical version: how a convolution is actually executed, which is by rearranging it into a single dense matrix product and calling the same library routine an MLP calls.

im2col — the lowering, in the series row conventionFor every output position (p, q), extract the Cᵢₙ × f × f window it reads and flatten it into a ROW. Stack the rows: P is (H′W′ × Cᵢₙ·f·f) one PATCH per row W is (Cᵢₙ·f·f × Cₒᵤₜ) one FILTER per column Z = P·W + b (H′W′ × Cₒᵤₜ) Reshape Z back to (Cₒᵤₜ, H′, W′) and you are done. That is part 1's X·W + b, with the pixels playing the role of the batch — the same row convention, the same shapes, the same routine. the tradeCOST P holds Cᵢₙf² · H′W′ numbers where V held only CᵢₙHW — a memory blow-up of about f² (NINE times for a 3×3 kernel) for the duration of the layer. Every input value is copied f² times. BUY one large dense matrix product, which runs at 60–90% of a machine's peak throughput, instead of a hand-rolled sliding loop, which does not. The blow-up is worth it and every framework does it, in tiles small enough to stay in cache.

Reading off the counts

The matrix form makes §12's arithmetic obvious rather than derived. The product (H′W′ × Cᵢₙf²)·(Cᵢₙf² × Cₒᵤₜ) costs H′W′ · Cᵢₙf² · Cₒᵤₜ MACs — exactly the count of §12, with no work. The weight matrix has Cᵢₙf²·Cₒᵤₜ entries — exactly the parameter count. And the reuse factor H′W′ is just the number of rows in P, i.e. the number of times each column of W is dotted with something.

The other two ways, and when they win

alternative 1
Direct / implicit GEMM
Build the patch rows on the fly in registers rather than materialising P in memory. Same arithmetic, none of the blow-up. This is what a modern library actually does; im2col is the mental model and the fallback.
alternative 2
The frequency domain
Convolution is pointwise multiplication of transforms — the convolution theorem. Cost drops from O(n²f²) to O(n² log n), independent of f. It wins for large kernels and loses badly for 3×3, where the transform overhead dwarfs nine multiplies. It is why large-kernel layers are less expensive than they look.
alternative 3
Winograd
A minimal-filtering algorithm that trades multiplications for additions on small tiles. For 3×3 kernels it cuts multiplications by a factor of 2.25 and is the standard fast path for exactly that case. It is numerically less stable, which matters in low precision (part 2 §27).
Why this matters beyond implementation. Once you see the layer as P·W, three things follow that are hard to see otherwise. (1) A 1×1 convolution has f = 1, so P is just the activation reshaped and no copying happens at all — which is why 1×1 layers are so cheap in practice as well as in theory (§16). (2) The rank of W is a meaningful quantity, and low-rank factorisation of it is a real compression technique. (3) The doubly block Toeplitz matrix M of §03 and the dense W here are two different matrices doing the same job: M is (H′W′ × HW), huge and sparse and tied; W is (Cᵢₙf² × Cₒᵤₜ), small and dense and free. The tying was moved out of the matrix and into the indexing, which is the whole trick.
im2col: the same layer as a sliding window and as one matrix product the two results are computed by different code paths and compared — they must agree exactly, and the memory blow-up is measured rather than quoted

27 What a convolutional network actually learns

The most reproducible empirical fact about convnets: the first layer always learns the same thing. Oriented edges at several scales and orientations, and a few colour-opponent blobs — whatever the dataset, whatever the task, whatever the initialisation. That regularity is not a coincidence and it has an explanation that requires no learning at all.

Why the first layer is always oriented edges

Take a large collection of small patches from natural images and compute their covariance. The principal components of that covariance — the directions of maximum variance, computed with no labels, no network and no gradient descent — are localised, oriented, band-pass filters. They look like the first layer of a trained convnet because they are answering the same question: what are the dominant modes of variation in a small window of a natural image? A first layer trained by gradient descent on almost any objective ends up spanning approximately the same subspace, because that is where the signal is.

Two consequences that matter in practice. (1) First layers transfer perfectly. Because they encode a property of natural images rather than of your task, the first layer of a network trained on one dataset is a good first layer for another — which is the foundation of transfer learning and why "freeze the early layers" is a sensible default. (2) A first layer that does not look like this is a diagnostic. Noisy, high-frequency or dead first-layer filters after training mean the learning rate was too high, the initialisation was wrong, or the layer never received usable gradient — see part 2 §14 and §11. Looking at the first layer is a five-second diagnostic that catches real bugs.

The tidy story, and where it fails. "Edges, then parts, then objects, cleanly separated by depth." It is a good first approximation and two things spoil it. (1) The hierarchy is not clean: a late layer still contains low-level information, and skip connections deliberately mix scales. (2) More seriously, an ImageNet-trained convnet turns out to be strongly biased toward texture rather than shape. Present it with a cat silhouette filled with elephant skin and it will say elephant. Measured on cue-conflict images, a standard convnet's decisions follow shape about 20% of the time; human observers follow shape about 95% of the time. So the "parts and objects" reading is at best incomplete: the network is often doing sophisticated texture statistics that correlate with objects on this dataset. Training on style-randomised images raises the shape bias substantially and improves robustness, which is the evidence that the bias is learned from the data rather than forced by the architecture.
