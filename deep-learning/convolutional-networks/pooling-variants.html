16 The 1×1 convolution: channel mixing, the bottleneck, and the cost it saves

A 1×1 kernel looks like a joke — it multiplies each pixel by a number. It is not, because of the channel axis. A 1×1 convolution is a full dense layer applied independently at every spatial position, and it is one of the two or three most consequential ideas in the whole architecture lineage.

what it actually computesK is (Cₒᵤₜ, Cᵢₙ, 1, 1), so the spatial sums collapse and Z[cₒ][p][q] = b[cₒ] + ∑ᶜ V[c][p][q] · K[cₒ][c] For EACH pixel independently, the Cᵢₙ-vector of that pixel's channel values is multiplied by a (Cᵢₙ × Cₒᵤₜ) matrix. Reshape the map to the (H·W × Cᵢₙ) matrix of pixel vectors, one pixel per ROW, and this is EXACTLY part 1's Z = X W + b W of shape (Cᵢₙ × Cₒᵤₜ) the row-convention dense layer, with the H·W pixels playing the role of the batch. That is not an analogy — it is the same computation.

So a 1×1 convolution does no spatial mixing whatsoever and complete channel mixing. It is the exact complement of a depthwise convolution (§21), which does complete spatial mixing and no channel mixing. Every efficient architecture in §28 is built by alternating those two.

The four things it is used for

use 1
Shrink the channel axis
Pooling reduces H and W and cannot touch C — it operates channel by channel. A 1×1 convolution is the only cheap way to go from 192 channels to 32. This is the original motivation and the one the source deck leads with.
use 2
The bottleneck
Put a cheap 1×1 before an expensive f×f to reduce its input depth, and another after to restore it. The expensive layer now works on a fraction of the channels. The arithmetic below is dramatic and it is the whole design of the deep residual bottleneck block.
use 3
Add non-linearity for free
Even at Cₒᵤₜ = Cᵢₙ, a 1×1 followed by a ReLU adds a layer of depth with almost no cost and no change of resolution. The "network in network" idea: a small MLP applied at every pixel.
use 4
Match shapes for a skip
A residual connection needs its two branches to have the same shape. When a block changes the channel count or the resolution, the shortcut is a 1×1 convolution — with stride if the resolution changed. Look at any residual network's "downsample" module and it is exactly this.

Where does the saving come from? The expensive layer's cost is proportional to Cᵢₙ · Cₒᵤₜ · f². The bottleneck replaces one factor of the full channel count with a reduced one, at the price of two extra 1×1 layers whose cost is proportional to Cᵢₙ · C_r with no f². Since f² = 9 or 25, the 1×1 layers are cheap by exactly that factor, and the reduction ratio C_r/Cᵢₙ multiplies the expensive term directly. The saving is real and is not an approximation — it is a different function class, one that factors the channel interaction through a low-dimensional bottleneck, and its success is evidence that the full channel interaction was low-rank all along.

The bottleneck: where the arithmetic goes, and what the reduction ratio buys the two bars are the bottleneck's two layers against the one layer they replace; the curve beneath sweeps the reduction width and is strictly increasing — the arithmetic alone always says "narrow it further", and only capacity says otherwise
The connection to the rest of the series. A 1×1 convolution is a dense layer, so everything part 1 and part 2 say about dense layers applies to it exactly and without translation. It is also, read the other way, the reason a transformer's position-wise feed-forward block and a convnet's 1×1 layer are the same operation — see Transformers. A ViT's patch embedding is a convolution with kernel and stride both equal to the patch size (§33), and its MLP block is a pair of 1×1 convolutions. The architectures are less different than their diagrams suggest.

17 Pooling: what it buys, and exactly what it destroys

Pooling replaces the values in a neighbourhood with a summary of them. It has no parameters, it is not learned, and it is the most contested component in the architecture — §30 is a sustained argument that it is a mistake. Understand precisely what it does first.

the definitions, and the one shape ruleMAX Y[c][p][q] = max over the k×k window of V[c][…][…] AVERAGE Y[c][p][q] = mean over the same window GLOBAL Y[c] = one number per channel, over the WHOLE map output size ⌊(n + p₀ + p₁ − k)/s⌋ + 1 — the SAME formula as §08 default k = 2, s = 2 — halves each spatial axis channels Cₒᵤₜ = Cᵢₙ, ALWAYS parameters 0 the channel rule is the one people forgetPooling acts CHANNEL BY CHANNEL. 28×28×10 pooled 2×2 gives 14×14×10, never 14×14×5 and never 14×14×1. If you need the channel count to change, that is a 1×1 convolution (§16) and nothing else can do it.

What it buys — three things, in decreasing order of honesty

buy 1 — the real one
The receptive field grows geometrically
§14's arithmetic: a stride-2 pool doubles j, and every layer after it contributes twice as much to r. Five pools take a 3×3 stack from a 27-pixel field to a 212-pixel one. This is the reason pooling exists, and it is not the reason usually given.
buy 2
Everything downstream gets cheaper
Halving both spatial axes quarters the map, so the next layer's MACs and activation memory both fall by four. Over five stages that is a factor of 1024. Convnets would not fit in memory without it.
buy 3 — the one usually quoted
Approximate local translation invariance
If a feature moves by less than the pool width, a max-pool's output may not change at all. That is a genuine and useful property — if you do not need to know where the feature was. §18 states it precisely and §30 argues it is the wrong thing to want.

What it destroys

Max pooling over a k×k window keeps one number out of k² and discards which of the k² positions it came from. At k = 2 that is exactly two bits of spatial information per channel per window, thrown away by construction. Over five pooling stages the accumulated loss is the reason a classifier can tell you an image contains a face and cannot tell you where the eyes are without an extra mechanism. Average pooling discards differently: it keeps a summary of all k² values and destroys the peak, so a sharp isolated response is attenuated by a factor of k².

18 Equivariance against invariance, stated precisely — the conceptual heart of the page

These two words are used interchangeably in conversation and they name opposite properties. Getting them straight is the difference between choosing an architecture and inheriting one, and the whole of §30's critique turns on the distinction.

the two properties, for a transformation gEQUIVARIANT h(g(x)) = g(h(x)) the output TRACKS the transform INVARIANT h(g(x)) = h(x) the output IGNORES the transform invariance is the special case where g acts trivially on the outputInvariance is equivariance with the output action set to the identity. So every invariant function is equivariant, and the interesting question is never "is it equivariant" but "equivariant WITH RESPECT TO WHAT ACTION on the output". A convolution's output action is the same shift; a global pool's output action is the identity. That is the entire difference. and the information-theoretic reading, which is the one that settles argumentsEQUIVARIANCE PRESERVES INFORMATION. From h(g(x)) you can recover h(x), because you can undo g. Nothing is lost. INVARIANCE DESTROYS INFORMATION. From h(g(x)) you cannot tell which g was applied, because by construction every g gives the same answer. Invariance is therefore never free and never reversible. It is a decision to throw something away, and it is correct exactly when you were never going to need it.

Read the pooling row carefully, because it is routinely overstated. The claim "max pooling gives translation invariance" is false as stated. A 2×2 stride-2 max pool over an input shifted by one pixel changes over 90% of its outputs on the figure's test map. What it gives is a partial, local, approximate reduction in sensitivity — genuinely useful, and about a quarter as strong as the folklore. The measurement is in the figure and it is not close.

Which one do you actually want?

want invariance
Classification
"Is there a cat?" has an answer that does not depend on where the cat is. Building the invariance into the architecture — via global pooling — is strictly better than learning it, because it cannot be got wrong and it costs no parameters.
want equivariance
Segmentation, detection, keypoints, depth, flow, generation
Every dense-prediction task needs to know where. An architecture that pooled away position must reconstruct it, which is what the entire decoder half of an encoder–decoder exists to do (§23), and what skip connections from the encoder are for. Pooling in these tasks is a cost you pay and then pay again to undo.
the tension
One backbone, both demands
Modern practice keeps the network equivariant for as long as possible and applies invariance once, at the very end, with a global pool — rather than eroding position gradually through five max-pool layers. That is a direct concession to §30's argument, arrived at empirically.

19 Strided convolution instead of pooling — and the aliasing both of them cause

Pooling is not the only way to shrink a map. A stride-2 convolution does the same job in the same layer, with parameters, and it has largely replaced pooling in modern architectures. The two are not equivalent, and both share a defect that neither of them advertises.

three ways to halve a map, and what each one isconv s1 → pool 2×2 s2 compute EVERY output, then discard 3 in 4. Goodfellow's framing: strided convolution is exactly unit-stride convolution followed by downsampling, and the two-step version is pure waste. conv s2 compute only the outputs you keep. Same result if the pooling were subsampling; 4× less arithmetic; and the downsampling is now LEARNED, because the kernel gets to choose what to preserve. pool 2×2 s2 alone no parameters, no learning, fixed summary. the arithmeticA stride-2 layer does 1/s² = 1/4 of the MACs of the stride-1 layer with the same kernel, and has EXACTLY THE SAME NUMBER OF PARAMETERS. Stride is free in parameters and quartic in arithmetic.

The defect they share: subsampling without a low-pass filter is aliasing

Downsampling a signal by 2 without first removing everything above the new Nyquist frequency causes high frequencies to fold onto low ones — the sampling theorem, which is Frequency Domain's material and is not re-derived here. A stride-2 convolution subsamples. A max pool subsamples, and worse: the max operator is non-linear, so it does not even have a frequency response to reason about, and it can create high frequencies before discarding them.

The visible consequence is a specific and famous failure: shift an image by one pixel and a modern classifier's prediction can change. Not because the network is fragile in some vague sense, but because a one-pixel shift moves the content relative to the stride-2 sampling grid, the aliased components change, and the change propagates. The fix that works is exactly the one the sampling theorem prescribes: blur before you subsample. Insert a small fixed low-pass filter — a binomial kernel — between the response and the stride, and shift-consistency improves measurably, usually with accuracy improving too.

Scope. The sampling theorem, the fold rule, the spectrum of a Gaussian and why the binomial kernel is the right small low-pass filter all belong to the Computer Vision series: sampling replicates the spectrum, aliasing and the fold rule, and box, tent and Gaussian. This section takes those results as given and asks only what they imply for a stride argument.
What subsampling does to a signal, and what a pre-filter does about it the bar is the fraction of energy sitting above the new Nyquist limit at the moment of sampling — that energy does not vanish, it folds, and the bars fall monotonically as the pre-filter improves
The claim that is almost right. "Strided convolution and pooling are interchangeable." They downsample by the same factor and they are not the same layer. A stride-2 convolution has parameters and can learn which linear combination survives the subsample; a max pool has none and always keeps the largest. Empirically the strided version is at least as good and the parameter-free version is more robust when data is scarce. The real reason architectures moved to stride is simpler: it merges two layers into one and removes a factor of s² from the arithmetic of the layer it replaces.

20 Grouped convolution: cutting the channel interaction

§10 established that every output channel sees every input channel. Nothing requires that. Split the channels into g groups, let each group of output channels see only the matching group of input channels, and both the parameters and the arithmetic fall by exactly g.

the definition and its exact costSplit Cᵢₙ into g blocks of Cᵢₙ/g and Cₒᵤₜ into g blocks of Cₒᵤₜ/g. Output block i is computed from input block i alone. Kernel shape becomes (Cₒᵤₜ, Cᵢₙ/g, f, f). parameters Cₒᵤₜ·(Cᵢₙ/g)·f·f + Cₒᵤₜ ≈ 1/g of the dense-channel version MACs H′W′·Cₒᵤₜ·(Cᵢₙ/g)·f·f = EXACTLY 1/g Verified: on a 14×14×256 → 256 layer with f = 3, g = 1 115 605 504 MACs g = 8 14 450 688 MACs (0.125 = 1/8) g = 2 57 802 752 MACs g = 32 3 612 672 MACs (0.03125 = 1/32) The ratio is 1/g to machine precision, for both counts, at every g. The BIAS term does not scale — it is Cₒᵤₜ regardless — so the parameter ratio is 1/g only in the limit of large kernels; the MAC ratio is exact.

Grouping was invented for a mundane reason — an early architecture split its channels across two GPUs because one card did not have enough memory — and it turned out to cost nothing in accuracy and sometimes to help. It has since become a first-class design knob.

What it costs: the channel blocking problem

With g groups the network is g independent networks running side by side that never talk to each other. Stack several grouped layers and information in group 1 can never reach group 2, no matter how deep the network gets. Two standard fixes:

fix 1
A 1×1 convolution between groups
A dense 1×1 layer (§16) mixes every channel with every other at a cost with no f² factor. Alternate grouped f×f with dense 1×1 and the blocking is gone. This is exactly the depthwise-separable pattern of §21, at g = Cᵢₙ.
fix 2
Channel shuffle
Permute the channel axis between grouped layers so that each group's outputs are redistributed across all groups. Costs zero parameters and zero arithmetic — it is a reindexing — and restores full connectivity after two layers. The trade is that it is a fixed, not learned, mixing.
and the caveat
Cheap in FLOPs, not always in time
A grouped convolution does 1/g of the arithmetic and issues g smaller matrix products instead of one large one. On hardware that is happiest with large dense products, a layer that is 8× cheaper on paper is frequently not 8× faster. §21's caveat is the same one and is even sharper there.
Grouped convolution: the block-diagonal kernel, the cost, and the blocking it causes the reachability matrix is computed by propagating connectivity through the drawn stack, so the blocking is measured rather than asserted — and the shuffle toggle is the cheapest possible fix

21 Depthwise-separable convolution, and the cost ratio derived

Take grouping to its limit — one group per channel — and you get a layer that mixes space and does not mix channels. Follow it with a 1×1 that mixes channels and does not mix space. Together they do the job of one ordinary convolution for roughly a ninth of the cost, and that factor is the basis of every mobile-scale architecture.

Three readings of that formula, all of which matter:

reading 1
For any real width, the ratio is ≈ 1/f²
At Cₒᵤₜ = 256 the 1/Cₒᵤₜ term contributes 0.0039 of 0.115 — 3.4% of the total. The saving is essentially f² and essentially independent of the width. For 3×3 that is the famous "8 to 9 times".
reading 2
At f = 1 it is a loss
The ratio becomes 1/Cₒᵤₜ + 1, which is greater than one. A "separable 1×1" is a 1×1 preceded by a pointless per-channel scaling. The formula does not just get small — it tells you exactly when the technique stops working.
reading 3
The parameter ratio is the same number
Both counts share the factor H′W′, so parameters and arithmetic fall by identical ratios. That is unusual — §16's bottleneck and §19's stride both change one and not the other — and it is why depthwise separation is the technique of choice when both budgets are tight.

The catch, stated honestly

A depthwise-separable layer is not a cheaper approximation of the ordinary layer. It is a strictly smaller function class: it can only represent channel interactions that factor as "mix space per channel, then mix channels". An ordinary convolution can learn a filter whose spatial pattern differs from channel to channel in a way no factorisation reproduces. Networks built from these layers compensate by being wider and deeper — spending some of the 8.7× back — and by placing the non-linearity carefully.

And the practical caveat: a depthwise convolution has a terrible arithmetic intensity. It does f² MACs per input element, against Cᵢₙ·f² for an ordinary layer, so it moves nearly as much memory for a fraction of the arithmetic. A layer that is 8.7× cheaper in MACs is routinely only 2–3× faster in wall-clock time on a GPU, and the gap is worse the wider the layer. MACs are not time, and this is the single clearest example of it in the whole architecture zoo.

22 Dilated (atrous) convolution: receptive field without cost

§14 showed that stride-1 stacking grows the receptive field far too slowly and that downsampling fixes it by throwing resolution away. Dilation is the third option: keep the resolution, keep the parameter count, keep the arithmetic, and grow the field geometrically anyway.

Against the alternatives, at the same PARAMETER COUNT: 4 layers of 3×3, d = 1 r = 9 resolution preserved 4 layers of 3×3, a 2×2 stride-2 pool after every second one r = 16 resolution ÷ 4 4 layers of 3×3, d = 1, 2, 4, 8 r = 31 resolution preserved All three have the SAME parameter count and the same MACs per output.

That last comparison is the whole argument. Dilation buys more receptive field than pooling does, at the same parameter count and the same arithmetic per output, and keeps every output position. For any task that must produce a full-resolution answer — segmentation above all — that is decisive, and it is why dilated convolutions became standard in segmentation backbones almost immediately.

The gridding artefact, and what fixes it

Stack several layers at the same dilation d and a problem appears: the units that feed a given output form a sparse lattice of spacing d, and neighbouring outputs read disjoint lattices. Information from positions between the lattice points is never combined. The result is a characteristic checkerboard of inconsistency in the output — "gridding". Three fixes are used:

vary d between layers (1, 2, 5, 9 …)orkeep some d = 1 layers in the stackorchoose dilations with no common factorthe rule of thumb:the dilations of consecutive layers should not share a common divisor greater than 1

The figure measures it. For each input position inside the theoretical receptive field it asks whether that position influences a fixed output at all — the influence map of §15, computed on a linear stack — and reports the coverage: the fraction of positions inside the field that actually contribute. A stack at d = 1 everywhere has coverage 100% by construction. A stack at constant d = 2 can only reach offsets that are sums of multiples of 2, so its coverage is a fraction of that, and every position it misses is a hole in the lattice. A coprime schedule such as 1, 2, 3 restores coverage to 100% while keeping most of the growth. That is the artefact and its fix, measured rather than described.

23 Transposed convolution, the checkerboard, and why "deconvolution" is the wrong word

Everything so far shrinks maps or keeps them the same size. Generation, segmentation and any encoder–decoder need the opposite: a learned layer that makes a map bigger. The operation exists, it is elementary, and it is burdened with the worst name in the field.

What it actually is

the definition, and where the name comes from§03: a convolution layer is multiplication by a sparse, tied matrix M: z = M x M is (H′W′ × HW), so it MAPS DOWN when H′ < H The TRANSPOSED convolution is multiplication by Mᵀ: y = Mᵀ u Mᵀ is (HW × H′W′), so it MAPS UP That is the entire definition. It is not an inverse, it is a TRANSPOSE — the adjoint of the forward operator with respect to the ordinary inner product. why it is exactly the backward pass, and vice versaFrom §25: the gradient of a convolution with respect to its INPUT is ∂L/∂x = Mᵀ (∂L/∂z) which is the transposed convolution applied to the output gradient. So the forward pass of a transposed convolution IS the backward pass of an ordinary one, and its backward pass is an ordinary forward pass. Frameworks implement one routine and call it from both places. the output size, and the ambiguity it inheritsn′ = (n − 1)·s − p₀ − p₁ + fₑ + output_padding Because §09 trap 4 showed the forward map is not injective, this inverse needs to be TOLD which of the possible sizes it should produce — that is what output_padding is for, and forgetting it is the standard reason a decoder's output is one pixel smaller than its encoder's input.
"Deconvolution". A deconvolution is the inverse problem — given y = k ∗ x and k, recover x — which is an ill-posed division in the frequency domain, is the subject of a large classical literature, and has nothing to do with this layer. A transposed convolution does not invert a convolution, does not recover the original input, and does not even try. Two better names are in use: transposed convolution (what it is) and fractionally strided convolution (what it does — a stride of 1/s, achieved by inserting s − 1 zeros between input positions before convolving at stride 1).

The checkerboard artefact, and exactly when it happens

Implement the transposed convolution the natural way — for each input position, add a scaled copy of the kernel into the output at stride s — and the copies overlap. How many copies land on a given output position depends on that position modulo s, and if s does not divide f the count is not constant. Output positions receiving more contributions get systematically larger values, in a periodic pattern with period s. That is the checkerboard, and it has an exact statement:

the overlap count, computedf = 3, s = 2 → contributions per output position: 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, … a period-2 pattern with a 2:1 amplitude ratio. CHECKERBOARD f = 4, s = 2 → contributions: 2, 2, 2, 2, … CLEAN f = 5, s = 2 → contributions: 3, 2, 3, 2, … CHECKERBOARD f = 6, s = 3 → contributions: 2, 2, 2, … CLEAN f = 4, s = 3 → contributions: 2, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, … CHECKERBOARD THE RULE: the overlap count is uniform ⟺ s divides f.

Two fixes, both standard. (1) Choose f divisible by s — a 4×4 kernel at stride 2 is the reason that combination is so common in generator architectures. (2) Replace the layer entirely with "resize by nearest-neighbour or bilinear interpolation, then apply an ordinary stride-1 convolution". This decouples upsampling from learning, has no overlap to be uneven, and is now the more common choice in image-generation and segmentation decoders. It costs more arithmetic — the convolution runs at the higher resolution — and produces visibly cleaner output.

Note that fix (1) is necessary and not sufficient. Making the overlap uniform removes the structural checkerboard, but a learned kernel can still produce one if the training signal encourages it, and stacking several transposed layers multiplies whatever periodicity survives. The interpolate-then-convolve fix removes the mechanism entirely, which is why it won.
