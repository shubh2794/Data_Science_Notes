Why Convolution: Sparsity, Sharing & Equivariance

Why a dense layer fails on a grid and what convolution replaces it with: the banded, tied weight matrix, the operation and its correlation-versus-convolution naming, then the three motivations — sparse interactions, parameter sharing as an infinitely strong prior, and translation equivariance with its boundaries.

02 Why a dense layer fails on a grid: the arithmetic, done explicitly

The standard motivation for convolution is "too many parameters". That is true and it is the least of the three problems. Do the arithmetic first, then read off all three.

The parameter count, for a real image

Take a 224×224 colour image — the input size the entire ImageNet lineage in §28 uses. Flatten it, as an MLP must, and feed it to a first hidden layer of 1000 units:

flattening a 224 × 224 × 3 image into a 1000-unit dense layerdᵢₙ = 224 · 224 · 3 = 150 528 W₁ is (150 528 × 1000) = 150 528 000 weights + 1 000 biases = 150 529 000 parameters at fp32, 4 bytes each = 602 MB — for ONE layer's weights with Adam (part 2 §22: two moment buffers + the gradient, all fp32) = 2.41 GB of optimiser state and that is the SMALL image. At 512 × 512 × 3dᵢₙ = 786 432 W₁ = 786 432 000 weights into 1000 units. against a convolution layer producing SIXTY-FOUR full-resolution feature maps64 filters, each 3 × 3 × 3, one bias each = 64 · (3·3·3) + 64 = 1 728 + 64 = 1 792 parameters output = 224 × 224 × 64 (same padding) The dense layer that produced the SAME 224·224·64 outputs from the SAME 150 528 inputs would need 150 528 · 3 211 264 ≈ 4.83 × 10¹¹ weights. Ratio of dense to convolutional parameters: 2.70 × 10⁸.

Two hundred and seventy million to one. And the convolution's 1 792 parameters produce more outputs (3 211 264 of them) than the dense layer's 150 million produce (1 000).

The three problems, in increasing order of severity

problem 1 — the obvious one
Memory and compute
602 MB of weights per layer, 2.41 GB of Adam state, and 150 million MACs per example for a layer that has not yet done anything useful. This is the problem everyone quotes and it is the easiest to fix by throwing hardware at it.
problem 2 — the statistical one
Sample efficiency
Each of those 150 million weights is used once per forward pass and must be estimated from data. A convolution's nine weights are used 50 176 times per forward pass, so every training image supplies 50 176 constraints on each of them. That is where the generalisation gap comes from, and no amount of hardware fixes it.
problem 3 — the fatal one
It does not know the pixels are a grid
Part 1 §24 proves it: shuffle every image's pixels by one fixed permutation and an MLP's achievable accuracy is exactly unchanged. Its optimum simply moves to the permuted weights. It must learn from data that pixel 5 and pixel 6 are neighbours and that pixel 5 and pixel 30 000 are not — and it must learn that separately for every pair.

Problem 3 is the one that matters, and it explains why problem 2 is unfixable by scale alone in the small-data regime. Convolution addresses all three at once with a single structural change, and §05–§07 name the three parts of that change.

Parameters, memory and weight reuse: dense against convolutional, on the same layer both bars are computed from the same layer specification; the reuse column is the one that predicts generalisation, and it is the one a parameter count hides
The misreading. "Convolution is a compression trick — it approximates the dense layer with fewer parameters." It is not an approximation of anything. A convolution layer is not a low-rank or sparse estimate of the dense layer it replaces; it is a different hypothesis class, chosen because the functions it can represent are the ones that are plausibly true of images and the ones it cannot represent are the ones that are not. If you want the compression framing, the honest version is part 2 §31's: it is a regulariser, and specifically it is the infinitely strong one of §06.

03 Banded and tied: what the constraint looks like as a matrix

Before any picture of a kernel sliding over an image, it is worth seeing the layer as part 1 would: a matrix. Convolution is a linear operation, so it is a matrix, and everything special about it is visible in that matrix's structure.

The 1-D case, written out

Take a 5-long input, a 3-long kernel (w₀, w₁, w₂), stride 1, no padding. Three outputs. The matrix is:

z = x Mᵀ, or equivalently zᵀ = M xᵀ — the 3 × 5 matrix M of a 1-D convolution layer ⎡ w₀ w₁ w₂ 0 0 ⎤ M = ⎢ 0 w₀ w₁ w₂ 0 ⎥ each row is the row above, shifted right by s ⎣ 0 0 w₀ w₁ w₂ ⎦ the two structural facts, namedBANDED 15 entries, of which 6 are structurally zero. The non-zeros of row i occupy columns i·s … i·s + f − 1 and nowhere else. TIED the 9 non-zero entries take only 3 DISTINCT VALUES. A matrix whose every diagonal is constant is a TOEPLITZ matrix; this is a Toeplitz matrix with its corners cut off. In 2-D the same object is a doubly block Toeplitz matrix — blocks of Toeplitz blocks. and the consequence for the countdense H′·H free parameters conv f free parameters, independent of H and H′ entirely.

The figure in §01 draws exactly this matrix, at whatever size and stride you choose, with one colour per free parameter. Two things are worth watching in it. First, stride removes rows, not columns: increasing s shrinks the output and therefore the matrix's height, while the band width stays f. Second, under valid padding the first and last columns carry fewer non-zeros than the middle ones — that is the border pixels being used less often, which is the entire motivation for padding in §08.

Why this matters more than it looks

Goodfellow's framing, and the best one: a convolutional network is a fully connected network with an infinitely strong prior over its weights. A prior is strong when it concentrates probability mass; it is infinitely strong when it puts zero mass on some parameter values, forbidding them regardless of what the data says. The convolutional prior says two things and both are infinitely strong:

every off-band weight is exactly 0andevery on-band weight equals its neighbour's, shiftedthereforethe layer's function is local and translation-equivariant, and nothing the data says can change that

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are easy to forget. (1) A convolution can underfit for structural reasons. If the true function needs a long-range interaction that no stack of local windows can reach, or if the right feature genuinely differs between the top and the bottom of the image, the prior is wrong and the training error — not just the test error — will be higher than a dense layer's. §29 is about exactly this case. (2) Benchmarks that compare a convnet to a non-convolutional model are comparing priors, not learning algorithms. A model that would be unaffected by shuffling the pixels is solving a strictly harder problem, and image datasets often keep separate leaderboards for the two.

The bridge to part 2. Because a convolution is a matrix, every single thing part 2 says still applies verbatim. The gradient is computed by the same reverse-mode sweep (part 2 §05); the vanishing-gradient analysis (§09) is the same product of Jacobians; the initialisation variance argument (§12) needs only the right fan-in, which §12 here supplies; the optimisers do not know they are optimising a kernel. The only new thing is that the parameter-tying makes the gradient of a shared weight a sum over all the positions that used it, which §25 derives and audits.

04 The operation itself, and the correlation-versus-convolution naming, stated once

There are two operations here and they differ by a flip of the kernel. Almost every framework implements one and calls it the other. This section says which is which, once, and then the page uses the framework convention everywhere and never mentions it again except where it genuinely matters — which is exactly one place, §25.

the two operations, on a 2-D input I and kernel K, indices from 0TRUE CONVOLUTION S(i, j) = (I ∗ K)(i, j) = ∑ₘ ∑ₙ I(i − m, j − n) · K(m, n) CROSS-CORRELATION S(i, j) = (I ⋆ K)(i, j) = ∑ₘ ∑ₙ I(i + m, j + n) · K(m, n) the only differenceI ∗ K = I ⋆ flip(K) where flip(K) reverses BOTH axes: flip(K)(m,n) = K(f−1−m, f−1−n) Convolution FLIPS the kernel; correlation does not. That is the whole of it. what the flip buys, and what it costsbuys commutativity: I ∗ K = K ∗ I. Useful for writing proofs, and it is what makes the convolution theorem (see Frequency Domain) work. costs nothing a network cares about. A LEARNED kernel will simply learn the flipped version of whichever kernel the other convention would learn, and the two networks are exactly as expressive.

So: every deep-learning framework's conv2d computes the cross-correlation and calls it convolution. This page follows that usage. When the flip actually matters — and it does, exactly once, in the backward pass — the page says "true convolution" or "flipped" explicitly.

The scope handover. Classical vision cares about the distinction for a different reason: there the kernel is designed, so a flip is a real change to a real filter, and it interacts with symmetry, separability and the frequency response. That treatment — with the same worked examples on both operations, all five border modes and the verified numerics — is Image Processing §correlation vs convolution. The framing that connects the two pages: a convolution layer is designed filtering with the kernel learned instead, and the flip stops mattering the moment the kernel becomes a variable.

The worked example, recomputed

The canonical teaching example is a 6×6 image whose left half is bright and right half is dark, correlated with the vertical-edge kernel. Every number below was recomputed rather than copied:

image I (6×6, left half 10, right half 0) ⋆ kernel K, valid → 4×4 ⎡10 10 10 0 0 0⎤ ⎡ 1 0 −1⎤ ⎢10 10 10 0 0 0⎥ ⎢ 1 0 −1⎥ I = ⎢ ⋮ (all six rows identical) K = ⎣ 1 0 −1⎦ ⎣10 10 10 0 0 0⎦ I ⋆ K = ⎡0 30 30 0⎤ I ∗ K = ⎡0 −30 −30 0⎤ ⎢0 30 30 0⎥ ⎢0 −30 −30 0⎥ ⎢0 30 30 0⎥ ⎢0 −30 −30 0⎥ ⎣0 30 30 0⎦ ⎣0 −30 −30 0⎦ read this carefully — it is the whole naming problem in one pictureThe two results differ ONLY IN SIGN, because K is antisymmetric under the flip. A "bright-to-dark edge" reads +30 under correlation and −30 under convolution. Nothing about the EDGE changed. If your edge map has the wrong sign, this is why — and if the kernel is learned, the network fixes it for free by learning −K.

Two more cases from the same worked example, both recomputed: reverse the image (dark on the left) and the correlation output becomes [0, −30, −30, 0] in every row — a dark-to-bright edge is the negative of a bright-to-dark one, which is why edge magnitude and edge sign are different questions. And on an image split into four quadrants (bright top-left and bottom-right), the same vertical kernel gives rows [0, 30, 30, 0], [0, 10, 10, 0], [0, −10, −10, 0], [0, −30, −30, 0] — the response fading and reversing as the window slides down across the quadrant boundary. The horizontal kernel gives the transpose of that pattern. All four agree with the source's arithmetic exactly.

Correlation, convolution, and the flip — one window at a time step the window and watch the products accumulate; the flip toggle changes only which kernel cell multiplies which pixel, and the two output maps differ by exactly the sign when the kernel is antisymmetric
A real trap, not a pedantic one. "The two are the same, so it does not matter." It matters in three specific places. (1) When you port a hand-designed kernel from a signal-processing reference into a network, you must flip it or your edge signs invert. (2) When you compare a learned first-layer filter against a textbook Sobel, they will look mirrored. (3) In the backward pass the flip is forced: §25 shows that the gradient with respect to the input is a true convolution with the kernel even though the forward pass was a correlation, and getting that wrong produces a network that trains to a plausible-looking but wrong solution rather than crashing.

05 Motivation 1 — sparse interactions, and what they cost you

The three motivations are sparse interactions, parameter sharing and equivariance to translation. They are often run together; they are three different things, each with its own arithmetic, and the next three sections take them one at a time. This one is about the band of §03 and nothing else — forget for a moment that the weights are also tied.

the statementDENSE every output unit is a function of EVERY input unit. m inputs, n outputs → m·n parameters, O(m·n) work per example. SPARSE every output unit is a function of at most k input units. m inputs, n outputs → k·n parameters, O(k·n) work per example. and k is tinyFor a 3 × 3 kernel on a 3-channel input, k = 27. For a 224×224×3 image, m = 150 528. The ratio k/m is 1.79 × 10⁻⁴ — four orders of magnitude, and that is BEFORE the weights are tied. Sparsity alone gets you from m·n to k·n; §06's tying then gets you from k·n to k.

Two named quantities fall out of the band, and both are used constantly for the rest of the page.

looking down
The projective field
Fix one input pixel and ask which outputs it can reach. For a single f×f layer at stride 1, that is an f×f block of outputs — 9 for a 3×3 kernel, out of however many outputs there are. Under a dense layer it is all of them.
looking up
The receptive field
Fix one output unit and ask which inputs can reach it. For one layer that is the f×f window. Stacked, it grows — and §14 works out exactly how fast, and measures it rather than asserting it.
the catch
A single layer cannot see far
Sparsity is a restriction. A one-layer 3×3 convolution simply cannot represent any function that depends on two pixels 50 apart. The answer is depth: §14 shows that stacking L layers of 3×3 gives a receptive field of 2L + 1, so the restriction is on each layer, not on the network. This is exactly why convnets are deep.

The efficiency, stated as a real transformation

The sharpest way to feel it: take the two-tap kernel [1, −1], which subtracts each pixel from its left neighbour and so reports vertical edges, and apply it to a 320×280 image. Both descriptions below define exactly the same linear map:

the same transformation, two waysAS A CONVOLUTION parameters 2 output 319 × 280 arithmetic 319 · 280 · 3 = 267 960 flops (2 multiplies + 1 add per output) AS A MATRIX MULTIPLICATION matrix (320·280) × (319·280) = 89 600 × 89 320 = 8 003 072 000 entries arithmetic 2 · 89 600 · 89 320 ≈ 1.6 × 10¹⁰ flops the two ratios, which are different numbers and are often conflatedREPRESENTATION 8 003 072 000 / 2 = 4.0 × 10⁹ times fewer numbers COMPUTATION 1.6 × 10¹⁰ / 267 960 = 5.97 × 10⁴ times less work A SPARSE matrix would close the computation gap — it stores 2·319·280 = 178 640 non-zeros and would do the same 267 960 flops. It would NOT close the representation gap, because it still stores every non-zero separately. Closing THAT gap is parameter sharing, which is the next section.

06 Motivation 2 — parameter sharing, and the infinitely strong prior

Sparsity says each output looks at a small window. Sharing says every window is looked at by the same weights. These are independent choices: a layer can be sparse without being shared, and §29 is about the architectures that make exactly that choice.

the three connectivity patterns, in increasing order of constraintFULLY CONNECTED every output sees every input, all weights distinct parameters m · n LOCALLY CONNECTED every output sees a k-window, all weights distinct (unshared conv) parameters k · n CONVOLUTIONAL every output sees a k-window, ALL WINDOWS SHARE ONE SET parameters k — independent of n entirely the runtime does NOT change between the last twoBoth do O(k·n) work per example. Sharing buys STORAGE and STATISTICAL EFFICIENCY, not arithmetic. This is the single most-missed point about convolution and it is why §12's MAC counts are so much less impressive than its parameter counts.

Why sharing is worth so much more than the parameter count suggests

Count the other way round. A dense weight is used once per forward pass, so one training example supplies one constraint on it. A convolutional weight is used once per output position, so one training example supplies H′·W′ constraints on it. On a 224×224 map that is 50 176 constraints per image, per weight. The estimation problem the optimiser faces is not "150 million parameters from 1.2 million images" but "1 792 parameters from 60 billion constraints" — and that difference, not the memory, is why a convnet generalises from data an MLP cannot learn from at all.

One parameter, many uses — and the same weight matrix under the three connectivity patterns the highlighted parameter is a single number; watch how many edges it controls as the pattern changes, and watch the parameter count collapse while the edge count does not

The infinitely strong prior, and the two things it predicts

§03 introduced the framing; here is the payoff. A prior distribution over the weights of a fully connected layer that puts zero probability on every non-convolutional weight vector is equivalent to a convolution layer. The prior says: the weights of one hidden unit must equal those of its neighbour, shifted; and they must be zero outside a small contiguous window. This is not an analogy, it is an exact restatement, and two non-obvious things follow from it.

prediction 1
Convolution can cause underfitting
A prior helps when it is true and hurts when it is false. If a task needs precise spatial information, or needs a genuinely different feature at the top of the image than at the bottom, the convolutional prior is wrong and the training error rises — not just the test error. That is the diagnostic signature: high training error that more capacity does not fix. Faces cropped and aligned to a fixed frame are the standard example, and §29 gives the architectures that relax the prior.
prediction 2
Only compare like with like
A model with no spatial prior would perform identically if you permuted every image's pixels (part 1 §24). It is solving a strictly harder problem. Benchmarks that mix the two are measuring the designer's prior knowledge, not the learning algorithm — which is why several image datasets keep separate permutation-invariant leaderboards.
and the corollary
Sharing is a regulariser with a name
Part 2 §31's map lists parameter sharing as a regularisation strategy alongside L2 and dropout, and this is why: it is the hard-constraint limit of a penalty that ties parameters together. Unlike L2 it costs nothing at inference and unlike dropout it changes the model class rather than the training procedure.

07 Motivation 3 — equivariance to translation, and its boundaries

The third motivation is the one that is stated loosely most often. Here it is precisely, with the caveats that make it almost true rather than true.

the definitionA function h is EQUIVARIANT to a transformation g when h(g(x)) = g(h(x)) "transform then process" and "process then transform" give the same answer. Contrast INVARIANCE, which is the strictly stronger and quite different h(g(x)) = h(x) the claim for convolutionLet Tₐ shift an image by a pixels. For an INFINITE image, or in the interior of a finite one, conv(Tₐ x) = Tₐ conv(x) (stride 1) Move the object; its representation moves the same amount and is otherwise unchanged. A feature map is therefore a MAP OF WHERE things are, not a summary of whether they are there.

Read the consequence carefully, because it is what makes the whole architecture work: a convolutional layer does not learn "there is an edge here" — it learns "this is what an edge looks like" and then reports where. One kernel is a statement about a pattern, applied everywhere at once. That is exactly what makes it reusable across positions and exactly why parameter sharing is legitimate.

The three ways the claim is not quite true

caveat 1
Borders
Equivariance is exact only where the shifted window still lies inside the image. Near the edge, padding invents values that do not shift with the content, so a shift and a convolution do not commute there. The figure measures the interior error (exactly zero) and the border error (not) separately.
caveat 2
Stride
A stride-s layer is equivariant only to shifts that are multiples of s. Shift by one pixel under stride 2 and the output is not a shifted version of anything — the sampling grid moved relative to the content. This is aliasing, and §19 is about it.
caveat 3
Everything that is not a translation
Convolution is not equivariant to rotation, to scale, to reflection or to any affine warp. Nothing in the construction gives you those; they must come from augmentation (part 2 §35), from an architecture that builds them in, or from data. This is the gap §30 and §31 are about.
Shift then convolve, against convolve then shift the difference map is the whole claim: it must be exactly zero in the interior, and the readout prints the interior and border errors separately rather than a single number that would hide the distinction
Equivariance is not invariance, and the difference is the subject of §18. A convolution layer is equivariant; a pooling layer is (approximately) invariant; a global pooling layer is exactly invariant to any permutation of positions, translation included. Which of the two you want depends entirely on the task, and choosing wrongly is a real and common architectural error. §18 states both properties precisely and measures them; §30 is the argument that the whole architecture chooses wrongly.
