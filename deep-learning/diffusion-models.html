Diffusion Models

Destroy data gradually with Gaussian noise, then train a network to walk the corruption backwards. Generation becomes a long chain of small, easy denoising problems — which is why these models train so stably and sample so slowly.

01 The idea: learn to undo a process you can already run

Nobody knows how to map noise to a photograph in one step. But everyone knows how to map a photograph to noise — just add a little at a time. A diffusion model learns the return trip, one small step at a time.

Two chains

  • Forward (fixed, no parameters): start from a real sample x₀, add a pinch of Gaussian noise, repeat for T steps. By the end, x_T is indistinguishable from a draw of 𝒩(0, I). Nothing is learned here — it is a recipe you write down.
  • Reverse (learned): a network εθ looks at a noisy xₜ and the timestep t, and predicts what noise is in it. Sampling starts from pure noise and applies that learned step T times (or far fewer, with a smarter solver).
  • The reason this works: reversing the whole map from data to noise is hopeless, but reversing one tiny step is an ordinary regression problem — "you added a bit of noise; which bit?"
  • The model never sees an adversary and never needs a tractable normalizing constant. The loss is a mean-squared error, so training is boring in the best possible way.
the two chainsforward x₀ → x₁ → x₂ → … → x_T ≈ 𝒩(0, I) reverse x_T → xₜ₋₁ → … → x₁ → x₀ using pθ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ)

Where it sits among generative models

  • A GAN learns a one-shot generator by playing against a discriminator: fast to sample, notoriously unstable, prone to mode collapse.
  • A VAE optimises a variational bound with a learned encoder; it is stable and fast but its samples tend to be blurry, because the decoder averages over the posterior.
  • A normalizing flow keeps exact likelihoods by restricting itself to invertible layers with cheap Jacobians — a real architectural tax.
  • Diffusion buys stability and coverage by paying at sample time: many network evaluations instead of one. Most of the field's engineering since then has been about cutting that bill.

02 The forward process

A fixed Markov chain that shrinks the signal and injects variance, designed so that you can jump to any timestep in closed form — the single trick that makes training cheap.

One step at a time

  • Each step scales the current sample down slightly and adds noise with variance βₜ. The scaling by √(1 − βₜ) is what keeps the total variance from exploding, so the chain converges to a standard Gaussian rather than drifting off.
  • Define αₜ = 1 − βₜ and the running product ᾱₜ = ∏s=1..t αₛ. Then ᾱₜ is the fraction of the original signal energy that survives to step t, and 1 − ᾱₜ is the noise energy.
forward chainq(xₜ | xₜ₋₁) = 𝒩( xₜ ; √(1 − βₜ) · xₜ₋₁ , βₜ I ) αₜ = 1 − βₜ , ᾱₜ = ∏s=1t αₛ

The closed form — and the reparameterization

  • Composing Gaussians gives another Gaussian, so you never have to simulate the chain: you can sample xₜ directly from x₀ for any t.
  • Written as a reparameterization, xₜ is a fixed linear blend of the clean sample and a single noise draw. Training a step therefore costs one noise sample and one network call — not t of them.
  • Note the coefficients satisfy (√ᾱₜ)² + (√(1 − ᾱₜ))² = 1: this is a variance-preserving schedule, so xₜ keeps unit variance throughout if the data is standardised.
  • Because ε is the only randomness, "predict x₀" and "predict ε" are the same information seen two ways — you can convert between them with algebra alone.
jump to any tq(xₜ | x₀) = 𝒩( xₜ ; √ᾱₜ · x₀ , (1 − ᾱₜ) I ) xₜ = √ᾱₜ · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε , ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) ⟺ x₀ = ( xₜ − √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε ) / √ᾱₜ

Why it matters: the closed form q(xₜ | x₀) turns a T-step simulation into one line of arithmetic. Every training step picks a random t, jumps straight there, and asks the network a single question.

03 Noise schedules and the signal-to-noise view

The schedule βₜ decides how fast information is destroyed. It is not a detail — it determines which noise levels the model spends its capacity on.

Linear vs cosine

  • Linear: βₜ ramps linearly (classically from about 1e−4 to 0.02 over T = 1000). Simple, but it destroys the image too early: the last quarter of the chain is nearly pure noise, so those steps teach the model almost nothing.
  • Cosine: define ᾱₜ directly with a squared cosine, then read βₜ back off it. Information is removed much more gently near the end, which measurably improves samples and likelihood — especially at low resolutions.

Reading a schedule as log-SNR

  • The cleanest way to compare schedules is the signal-to-noise ratio SNR(t) = ᾱₜ / (1 − ᾱₜ), usually viewed as log SNR. A schedule is really just a monotone curve of log-SNR from "clean" down to "noise".
  • At high SNR the model learns fine texture and detail; at low SNR it learns global layout and colour. Where the schedule lingers is where the model gets good.
  • High-resolution and latent models shift the schedule toward more noise (or rescale it) because neighbouring pixels are redundant: a given βₜ destroys far less information in a 1024² image than in a 32² one. Fixing the terminal SNR so that ᾱ_T = 0 exactly also matters — otherwise the model never trains on the pure-noise input it is asked to start from.

04 The reverse process

The reverse of a diffusion is, in general, an intractable distribution. The saving grace: if each forward step is tiny, each reverse step is approximately Gaussian too — so a network only has to predict a mean.

Why Gaussians are allowed

  • q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) depends on the whole data distribution and has no closed form. But a classical result says that for a diffusion with small enough steps, the reverse transition has the same functional form as the forward one — Gaussian with a shifted mean.
  • So the model is defined as a Gaussian whose mean is produced by a network, and whose variance is either fixed to a schedule constant or learned as an interpolation between two natural bounds.
  • This is the real reason T is large. Big steps break the Gaussian assumption; small steps make a very expressive distribution out of a chain of very simple ones.
learned reverse steppθ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) = 𝒩( xₜ₋₁ ; μθ(xₜ, t) , Σθ(xₜ, t) ) p(x_T) = 𝒩(0, I)

The posterior that supervises it

  • If you are allowed to peek at x₀, the reverse step is tractable: q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀) is Gaussian with a mean that is a fixed convex-style blend of x₀ and xₜ, and a variance that depends only on the schedule.
  • Training makes μθ match this posterior mean. Substituting the reparameterization turns that target into something strikingly simple: subtract a scaled noise estimate from xₜ and rescale.
  • The variance choice matters mainly at low step counts: βₜ and β̃ₜ are the two natural bounds, and learning an interpolation between them lets a model sample well with far fewer steps.
tractable posteriorq(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀) = 𝒩( μ̃ₜ(xₜ, x₀), β̃ₜ I ) μ̃ₜ = ( √ᾱₜ₋₁ · βₜ / (1 − ᾱₜ) ) · x₀ + ( √αₜ · (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) / (1 − ᾱₜ) ) · xₜ β̃ₜ = βₜ · (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) / (1 − ᾱₜ) after substituting x₀ = (xₜ − √(1−ᾱₜ) ε) / √ᾱₜμθ(xₜ, t) = (1/√αₜ) · ( xₜ − ( βₜ / √(1 − ᾱₜ) ) · εθ(xₜ, t) )
Takeaway: the network never has to model a complicated distribution. It predicts a vector — the noise — and the rest of the reverse step is fixed schedule arithmetic.

05 The training objective

Start from a variational bound, notice that almost every term is a Gaussian KL between two known means, drop the weights — and you are left with one mean-squared error.

From ELBO to MSE

  • The negative log-likelihood is bounded by a sum of terms: a prior term (constant, since the forward chain is fixed), a reconstruction term at t = 1, and T − 1 KL divergences comparing pθ(xₜ₋₁|xₜ) against the tractable posterior q(xₜ₋₁|xₜ,x₀). Both are Gaussians with known variance, so each KL collapses to a squared difference of means — and after substituting the mean expressions, to a squared difference of noise vectors times a schedule-dependent weight.
  • Dropping that weight (setting it to 1) gives the famous simplified objective. It is no longer a bound on likelihood, but it emphasises the harder, noisier timesteps and produces much better samples. In hindsight it is a re-weighted ELBO, not a different loss.
simplified training lossLsimple = 𝔼x₀, ε ~ 𝒩(0,I), t ~ U{1..T} ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) ε , t ) ‖²

The training loop, in five lines

  1. Draw a real sample x₀ from the dataset.
  2. Draw a timestep t (uniform, or from an importance/loss-aware distribution).
  3. Draw noise ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) and form xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) ε.
  4. Predict ε̂ = εθ(xₜ, t) — the same network for every t, told which t via a time embedding.
  5. Take a gradient step on ‖ε − ε̂‖². Keep an EMA copy of the weights; sample from the EMA, not the raw weights.

What the network predicts

  • Three parameterizations carry the same information but weight the loss differently across noise levels — which changes optimisation, not expressiveness.
  • v-prediction is the workhorse for high resolutions, distillation and few-step sampling, because its target stays well-conditioned at both ends of the schedule (unlike ε near t = 0 and x₀ near t = T).
  • Timestep sampling is a quiet lever: uniform t is the default, but min-SNR weighting or loss-aware importance sampling cuts gradient variance noticeably.

The whole objective: "noise this sample by a random amount, then guess the noise." Everything else on this page is machinery around that one line.

06 Score matching and the SDE view

Predicting noise and estimating the gradient of the log-density are the same task in different units. Seeing that unlocks the continuous-time picture — and every fast sampler.

Noise prediction is score estimation

  • The score of a distribution is ∇x log q(x) — a vector field pointing uphill in density. It is attractive because it does not involve the normalizing constant.
  • For the Gaussian q(xₜ|x₀), the score is exactly −ε / √(1 − ᾱₜ), so a trained noise predictor is a rescaled score model for the noisy marginal at every level.
  • This is why the field converged: denoising diffusion and noise-conditional score matching, developed separately, are the same algorithm.
score ⟺ noise∇xₜ log q(xₜ) ≈ − εθ(xₜ, t) / √(1 − ᾱₜ)

Continuous time: forward SDE, reverse SDE, probability-flow ODE

  • Let the step size go to zero and the chain becomes a stochastic differential equation. The variance-preserving SDE is the continuous limit of the schedule above; a variance-exploding variant instead grows the noise without shrinking the signal.
  • Every forward SDE has an exact reverse-time SDE that depends only on the score. Plugging in the learned score and integrating backwards is ancestral sampling.
  • There is also a deterministic ODE — the probability-flow ODE — whose marginals at every time match the SDE's exactly. Same distribution, no randomness, and it gives a bijection between noise and data (so you can invert an image back to its latent, and compute exact likelihoods).
  • Consequence: a sampler is just a numerical integrator. Choosing a sampler is choosing an ODE/SDE solver and a discretisation, which is why classical numerical-analysis ideas (higher-order steps, exponential integrators, adaptive stepping) transferred straight over.
forward (variance preserving)dx = −½ β(t) x dt + √β(t) dw reverse SDEdx = [ −½ β(t) x − β(t) ∇x log pₜ(x) ] dt + √β(t) dw̄ probability-flow ODE (same marginals, deterministic)dx/dt = −½ β(t) [ x + ∇x log pₜ(x) ]
Reframe: the network is a score field over noise levels; sampling is transporting a Gaussian cloud down that field into the data manifold. Steps, noise levels and solvers are all choices made after training.

07 Sampling: from 1000 steps to 4

Training fixes the score field; sampling is a separate engineering problem. Nearly all of the speedups came from integrating better, not from retraining.

Ancestral (DDPM) sampling

  • Walk the chain step by step: subtract the predicted noise, rescale, add fresh noise of the schedule's variance. Faithful, stochastic, and slow — the original recipe uses every one of T = 1000 steps.
  • The injected noise σₜ z is what makes each run different and helps the sampler escape small errors, but it also means you cannot skip steps freely.
ancestral stepxₜ₋₁ = (1/√αₜ) ( xₜ − ( (1 − αₜ)/√(1 − ᾱₜ) ) εθ(xₜ, t) ) + σₜ z , z ~ 𝒩(0, I)

DDIM: drop the noise, skip the steps

  • DDIM builds a non-Markovian forward process with the same marginals q(xₜ|x₀). Since training only ever used those marginals, the very same weights can be sampled a different way — no retraining.
  • The update predicts x̂₀, then re-noises it to the next level on your chosen subsequence of timesteps. With η = 0 it is fully deterministic (a first-order probability-flow ODE solver); with η = 1 it reduces to ancestral sampling.
  • Determinism buys real features: the same seed always gives the same image, latents can be interpolated smoothly, and images can be inverted back to noise for editing.
  • Because you only ever evaluate on a subsequence, 1000 steps becomes 50 — or 20 — with modest quality loss.
DDIM step from t to an earlier sx̂₀ = ( xₜ − √(1 − ᾱₜ) εθ(xₜ, t) ) / √ᾱₜ xs = √ᾱs · x̂₀ + √(1 − ᾱs − σ²) · εθ(xₜ, t) + σ z σ = η · √( (1 − ᾱs)/(1 − ᾱₜ) ) · √( 1 − ᾱₜ/ᾱs )

Higher-order solvers and distillation

  • Higher-order solvers (DPM-Solver and friends, Heun-style correctors, multistep/exponential integrators) exploit the semi-linear structure of the ODE to reach comparable quality in roughly 10–20 network evaluations.
  • Distillation attacks the same bill from the training side: train a student to take in one step what the teacher takes in two (progressive distillation), or to map any point on a trajectory directly to its endpoint — the consistency-model idea — giving 1–4 step generation. Adversarial and distribution-matching variants push single-step quality further still.
  • The rough shape of the trade-off: quality rises steeply up to a few tens of steps, then flattens. Guidance strength, resolution and solver order all shift where that knee sits.

What to notice: at w = 0 and 40 steps the particles reproduce both modes at roughly 50/50 — the model has learned the distribution, not one answer. Drop to 2–4 steps and a third of the mass strands in the empty valley between them: pure discretisation error, the same artefact that makes 5-step images look smeared. Raise w and the left mode dies out entirely by w ≈ 1, then the surviving mode tightens and drifts past its true centre — fidelity and obedience bought with diversity and a bias.

08 The denoiser network

One network, shared across all timesteps, that maps a noisy tensor plus a timestep to a same-shaped prediction. Two families dominate: the U-Net and the diffusion transformer.

The U-Net backbone

  • Shape-preserving by construction: an encoder downsamples through residual blocks, a bottleneck operates at low resolution, a decoder upsamples back, and skip connections carry high-frequency detail across. Input and output are the same tensor shape, which is exactly what ε-prediction needs.
  • Time conditioning: the scalar t becomes a sinusoidal (or Fourier) embedding, passes through a small MLP, and is injected into every residual block — typically as a per-channel scale-and-shift on the normalisation. This is what lets one set of weights behave differently at every noise level.
  • Attention blocks at the lower-resolution stages give the global coherence convolutions lack (so eyes match, and a scene stays consistent). Self-attention mixes within the image; cross-attention injects the text condition — see Attention.
  • Practical details that matter more than they look: group normalisation, SiLU activations, and zero-initialised output layers so each block starts as the identity.

Diffusion transformers (DiT)

  • Replace the U-Net with a plain transformer: patchify the (latent) image into tokens exactly as a vision transformer does, run a stack of blocks, then unpatchify back to a noise prediction.
  • Conditioning enters through adaptive layer norm — the timestep and class/text embedding produce the scale, shift and residual-gate parameters of each block, with the gate zero-initialised so training starts from a stable identity path.
  • Quality scales cleanly with compute (more tokens, wider blocks), which is why the largest recent image and video models are transformer-based rather than convolutional.
Constant across both: same shape in, same shape out; one shared network; the timestep enters as a modulation of normalisation rather than as an extra input channel.

09 Conditioning and guidance

Conditioning tells the model what to draw. Guidance is a separate, sampling-time knob that decides how hard to insist on it — and it is the single biggest lever on perceived quality.

How the condition gets in

  • Class labels and the timestep share the same path: an embedding added to the time embedding, modulating every block.
  • Text is encoded by a frozen text encoder into a sequence of token embeddings, then read by cross-attention in each block: image tokens form the queries, text tokens the keys and values. Contrastively trained encoders such as CLIP are common; large text-only encoders improve prompt fidelity, and many systems concatenate several.
  • Structural conditions (depth maps, edges, poses, a masked region for inpainting) enter as extra input channels or through an adapter branch added to the backbone.

Classifier vs classifier-free guidance

  • Classifier guidance adds the gradient of a separately trained noise-robust classifier to the score, nudging samples toward class c. It works, but it needs an extra model trained on noisy inputs — a real burden.
  • Classifier-free guidance removes that model. During training the condition is randomly dropped (say 10% of the time) and replaced by a null token ∅, so the same network learns both the conditional and unconditional score. At sampling, extrapolate away from the unconditional prediction.
  • Algebraically this is an implicit classifier gradient. Practically it costs two forward passes per step (batched together) and transforms output quality.
classifier-free guidanceε̃(xₜ, c) = εθ(xₜ, ∅) + w · ( εθ(xₜ, c) − εθ(xₜ, ∅) ) w = 0 → unconditional w = 1 → plain conditional w > 1 → sharpened toward c
  • The trade-off is direct: raising w improves prompt alignment and apparent sharpness while collapsing diversity — it is a precision-for-recall trade, visible as worse FID but better CLIP score.
  • Push too far and you get burnt, over-saturated images with blown-out contrast, because the extrapolated prediction leaves the range the model was trained on. Standard fixes: rescale or clip the guided prediction back toward the conditional statistics, or anneal w over the trajectory (weak early, strong late).
  • Typical settings sit around w ≈ 3–8 for text-to-image; distilled few-step models often bake guidance into the student and expose a much smaller range. Use the guidance slider in §07 to see the mode sharpen.
Mental model: guidance does not make the model smarter — it re-weights the distribution it already learned, trading variety for obedience.

10 Latent diffusion, and diffusion beyond images

Diffusing pixels is wasteful — most pixels are redundant. Compress first, diffuse in the small space, decode at the end.

Latent diffusion

  • Stage one: train an autoencoder (with a perceptual and adversarial reconstruction objective, plus mild regularisation of the latent) that maps an image to a compact latent, typically downsampling each spatial axis by 8. A 512×512×3 image becomes roughly 64×64×4 — about a 48× reduction in elements.
  • Stage two: train the diffusion model entirely in that latent space, with cross-attention conditioning. At sampling time, run the reverse chain on latents and decode once.
  • Why it is cheaper: attention cost grows quadratically in the number of spatial positions, and every one of the tens of denoising steps pays it. Shrinking the canvas discounts all of them, which is what put high-resolution text-to-image on a single consumer GPU.
  • The cost: the autoencoder is a hard ceiling. Fine text, faces at small scale and high-frequency texture that the encoder discards can never be recovered — hence steady work on better and higher-channel autoencoders.

Other modalities

  • Audio: diffuse spectrograms or waveforms — vocoders, text-to-speech and text-to-music all use the same machinery with a 1-D or time-frequency backbone.
  • Video: add a temporal axis and factorised space-time attention; latent video diffusion with transformer backbones is the current recipe. Consistency across frames is the whole difficulty.
  • Molecules and proteins: diffuse over 3-D coordinates with equivariant networks so predictions respect rotation and translation — structure generation and docking are now diffusion problems.
  • Robotics: diffuse over action sequences ("diffusion policies"), which naturally represent multi-modal behaviour where a regression policy would average two valid plans into an invalid one.
  • Text: discrete state spaces need a different corruption — masking or category-swapping instead of Gaussian noise — but the same denoise-iteratively idea gives parallel, non-left-to-right decoding. See Diffusion LLMs.
Pattern to reuse: pick a corruption that ends in something you can sample from, keep its steps small, and learn to reverse one step. The modality only changes the corruption and the backbone.

11 Evaluation, pitfalls, and what to remember

Generative quality has no single number. Every metric here is a proxy that can be gamed by the guidance scale alone — which is exactly why they are reported in pairs.

Metrics

  • FID compares Gaussian fits to features of real and generated sets; lower is better. It is sensitive to sample count, resizing and the feature extractor, and it rewards diversity, so it degrades as guidance rises even when images look better to people.
  • Precision / recall (or density / coverage) split FID's single number into fidelity and diversity — far more informative when comparing guidance scales.
  • CLIP score measures prompt alignment via image-text similarity, so it moves the opposite way from FID under guidance. Report the FID–CLIP trade-off curve swept over w, not one point.
  • Human preference remains the ground truth for aesthetics, prompt following and artefacts; automatic preference models trained on such data are the standard cheap stand-in.

Practical pitfalls

  • Schedule / resolution mismatch: a schedule tuned at 64² destroys too little information at 512² or in latent space. Rescale it (shift toward more noise) and force zero terminal SNR, or the model will never see the pure noise it must start from — a classic cause of washed-out, mid-grey outputs.
  • Too few steps with the wrong solver: first-order integration at 5–10 steps smears detail. Use a higher-order solver or a distilled model rather than simply cutting steps.
  • Guidance too high: saturation, high contrast, mangled anatomy, and a collapse of diversity across seeds. Turn w down before blaming the model.
  • Exposure bias: training always conditions on exactly noised real data, while sampling conditions on the model's own imperfect output. Errors compound along the trajectory; stochastic samplers partly wash them out, deterministic ones do not.
  • Memorisation: highly duplicated training images can be reproduced near-verbatim, especially with a specific prompt. Deduplicate the data and audit outputs.
  • Cost bookkeeping: the real currency is network evaluations, and guidance doubles them. "50 steps" with CFG is 100 forward passes.

What to remember

  • The forward process is fixed and closed-form: xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) ε. Everything else follows from that line.
  • The loss is a denoising regression, ‖ε − εθ(xₜ, t)‖² — a re-weighted ELBO in disguise.
  • The trained network is a score field; sampling is solving an ODE/SDE, so step count, solver and stochasticity are post-hoc choices.
  • Guidance trades diversity for fidelity; latent space trades a quality ceiling for a large constant-factor speedup.
  • The remaining frontier is the same as it ever was: fewer network evaluations for the same sample.
In one sentence: a diffusion model is a single denoiser, conditioned on noise level, whose repeated application transports Gaussian noise onto the data distribution.
