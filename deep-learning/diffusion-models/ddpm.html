DDPM: Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

The hub page already gives the forward closed form, the reverse step, the ε-prediction loss and the samplers. This page reads the original study closely for what the summary skips: the variational bound term by term, the per-step weight that L_simple throws away and what that does, the two fixed reverse variances, why predicting ε turns each term into denoising score matching, the discrete decoder that makes the bound a real codelength, the exact setup, the ablation and result tables, and the rate–distortion reading of the sampler that explains why such good samples come with only middling likelihoods.

01 What the original study contributed, and what this page adds to the hub

Diffusion probabilistic models already existed; the original study made them produce high-quality images. It did so with three choices: fix the forward variances, parameterise the reverse mean through a noise predictor εθ, and train on an unweighted version of the bound. It then showed the result is simultaneously a denoising score matcher, an annealed-Langevin sampler and a progressive lossy compressor.

02 The variational bound, term by term

The model is a latent-variable model whose latents x₁ … x_T have the same shape as the data. Its negative log-likelihood is bounded exactly as a VAE's ELBO is, with the fixed forward chain q playing the encoder. Rearranged so that every term compares two Gaussians, the bound splits into three kinds of term.

the bound, rewritten with the tractable posterior𝔼[ −log pθ(x₀) ] ≤ 𝔼q[ L_T + ∑t>1 Lt−1 + L₀ ] = L L_T = D_KL( q(x_T | x₀) ‖ p(x_T) ) prior matching Lt−1 = D_KL( q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀) ‖ pθ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) ) t = 2 … T, one denoising step each L₀ = −log pθ(x₀ | x₁) reconstruction by the decoder
  • Why this form matters. Every Lt−1 compares the model's reverse step against the posterior q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀), which is Gaussian in closed form (hub §04). So all KL terms are Gaussian-against-Gaussian, computable exactly, with no high-variance Monte Carlo estimate of a log ratio.
  • Why L_T is a constant. The forward variances βₜ are fixed, not learned (the study notes they could be learned by reparameterisation, but chose not to). q then has no learnable parameters and p(x_T) = 𝒩(0, I) is fixed, so L_T is a constant that is ignored in training. It is also tiny: with the schedule of §08 the study reports L_T ≈ 10⁻⁵ bits/dim.
  • Each middle term is a squared distance between means. With the reverse variance fixed to σₜ² I (§03), the KL between two Gaussians of equal isotropic covariance reduces to a scaled squared distance between their means, plus a constant.
one middle term, with a fixed reverse varianceLt−1 = 𝔼q[ (1 / 2σₜ²) · ‖ μ̃ₜ(xₜ, x₀) − μθ(xₜ, t) ‖² ] + C sanity check of the reported prior term (own arithmetic, rough)KL per dimension ≈ ½ · ᾱ_T · x₀² for small ᾱ_T ; ᾱ_T ≈ 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ for the linear schedule x₀ in [−1, 1] with 𝔼[x₀²] ≈ 0.25 ⇒ ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁶ nats ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁶ bits per dimension ~ 10⁻⁵ ✓

The 𝔼[x₀²] value is an assumption about natural-image statistics, used only to confirm the order of magnitude. It is the reason the schedule was required to keep L_T ≈ 0 (§08): a schedule that ended with noticeable signal would leave a mismatch between q(x_T | x₀) and the Gaussian that sampling starts from.

03 The reverse variance: two fixed choices, and why not learn it

The study set Σθ(xₜ, t) = σₜ² I to untrained, time-dependent constants and tried two values. They are the two extremes of the reverse step's entropy, and they gave similar results.

the two candidatesσₜ² = βₜ optimal if x₀ ~ 𝒩(0, I) σₜ² = β̃ₜ = βₜ · (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) / (1 − ᾱₜ) optimal if x₀ is one fixed point β̃ₜ ≤ βₜ always, since (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) < (1 − ᾱₜ) — they differ most at small t, and β̃₁ = 0 because ᾱ₀ = 1
  • Why these two. If the data were itself standard Gaussian, the true reverse step would add exactly the forward variance back; if the data were a single point, the reverse step would be the posterior q(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ, x₀), whose variance is β̃ₜ. Real data sits between, and the study describes the two as upper and lower bounds on reverse-process entropy for data with coordinatewise unit variance.
  • They behaved alike in experiments, which is what one expects when T = 1000: the steps are so small that the two values differ appreciably only over the first few dozen steps (own reading of the schedule; the toggle in §06 shows the gap).
  • Learned variances were unstable. Learning a diagonal Σθ led to unstable training and worse samples than a fixed variance. With mean prediction and the true bound it trained but scored FID 23.69 against 13.22 for fixed; with ε-prediction it did not train stably at all (Table 2, §09).
Later work, not from the study. Learned variances were later made to work by predicting an interpolation between log βₜ and log β̃ₜ and adding a small bound term to the simple loss; that is what lets ancestral sampling use far fewer steps. The hub's §04 mentions this interpolation.

04 Predicting ε: why each term becomes denoising score matching

The posterior mean that μθ must match is a fixed function of xₜ and the noise ε that produced it. Since the network sees xₜ, the only unknown is ε — so parameterise the mean through a noise predictor and each bound term becomes a weighted noise-regression.

substitute xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ) ε into the posterior meanμ̃ₜ = (1/√αₜ) · ( xₜ − (βₜ / √(1 − ᾱₜ)) · ε ) choose μθ(xₜ, t) = (1/√αₜ) · ( xₜ − (βₜ / √(1 − ᾱₜ)) · εθ(xₜ, t) ) μ̃ₜ − μθ = ( βₜ / (√αₜ · √(1 − ᾱₜ)) ) · ( εθ − ε ) so each middle term is a weighted noise MSELt−1 − C = 𝔼x₀, ε [ ( βₜ² / (2σₜ² αₜ (1 − ᾱₜ)) ) · ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) ε , t ) ‖² ]
  • Denoising score matching across noise levels. Each term asks the network to recover the noise added to x₀ at scale √(1 − ᾱₜ). Summed over t, this resembles denoising score matching over multiple noise scales indexed by t — the same objective a noise-conditional score network uses. Why denoising is score estimation is shown on the denoising-equals-score page.
  • Sampling resembles annealed Langevin dynamics. The reverse step xₜ₋₁ = μθ(xₜ, t) + σₜ z moves xₜ a small distance along −εθ and adds fresh Gaussian noise. That is the shape of a Langevin step, with εθ acting as a learned gradient of the data density, run at a decreasing noise level. Langevin and other gradient-driven MCMC samplers are covered under MCMC.
  • The connection is one of shape, not of procedure. Unlike a score network sampled with a hand-tuned Langevin schedule, here the sampler's step sizes and noise levels come from the forward process, and the sampler is trained directly as a latent-variable model by the bound.
The study's own caveat. It is explicit that ε-prediction is just another parameterisation of pθ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ): it adds no expressiveness, and its value is an empirical question. Table 2 (§09) is that evidence — on the true bound ε-prediction performs about as well as predicting μ̃, and it is clearly better only in combination with the simplified objective.

05 The discrete decoder: making the bound a real codelength

Images are integers. A continuous density on x₀ can assign arbitrarily high likelihood by collapsing onto those integer points, so a bound in nats of density is not a codelength. The last reverse step therefore outputs a probability mass per pixel value.

data and the final steppixel values {0, 1, …, 255} scaled linearly to [−1, 1] (consistent scaling from x₀ through to p(x_T) = 𝒩(0, I)) pθ(x₀ | x₁) = ∏i=1D ∫ from δ₋(x₀ⁱ) to δ₊(x₀ⁱ) 𝒩( x ; μθⁱ(x₁, 1) , σ₁² ) dx δ₊(x) = x + 1/255 (or +∞ if x = 1) δ₋(x) = x − 1/255 (or −∞ if x = −1) D = data dimensionality, i = one coordinate
  • Each pixel gets the Gaussian mass of its own bin, of width 2/255 around its value; the two end bins absorb the tails. The probabilities over the 256 values sum to 1, so L₀ is a genuine discrete negative log-likelihood. (The same discretised-Gaussian idea appears in the decoder likelihoods of VAEs.)
  • Consequence: the whole bound is a lossless codelength on discrete data, in bits per dimension, with no added noise needed (no dequantisation) and no Jacobian to log. That is what makes the NLL column of Table 1 comparable with autoregressive models.
  • A simple choice, deliberately. The study notes that a more powerful decoder, such as a conditional autoregressive model, would be straightforward to plug in, and leaves it to future work. At sampling time the final step simply outputs μθ(x₁, 1) with no noise (§07).

06 Dropping the weight: what L_simple actually changes

L_simple is the bound of §04 with the coefficient set to 1 and t drawn uniformly. The study calls it a weighted variational bound that emphasises different aspects of reconstruction. The plot shows exactly which aspects: relative to the true bound, it removes most of the weight on the first few dozen steps.

the simplified objectiveL_simple(θ) = 𝔼t ~ U{1…T}, x₀, ε [ ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) ε , t ) ‖² ] t = 1 stands in for L₀: the discrete integral approximated by the Gaussian density × bin width, ignoring σ₁² and the edge bins t > 1 is Lt−1 with its coefficient βₜ² / (2σₜ² αₜ (1 − ᾱₜ)) replaced by 1 the dropped coefficient, with each variance choice (own algebra)σₜ² = βₜ : wₜ = βₜ / ( 2 αₜ (1 − ᾱₜ) ) σₜ² = β̃ₜ : wₜ = βₜ / ( 2 αₜ (1 − ᾱₜ₋₁) ) → grows without bound as t → 1
How heavily the true bound weights each timestep, against L_simple's flat weight computed from the study's linear schedule; move t to read off the share of weight below it
  • Small t is where the bound puts its weight. With σ² = β, the true bound weights step 2 about 29× as heavily as L_simple does (about 59× with β̃), and steps 2–10 — under 1% of the chain — carry about 13% (18% with β̃) of its total weight. These are the steps where the noise is so small that the network is only polishing nearly clean images.
  • Dropping the weight hands that budget to harder steps. Relative to the bound, L_simple up-weights the middle of the chain by roughly 2× (the bound's minimum is about 0.5× flat near t ≈ 350) and leaves large t about unchanged. The study's reading: down-weighting the easy small-t terms lets the network focus on the more difficult denoising at larger t. (The specific ratios here are own arithmetic from the schedule.)
  • The price is codelength. The bound is what measures bits; reweighting it improves samples and worsens likelihood — 3.75 vs 3.70 bits/dim, but FID 3.17 vs 13.51 (Table 1, §10). Small-t terms dominate the bits because they encode fine detail, which is also why §12 finds most of the codelength spent on imperceptible distortions.
  • Caveat on the picture. This plots the weight only. The actual contribution of each step to the loss is weight × that step's noise-prediction error, which itself varies with t.

07 Algorithms 1 and 2, side by side

Training touches one random timestep per example per update; sampling must visit all T of them in order. That asymmetry is the whole cost profile of the method.

Algorithm 1 · training
repeat
  x₀ ~ q(x₀)                    a training image
  t ~ Uniform({1, …, T})
  ε ~ 𝒩(0, I)
  gradient step on
    ∇θ ‖ ε − εθ( √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ) ε , t ) ‖²
until converged
Algorithm 2 · sampling
x_T ~ 𝒩(0, I)
for t = T, …, 1:
  z ~ 𝒩(0, I)  if t > 1,  else  z = 0
  xₜ₋₁ = (1/√αₜ) · ( xₜ − ((1−αₜ)/√(1−ᾱₜ)) · εθ(xₜ, t) )
          + σₜ z
end for
return x₀
  • z = 0 at t = 1. The last step returns the decoder's mean with no added noise — the noiseless display of §05's discrete decoder.
  • One network call per training example, T = 1000 per sample. The study chose T = 1000 so that the number of network evaluations during sampling matches previous work. How the same trained network was later sampled in tens of steps is the hub's §07.
  • (1 − αₜ) = βₜ, so the sampling line is the mean of §04 plus σₜ z.

12 Progressive coding: where the bits go

Read the bound as a transmission protocol and the gap between great samples and middling likelihoods explains itself. Send x_T, then each xₜ given xₜ₊₁, then x₀; each Lt is the bits for one message and L₀ is the last. The middle terms are the rate, L₀ the distortion.

the decomposition for the best-sample CIFAR-10 modelrate L₁ + … + L_T = 1.78 bits/dim distortion L₀ = 1.97 bits/dim ≈ RMSE 0.95 on the 0–255 scale total ≈ 3.75 bits/dim (own arithmetic: 1.78 + 1.97, the Table 1 bound) what a receiver can do mid-streamholding xₜ, estimate x̂₀ = ( xₜ − √(1 − ᾱₜ) · εθ(xₜ) ) / √ᾱₜ rate at t = cumulative bits received so far distortion at t = RMSE( x₀ , x̂₀ ) on [0, 255]

The study states that more than half of the lossless codelength describes imperceptible distortions: 1.97 of 3.75 bits/dim go to the last step, encoding x₀ given x₁, by which point the receiver's estimate is already within about one grey level (RMSE 0.95). L_simple's reweighting (§06) spends less effort on precisely those bits, which costs likelihood but not perceived quality.

Rate and distortion along the reverse chain — CIFAR-10 test set, as reported step through the chain: distortion falls for hundreds of steps on a sliver of bits, then the final hundred steps take almost all of them
  • Distortion falls steeply at low rate. After 900 reverse steps the receiver has 0.11994 bits/dim — about 7% of the final rate (own arithmetic) — and already a reconstruction at RMSE 12.0. The study's reading: the majority of the bits are allocated to imperceptible distortions.
  • The last hundred steps are expensive. Going from step 900 to 1000 costs about 1.66 of the 1.78 bits/dim (own arithmetic from the table) to move RMSE from 12.0 to 0.95.
  • A proof of concept, not a codec. The protocol assumes a procedure, such as minimal random coding, that transmits a sample from q using about D_KL(q ‖ p) bits when only p is shared. The study is explicit that this is not tractable for high-dimensional data; the rates are what such a scheme would achieve.

13 Progressive generation, shared latents, interpolation — and the autoregressive view

Watching x̂₀ during sampling shows what the rate–distortion curve implies: large-scale features appear first and details last.

  • Coarse to fine. Predicting x̂₀ at intermediate steps of the CIFAR-10 sampler shows layout and colour settling early and texture resolving at the end — the hub's log-SNR view (low SNR learns global structure, high SNR learns detail) seen from the sampling side.
  • Shared xₜ, different samples. Fix one xₜ and draw several x₀ ~ pθ(x₀ | xₜ). On CelebA-HQ, when t is small all but fine details are preserved; when t is large only large-scale features are. How much of the image is "decided" depends on how far down the chain you condition.
  • Interpolation in xₜ. Encode two source images with the forward process, xₜ, x′ₜ ~ q(xₜ | x₀), interpolate linearly, and decode with the reverse chain. On CelebA-HQ 256×256 with t = 500 this gives high-quality reconstructions and plausible interpolations that smoothly vary attributes such as pose, skin tone, hairstyle, expression and background — but not eyewear; larger t gives coarser, more varied interpolations, and at t = 1000 the results are novel samples. The reverse process is what makes it work: interpolating with zero diffusion steps just blends pixels.

Diffusion as autoregression with a generalised bit ordering

the thought experiment (not the trained setup)set T = D (the number of coordinates), and let the forward step t mask out coordinate t ⇒ pθ(xₜ₋₁ | xₜ) has to predict one coordinate given the ones still visible ⇒ the bound is exactly an autoregressive model's likelihood, in a fixed coordinate order

The study's point is the converse: Gaussian diffusion with small steps can be seen as a kind of autoregressive model with a generalised bit ordering that cannot be expressed by reordering data coordinates. It reveals information as progressively finer-grained noise removal, not pixel by pixel. Autoregressive models owe part of their image quality to their ordering bias; the study speculates that Gaussian diffusion serves a similar purpose, perhaps to greater effect, since Gaussian noise may be more natural to add to images than masking. That is a hypothesis, not a measured result. (Its experiments use T = 1000, not T = D.)

Everything in this section is later work, not a claim of the original study. Most of it attacks exactly the limitations of §14.

The core recipe of this study — the fixed Gaussian forward chain, ε-prediction, the uniformly weighted MSE and a time-conditioned U-Net trained with an EMA — survived nearly unchanged as the default starting point.

16 Common misconceptions

"L_simple is a different loss from the ELBO." It is the same sum of terms with different weights. Each term is the bound's Lt−1 with its coefficient set to 1 (§06). It is no longer a bound on likelihood, but it is not a new objective either — the study calls it a weighted variational bound.
"Predicting ε is what made DDPM work." Only together with the unweighted loss. On the true bound, ε- and μ̃-prediction score FID 13.51 and 13.22 (§09). The study itself calls ε-prediction just another parameterisation.
"DDPM learns the reverse variance." Not in the original study. Variances were fixed to βₜ or β̃ₜ; learning them was unstable (§03). Learned variances are later work.
"Good samples mean good likelihoods." This paper is the counterexample. The best-sample model is 3.75 bits/dim, worse than the bound-trained variant and far from autoregressive models, because more than half its bits encode imperceptible detail (§12).
"DDPM is a score-based model run with Langevin dynamics." It resembles one. The objective resembles denoising score matching and the sampler resembles annealed Langevin dynamics, but the sampler's steps come from the forward process and it is trained as a latent-variable model by a bound (§04).
"T = 1000 was tuned." It was set without a sweep, to match prior work's number of network evaluations (§07–§08).

17 Takeaways

  • The bound splits into a constant prior term L_T (≈ 10⁻⁵ bits/dim, because βₜ is fixed), T − 1 Gaussian KLs and a discrete decoder term L₀.
  • Predict ε and each KL becomes βₜ² / (2σₜ² αₜ (1 − ᾱₜ)) · ‖ε − εθ‖² — a denoising-score-matching term at noise level t; sampling resembles annealed Langevin dynamics.
  • Drop that coefficient and you get L_simple: much less weight on the tiny-noise steps, more on harder ones — FID 13.51 → 3.17, NLL 3.70 → 3.75 bits/dim.
  • Fix the reverse variance to βₜ or β̃ₜ (similar results); learning it was unstable.
  • The discrete decoder on {0…255} scaled to [−1, 1] makes the bound a lossless codelength.
  • Setup: T = 1000, linear β from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02, a 35.7M-parameter U-Net with group norm, sinusoidal time embedding and 16×16 attention, Adam 2 × 10⁻⁴, EMA 0.9999.
  • Rate–distortion: 1.78 bits/dim of rate, 1.97 of distortion; most bits encode imperceptible detail, and generation runs coarse to fine — an autoregression over a generalised bit ordering.
