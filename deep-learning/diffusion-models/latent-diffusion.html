Latent Diffusion Models

Train an autoencoder once, then run the whole diffusion process in its small latent space and decode a single time at the end. The hub's §10 gives the four-line summary; this page goes through the original study: why it separates perceptual from semantic compression, what the KL- and VQ-regularised autoencoders look like, the downsampling-factor ablation that lands on f = 4–8, why the latent has to be rescaled, how a domain encoder τθ enters through cross-attention, and what the results and the compute bill actually were.

01 Why leave pixel space: the bill, and where the bits go

A pixel-space diffusion model spends its compute on every pixel at every step, even though most of an image's bits encode detail no viewer can perceive. The original study's fix is to remove that detail once, with a separately trained autoencoder, and let the diffusion model work only on what is left.

  • The bill. The paper puts the strongest pixel-space diffusion models of the time at 150–1000 V100-days of training, and producing 50 000 samples at about 5 days on a single A100. Sampling typically takes 25–1000 sequential network evaluations, each over the full-resolution tensor (the step-count side of this is on the hub's sampling section).
  • The loss can ignore detail; the compute cannot. A diffusion model's training objective can down-weight imperceptible detail, but gradients and every forward pass still run over all pixels. The cost is paid whether or not the model ends up caring.
  • Two kinds of compression. The paper reads the rate–distortion trade-off of a trained pixel model in two regimes: a perceptual compression stage that discards high-frequency detail but learns little semantics, and a semantic compression stage where the generative model actually learns the conceptual composition of the data. Most bits of a digital image fall in the first regime.
the division of labourstage 1 perceptual compression autoencoder ℰ, 𝒟 removes imperceptible detail trained ONCE, reused across many diffusion models and tasks stage 2 semantic compression diffusion model εθ learns the data distribution on z = ℰ(x), a perceptually equivalent but smaller space
The point of the split. Earlier two-stage generators had to pick one compression rate that served both reconstruction and generation. Because the second stage here is a convolutional diffusion model that handles a 2-D latent well, the first stage can be mild — remove only what is imperceptible — and reconstructions stay very good (§03).

02 The shape of the computation

An image x ∈ ℝ^(H×W×3) is encoded to a latent z = ℰ(x) ∈ ℝ^(h×w×c) with a spatial downsampling factor f. The diffusion model is trained on z, not x, and at sampling time its output is decoded once.

tensors, training and samplingdownsampling factor f = H / h = W / w = 2ᵐ , m ∈ ℕ training x ──ℰ──▶ z₀ ∈ ℝ^(h×w×c) ──q(zₜ | z₀)──▶ zₜ ──εθ(zₜ, t)──▶ ε̂ (ℰ frozen) sampling z_T ~ 𝒩(0, I) ──T′ reverse steps on h×w×c──▶ z₀ ──𝒟──▶ x̃ ∈ ℝ^(H×W×3) (𝒟 runs ONCE) example (own arithmetic, Table 8 shapes): 256×256×3 = 196 608 numbers f = 4, c = 3 → 64×64×3 = 12 288 (16× fewer) f = 8, c = 4 → 32×32×4 = 4 096 (48× fewer)
  • The latent keeps its 2-D layout. Earlier two-stage approaches that modelled a learned latent autoregressively relied on an arbitrary 1-D ordering of it, which forced aggressive compression to keep sequences short. A latent diffusion model keeps z as a small image and uses a time-conditional U-Net built mainly from 2-D convolutions, so it inherits the spatial inductive biases that make convolutions good at images (the backbone itself is on the hub's network section).
  • The encoder is cheap at training time. The forward process is fixed, so zₜ is obtained from ℰ(x) in closed form — one encoder call per example, exactly as the pixel-space recipe on the DDPM page obtains xₜ from x₀.
  • Everything sequential happens small. All T′ reverse steps run on h×w×c; the decoder is paid once per sample, not once per step.

03 Stage one: a perceptual autoencoder, KL- or VQ-regularised

A plain pixel-MSE autoencoder blurs; a perceptual loss plus a patch-based adversarial loss keeps reconstructions on the image manifold. A light regulariser then stops the latent from taking arbitrarily high variance.

  • Reconstruction objective. The autoencoder is trained with a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial objective: a discriminator that scores local patches rather than whole images, which enforces local realism and avoids the blur that pixel-space L2 or L1 losses alone produce. That discriminator is the PatchGAN idea covered on the GAN page's Pix2Pix section.
  • KL-reg. A slight KL penalty pulls the latent toward a standard normal, as in a VAE but with a very small weight — enough to bound the latent's scale, far too little to make it a good sampling prior on its own. The rate/distortion knob it sets is the β trade-off on the β-VAE page.
  • VQ-reg. A vector-quantisation layer with a learned codebook of size |𝒵|, as in VQ-VAE — but absorbed into the decoder. The diffusion model works on the continuous pre-quantisation latent; snapping to the codebook is part of decoding.
the two regularisers (schematic)KL-reg: ℰ(x) = (μ, σ) , z = μ + σ ⊙ ε₀ , penalty w · KL( 𝒩(μ, σ²) ‖ 𝒩(0, I) ) , w small VQ-reg: z = ℰ(x) continuous ; 𝒟(z) = 𝒟′( quantise(z) ) quantise: nearest of |𝒵| codes per position

04 Stage two: the same denoising loss, on z

Nothing about the diffusion objective changes. Replace x by z = ℰ(x) and the simplified ε-prediction loss of the hub's §05 becomes the latent diffusion loss.

pixel loss → latent lossLDM = 𝔼x, ε~𝒩(0,1), t ‖ ε − εθ(xₜ, t) ‖²₂ LLDM = 𝔼ℰ(x), ε~𝒩(0,1), t ‖ ε − εθ(zₜ, t) ‖²₂ zₜ = √ᾱₜ · ℰ(x) + √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε conditional version (§07)LLDM = 𝔼ℰ(x), y, ε~𝒩(0,1), t ‖ ε − εθ(zₜ, t, τθ(y)) ‖²₂ εθ and τθ optimised jointly
  • ℰ and 𝒟 are frozen during stage two. No gradient flows into the autoencoder, so one first stage can serve every diffusion model trained on top of it.
  • The schedule is the pixel one. The training-hyperparameter tables list 1000 diffusion steps with a linear schedule for the reported models; the main result tables sample with DDIM, mostly 100–500 steps depending on the experiment.

05 The latent's scale sets the signal-to-noise ratio

The forward process assumes data of roughly unit variance. A KL-regularised latent can have a much larger scale, and then the same noise schedule leaves far more signal at every step than intended.

why scale matters, and the fix (Appendix D.1 and G)zₜ = √ᾱₜ · z₀ + √(1 − ᾱₜ) · ε effective SNR(t) ∝ Var(z₀) · ᾱₜ / (1 − ᾱₜ) large Var(z₀) ⇒ high SNR at every t ⇒ semantic content is still readable late into the chain rescale once: σ̂² = mean over batch, channel, height, width of (z − μ̂)² — from the FIRST batch z ← z / σ̂ → unit variance VQ-reg latents: already close to unit variance, not rescaled
  • The paper reports that this signal-to-noise ratio, induced by the latent's scale, significantly affects results: it compares the raw f = 4 KL latent with a version rescaled by its component-wise standard deviation, in the convolutional-sampling experiments of §09.
  • This is the same lesson as the hub's schedule discussion (§03): what matters is SNR over time, and the data's own scale is part of that.
  • Later, not from this paper: the public text-to-image release built on this work hard-codes a single scalar, 0.18215, multiplied into its f = 8 KL latents. It is this same "divide by the latent's standard deviation" step, estimated once and frozen.

06 Choosing f: the downsampling-factor ablation

The study trains class-conditional ImageNet models with f ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} — written LDM-f, with LDM-1 the pixel-space baseline — on a single A100 with the same number of training steps and the same parameter count. Too little compression trains slowly; too much caps quality.

  • Small f (LDM-1, LDM-2) trains slowly. Most of the network's work is still perceptual; after 2M training steps there is an FID gap of 38 between pixel-space LDM-1 and LDM-8 (Fig. 6, 100 DDIM steps).
  • Large f stagnates. At f = 32 too much information is gone in the first stage, and sample quality stops improving early — the autoencoder's ceiling, not the diffusion model, is binding.
  • The middle wins. LDM-4 to LDM-16 strike a good balance between efficiency and perceptually faithful results; on sample throughput vs FID (Fig. 7, CelebA-HQ and ImageNet, 10–200 DDIM steps) LDM-4 to LDM-8 reach much lower FID than LDM-1 while also sampling much faster. Complex data such as ImageNet needs less compression than faces. The paper's conclusion: LDM-4 and LDM-8 offer the best conditions for high-quality synthesis.
Downsampling-factor explorer: latent shape, size, attention cost and reconstruction quality slide f from pixel space to 32×; watch the element count and the attention cost collapse while reconstruction error rises
  • Element count is not the whole story. For the KL autoencoders, f = 8, 16 and 32 all give 4 096 numbers per 256² image — the channel count grows as fast as the grid shrinks. What changes is how many spatial positions the U-Net sees (1 024, 256, 64) and how much each position has to encode. The attention-cost bar is own arithmetic for a single self-attention layer over the whole latent grid, proportional to (h·w)²; the paper's U-Nets only place attention at a few low resolutions, so treat it as the trend, not a measured cost.
  • The reconstruction column is a floor, not a forecast. It is how well the frozen autoencoder can redraw a real image; samples cannot be better than the decoder allows, but they can be much worse if the diffusion model struggles on that latent (the f = 32 case).

07 Conditioning: a domain encoder τθ and cross-attention

The study's general conditioning mechanism: encode the condition y — text, a class label, a layout — with a domain-specific encoder τθ into a sequence of M tokens, and let the U-Net read it with cross-attention at several resolutions. The general picture of cross-attention and guidance is on the hub's §09 and the attention page; this is the paper's exact form.

cross-attention inside the U-Netτθ(y) ∈ ℝ^(M × dτ) condition as M tokens φᵢ(zₜ) ∈ ℝ^(N × dᵢ) flattened U-Net features at block i, N = hᵢ·wᵢ positions Q = W_Q⁽ⁱ⁾ · φᵢ(zₜ) K = W_K⁽ⁱ⁾ · τθ(y) V = W_V⁽ⁱ⁾ · τθ(y) Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d ) · V (N × M) weights, output N × d
  • Queries come from the image, keys and values from the condition. Every latent position asks "which condition tokens are relevant here?" The weight matrix is N × M, so its cost is linear in the number of latent positions — another place where a smaller latent pays off.
  • τθ is trained jointly with the denoiser from scratch for the reported text model; it is not a frozen pretrained encoder (contrast §12).
  • Not everything goes through attention. Spatially aligned conditions — a low-resolution image for super-resolution, a masked image for inpainting, a semantic map — are simply concatenated with zₜ as extra input channels.

Layouts, semantic maps and sampling beyond the training size

  • Layout-to-image (Table 9). Bounding-box layouts encoded by τθ and read through cross-attention: LDM-4 reaches FID 32.02 on OpenImages 256² (VQGAN+transformer 45.33) and 40.91 on COCO when fine-tuned from OpenImages.
  • Convolutional sampling. Because the denoiser is convolutional and the condition is spatial, a semantic-synthesis model trained on 256² crops generates coherent 512 × 1024 landscapes; super-resolution and inpainting models likewise produce 512²–1024² outputs. This is where the latent SNR of §05 matters most.

11 Limitations and societal impact the original study states

speed
Still sequential
Latent diffusion cuts the cost per step, not the number of steps. Sampling remains slower than a one-pass GAN.
precision
The autoencoder is a ceiling
Where fine pixel-level accuracy is required, reconstruction quality becomes the bottleneck. The super-resolution models are already somewhat limited by it — the low PSNR / SSIM of §09.
misuse
Cheaper means more accessible
Lower training and inference cost makes the technology broadly available, and with it the creation and spread of manipulated imagery and disinformation.
data
Leakage and bias
Generative models can reveal training data, and can reproduce or amplify biases already in the data — important when it is sensitive or was collected without consent.

13 Common misconceptions

"The latent is a VAE latent, so you could just sample it from 𝒩(0, I)." No — the KL weight is tiny. The regulariser only bounds the scale. The latent's distribution is complicated, which is exactly why a diffusion model is trained on it.
"More compression is always faster and therefore better." Not past f ≈ 16. At f = 32 quality stagnates early because the first stage throws away information the generator would need (§06).
"Latent diffusion uses a pretrained CLIP text encoder." Not in the original study. Its text encoder τθ is trained jointly with the U-Net; the frozen-CLIP setup is a later public-release choice (§12).
"The decoder runs at every denoising step." Once per sample. All reverse steps stay in latent space; 𝒟 is applied to the final z₀ only (§02).
"VQ-reg means the diffusion model works on discrete codes." It works on continuous latents. The quantisation layer is absorbed into the decoder (§03).

14 Takeaways

  • Split the work: a once-trained perceptual autoencoder removes imperceptible detail; the diffusion model only learns semantics, on z = ℰ(x).
  • Mild compression suffices because the latent stays 2-D and the denoiser is convolutional; perceptual + patch-adversarial losses keep reconstructions sharp; KL- or VQ-reg keeps the latent tame.
  • The loss is unchanged: ‖ε − εθ(zₜ, t, τθ(y))‖², with conditions entering through cross-attention or concatenation.
  • f = 4–8 is the sweet spot: smaller trains slowly, f = 32 caps quality.
  • Rescale the latent to unit variance, or the noise schedule's SNR is wrong.
  • The payoff: FID 3.60 on class-conditional ImageNet for 271 V100-days, against 4.59 for 962; the price is a reconstruction ceiling visible in pixel-accurate metrics.
