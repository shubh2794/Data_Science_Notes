Distributed Training

Frontier models don't fit — or don't train in reasonable time — on one device. Distributed training splits the work across many GPUs, by replicating the model, slicing it, or staging it, often all at once.

01 Two reasons to distribute

  • Throughput: a huge dataset trains too slowly on one GPU — spread the batch to go faster.
  • Capacity: the model (plus its gradients and optimizer state) doesn't fit in one GPU's memory — split it.
  • The three classic strategies — data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism — address these and combine into "3D parallelism"; sequence/context and expert parallelism add two more axes, and sharded data parallelism (ZeRO/FSDP) removes data parallelism's memory redundancy.

Six axes you can split along

A training step is a computation over a four-dimensional object: a batch of B sequences, each N tokens long, pushed through L layers whose weight matrices are h wide. Every parallelism scheme picks one of those dimensions (or the optimiser state that rides alongside the weights) and cuts it across devices. What you cut decides what you must communicate, how often, and whether that communication can hide behind arithmetic.

22 Pitfalls

Most distributed-training bugs do not crash. They make the run slower than it should be, or quietly train a different model.

Correctness

  • Per-rank loss normalisation. Dividing by each rank's own token count and then averaging across ranks weights ranks, not tokens (§03). Normalise by the global count.
  • Forgetting sampler.set_epoch. Every epoch then repeats the same shuffle on every rank.
  • Different random seeds where they must match (initialisation under TP must be consistent across the group, and dropout inside TP regions must differ across ranks in the right places), or the same seed where they must differ (data order across DP ranks).
  • Splitting a fused weight with a plain shard. A gate_up matrix split into contiguous halves gives one rank all of the gate. The shapes are right, so nothing fails (§20).
  • Mismatched DeepSpeed and Trainer values (batch size, accumulation) when not set to "auto". Training continues with the wrong ones.
  • Loading the model before ZeRO-3 is configured. Every rank materialises the full model first and no memory is saved.

Performance

  • Accumulation without no_sync: one all-reduce per micro-batch instead of per step (§09).
  • Tensor parallelism across nodes: the §11 ratio exceeds 1 on InfiniBand for realistic widths.
  • Wrapping the whole model as one FSDP unit: the gathered copy is the whole model and the saving disappears (§05).
  • Too few micro-batches per pipeline after raising DP at a fixed global batch: the bubble grows as (p−1)/(m+p−1) (§15).
  • Contiguous context-parallel chunks with a causal mask: utilisation tends to 50% (§13).
  • Offload as a first resort: PCIe and DRAM bandwidth are an order of magnitude below NVLink (§06).
  • Tiny buckets on large rings: the latency term dominates (§08).
Takeaway: write down the expected communication-to-compute ratio of each axis before launching. A measured step time that disagrees with it by more than the overlap you expect is almost always one of the bugs above.

24 What to remember

Ten statements, each backed by a section above.

  • Data parallel replicates the model, splits the batch and all-reduces gradients. It is exact large-batch training, and its traffic hides behind compute only while each GPU has enough tokens.
  • Training costs about 16 bytes per parameter before activations. A 7 B model does not fit plain DDP on an 80 GB GPU.
  • ZeRO-1/2 are free in bandwidth; ZeRO-3/FSDP costs 1.5× and puts gathers on every layer's critical path, but divides everything by N.
  • All-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather, about 2S per rank whatever n is; at scale the latency term, not the bytes, is what breaks flat rings.
  • Tensor parallelism splits matrices column-then-row with two all-reduces per block each way. Its cost ratio does not improve with more tokens, so it lives inside the NVLink domain.
  • Sequence parallelism removes the activations TP leaves replicated, at no extra bandwidth. Selective recompute removes the s² term.
  • Context parallelism splits attention itself: a ring of K/V blocks merged with online softmax, or a head all-to-all. Causal masks need a balanced layout.
  • Pipeline parallelism is cheap in bandwidth and pays a (p−1)/(m+p−1) bubble. 1F1B bounds memory, interleaving and zero-bubble shrink the bubble.
  • Expert parallelism costs two all-to-alls per MoE layer each way, and routing skew is a systems problem, capped by capacity.
  • Compose by bandwidth: [TP, CP, PP, DP] from the fastest link to the slowest, and re-choose the degrees when the GPU count changes at a fixed batch. Scales up Neural Network Training, and is essential for LLMs.
