Deep Learning · adaptation

Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning

Don't train from scratch — start from a model that already learned useful representations on a large dataset, and adapt it to your task with far less data and compute. This page covers why that works (features become less general with depth), the two basic moves (feature extraction vs fine-tuning), a decision grid for how much to unfreeze given your data size and domain similarity, the concrete recipe (freeze, train the head, unfreeze the top with a low LR — and the BatchNorm trap), discriminative learning rates and gradual unfreezing, catastrophic forgetting and its mitigations (L2-SP, EWC, replay), domain adaptation, the NLP path from ULMFiT to BERT to parameter-efficient methods, linear probing vs full fine-tuning (LP-FT), and the evaluation pitfalls that make transfer results look better than they are.

01 Why transfer learning works

A network trained on a large, diverse source task has already solved the expensive part of most related problems: turning raw pixels or tokens into a representation in which the answer is easy to read off.

Formally, transfer learning uses what was learned under a source distribution P_S (and task) to improve generalisation under a target distribution P_T. It works when many of the factors of variation that explain P_S also explain P_T: edges, textures, shapes, lighting and viewpoint for images; morphology, syntax, coreference and world facts for text. A representation that disentangles those factors lets a small labelled target set fit only what is genuinely new.

If the source features are irrelevant — or the target data is huge — the benefit shrinks toward "a good initialisation" (§04). Unrelated sources can even hurt: negative transfer.

Two further regularities matter. First, better source models transfer better: across many architectures, ImageNet accuracy correlates strongly with accuracy after fine-tuning on other image datasets (Kornblith, Shlens & Le 2019). Second, transfer works best for deep networks with rich hierarchical features; reusing the lower layers of a small dense network gives little, because its layers are not general in the first place.

One sentence: reuse a pretrained model's representations, adapt only the parts that need to change for your task, and change them gently enough not to destroy what made them worth reusing.

02 Feature generality by layer

Lower layers learn features that almost every visual task needs; upper layers learn features specific to the source task's labels. That gradient of generality is what decides which layers to reuse.

Depth in a CNNWhat units respond to (from visualisation studies)Transferability
Layer 1oriented edges, colour blobs, Gabor-like filters — learned by nearly every image network, whatever its taskalmost universal
Layers 2–3corners, textures, repeated patterns, simple shapeshigh
Middleobject parts: eyes, wheels, text strokes, furmedium; depends on domain
Topclass-specific compositions ("dog face", "sunset") and the source label spacelow; replace the output layer always

Yosinski et al. (2014) measured this directly by splitting ImageNet's 1000 classes into two 500-class halves A and B, training a network on each, and transplanting the first n layers of the A-network into a fresh network trained on B:

  • Frozen transplanted layers (AnB): performance on B drops as n grows — mildly for layers 1–3, sharply for layers 6–7 — because upper features are specific to A.
  • A second, subtler cause: even transplanting B's own layers back into a B network and freezing them (BnB) hurt in the middle (layers 3–5). Neighbouring layers co-adapt; splitting them and retraining only one side cannot recover the joint solution. Fine-tuning fixes this.
  • Fine-tuned transplants (AnB⁺) beat training from scratch — the boost from pretraining persisted even after full fine-tuning on the target.
  • Distance matters: when A and B were split as man-made vs natural objects, transfer degraded faster with depth. Yet even features from a distant task beat random weights.

The same pattern holds in language models: lower Transformer layers encode more surface and syntactic information, upper layers more task- and objective-specific information — which is why NLP fine-tuning also gives the top layers the most freedom (§07). The mirror case exists too: if the output semantics are shared but inputs differ (speech from different speakers, sensors of different types), it can make more sense to share the upper layers and give each input domain its own lower layers.

03 Feature extraction vs fine-tuning

A spectrum, from "train only a new head on frozen features" to "update every weight". Where you should sit depends on how much target data you have and how far it is from the source.

The trade-off is bias vs variance. Freezing more layers is a strong prior: "the source features are right". Unfreezing more is more capacity to fix wrong features — but capacity that must be paid for with data, or it overfits and forgets. What the model encodes, and what real experiments show: with a small target set the curve peaks early (feature extraction or one block) and then falls as trainable parameters overfit; with a large set it keeps rising almost to full fine-tuning (for a very similar domain the gain saturates after a few blocks, so partial fine-tuning is as good and cheaper). 04 Which layers to freeze: data size × domain similarity

Two questions decide almost everything: how much labelled target data do you have, and how similar is it to what the model was pretrained on?

Similar domain (e.g. ImageNet → pets, products)Different domain (e.g. ImageNet → X-rays, satellite, spectrograms)

Top-layer features are wrong and there is little data to fix them. Two nuances the grid hides. "Large data" is relative to model size: 50,000 images is a lot for a ResNet-18 head and not much for a ViT-L. And "similar" is empirical: the cheapest similarity test is to train a linear probe on frozen features and compare it with a small fine-tune — a big gap means the features need to move. With very large target data, pretraining mainly buys faster convergence: He, Girshick & Dollár (2019) found that on COCO detection, randomly initialised models trained long enough matched ImageNet-pretrained ones.

05 The recipe: freeze, train the head, unfreeze the top with a low LR

The order matters. A randomly initialised head produces large, essentially random gradients; if the backbone is trainable at that moment, those gradients flow back and wreck the pretrained features before the head has learned anything.

  1. Load a pretrained backbone without its top; add a new head (global pooling → dropout → dense for the target classes).
  2. Freeze the backbone and train the head to convergence (a few epochs; normal LR such as 1e-3 with Adam).
  3. Unfreeze the top block(s) — keep BatchNorm layers in inference mode (§06).
  4. Recompile / rebuild the optimiser with a much lower LR (10–100× lower, e.g. 1e-5 to 1e-4) and train jointly, ideally with layer-wise LRs (§07). Monitor validation loss; stop early.
# Keras base = keras.applications.ResNet50(weights="imagenet", include_top=False, input_shape=(224, 224, 3)) base.trainable = False # 2. freeze inputs = keras.Input((224, 224, 3)) x = keras.applications.resnet50.preprocess_input(inputs) # the SAME preprocessing as pretraining x = base(x, training=False) # BN stays in inference mode, even after unfreezing x = layers.GlobalAveragePooling2D()(x); x = layers.Dropout(0.3)(x) outputs = layers.Dense(n_classes, activation="softmax")(x) model = keras.Model(inputs, outputs) model.compile(keras.optimizers.Adam(1e-3), "sparse_categorical_crossentropy", ["accuracy"]) model.fit(train_ds, validation_data=val_ds, epochs=5) # train the head base.trainable = True # 3. unfreeze the top … for layer in base.layers[:-30]: layer.trainable = False # … keep the rest frozen model.compile(keras.optimizers.Adam(1e-5), "sparse_categorical_crossentropy", ["accuracy"]) # 4. RECOMPILE, low LR model.fit(train_ds, validation_data=val_ds, epochs=10, callbacks=[keras.callbacks.EarlyStopping(patience=3, restore_best_weights=True)])

  • Recompile after changing trainable (Keras): the flags are read when the training step is built. In PyTorch, the optimiser must actually contain the newly unfrozen parameters.
  • Use the model's own preprocessing (mean/std normalisation, channel order, input resolution, tokenizer). Mismatched preprocessing silently costs accuracy.
  • Data augmentation matters more with small targets. If you precompute frozen features to save time, you give up augmentation; running the frozen backbone in the loop keeps it.
  • A common library default for images: 1 epoch head-only, then unfreeze everything and train with LRs spread geometrically from lr/100 (first layers) to lr (head), under a one-cycle schedule.

06 BatchNorm when frozen: the trap that ruins fine-tunes

BatchNorm has two kinds of state: learnable (γ, β) and non-learnable running statistics (μ, σ²). "Freezing" the weights does not freeze the statistics unless the layer is in inference mode.

training modeŷ = γ · (x − μ_batch) / √(σ²_batch + ε) + β and μ_run ← (1 − m)·μ_run + m·μ_batch inference modeŷ = γ · (x − μ_run) / √(σ²_run + ε) + β statistics fixed

If a "frozen" backbone runs its BN layers in training mode during fine-tuning, three things go wrong at once: (1) activations are normalised with the small-batch statistics of the new data, which are noisy and different from what the downstream layers were trained to expect; (2) the running statistics are overwritten by target-data estimates, so the model evaluated later is not the one validated during training; (3) when the backbone is later unfrozen, the large shift in normalised activations produces large updates that destroy learned features. The symptom is characteristic: training accuracy fine, validation accuracy far worse — or a sharp drop right after unfreezing.

  • Keras (TF 2.x): setting trainable = False on a BatchNormalization layer makes it run in inference mode. Calling the base model with training=False keeps it so even after you set base.trainable = True — the recommended pattern.
  • PyTorch: requires_grad = False does not affect running statistics; you must call .eval() on the BN modules, and model.train() resets them, so re-apply after every call.
  • Exception — deliberate re-estimation: when the target domain differs strongly (different sensor, stain, lighting), recomputing BN statistics on target data with the weights fixed ("AdaBN", Li et al. 2016) is a cheap and effective domain-adaptation step (§10). Do it on purpose, with large batches, not by accident.
  • LayerNorm / RMSNorm (Transformers) have no running statistics, which is one reason Transformer fine-tuning avoids this whole class of bugs.

07 Discriminative learning rates and gradual unfreezing

If lower layers are more general (§02), they should move less. Instead of one learning rate, give each layer group its own — smallest at the bottom, largest for the new head — and bring layers into training one group at a time.

discriminative fine-tuning (Howard & Ruder 2018, ULMFiT)θ_t^{(l)} = θ_{t−1}^{(l)} − η^{(l)} · ∇_{θ^{(l)}} J(θ) one LR per layer group l η^{(l−1)} = η^{(l)} / 2.6 ⇒ η^{(l)} = η_top / 2.6^{(top − l)} slanted triangular LR (STLR), per stagecut = ⌊T · cut_frac⌋ p = t / cut if t < cut p = 1 − (t − cut) / (cut · (1/cut_frac − 1)) otherwise η_t = η_max · (1 + p·(ratio − 1)) / ratio cut_frac = 0.1, ratio = 32 gradual unfreezingepoch 1: train the last group only; epoch 2: last two groups; … until all groups are trainable

Worked numbers: with the top group at η = 1e-3 and a factor 2.6, a 4-group model trains its groups at 1e-3, 3.8e-4, 1.5e-4 and 5.7e-5 — the bottom moves 17.6× more slowly than the top. A geometric spread like "1e-6 to 1e-4" over all layer groups is the same idea written as endpoints. STLR warms up quickly (the first 10% of steps) so the model gets to a good region fast, then decays slowly to refine. Gradual unfreezing protects the general lower layers until the head and top layers have stopped producing large, random gradients — and was the single most important ingredient for ULMFiT's text classifiers; in vision, unfreezing all at once after the head-only phase usually works as well.

Per-layer-group learning rate over training: discriminative factor × schedule × unfreezing rows = layer groups (head at the top), columns = training steps; colour = log₁₀ LR, grey = frozen; right bars = each group's peak LR

Set the factor to 1 and you get a single global LR: the whole backbone then moves as fast as the head. Set unfreezing to "all at once" with a high head LR and imagine the random head's gradients reaching the grey rows on step one — that is exactly what the head-first phase exists to prevent. Newer variants of the same idea: layer-wise LR decay (LLRD) in BERT/ViT fine-tuning (e.g. factor 0.65–0.95 per Transformer layer), and re-initialising the top 1–3 layers of a pretrained Transformer before few-sample fine-tuning (their pretraining-specific features can hurt).

08 Catastrophic forgetting and its mitigations

Gradient descent on the target task has no reason to preserve performance on the source. Unconstrained, it will move weights wherever the target loss prefers — including out of the region that made the source features good.

Catastrophic forgetting (McCloskey & Cohen 1989) is abrupt loss of earlier abilities when a network is trained on something new. In transfer learning it matters two ways: directly, when you want the model to keep its general abilities (an LLM that should still chat after domain tuning); and indirectly, because the general features are what provide robustness and out-of-distribution performance on the target task itself (§09).

L2-SP — decay toward the pretrained point, not toward zero (Li, Grandvalet & Davoine 2018)Ω(w) = (α/2)·‖w_S − w_S⁰‖² + (β/2)·‖w_new‖² w_S = transferred weights, w_new = new head EWC — weight each parameter by how much the source task needs it (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017)L(θ) = L_T(θ) + ∑ᵢ (λ/2)·Fᵢ·(θᵢ − θ*_{S,i})² Fᵢ = diagonal Fisher information on the source task Fᵢ = E[(∂ log p(y|x; θ)/∂θᵢ)²] ≈ curvature of the source loss along θᵢ

Plain weight decay pulls weights toward zero — the wrong anchor for a pretrained model; it actively erases pretraining. L2-SP pulls toward the starting point, uniformly. EWC is L2-SP with a per-parameter stiffness: parameters the source task is sensitive to (high Fisher) are pinned; parameters it does not care about are free. The toy below makes the geometry visible.

Fine-tuning on a 2-parameter toy: source loss (blue contours) vs target loss (orange), four regularisers every path starts at the source optimum θ*_S and runs gradient descent on L_T + penalty; the endpoint readout reports both losses

Read the toy: the source loss is a narrow valley — steep in one direction, flat in the other. Unregularised fine-tuning walks straight to the target optimum and pays a large source loss. L2-SP stops part-way along the straight line. EWC slides along the source valley, where the source loss barely changes, and reaches a far better compromise. Weight decay toward zero drifts toward the origin — away from both. Fewer steps or a lower LR is a crude but real mitigation too: forgetting is partly a matter of how far you walk.

09 Linear probing vs full fine-tuning, and LP-FT

Full fine-tuning usually wins in-distribution. It can lose out-of-distribution — because a random head makes the early updates distort the pretrained features.

Kumar et al. (2022) analysed the two extremes. A linear probe (frozen features, trained linear head) keeps the pretrained features intact; full fine-tuning adapts them. With good pretrained features, full fine-tuning gave higher in-distribution (ID) accuracy but often lower out-of-distribution (OOD) accuracy than linear probing. Their explanation: at the start of fine-tuning the head is random, so the gradient reaching the backbone is large and points in directions determined by the head's errors. The features change only in the subspace spanned by the training data; features for inputs outside that subspace — the OOD ones — are left inconsistent with the changed head. The fix is the recipe in §05, given a name:

LP-FTstage 1 (LP): freeze h_θ, train the head g_φ to convergence → φ* stage 2 (FT): initialise the head at φ*, fine-tune everything with a small LR

Starting FT with a good head means the initial loss and gradients are small, so features move less and more uniformly. Kumar et al. report LP-FT beating both extremes — on average about 10% better OOD and 1% better ID accuracy than full fine-tuning across their distribution-shift benchmarks. The practical rule: never start full fine-tuning with a random head, and always report the linear-probe number as a baseline — if it is close to fine-tuning, the simpler, more robust model may be the better choice.

10 Domain adaptation basics

Transfer learning changes the task; domain adaptation keeps the task and changes the input distribution — the same sentiment classifier applied to electronics reviews instead of book reviews, the same detector on a new camera.

ShiftWhat changesClassic remedy
Covariate shiftP(x) changes, P(y|x) fixedimportance weighting: train with weights w(x) = p_T(x)/p_S(x), estimated by a domain classifier
Label shiftP(y) changes, P(x|y) fixedre-estimate class priors on target, re-weight or re-calibrate outputs
Concept shift / driftP(y|x) itself changesneeds new target labels; monitor and retrain
why aligning representations helps (Ben-David et al. 2010)ε_T(h) ≤ ε_S(h) + ½·d_{HΔH}(D_S, D_T) + λ* target error ≤ source error + domain distinguishability + best joint error
  • Unsupervised domain adaptation (labelled source, unlabelled target) attacks the middle term: learn features in which source and target are indistinguishable. DANN (Ganin & Lempitsky 2015) trains a domain classifier on the features through a gradient-reversal layer — the feature extractor is pushed to fool it while still solving the source task. MMD / CORAL losses match feature statistics directly.
  • AdaBN: recompute BatchNorm statistics on target data (§06) — no training, often a large gain for sensor/lighting shifts. Test-time adaptation methods extend this at inference.
  • Self-training: pseudo-label confident target examples and retrain; works well when the source model is already decent on target.
  • Continued pretraining on unlabelled in-domain data before fine-tuning — domain-adaptive and task-adaptive pretraining (Gururangan et al. 2020) gave consistent gains for biomedical, CS, news and review text. The vision analogue is self-supervised pretraining on in-domain images (Contrastive Learning).

11 Transfer in NLP: from ULMFiT to BERT fine-tuning

Vision had ImageNet-pretrained backbones since 2014; NLP mostly transferred only word embeddings until 2018, when pretrained language models made whole-network transfer standard.

  1. ULMFiT (2018) — three stages: pretrain an LSTM language model on a general corpus; fine-tune the LM on the target domain's text (unlabelled, with discriminative LRs and STLR); then add a classifier and fine-tune with gradual unfreezing. With 100 labelled examples it matched training from scratch on 100× more data.
  2. BERT (2019) — pretrain a bidirectional Transformer encoder with masked language modelling on a huge corpus (BERT). Fine-tune by adding one small layer: a classifier on the [CLS] vector for classification, per-token classifiers for tagging (NER), start/end span pointers for extractive QA — and train all parameters for a few epochs.
BERT fine-tuning hyperparameterTypical rangeWhy
Learning rate2e-5 … 5e-5 (AdamW)the pretrained weights should move a little; 1e-3 destroys them

Epochs2 – 4enough to fit the head and adapt; more tends to overfit on mid-size data
Warmup≈ 6–10% of steps, then linear decayavoids early large updates from the random head
Weight decay0.01 (not on biases / LayerNorm)

Known issues with the recipe: on small datasets fine-tuning is unstable — some random seeds fail completely, and seed alone (head initialisation and data order) can move results by several points (Dodge et al. 2020). Mosbach et al. (2021) traced much of the instability to optimisation, not to catastrophic forgetting: an Adam variant without bias correction, too few steps, too high LR. Their fix is simple — use bias-corrected AdamW, a small LR with warmup, and train for more iterations. Other remedies: re-initialise the top layers, layer-wise LR decay, and report mean ± sd over several seeds. For generative (decoder) models the same ideas apply, but full fine-tuning is expensive enough that parameter-efficient methods dominate (§12). See also Encoder vs Decoder Models.

12 From full fine-tuning to parameter-efficient fine-tuning

At LLM scale, "one full model copy per task" and "optimiser state for every weight" are the dominant costs. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning freezes the base and trains a tiny add-on.

LoRA — a low-rank residual branch on a frozen weight (Hu et al. 2021)h = W₀x + ΔW x = W₀x + (α/r)·B A x W₀ ∈ ℝ^{d×k} frozen B ∈ ℝ^{d×r}, A ∈ ℝ^{r×k}, r ≪ min(d, k) trainable per adapted matrix: r·(d + k) worked number — BERT-base, r = 8 on W_q and W_v in all 12 layers12 layers × 2 matrices × 8 × (768 + 768) = 294,912 trainable weights ≈ 0.27% of 110M
  • Adapters (Houlsby et al. 2019): small bottleneck MLPs inserted into each layer (a residual branch again); ~3.6% of parameters per task came within 0.4% of full fine-tuning on GLUE. BitFit trains only the biases. Prompt / prefix tuning learns input vectors.
  • Why forgetting is contained: the base weights never change, so removing the add-on recovers the original model exactly; the add-on can still override behaviour while attached.
  • Where it fits in the grid: PEFT sits between feature extraction and full fine-tuning — it can change every layer's function, but only within a low-dimensional subspace, which is itself a strong regulariser on small data.

13 Evaluation pitfalls

Transfer results are easy to overstate. Most mistakes come from comparing unequal things or from leakage between pretraining, training and test data.

Leakage through the pretraining data: the test images or benchmark questions may be in the pretraining corpus (near-duplicate images across ImageNet/CIFAR splits; benchmark text on the web for LLMs). Fix: de-duplicate against the pretraining set where possible; prefer fresh or held-out test sets; say what you checked.
Group leakage in small target sets: the same patient, product, speaker or document appears in both train and validation splits. Fix: split by group; with tiny data, use grouped k-fold cross-validation.
Tuning on the test set: choosing how many layers to unfreeze, the LR or the epoch count by test accuracy. Fix: a separate validation split for all such choices; touch the test set once.
Unequal baselines: a carefully tuned fine-tune compared against a from-scratch model trained with the fine-tuning hyperparameters. Fix: give each arm its own tuning budget and schedule; include the linear-probe baseline.
One seed: reporting a single fine-tuning run, when small-data fine-tunes vary by several points across seeds. Fix: mean ± sd over ≥ 3–5 seeds; report failed runs.
In-distribution only: claiming robustness from ID accuracy. Fix: evaluate on a shifted split too — this is where LP vs FT vs LP-FT differ (§09).
Train/eval mode bugs: BN or dropout left in training mode at evaluation, or preprocessing different between training and serving. Fix: model.eval() / training=False; one shared preprocessing function.
Source-task regressions unmeasured: when the base's general abilities matter, not checking them after tuning. Fix: keep a small source/general evaluation suite and track it alongside the target metric.

For the general principles of validation design, see Model Evaluation.

14 Takeaways

  • Transfer works because lower-layer features are general; upper layers are source-specific — replace the head, reuse the rest.
  • Spectrum: feature extraction → partial → full fine-tuning; choose by data size × domain similarity.
  • Recipe: freeze, train the head, then unfreeze the top with a 10–100× lower LR; recompile; stop early.
  • BatchNorm: keep frozen BN in inference mode (unless deliberately re-estimating statistics for a new domain).
  • Discriminative LRs (÷2.6 per group), STLR and gradual unfreezing protect general layers.
  • Forgetting: anchor to the pretrained weights (L2-SP, EWC), replay source data, interpolate weights, or use PEFT — never plain decay toward zero.
  • LP-FT: never start full fine-tuning with a random head; report the linear-probe baseline and an OOD number.
  • Evaluate honestly: leakage, seeds, equal baselines.
  • Builds on Neural Network Training and CNNs; specialised form is PEFT; related: Distillation, Residual Connections (the backbones people fine-tune).
