Knowledge Distillation

Train a small, fast student to imitate a large, accurate teacher. The supervision is not only the correct answer. It is the teacher's whole probability distribution: the tiny probabilities it gives to the wrong classes carry the similarity structure it learned, and that dark knowledge is worth far more per example than a one-hot label. This page follows the original study closely. It covers the temperature softmax and the two-term objective, the derivation showing that logit matching is the high-temperature limit, the MNIST experiments including a digit the student never sees, distilling a 10-model speech ensemble into one model, specialist models on a 15,000-class image set, and soft targets as a regulariser when data is scarce.

01 The idea: training and deployment want different models

The model that is best to train is rarely the model that is best to deploy. Training can afford huge models, ensembles and heavy regularisation, because it runs once on as much compute as you can find. Deployment runs millions of times under latency and memory limits. Distillation is the bridge between the two: first train the cumbersome model, then transfer what it knows into a small one.

  • The larva analogy. Many insects have one larval form built for extracting energy from the environment and a completely different adult form built for travel and reproduction. The original study argues that machine learning should copy this split rather than use one model for both jobs.
  • What "knowledge" is. It is tempting to identify a network's knowledge with its weights, which makes it hard to imagine changing the architecture and keeping the knowledge. The reframing that makes distillation possible is that the knowledge is the learned mapping from inputs to outputs. Any model that reproduces the mapping has the knowledge, whatever its parameters are.
  • Why the outputs, specifically. The objective we really care about is generalisation, but a model trained the ordinary way is only optimised to fit the training labels. A large model that generalises well, for example because it is an average of many models, can show the small model how to generalise. It does this by giving its full output distribution as the target, not just the argmax.
One sentence: a small student learns to match a large teacher's softened output distribution, and inherits much of its generalisation at a fraction of the cost.

02 Dark knowledge, and the temperature that reveals it

A hard label says "cat". The teacher's distribution says "mostly cat, a little dog, a little fox, definitely not car". That relative structure is the signal, and at the ordinary temperature it is almost invisible.

The original study's MNIST example makes the point. One image of a 2 might get probability 10⁻⁶ of being a 3 and 10⁻⁹ of being a 7, while another 2 gets the reverse. Those ratios say which 2s look like 3s and which look like 7s, which is real information about the data. But the probabilities are so close to zero that they barely affect a cross-entropy loss. The fix is to raise the temperature of the final softmax until the teacher produces a soft enough distribution, and to train the student at that same temperature.

softmax at temperature T (z = logits)qᵢ = exp(zᵢ / T) / ∑ⱼ exp(zⱼ / T) T = 1 the ordinary softmax T > 1 a softer distribution: the ratios between small probabilities become visible T → ∞ uniform; T → 0 one-hot on the argmax
Softening the teacher's logits with temperature T Higher T reveals the small probabilities (dark knowledge) the student learns from

Temperature never changes the ranking of the classes. It changes how much the non-top classes contribute to the loss. On the receiving side the same knob is used at test time for a different purpose, calibration: see the temperature-scaling discussion on Image Classification.

03 The objective: two cross-entropies, and the T² that balances them

The student is trained on a transfer set. For each case the target is the teacher's distribution at a high temperature T, and the student's softmax uses the same T during training. After training, the student runs at T = 1.

the distillation objective (p = teacher soft targets at T, q = student at T)soft term C_soft = − ∑ᵢ pᵢ(T) · log qᵢ(T) = KL(p ‖ q) + H(p) (H(p) is constant in the student) hard term C_hard = − log q_y(1) the true label y, student at T = 1 L = w · T² · C_soft + (1 − w) · C_hard the study's advice: a considerably lower weight on C_hard
  • The transfer set can be unlabelled data or the original training set. If labels are available, the hard term helps: the teacher is not perfect, and the true label corrects it. The study reports its best results with a considerably lower weight on the hard term.
  • Why T². The soft-target gradients scale as 1/T² (derived in §04). Without a correction, raising the temperature would quietly shrink the soft term relative to the hard one. Multiplying the soft term by T² keeps the relative contribution of the two terms roughly unchanged when you try different temperatures. This is the T² that is often left out of reimplementations.
  • The direction of the divergence. The soft term is a cross-entropy with the teacher as the target, so it equals KL(teacher ‖ student) up to a constant. It is not KL(student ‖ teacher). The forward direction is the one that makes the student cover every class the teacher puts mass on, which is the point of the exercise.

04 Logit matching is the high-temperature limit

Earlier model-compression work trained the small model to match the big model's logits with a squared error. The original study shows that this is a special case of distillation. Raise T far enough and the soft-target gradient becomes exactly a logit-matching gradient.

the derivation (z = student logits, v = teacher logits, N classes)soft-term gradient on each student logit: ∂C/∂zᵢ = (1/T) · (qᵢ − pᵢ) = (1/T) · ( e^(zᵢ/T) / ∑ⱼ e^(zⱼ/T) − e^(vᵢ/T) / ∑ⱼ e^(vⱼ/T) ) if T is large compared with the logits, e^(x/T) ≈ 1 + x/T: ∂C/∂zᵢ ≈ (1/T) · ( (1 + zᵢ/T) / (N + ∑ⱼ zⱼ/T) − (1 + vᵢ/T) / (N + ∑ⱼ vⱼ/T) ) if, in addition, the logits are zero-mean for each case (∑ⱼ zⱼ = ∑ⱼ vⱼ = 0): ∂C/∂zᵢ ≈ (1 / (N·T²)) · (zᵢ − vᵢ) which is the gradient of (1/(N·T²)) · ½ (zᵢ − vᵢ)² → logit matching, with a 1/T² factor.

Two things come out of the last line. First, the 1/T² is the reason for §03's T² correction. Second, distillation at high temperature is squared-error logit matching. At lower temperatures it differs in a specific way: it pays much less attention to matching logits that are much more negative than average.

Is ignoring very negative logits good or bad? Those logits are almost unconstrained by the teacher's training loss, so they may be noise, which argues for a lower temperature. They may also carry useful knowledge, which argues for a higher one. The study found that when the student is too small to capture all of the teacher's knowledge, intermediate temperatures work best. Its MNIST students with 300 or more units per hidden layer gave fairly similar results for every temperature above 8. Students cut down to 30 units per layer did significantly better at T = 2.5–4 than at higher or lower values.

The exact soft-target gradient against the logit-matching approximation Drag T; both are scaled by T² so they can be compared at any temperature

The figure is computed live from fixed, zero-mean teacher and student logits (not data from the study). Solid bars are the exact gradient T²·∂C/∂zᵢ = T·(qᵢ − pᵢ). Outlined bars are the approximation (zᵢ − vᵢ)/N. At T = 1 they disagree badly: the exact gradient is concentrated on the classes with large logits, and the very negative logits get almost nothing. As T grows the two converge. Raising the logit spread makes the agreement need a larger T, because the expansion needs T to be large compared with the logits.

05 MNIST: what transfers, including a digit the student never saw

The cleanest experiments in the study. A large, heavily regularised teacher, an unregularised small student, and then two ablations of the transfer set that test how much knowledge travels through soft targets alone.

Model (all trained on the 60,000 MNIST training cases)Test errors (of 10,000)
Teacher: 2 hidden layers × 1200 ReLU, dropout, inputs jittered by up to two pixels67
Student, hard labels only: 2 hidden layers × 800 ReLU, no regularisation146
Same student, plus matching the teacher's soft targets at T = 20 (the only added regulariser)74

The student recovers most of the gap to the teacher, 146 → 74 against the teacher's 67, without dropout and without ever seeing a jittered image. The study's reading is that soft targets can transfer a great deal of knowledge to the distilled model, including how to generalise, which the teacher learned from the translated training data.

The striking result is the first row. Most of the errors come from the learned bias for class 3 being far too low, because the student never had a positive example. With that single number corrected, the student classifies 98.6% of test 3s correctly having never been trained on one. It learned what a 3 is from the teacher's opinion that some 5s and 8s look slightly like 3s. One honest caveat: the study chose both bias shifts to optimise test performance, so the "after" column is an upper bound on what a deployed fix would get, not a clean held-out result.

06 Speech recognition: distilling a 10-model ensemble into one model

The practical case for distillation. An ensemble is reliably better than its members and too expensive to serve. Can one network of the same size as a member keep the ensemble's gain?

  • The acoustic model. A DNN with 8 hidden layers of 2560 ReLU units and a softmax over 14,000 tied HMM states, about 85M parameters. Its input is 26 frames of 40 Mel-scaled filterbank coefficients (10 ms per frame), and it predicts the state of the 21st frame.
  • The data. About 2000 hours of spoken English, roughly 700M training examples.
  • The ensemble. 10 models of the same architecture with different random initialisations. For distillation the study tried T ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10} and used a relative weight of 0.5 on the hard-target cross-entropy; T = 2 worked best.
SystemTest frame accuracyWord error rate
Baseline (one model)58.9%10.9%
10× ensemble61.1%10.7%
Distilled single model60.8%10.7%

The ensemble gains 2.2 points of frame accuracy over a single model and the distilled model keeps 1.9 of them. That is about 86% of the improvement (our arithmetic; the study says "more than 80%"). The word error rate gain is matched completely, at one tenth of the serving cost. Where these models sit in a recogniser is covered in Speech Processing.

07 Specialists: an ensemble you can afford to train

Distilling an ensemble assumes you can train the ensemble. On a large enough dataset you cannot: the study's generalist image model on JFT, an internal set of 100M labelled images over 15,000 classes, had been trained for about six months with asynchronous SGD on many cores. The proposed alternative is one generalist plus many cheap specialists, each trained only on a group of classes the generalist confuses.

  • Finding the confusable groups. Cluster the columns of the covariance matrix of the generalist's predictions with online K-means, so that classes the generalist often predicts together land in the same cluster. True labels are not needed.
  • Training a specialist. Each covers its subset of classes plus a single "dustbin" class for everything else. It starts from the generalist's weights and is trained on data that is half from its subset and half random. Afterwards the oversampling is corrected by raising the dustbin logit by the log of the oversampling factor.
  • Inference. For each test image take the generalist's top class (the study used n = 1). Activate every specialist whose subset contains it. Then find a full distribution q that agrees with all of them:
combining the generalist g with the active specialists Aₖq* = argmin_q KL(p^g ‖ q) + ∑_{m ∈ Aₖ} KL(p^m ‖ q) p^m is over the specialist's classes plus its dustbin; q's probabilities are summed into the same coarse classes before comparing. q = softmax(z), z found by gradient descent per image.

The second table carries the argument. The gain rises fairly steadily with the number of specialists that cover the correct class, so more, overlapping specialists keep helping. Specialists also train independently and quickly, in parallel, because none of them depends on another. Two limits are worth stating plainly. The specialists here are an ensemble and were not distilled back into a single model in the study, and the absolute accuracy (26.1% top-1 on 15,000 classes) is low, so the 4.4% is a relative gain on a hard problem.

08 Soft targets as a regulariser: 3% of the data

A hard target carries at most log₂ K bits per case, and often far fewer. A soft target constrains the whole output vector. If that really is much more information, a model trained on soft targets should overfit much less when data is scarce. The study tested this directly on the speech model.

Speech modelTrain frame accuracyTest frame accuracy
Baseline, 100% of training set63.4%58.9%
Baseline, 3% of training set (hard targets)67.3%44.5%
Soft targets, 3% of training set65.4%57.0%

With hard targets, 3% of the data overfits badly. Training accuracy goes up and test accuracy falls by 14.4 points, even with early stopping. With soft targets from a model trained on the full set, the same 3% recovers 57.0% test accuracy, within 1.9 points of the full-data baseline (our arithmetic), and needs no early stopping. The study notes that the soft-target run simply converged to 57%. The same logic is offered for specialists, which see little data per class and could be trained with the generalist's soft targets to stop them overfitting.

Compare label smoothing, which also softens the target but with a uniform distribution over the wrong classes. Label smoothing adds the softness without the similarity structure, so it regularises without transferring any knowledge.

09 Specialists against mixtures of experts

Specialists resemble a mixture of experts, since both use many models that each own part of the problem. The study explains why it did not use one.

mixture of experts
Hard to parallelise
The gating network is learned jointly with the experts. Each expert's effective training set depends on what the gate and the other experts are doing, so the experts' training sets keep changing, and the gate must compare experts on every example.
generalist + specialists
Embarrassingly parallel
Train the generalist once. Define the subsets from its confusions. Then train every specialist entirely independently. At test time only the specialists relevant to the generalist's top guess are run.

Modern sparse MoE layers are routed per token inside one network and trained end-to-end, which is a different trade-off (see the MoE page). The point here is narrower: a fixed partition decided in advance makes the parts independent.

10 Flavours of distillation that came after

General knowledge, not from the original study. Its recipe matches the teacher's output distribution. Later work widened what the student matches.

TypeWhat the student matches
Response (logit)The teacher's output distribution, the classic recipe on this page
FeatureIntermediate hidden representations or attention maps, through a learned projection when widths differ
Self-distillationA model teaches a same-size (or later) copy of itself; it often helps even without a size gap
Data / sequenceFor language models: train on teacher-generated sequences (sequence-level) or match per-token distributions (word-level); compact BERT-style students combine soft targets with hidden-state losses

11 Where it's used

  • Shipping small, fast models for on-device or low-latency serving, such as compact BERT-style encoders.
  • Compressing big LLMs into cheaper ones that keep much of their capability, and collapsing ensembles into one model as in §06.
  • One tool in the model acceleration kit, alongside quantization and pruning. Distillation changes the architecture; the other two keep it.

12 Common misconceptions

"Higher temperature is always better: it shows more dark knowledge." Not for small students. High T is logit matching (§04), which also forces the student to fit very negative logits that may be noise. Students too small to absorb everything did best at intermediate T (2.5–4 for 30-unit layers).
"The temperature only matters in the teacher." Both run at T. The student's softmax uses the same T during training and T = 1 afterwards; softening only the teacher's outputs would compare distributions at different scales.
"The T² factor is a cosmetic constant." It sets the balance. The soft gradient shrinks as 1/T², so without T² a temperature sweep is also, unintentionally, a sweep of the hard/soft weighting.
"Distillation needs the original labelled data." Any transfer set works. The soft targets are the supervision. Unlabelled data is fine, and labels only add the optional hard term. §05's student learned the digit 3 from a transfer set containing no 3s.
"It is the same thing as label smoothing." Only the softness is shared. Label smoothing spreads mass uniformly. Soft targets spread it according to the teacher's learned similarities, and that structure is what transfers (§08).

13 Takeaways

  • Train big, deploy small. Knowledge is the input→output mapping, so it can move between architectures.
  • Soft targets at temperature T expose the teacher's similarity structure; teacher and student both use T in training.
  • Loss = T²·CE(teacher_T, student_T) + a lower-weighted CE(label, student₁); the T² cancels the 1/T² in the soft gradient.
  • Logit matching is the high-T limit: ∂C/∂zᵢ ≈ (zᵢ − vᵢ)/(NT²) for zero-mean logits. Intermediate T can be better for small students.
  • Evidence: MNIST 146 → 74 errors (teacher 67); a student that never saw a 3 gets 98.6% of 3s after one bias fix; a single speech model keeps more than 80% of a 10-model ensemble's gain; soft targets on 3% of the data give 57.0% vs 44.5% test frame accuracy.
  • Specialists trained on the generalist's confusable classes are an ensemble you can train in parallel (+4.4% relative top-1 on 15,000 classes), unlike a jointly gated mixture of experts.
  • Distilling several teachers into one student is the training-based way to combine models. The training-free alternative, averaging or adding their weights when they share an initialisation, is Model Merging.
