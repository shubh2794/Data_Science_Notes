Neural Network Training

An architecture is only a hypothesis. Training is the procedure that turns it into a model: a forward pass produces a loss, backpropagation produces gradients, an optimizer nudges the weights, and the whole thing repeats for millions of steps. Almost everything that goes wrong in deep learning goes wrong here — in the learning rate, the initialization, the normalization, or the numerics. This page is the field manual for that loop.

Deep Learning Optimization Fundamentals Recipes

01 The training loop

Four stages, repeated. Everything else on this page is a modification to one of them.

forward → loss → backward (gradients) → optimizer step → repeat
  • Forward. Push a mini-batch of inputs through the network, caching the intermediate activations that the backward pass will need.
  • Loss. Reduce the batch's predictions and targets to one scalar. That scalar is the only thing the optimizer can see — if it does not encode what you care about, nothing downstream can fix it.
  • Backward. Backpropagation computes ∂L/∂θ for every parameter in a single reverse sweep.
  • Update. The optimizer converts the gradient into a step, using its own state (momentum buffers, second-moment estimates), and writes new weights.
empirical risk on a mini-batch BL(θ) = (1/|B|) ∑i ∈ B ℓ( fθ(xᵢ), yᵢ ) one optimization stepgₜ = ∇θ L(θₜ₋₁) θₜ = θₜ₋₁ − η · u(gₜ, state)

Vocabulary that people get wrong

  • A step (or iteration) is one optimizer update, i.e. one mini-batch. An epoch is one full pass over the training set. Large-scale runs are budgeted in steps or tokens, not epochs.
  • The training state is more than the weights: optimizer buffers, the learning-rate schedule position, the data-loader position and RNG seeds all have to be checkpointed for a run to resume identically.
  • The gradient of a mini-batch is an unbiased but noisy estimate of the full-dataset gradient. That noise is not purely a nuisance — it is part of why SGD generalizes.
Takeaway: the loop is trivial; the difficulty is that every stage has a scale. Get the scale of the loss, the gradients, the step size or the numerics wrong and the loop still runs — it just learns nothing.

02 Loss functions

The loss defines what "good" means. Pick it from the geometry of the output: a real number, a probability over classes, a set of independent labels, or a position in an embedding space.

Why cross-entropy trains so cleanly

  • Compose softmax with cross-entropy and the gradient with respect to the logits collapses to ŷ − y: the error signal is simply "how much probability mass sat in the wrong place". No saturating derivative multiplies it away, which is exactly the failure mode of MSE-on-sigmoid.
  • Always compute it from logits, never from probabilities. Libraries fuse the log and the softmax through the log-sum-exp trick, subtracting the max logit before exponentiating so that large logits cannot overflow.
softmax + cross-entropy, gradient w.r.t. logits zŷk = e^{zk} / ∑j e^{zj} ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y numerically safe log-sum-explog ∑j e^{zj} = m + log ∑j e^{zj − m}, m = maxj zj
Takeaway: the loss is the only channel through which your intent reaches the optimizer. If the metric you report and the loss you minimize disagree (accuracy vs cross-entropy, BLEU vs token likelihood), expect the model to optimize the loss and disappoint you on the metric.

03 Backpropagation

Backprop is the chain rule scheduled in the cheapest possible order: one reverse sweep that hands every parameter its gradient.

per-layer gradients (δ = error signal at a layer)δL = ∇aL ⊙ σ′(zL) δℓ = (Wℓ+1)ᵀ δℓ+1 ⊙ σ′(zℓ) ∂L/∂Wℓ = δℓ (aℓ−1)ᵀ ∂L/∂bℓ = δℓ

Why reverse mode, and what it costs

  • Automatic differentiation can traverse the computation graph forwards or backwards. Forward mode costs one sweep per input dimension; reverse mode costs one sweep per output dimension. A loss has exactly one output and millions of parameters, so reverse mode wins by a factor of millions.
  • The backward pass costs roughly 2× the forward pass in FLOPs, so a training step is about 3× an inference pass — the standard rule of thumb behind compute estimates.
  • Each layer contributes a vector-Jacobian product: you never build the Jacobian, you only multiply by it. That is why backprop is memory-heavy but not compute-catastrophic.

The memory bill

  • Every activation needed by a backward rule must be kept alive from the forward pass. Activation memory scales with batch × depth × width and usually dwarfs the parameter memory in large models.
  • Activation checkpointing stores only a sparse set of activations and recomputes the rest during the backward pass — roughly √n memory for ~30% extra compute.
  • Frameworks build the graph automatically, but detach, in-place operations and non-differentiable ops silently cut it. A gradient that is None or zero is almost always a severed graph, not a maths problem.
Takeaway: backprop is exact and cheap; what it is not is free of memory. Most "out of memory" failures are activation storage, and the first lever is batch size, the second is checkpointing.

04 The loss landscape and the learning rate

Training is descent on a surface in millions of dimensions. The learning rate decides how big a step you take on it — and it is the single most consequential number in the run.

Gradient descent on a loss surface x ← x − lr · ∇loss · raise the learning rate until it overshoots

What the surface is actually like

  • In high dimensions, local minima are rarely the obstacle — a critical point is a minimum only if all curvature directions are positive, which becomes vanishingly unlikely. Saddle points and long, flat plateaus dominate, and momentum is what carries you off them.
  • The landscape is ill-conditioned: curvature varies wildly across directions. The largest stable step for gradient descent on a quadratic is η < 2/λmax, while progress along the flattest direction goes as λmin. The ratio λmax/λmin is why plain SGD zig-zags.
  • Deep networks have enormous symmetry — permute units, rescale a ReLU layer up and the next one down — so minima come in connected valleys, not isolated points.
  • Flat minima tend to generalize better than sharp ones, and small batches with a healthy learning rate bias the search towards them. See Optimization for the underlying theory.
stability bound on a quadratic with curvature λθₜ₊₁ = θₜ − η λ θₜ = (1 − ηλ) θₜ converges iff 0 < η < 2/λ
Takeaway: tune the learning rate before you tune anything else. A 3× change in η moves the loss curve more than a redesigned architecture will.

05 Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW

An optimizer is a rule for turning a noisy gradient into a step. All the popular ones add memory of past gradients — the differences are what they remember and how they use it.

SGD and momentum

  • Plain SGD steps straight down the mini-batch gradient. It is unbiased, cheap in memory, and slow in ravines: it oscillates across the steep direction while creeping along the flat one.
  • Momentum keeps an exponentially decayed running average of gradients. Consistent directions accumulate (an effective speed-up of about 1/(1−μ), ≈10× at μ = 0.9), while oscillating directions cancel.
  • Nesterov evaluates the gradient after the momentum jump, giving a slightly better-damped correction.
SGD with momentumvₜ = μ vₜ₋₁ + gₜ θₜ = θₜ₋₁ − η vₜ (typical μ = 0.9)

Adam and the decoupled-decay fix

  • Adam tracks a first moment (direction) and a second moment (per-parameter gradient scale), then divides one by the square root of the other. Parameters with small, rare gradients — embeddings, layer-norm gains — get large effective steps; noisy ones get small steps.
  • The bias correction matters: both moments start at zero, so early estimates are shrunk towards zero and must be divided by 1 − βᵗ. Without it the first few hundred steps take wildly wrong-sized steps.
  • L2 penalty ≠ weight decay under Adam. Adding λθ to the gradient sends it through the same 1/√v̂ division, so parameters with large gradients get less decay — the opposite of the intent. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive rescaling, and is the default for transformers.
Adammₜ = β₁ mₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₁) gₜ vₜ = β₂ vₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₂) gₜ² m̂ₜ = mₜ / (1 − β₁ᵗ) v̂ₜ = vₜ / (1 − β₂ᵗ) θₜ = θₜ₋₁ − η · m̂ₜ / (√v̂ₜ + ε) AdamW — decay decoupled from the adaptive termθₜ = θₜ₋₁ − η ( m̂ₜ / (√v̂ₜ + ε) + λ θₜ₋₁ ) defaultsβ₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 (0.95 for large language models), ε = 10⁻⁸

Gradient clipping

  • Rare batches produce enormous gradients — a duplicated document, a degenerate sample, an unlucky attention pattern. One such step can undo thousands of good ones.
  • Global-norm clipping rescales the whole gradient vector when its norm exceeds a threshold c, preserving direction and only shrinking magnitude. Clip by global norm, not per-tensor, or you distort the relative scale between layers.
  • Typical thresholds are c = 1.0 for transformers and 0.25–5 for recurrent models. Log the pre-clip norm: a rising baseline is an early warning of divergence.
clip by global normg ← g · min(1, c / ‖g‖₂)

06 Learning-rate schedules and finding the LR

A constant learning rate is almost never right. You want a large step while the loss surface is coarse, and a small one while you settle into a basin — plus a gentle start so the optimizer's own statistics can warm up.

Pick a schedule and drag the step position; the readout gives the current rate and the rule that produced it.

Learning-rate schedules over a 1000-step run warmup fraction · schedule shape · where you are in the run

Warmup, decay, and the shapes that matter

  • Warmup ramps the rate from near zero over the first few hundred to few thousand steps. Early on, Adam's second-moment estimate is built from a handful of samples and its variance is huge; a full-size step taken on that estimate is what blows up deep transformers in the first minutes of training.
  • Cosine decay anneals smoothly to (nearly) zero over the planned run length. It has no milestones to tune and is the standard for large models — but it commits you to a horizon, since stopping early leaves the rate high.
  • Step decay cuts the rate by 10× at fixed milestones. Simple, still competitive on convnets, and the source of the characteristic staircase in loss curves.
  • One-cycle ramps up to a peak well above the "safe" rate, then back down, often with momentum moving in the opposite direction. The high-rate phase acts as a regularizer and can cut training time substantially.
  • Inverse square root decays as 1/√t after warmup and is horizon-free, which is why it suits runs of unknown length.
linear warmup then cosine, over T steps with Tw warmupηₜ = ηmax · t / Tw for t ≤ Tw ηₜ = ηmin + ½ (ηmax − ηmin) (1 + cos(π · (t − Tw)/(T − Tw))) for t > Tw inverse square rootηₜ = ηmax · √( Tw / max(t, Tw) )

Finding the peak rate

  • The range test: run a few hundred steps while multiplying the learning rate exponentially each step, and plot loss against rate. The loss falls, bottoms out, then explodes. Pick roughly an order of magnitude below the explosion — often near the steepest descent point, not the minimum.
  • Sanity ranges: 1e-3 to 3e-4 for AdamW on transformers (smaller as models get bigger), 0.1 for SGD+momentum on convnets with batch 256, and 1e-5 to 5e-5 when fine-tuning a pretrained model.
  • Never tune the learning rate against a schedule you will not use at full length — a rate that survives 200 steps of warmup may still diverge at step 5000.
Takeaway: warm up, then decay to near zero by the end of the planned budget. If the loss spikes in the first thousand steps, lengthen warmup before lowering the peak rate.

07 Batch size, memory, and mixed precision

Batch size is a statistics knob disguised as a systems knob. It sets both the gradient's noise level and the hardware's utilization, and the two pull in opposite directions.

What batch size actually changes

  • The gradient's standard error falls as 1/√|B|. Doubling the batch halves the noise only by √2, but doubles the compute per step — so there are diminishing returns, and beyond a critical batch size you buy almost nothing.
  • Small batches inject gradient noise that behaves like a regularizer and biases the search towards flatter minima; very large batches often need extra regularization or longer training to match the same accuracy.
  • The linear scaling rule: when you multiply the batch size by k, multiply the learning rate by k too, so the total distance travelled per example stays constant. It holds well for SGD across a wide range and needs a warmup to survive the first steps. For Adam, a √k scaling is usually the better guess.
gradient noise vs batch sizeVar[ ĝ ] = σ² / |B| SE[ ĝ ] ∝ 1/√|B| linear scaling rule|B| → k·|B| ⟹ η → k·η (with warmup)

Fitting it in memory

  • Gradient accumulation sums gradients over several micro-batches before stepping, reproducing a large batch on small hardware. Remember to divide by the number of micro-batches, or your effective learning rate silently multiplies.
  • Padding and packing. Variable-length sequences are padded to a common length with an attention mask, which wastes compute proportional to the padding; packing concatenates short examples into one full-length sequence (with a segment mask) and can nearly double throughput on skewed length distributions.
  • Bucketing by length before batching keeps padding waste low without changing the model at all.

Mixed precision and loss scaling

  • Store and compute in 16-bit (fp16 or bf16) for matrix multiplies, keep a 32-bit master copy of the weights and accumulate in 32-bit. Roughly 2× throughput and half the activation memory, with no accuracy loss when done correctly.
  • fp16 has ~10 bits of mantissa and a narrow exponent range: small gradients underflow to zero. The fix is loss scaling — multiply the loss by a large constant S so gradients land in representable range, then divide the gradients by S before the optimizer step. Dynamic loss scaling raises S while steps are finite and halves it on any overflow.
  • bf16 has the same exponent range as fp32 and needs no loss scaling; it trades mantissa bits for range, which is the right trade for gradients. It is the default on hardware that supports it.
  • Keep reductions — softmax, layer norm, loss accumulation — in 32-bit regardless. That is where precision actually bites.
loss scaling (fp16)L′ = S · L ⟹ g′ = S · g step on g = g′ / S, skip the step if any g′ is Inf or NaN
Takeaway: choose the largest batch that fits and keeps the accelerator busy, then set the learning rate relative to it. Never compare two runs with different batch sizes at the same learning rate — you changed two things.

08 Weight initialization

Initialization decides whether the first forward pass produces usable numbers at all. The goal is simple: keep the variance of activations and of gradients roughly constant across depth.

The variance argument

  • For a linear layer with nin inputs and independent zero-mean weights, Var(y) = nin · Var(w) · Var(x). If nin·Var(w) > 1, activation magnitude grows geometrically with depth and saturates or overflows; if it is < 1, the signal decays to nothing and so does the gradient.
  • Xavier/Glorot compromises between the forward and backward conditions using both fan-in and fan-out — designed for symmetric, roughly linear-at-the-origin activations like tanh.
  • He/Kaiming doubles the variance because ReLU zeroes half its inputs and therefore halves the variance passing through. Use it whenever the activation is ReLU-like.
Xavier / Glorot (tanh, sigmoid)Var(w) = 2 / (nin + nout) uniform: w ~ U[ −√(6/(nin+nout)), +√(6/(nin+nout)) ] He / Kaiming (ReLU, GELU)Var(w) = 2 / nin normal: w ~ N(0, √(2/nin))

Practical rules

  • Biases start at zero — there is no symmetry to break once the weights are random. The exception is an output bias set to the base rate of the data (for example log(p/(1−p)) on an imbalanced binary task), which removes the pointless first phase where the model just learns the prior.
  • Zero-init the last layer of each residual branch so every block starts as the identity. Deep residual stacks then begin with unit-scale signal end to end and train stably without heavy warmup.
  • Transformers commonly scale the residual-projection initialization by 1/√(2N) for N layers, so that the variance added by N residual branches stays bounded.
  • Never initialize all weights to the same value: identical units receive identical gradients forever and the layer collapses to one unit.
Takeaway: good initialization is not a small optimization — it is the difference between a network that trains and one that emits NaN on step 3. Normalization layers make you less sensitive to it, but they do not make you immune.

09 Activation functions

Without a non-linearity, a stack of linear layers collapses to a single linear map — depth buys nothing. The activation is what makes depth mean something, and its derivative is what decides whether gradients survive it.

  • ReLU max(0, x) — derivative is exactly 1 on the positive side, so it never attenuates gradient there. Cheap, sparse, and the reason very deep networks became trainable. Its failure mode is the dead unit: a large negative bias pushes a unit below zero for every input, its gradient is permanently zero, and it never recovers.
  • Leaky ReLU / PReLU / ELU keep a small slope (or a smooth exponential tail) on the negative side, so dead units can come back. Mostly a fix looking for a problem once you use a sane learning rate.
  • Sigmoid and tanh saturate at both ends, where the derivative goes to zero. Stacking them multiplies small numbers and is the classic vanishing-gradient story. They survive where a bounded output is the point: gates in LSTMs, probability outputs, attention masks.
  • GELU and SiLU/Swish are smooth and non-monotonic near the origin, weighting the input by (roughly) how far above zero it is. They cost a little more and are the standard inside transformer feed-forward blocks.
  • Gated variants (GLU, SwiGLU) split the projection in two and multiply one half through a sigmoid or SiLU gate. Modern LLM feed-forward blocks use these, sizing the hidden dimension down by ⅔ to keep the parameter count fixed.
the common activationsReLU(x) = max(0, x) SiLU(x) = x · σ(x) GELU(x) = x · Φ(x) ≈ x · σ(1.702 x) gated feed-forwardSwiGLU(x) = ( SiLU(x W₁) ⊙ (x W₂) ) W₃
Takeaway: ReLU for convnets, GELU/SiLU (or a gated variant) for transformers, saturating functions only where you need a bounded value. If a large fraction of a layer's units output zero for every batch, you have dead units and your learning rate was too high.

10 Normalization layers

Normalization re-centres and re-scales activations mid-network so that each layer sees a stable input distribution. It buys larger learning rates, faster convergence, and much less sensitivity to initialization.

The three formulations

  • BatchNorm standardizes each channel using statistics over the batch (and spatial positions). It works beautifully on convnets and adds a mild regularizing noise — but the statistics depend on the batch, so tiny or heterogeneous batches break it, and it needs a separate inference path using running averages. That train/eval divergence is a perennial source of bugs.
  • LayerNorm standardizes across the feature dimension of each example independently. Batch-independent, identical at train and eval time, and therefore the choice for sequence models, transformers and anything with batch size 1.
  • RMSNorm drops the mean subtraction and rescales by the root-mean-square alone. Cheaper, one fewer reduction, empirically as good in large transformers — now the default in most modern LLMs.
  • GroupNorm splits channels into groups and normalizes within each — batch-independent like LayerNorm, but keeps the per-channel structure convnets want. The usual swap for detection and segmentation, where batches are small.
BatchNorm — statistics over (N, H, W) per channelx̂ = (x − μB) / √(σB² + ε) y = γ x̂ + β LayerNorm — statistics over the feature axis, per exampleμ = (1/d) ∑i xᵢ σ² = (1/d) ∑i (xᵢ − μ)² y = γ (x − μ)/√(σ² + ε) + β RMSNorm — scale only, no centringy = γ · x / √( (1/d) ∑i xᵢ² + ε )

Placement matters

  • Post-norm (normalize after the residual add) was the original transformer design and needs careful warmup — the residual stream is renormalized every block, so gradients to early layers are attenuated.
  • Pre-norm (normalize the branch input, leave the residual stream untouched) gives gradients a clean identity path from the loss to the first layer and trains deep stacks with far less tuning. It is what essentially every modern large model uses.
  • Omit the conv/linear bias immediately before a normalization layer — it is cancelled by the mean subtraction and only wastes parameters.
  • Exclude normalization gains and biases from weight decay. Decaying γ towards zero throttles the layer for no benefit.

11 Regularization, early stopping, checkpointing

Regularization is everything you do to stop the network memorizing the training set. In deep learning it is less about shrinking capacity than about adding noise and constraints during training.

The main levers

  • Dropout zeroes each activation independently with probability p during training and scales the survivors by 1/(1−p), so the expected activation is unchanged and inference needs no adjustment. It prevents units from co-adapting and approximates an ensemble over sub-networks. Large pretrained transformers often use p = 0 because the data itself is the regularizer.
  • Weight decay pulls weights towards zero every step. Under SGD it is equivalent to an L2 penalty; under Adam it is not, which is why you want the decoupled AdamW form. Typical values: 0.01–0.1 for AdamW on transformers, 1e-4 for SGD on convnets. Exclude biases and normalization parameters.
  • Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target with a slightly softened distribution. It caps the logit gap the model chases, improves calibration and stops the network becoming over-confident on noisy labels; α = 0.1 is the standard.
  • Data augmentation is usually the strongest regularizer available, because it adds real information about the invariances you want. Crops, flips, colour jitter, mixup and cutmix in vision; span masking and noise in text.
  • Stochastic depth randomly drops whole residual branches, and weight averaging (an exponential moving average of the weights, or averaging late checkpoints) buys a free accuracy bump by landing in a flatter part of the basin.
inverted dropout (training)y = (m ⊙ x) / (1 − p), mᵢ ~ Bernoulli(1 − p) label smoothing over K classesy′k = (1 − α)·yk + α/K decoupled weight decayθ ← (1 − ηλ)·θ − η · (adaptive step)

Early stopping and checkpointing

  • Early stopping monitors validation loss and halts once it has failed to improve for a patience window. It is regularization by time: the model never gets the epochs it would need to memorize.
  • Always keep the best checkpoint by validation metric, not the last — validation loss frequently rises while accuracy is still climbing, so decide up front which quantity you are selecting on.
  • Checkpoint the full training state (weights, optimizer buffers, schedule step, data position, RNG) so a preempted run resumes rather than restarts. Save periodically by step count, and keep a rolling window plus the best.
  • See Regularization for the statistical framing and Bias–Variance for why any of it helps.
Takeaway: add regularization only after you have confirmed the model can overfit. Regularizing an underfitting model is the most common way to waste a week.

12 Debugging a training run

Training failures are not mysterious; they are a small set of recurring causes. Work through them in order and instrument the run so the evidence is already there when something breaks.

The four checks, in order

  • Check the initial loss. Before any training, a K-class classifier should report ln K (≈2.30 for 10 classes, ≈6.91 for 1000). A different starting value means your labels, your reduction or your output layer is wrong — not your optimizer.
  • Overfit one batch. Take 8–32 examples, turn off augmentation, dropout and weight decay, and train until the loss reaches ~0. If it cannot, the bug is in the model, the loss or the data pipeline; no hyperparameter will save you. This single test catches most wiring errors.
  • Log gradient norms per layer. A healthy run has update-to-weight ratios ‖Δθ‖/‖θ‖ around 10⁻³. Orders of magnitude smaller means the layer is frozen or the signal is vanishing; orders larger means the step is too big.
  • Watch activation statistics. Track the fraction of zero activations per layer (dead ReLUs) and the mean/variance drift through depth. Both diagnose initialization and normalization problems that the loss curve hides.
sanity check at initializationL₀ ≈ ln K for uniform predictions over K classes healthy update ratio‖ η · Δθ ‖ / ‖ θ ‖ ≈ 10⁻³

13 Pitfalls and what to remember

  • The loop is four lines; the scales are everything. Forward → loss → backward → step, over mini-batches, with an optimizer that carries state between steps.
  • Backprop is exact and cheap in compute, expensive in memory. Activation storage, not FLOPs, is what usually decides your batch size.
  • Learning rate first, always. Find the peak with a range test, warm up into it, decay to near zero by the end of the budget. If you change batch size, rescale the learning rate with it.
  • AdamW for transformers, SGD+momentum for convnets, and exclude biases and norm parameters from weight decay in both.
  • Init and normalization keep signal alive through depth. He init for ReLU-like activations, zero-init the last layer of residual branches, pre-norm placement, and a batch-independent norm whenever batches are small.
  • Clip gradients, prefer bf16, and keep reductions in fp32. These three lines prevent most numerical blow-ups.
  • Regularize only after you can overfit. Augmentation is usually stronger than any explicit penalty.
  • Common own-goals: forgetting eval mode, forgetting to zero gradients, averaging accumulated micro-batches incorrectly, tuning on the test set, and comparing runs that differ in two things at once.
  • Everything here scales up through Distributed Training, shrinks down through Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning, and rests on the theory in Optimization.
Takeaway: a training run is an experiment. Change one thing, log everything, keep the seed fixed, and let the curves — not intuition — tell you which stage of the loop is broken.
