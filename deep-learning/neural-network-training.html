01 The training loop, and what one step actually costs

Everything on this page is a modification of six lines. Learn the six lines first, because every optimiser, every schedule, every normalisation layer and every regularisation trick is a change to exactly one of them, and knowing which one is most of understanding what a technique does.

the loop, in fullrepeat until stopping criterion met: 1 sample a minibatch (X, Y) of B examples ← DATA 2 ŷ = f(X; θ) forward pass ← MODEL 3 L = (1/B) ∑ᵢ ℓ(ŷᵢ, yᵢ) [+ Ω(θ)] the objective ← LOSS 4 g = ∇_θ L backward pass ← BACKPROP 5 Δ = A(g, state, t) the update direction ← OPTIMISER 6 θ ← θ + Δ apply it Line 1 is §26 (batch size) and §35 (augmentation). Line 2 is all of part 1, plus §29–§30 (normalisation layers) and §33 (dropout). Line 3 is §02–§03 and §31–§35. Line 4 is §05–§08. Line 5 is §18–§25 and §36. Line 6 is where weight decay does or does not live (§32) — which turns out to be the whole AdamW argument.

Two vocabulary items that get muddled constantly. A step (or iteration, or update) is one pass through lines 1–6: one minibatch in, one change to θ out. An epoch is one pass over the whole training set, so it contains ⌈N/B⌉ steps. Learning-rate schedules, warmup lengths and early-stopping patience are sometimes quoted in steps and sometimes in epochs, and the two differ by a factor of N/B — three orders of magnitude is common. Converting a schedule from one paper to another dataset without converting the units is a routine and completely silent way to run the wrong experiment.

What one step costs, exactly

The forward pass on a dense chain does ∑_l dₗ·d_{l+1} multiply-accumulates per example — the count part 1 §10 built. The backward pass does the same work twice: once to push the gradient signal back to the previous layer, and once to turn that signal into a gradient for this layer's weights. That gives the rule of thumb every capacity estimate rests on:

the 1 : 2 : 3 ruleforward ≈ 1 unit of arithmetic (one matrix product per layer) backward ≈ 2 units (∂L/∂A and ∂L/∂W: two products) one training step ≈ 3 units of a forward pass why the backward pass is exactly two products, in row conventionforward Z = A W + b A is (B × dᵢₙ), W is (dᵢₙ × dₒᵤₜ) backward ∂L/∂W = Aᵀ (∂L/∂Z) (dᵢₙ × B)(B × dₒᵤₜ) → (dᵢₙ × dₒᵤₜ) ∂L/∂A = (∂L/∂Z) Wᵀ (B × dₒᵤₜ)(dₒᵤₜ × dᵢₙ) → (B × dᵢₙ) Both have the same B·dᵢₙ·dₒᵤₜ inner-product count as the forward product. The FIRST layer does not need ∂L/∂A unless the inputs themselves are being optimised, so a very shallow net is slightly under 3×; nothing else is.

Memory is the other half of the cost and it does not follow the same rule. To compute ∂L/∂W for layer l the backward pass needs A⁽ˡ⁾, the input to that layer, which was computed on the way forward. So every layer's activations must be kept alive from the forward pass until the backward pass reaches them. That is why activation memory scales with B × depth × width while parameter memory scales with depth × width² and does not see the batch at all — and why "reduce the batch size" is the first thing anyone tries when a run runs out of memory. §28 is about buying that memory back.

02 The objective: the risk you want, the risk you minimise, and the surrogate for it

Training is not optimisation. It uses an optimisation algorithm, on an objective that is a proxy for a second objective, which is itself a proxy for the thing you actually care about. Two substitutions, each made for a specific reason, each with a specific cost. Losing track of which one you are looking at is how people end up reporting a training loss as if it meant something.

what you want — the RISK, an expectation over the true data distributionR(θ) = 𝔼_{(x,y) ~ p_data} [ ℓ( f(x; θ), y ) ] Not computable. p_data is unknown; you have samples from it and nothing else. substitution 1 — the EMPIRICAL RISK, the average over the sample you haveR̂(θ) = (1/N) ∑_{i=1}^{N} ℓ( f(xᵢ; θ), yᵢ ) Computable, and an unbiased estimate of R at any FIXED θ. It stops being unbiased the moment θ is chosen to minimise it — which is the entire content of "overfitting", and the reason a held-out split is not optional. substitution 2 — a SURROGATE loss, when ℓ has no usable derivative0–1 loss ℓ₀₁(ŷ, y) = 1[argmax ŷ ≠ y] what you are scored on ∇ℓ₀₁ = 0 almost everywhere, undefined on the boundary surrogate ℓ_ce(z, y) = −log softmax(z)_y smooth, convex in z, an upper bound on ℓ₀₁ up to a constant, and its gradient points somewhere useful even when the prediction is correct

The second substitution is worth dwelling on, because it is the one people forget exists. Nobody minimises accuracy. The 0–1 loss is flat: nudging a weight by 10⁻⁶ changes no prediction, so its derivative is zero everywhere it is defined, and gradient descent on it is a random walk. The surrogate exists to give the flat function a slope. It buys differentiability by caring about things the metric does not — how confident a correct prediction is, and how far a wrong one is from the boundary. That is a feature while training and a trap when interpreting: cross-entropy and accuracy can and do move in opposite directions, and a model whose validation loss is rising while its validation accuracy is still climbing is not broken. It is becoming more confident on the examples it gets right and more confidently wrong on a few it does not, and the loss weights the second far more heavily than the metric does.

The tempting shortcut. "The training loss went down, so the model got better." The training loss is the one quantity guaranteed to go down. Gradient descent minimises exactly that number and nothing else; a model with enough capacity drives it to zero on randomly permuted labels. The only readings that carry information about the model are on data the optimiser never touched, and the only reading that carries information about deployment is on data the experimenter never touched either — which is why a test set that has been consulted forty times during hyperparameter search has quietly become a second validation set.

Why the optimisation and the learning are different problems

Pure optimisation asks you to make R̂ as small as possible. Learning asks you to make R small, and stopping the optimiser early — before R̂ is minimised — is often the correct move (§34). It is worth being explicit about the three ways this changes what "a good algorithm" means:

  • Convergence to a global minimum is not the goal. Any parameter setting with low held-out risk will do, and there are astronomically many of them. This is why the non-convexity of the problem is much less alarming in practice than it sounds in theory, and why §17's saddle points are survivable.
  • An exact gradient is not worth paying for. The gradient of R̂ over all N examples is itself only an estimate of the gradient of R. Spending N/B times the arithmetic to remove sampling noise from an estimate that is already noisy for a deeper reason is a bad trade, and §15 makes that argument quantitatively.
  • Noise in the update can help. A noisy gradient perturbs the trajectory, which is a mechanism for escaping the flat regions of §17 and — the evidence suggests — for preferring the wider minima that generalise better. An algorithm that removes all the noise removes that too.

03 The loss from the optimiser's side: magnitudes, imbalance, and stability

Part 1 derived which loss goes with which output unit, from maximum likelihood, and showed that every correct pairing gives the same clean gradient at the logits. This section takes that as given and asks the four questions the optimiser cares about: how big is the gradient, how does its size vary across examples, what happens when the classes are unbalanced, and what breaks numerically.

the one fact this section inherits — part 1 §17For every maximum-likelihood pairing of output unit and loss, ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y at the LOGITS z with no factor of g′(z) anywhere in it. Squared error on a sigmoid instead gives (ŷ − y)·σ′(z), and σ′ = 1/(2 + eᶻ + e⁻ᶻ) is 4.5e−05 at |z| = 10.

1 · Gradient magnitude, per example

Because the gradient at the logits is ŷ − y, its magnitude is bounded by 1 per output unit and is largest exactly where the model is most wrong. That is the well-behaved case, and it means the scale of the weight gradients is set by the activations flowing into each layer rather than by the loss — which is why standardising the inputs matters so much (§12) and why a normalisation layer changes the optimisation so dramatically (§29).

Two commonly used losses break that bound. Squared error on unnormalised regression targets gives ∂L/∂ŷ = ŷ − y with no bound at all: if y is measured in dollars and ranges to 10⁶, the initial gradients are of order 10⁶ and the first update destroys the network. Standardise regression targets, or expect to spend an afternoon on it. And the exponential loss grows without limit as the margin goes negative, so a single mislabelled example can dominate the entire batch gradient — which is exactly the property that makes boosting sensitive to label noise.

2 · Class imbalance

With 99 negatives per positive, the mean loss is dominated by negatives. The model that predicts "negative" always has a mean cross-entropy of H(0.01) = 0.05600 nats and an accuracy of 99%, and gradient descent finds it in a handful of steps because it is a genuine, sharp local basin of the objective you wrote down. The optimiser did not fail; the objective was wrong. Three fixes, with different failure modes:

reweight the loss
Multiply each example's loss by wᵧ
Usually w_c ∝ 1/n_c. Cheap and exact — it changes the objective to the one you meant. It also multiplies the gradient variance of the rare class by w², so the effective learning rate on the rare examples goes up and training gets noisier. Halve the learning rate when you turn this on.
resample the data
Oversample the rare class, or undersample the common one
Equivalent to reweighting in expectation, but the variance behaves differently: oversampling repeats the same rare examples many times per epoch, so the model can memorise them; undersampling throws away real data. Resampling inside the minibatch (a fixed number of positives per batch) is usually better than either.
fix the bias, not the loss
Initialise the output bias to the log-odds
Set b_out = log(p/(1−p)) so the model starts at the base rate. This costs nothing, does not distort the objective, and removes the several hundred wasted steps the network otherwise spends discovering the class prior. For softmax with prior c, solve softmax(b) = c. Do this always; the other two are optional.

3 · Label smoothing

Cross-entropy with a one-hot target has no finite minimiser. Driving softmax(z)_y to exactly 1 requires z_y − z_j → ∞, so the loss keeps rewarding larger logits forever, long after the classification is settled. The result is a model whose confidence is systematically higher than its accuracy, and weights that keep growing for no gain. Label smoothing replaces the target y with (1 − ε)·y + ε/K, which gives the loss a finite minimiser at a finite logit gap:

label smoothing with K classes and smoothing εtarget q_j = (1 − ε)·1[j = y] + ε/K loss L = −∑ⱼ q_j · log softmax(z)_j gradient ∂L/∂zⱼ = softmax(z)_j − q_j the SAME clean form minimiser softmax(z) = q, so with K = 1000 and ε = 0.1 the optimal confidence is 0.9001 rather than 1, and the optimal logit gap between the true class and any other is log(0.9001/0.0001) = 9.105

That last number is the point. Without smoothing the optimal logit gap is infinite; with ε = 0.1 and 1000 classes it is 9.105, a perfectly ordinary number that the network reaches and then stops pushing on. Label smoothing is a calibration device that happens to also be a regulariser, and it costs one line. It does mildly hurt if you need the raw probabilities to be sharp — knowledge distillation and any downstream use of the logits as scores are the cases to watch.

4 · Numerical stability in the loop

Part 1 §16 showed that −log σ(z), written as log(eᶻ/(1+eᶻ)), overflows to infinity below z ≈ −103.3 in float32 while the true answer is about 103. In a training loop that is not a cosmetic problem: one infinite loss produces an infinite gradient, which produces NaN parameters, and from then on every forward pass returns NaN and the run is dead with no error raised. The three rules are short.

  • Never compute probabilities and then take their log. Every framework provides a fused logits-to-loss function precisely because the fused form is the stable one: xent(z, k) = logsumexp(z) − z_k and bce(z, y) = softplus((1 − 2y)·z), both finite for every finite input. If the function name contains with_logits, feed it logits.
  • Algebraically identical is not numerically identical. log(1/(1+e⁻ᶻ)) and log(eᶻ/(1+eᶻ)) are the same function on paper. In float32 the first returns −∞ below z ≈ −88.8, where e⁻ᶻ overflows; the second survives to z ≈ −103.3, where eᶻ falls off the bottom of the subnormal range. Neither is safe and the failure point depends on which rearrangement you happened to type. The figure below measures both.
  • Never take the log of something a softmax produced. A softmax output can be exactly 0 in floating point when one logit dominates; log 0 is −∞. Use logSoftmax, which subtracts the log-sum-exp instead of dividing by the sum.
  • Check for NaN at a cost you can afford. A single isfinite on the loss scalar every step is free relative to the step itself, and it converts "the run silently produced garbage for six hours" into "the run stopped at step 4 117". Skipping the update on a non-finite gradient — rather than crashing — is exactly what the mixed-precision loop of §27 does for a living.

04 The computational graph: the object backpropagation actually runs on

Backpropagation is not defined on neural networks. It is defined on a directed acyclic graph of variables and operations, and networks happen to be one. Making that graph explicit is what turns "apply the chain rule" from a symbolic exercise into an algorithm with a cost you can count, and it is why the same machinery differentiates a loop, a convolution, an attention block or a physics simulator without knowing what any of them are.

the languagenode a VARIABLE — a scalar, vector, matrix or tensor. operation a simple function of one or more variables, returning ONE variable (a multi-output op is an implementation convenience, not a conceptual one). edge drawn from x to y when y is computed by applying an op to x. Pa(u) is the set of parents of u — the inputs of its op. Every node uⁱ is computed as uⁱ = fⁱ( Pa(uⁱ) ). The graph is ACYCLIC, so the nodes can be topologically ordered; the forward pass IS that order. what the graph is forforward evaluate the nodes in topological order, u⁽ⁿ⁾ last. backward walk the SAME order in REVERSE, and at each node accumulate ∂u⁽ⁿ⁾/∂uʲ = ∑_{i : j ∈ Pa(uⁱ)} (∂u⁽ⁿ⁾/∂uⁱ)·(∂uⁱ/∂uʲ) The sum is over the CHILDREN of j: a variable used in three places receives three contributions and they ADD. That is the only rule; everything else is bookkeeping about when a node's children are all finished.

Two facts follow immediately from this framing, and both are worth stating before any derivation.

  • The nodes are not layers. A dense layer is at least three nodes — a matrix product, a bias add, an activation — and a framework's graph is finer still. The pictures in this domain that show one circle per layer are drawings of the model; the graph the differentiator walks has one node per intermediate value. When a framework reports a shape error inside a fused kernel you are reading the second graph.
  • A variable used twice gets two gradient contributions. This is the single rule that people re-derive wrongly. If w feeds both the prediction and a weight-decay penalty, then ∂L/∂w is the sum of the two paths. Forget the sum and your weight decay silently does nothing, or your residual branch trains and your skip branch does not.
One graph, walked forwards then backwards, with every value and every adjoint step through it — the highlighted node is the one being evaluated; on the backward sweep the incoming arrows are the children whose contributions are being summed

Why the graph must be stored, and what that costs

The backward pass at node j needs the local partial ∂uⁱ/∂uʲ, and that partial is generally a function of the values at the nodes. Differentiating a = relu(z) needs the sign of z; differentiating Z = AW needs A and W. So the forward pass cannot throw its intermediates away — it must keep them alive until the reverse sweep arrives. That is the memory cost §01 counted, and it is the price reverse mode pays for its speed. §28 is about buying some of it back by recomputing intermediates instead of storing them, which is the classic time-for-memory trade and, on the graph, is simply the choice to re-run a subgraph rather than cache it.

05 Backpropagation, derived

The derivation is three lines of the chain rule plus one decision about the order of evaluation, and the decision is the entire algorithm. Everything that makes backpropagation remarkable — that the gradient with respect to every parameter costs about the same as one forward pass, no matter how many parameters there are — comes from that ordering and not from the calculus.

The vector chain rule, stated once

scalary = g(x), z = f(y) ⇒ dz/dx = (dz/dy)·(dy/dx) vector, x ∈ ℝᵐ, y ∈ ℝⁿ, z ∈ ℝ∂z/∂xᵢ = ∑ⱼ (∂z/∂yⱼ)·(∂yⱼ/∂xᵢ) or ∇_x z = (∂y/∂x)ᵀ ∇_y z where ∂y/∂x is the (n × m) JACOBIAN of g. The whole backward pass is one Jacobian-transpose-times-gradient product per operation — never a Jacobian built explicitly, only its action on a vector.

That last sentence is the practical content. A dense layer with 4096 inputs and 4096 outputs has a Jacobian with 4096²·B entries; nobody ever forms it. What the backward pass computes is the product of that Jacobian's transpose with a vector, and for an affine map that product is just another matrix multiply by Wᵀ. Every operation in every framework ships with a vjp — a vector-Jacobian product — and never with a Jacobian.

The MLP recursion, in the row convention

Series law, declared once and never broken: X is (N × d) with one example per row, and W⁽ˡ⁾ is (d_{l−1} × dₗ). A layer is Z = A W + b with the bias broadcast down the rows. With L parameterised layers, A⁽⁰⁾ = X, and a loss L that is a mean over the N rows:

The transposes are not decoration: ∂L/∂W has the shape of W and ∂L/∂A has the shape of A, always, in every framework, for every op. If a derivation you have written produces a gradient whose shape differs from its parameter's, the derivation is wrong and no amount of staring will fix it.
The tempting shortcut. "It is the chain rule, so I can write Wᵀ or W depending on what makes the shapes work." Only one of them is the derivative; the other happens to have the same shape when the layer is square. The safe habit: fix the convention (rows here), derive one scalar entry ∂L/∂W_{ij} by hand once, and read the matrix form off it. Z_{nj} = ∑ᵢ A_{ni} W_{ij}, so ∂L/∂W_{ij} = ∑ₙ Δ_{nj} A_{ni} = (Aᵀ Δ)_{ij}. That is where the transpose comes from, and it is the only place it can come from. Square layers hide the error until you build a real network and then it appears as a plausible-looking model that will not learn.

What the four lines cost

Look at what is not in the recursion. There is no per-parameter loop and no term that grows with the number of parameters beyond the matrix products themselves. Layer l does two products of the same size as its forward product, so the whole backward pass is 2× the forward pass regardless of P. The gradient with respect to ten million parameters costs the same as the gradient with respect to ten — per parameter, the cost of the gradient is constant. That is the property that makes deep learning possible at all, and §07 shows exactly what it is bought with.

What backpropagation is not. It is not the learning algorithm. It computes ∇_θ L and stops. Something else — SGD, Adam, anything in §19 — decides what to do with that vector. Saying "the network was trained with backpropagation" is like saying a program was written with addition. It is also not specific to neural networks: reverse-mode differentiation applies to any acyclic computation with differentiable pieces, which is why the same libraries differentiate ODE solvers and ray tracers. And it is not the chain rule — §08.

The sign factor in that fused loss is the single most common place to lose a minus sign, so it is worth fixing in place. The identity is ℓ(z, y) = softplus((1 − 2y)·z): for y = 1 the factor is −1, giving softplus(−z) = −log σ(z); for y = 0 the factor is +1, giving softplus(z) = −log(1 − σ(z)). Two checks that catch a flipped sign instantly. At z = 0 both labels must give log 2 = 0.693147, and both do. And ℓ must decrease as z moves toward the label: row 2 has y = 0 and its loss 0.6443967 sits just below log 2, because z = −0.10 is only barely on the correct side. 07 Reverse mode, forward mode, and why finite differences are not an option

There are three ways to get a derivative out of a program, and the choice between them is a matter of arithmetic cost, not of taste. The reason deep learning uses the one it uses is a single structural fact about the problem: the output is one number and the inputs are millions.

The matrix-chain picture

A composition x → u₁ → u₂ → ⋯ → uₖ → L has a total derivative that is a product of Jacobians. Matrix multiplication is associative, so the value does not depend on the order — but the cost does, enormously.

the same product, two bracketingsdL/dx = J_L · J_k · … · J₂ · J₁ J_i is (nᵢ × n_{i−1}) FORWARD mode ( … ((J_L · J_k) · … ) · J₁ ) right to left… no — build from the INPUT end: J₁ first, then J₂·J₁, then J₃·(J₂J₁). Each step is a MATRIX × MATRIX product. Cheap only if n₀ is small. REVERSE mode start from the OUTPUT end: J_L first (a ROW vector when L is a scalar), then (J_L·J_k), then ((J_L·J_k)·J_{k−1}). Each step is a VECTOR × MATRIX product. Cheap when n_L is small. the cost, for a chain of k layers all of width n, with d inputs and 1 outputforward mode k · n² · d one full forward sweep PER INPUT variable reverse mode k · n² · 1 one full backward sweep, TOTAL ratio d d = the number of parameters. For a 10⁷-parameter model that is a factor of ten million.

Stated as a rule: reverse mode costs one sweep per output; forward mode costs one sweep per input. Training a network has one output (the scalar loss) and P inputs (the parameters), so reverse mode wins by a factor of P. Flip the shape of the problem and the answer flips with it: computing the sensitivity of a whole layer's activations to one scalar hyperparameter is a one-input, many-output problem and forward mode is the right tool. Forward mode also needs no tape — it carries derivatives alongside values on the way forward and never stores anything — which is why it appears in memory-constrained and real-time settings.

Finite differences: why not

The obvious method is to perturb each parameter and re-evaluate. It is correct, it is trivially easy to implement, and it is unusable for two independent reasons.

cost
One forward pass per parameter
Central differences need two. For P = 10⁷ that is 2 × 10⁷ forward passes to get one gradient that reverse mode produces in the cost of two. If a forward pass takes 10 ms, one gradient step takes 2.3 days instead of 30 ms.
accuracy
Two error sources that pull in opposite directions
Truncation error falls as h²; cancellation error — subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers — grows as ε/h. The total is minimised at h ≈ ε^{1/3}, about 6×10⁻⁶ in double precision, and even there you keep only about eleven of the sixteen significant digits — measured, on the figure below, as a best relative error of 2.9×10⁻¹¹ at h ≈ 3×10⁻⁶. In single precision the optimum moves to h ≈ 5×10⁻³ and the best relative error is about 7×10⁻⁵: four good digits, from a function evaluated to seven.
but keep it
It is the gradient check
Finite differences are useless as a training algorithm and indispensable as a test. A hand-written backward pass, a custom operation, a re-derived attention kernel — all of them should be checked against central differences on a tiny random input before they are trusted. §39 gives the recipe and the tolerance.

08 Why backpropagation is not "the chain rule"

The chain rule is a fact about derivatives. Backpropagation is an evaluation schedule for that fact — a specific decision about which subexpressions to compute once and store, rather than recompute on demand. Without the schedule the same identity produces an algorithm that is exponentially slower, and seeing the blow-up explicitly is the fastest way to understand what the algorithm is actually contributing.

The blow-up, explicitly

Take the simplest possible deep computation: a scalar passed through a chain of functions, x → u₁ = f(x) → u₂ = f(u₁) → ⋯ → u_k = f(u_{k−1}). Write the chain rule out symbolically and every factor is itself a function of the previous u, which is a function of the one before it. Expand the whole thing into an expression in x alone:

the identity — true, and a trapdu_k/dx = f′(u_{k−1}) · f′(u_{k−2}) · … · f′(u₁) · f′(x) substitute uⱼ = f(f(…f(x)…)) everywhere before evaluatingdu₃/dx = f′(f(f(x))) · f′(f(x)) · f′(x) Evaluating that expression as written recomputes f(x) twice, f(f(x)) once from scratch. At depth k the count of f-evaluations grows EXPONENTIALLY in k if every nested call is expanded and none is shared. STORE u₁ … u_{k−1} on the way forward and the count is LINEAR in k. That choice — compute once, store, reuse — is dynamic programming, and it is the entire difference between the chain rule and back-propagation.

The same point in the graph language of §04: the naive recursive expansion visits a node once per path from that node to the output, and the number of such paths can be exponential in the depth. Backpropagation visits each edge exactly once, so its cost is O(#edges) — the same order as the forward pass. It gets there by storing one number (or tensor) per node, which is the memory §01 counted. The trade is explicit: exponential time, or linear time and linear memory. Backpropagation takes the second, and §28's checkpointing walks partway back toward the first on purpose.

Three consequences of it being a schedule, not an identity

  • It can be beaten, and finding the best schedule is hard. Backpropagation eliminates repeated subexpressions but does not simplify. Given a softmax built out of exponentials, a sum and a division, followed by a cross-entropy built out of logs, it will faithfully differentiate through every one of those operations — even though a human can see the answer collapses to q − p. That collapse is exactly what the fused loss functions of §03 hard-code. Choosing the cheapest possible order of operations for a general graph is NP-complete, so real frameworks apply a library of pattern-matched simplifications and stop there.
  • The memory schedule is a free parameter. Nothing forces "store every node". Recomputing a cheap node instead of storing it is always legal, and §28's activation checkpointing is precisely the policy "store one node per segment, recompute the rest", which lands at O(√k) memory for O(1) extra forward passes per segment.
  • The cost statement needs the scalar output. "The gradient costs the same as the forward pass" is true because the loss is one number. Ask for a full Jacobian of a k-output function and the naive extension costs k backward passes. This is why per-example gradients, Jacobian-vector products for second-order methods, and per-sample influence computations are all expensive in a way that ordinary training is not, and why they get their own specialised implementations.
The name, and where the confusion comes from. The literature uses "backpropagation" for three different things: the reverse sweep itself (correct), the reverse sweep plus the parameter update (loose but common), and the entire enterprise of training neural networks (wrong and actively unhelpful). This page uses the first. When someone says a model "was trained end-to-end with backpropagation", what they are actually claiming is that the whole pipeline is a single differentiable graph with no non-differentiable stage in the middle — which is a real and interesting claim about the architecture, not about the differentiation.

09 Vanishing and exploding gradients, quantitatively

The backward recursion of §05 multiplies. Multiplying L numbers together does one of three things — it converges to zero, it diverges, or it stays put — and only the third is useful. Depth alone is enough to cause the problem, with no bad luck and no bad data, and the fact that it is multiplicative is why it is a cliff rather than a slope: fifty layers do not make training fifty times harder, they make the gradient 2⁵⁰ times wrong.

The product

The reason this is not a bound you can shrug off is that c is very nearly determined by two numbers you already chose. Why the recurrent case is worse, and why residual connections fix the feedforward one

A feedforward network uses a different matrix at each layer, so the log of the per-layer factor performs a random walk: it drifts, but the drift is slow and the variance grows only as √L. A recurrent network applies the same matrix at every step, so the factors are perfectly correlated and the product is λᴸ exactly — a deterministic explosion or collapse governed by one eigenvalue. That is why recurrence hit this wall first and hardest, and why the gating mechanisms of an LSTM exist: they create a path along which the effective factor is 1 by construction.

The feedforward fix is the same idea with less machinery. If a block computes h + F(h) rather than F(h), its Jacobian is I + ∂F/∂h — the identity plus something small — and the product of L such Jacobians expands into a sum whose leading term is 1. The gradient reaches the bottom along the identity path regardless of what the residual branches do. That is Residual & Skip Connections, and it is the reason depth stopped being a research problem.

The tempting shortcut. "Vanishing gradients mean training is slow." They mean the early layers do not train at all while the late ones train normally. The loss does go down — the top of the network is perfectly healthy — so the run looks like it is working. What you actually have is a shallow network with a large randomly-initialised feature extractor bolted underneath it, and no amount of extra epochs will fix it, because the gradient reaching the bottom is smaller than the floating-point noise on the weights there. The diagnostic is the per-layer gradient norm, printed once per hundred steps; §39.

11 The dying ReLU, as an optimisation pathology

A rectified unit whose pre-activation is negative for every example in the training set outputs zero for every example, receives exactly zero gradient for every example, and is therefore frozen forever. It is not slow, it is not attenuated — it is removed from the model, and no optimiser will bring it back. This is the one failure mode on this page that is genuinely irreversible.

The mechanism, in three lines

why the freeze is permanentunit j is dead ⟺ wⱼ·xᵢ + bⱼ < 0 for every training example i ⟹ aᵢⱼ = 0 for all i (contributes nothing forward) ⟹ relu′(zᵢⱼ) = 0 for all i (receives nothing backward) ⟹ ∂L/∂wⱼ = 0 and ∂L/∂bⱼ = 0 exactly ⟹ the update is 0, so the hyperplane never moves, so the unit is dead at step t + 1 for exactly the same reason. It is a FIXED POINT of the training dynamics. Compare a saturated sigmoid, whose derivative is 1.8e−02 rather than 0: tiny, but not zero, so the unit drifts and can in principle recover. Zero is a different kind of number.
The step from "zero gradient" to "permanently dead" only closes in the FIRST hidden layer. The argument above needs both halves: the unit's weights stop moving, and its inputs stop moving. In the first hidden layer the inputs are the data, which never change, so both hold and the unit is finished. In any deeper layer the inputs are the previous layer's activations, which keep changing — so a unit with frozen weights can still find its pre-activation pushed back above zero by the layer below it. Measured on the figure at the end of this section: with a plain rectifier at a learning rate high enough to do damage, the first layer's dead count is monotone non-decreasing for the entire run, while the second layer's rises and falls repeatedly. Both are losing capacity right now; only the first loss is irreversible. This distinction is missing from most tellings, and it is why "dying ReLU" is a first-layer and input-layer problem far more than a deep-layer one.

How units die

Almost always the same way: one oversized update. The gradient ∂L/∂b_j is a sum over the batch of the deltas reaching that unit; if the learning rate is too large, a single step can push b_j far enough negative that z is below zero for the entire dataset. The unit was alive at step t, participated in a large update, and is dead at step t + 1. Two consequences that are worth stating plainly.

  • Dying ReLUs are a symptom of the learning rate, not of the activation. The activation determines that the failure is permanent; the learning rate determines whether it happens. Measured on the figure below, on one fixed problem: at η = 1 nothing dies, at η = 4 the first hidden layer ends 20% dead and the second 41%, and at η = 6.3 the network is 94% dead and the loss has snapped back to exactly log 2. The whole transition happens over a factor of six in one hyperparameter.
  • Warmup fixes it almost completely, and costs nothing. The same run at η = 4 with 200 steps of linear warmup ends with 0% of the first layer dead instead of 22%, and its final loss is 4.3×10⁻⁴ against 2.5×10⁻⁴ — statistically the same model, with none of the damage. §24.
  • In the first hidden layer they are cumulative and monotone. That layer's dead fraction never goes down under plain ReLU, which makes it a uniquely good diagnostic: it is a one-way ratchet, so a slow climb is visible long before the loss curve shows anything. Deeper layers are noisier for the reason in the callout above, so watch the first one.
first

The tempting shortcut. "My network is not learning, so I will swap ReLU for something fancier." Measure the dead fraction first. If it is near zero, the activation is not your problem and swapping it will waste a day. If it is 40%, the activation swap will help — and lowering the learning rate would have helped more, because it would have prevented the loss of capacity instead of merely making it recoverable. Part 1 §13 makes the same point from the function-class side: it is almost never the activation.

12 Initialisation: the variance argument, derived

Deep networks are trained by an iterative algorithm, so somebody has to choose the starting point, and the choice determines whether the algorithm converges at all. The reasoning behind the standard rules is one paragraph of second-moment algebra, and it is worth doing rather than memorising, because the assumptions it makes are exactly the assumptions that a normalisation layer later removes.

What has to be true before anything else can be

requirement 1
Break the symmetry
Initialise every weight to the same value — zero, or anything — and two units in the same layer, reading the same inputs, compute the same function, receive the same gradient, and take the same step. They stay identical forever. A layer of n such units is a layer of one unit. This is why the weights must be random, and it is the one property nobody argues about.
requirement 2
Do not saturate
If the weights are large, the pre-activations are large, and a saturating unit lands where g′ ≈ 0. §10's table: tanh′(4) = 0.00134. The network starts in a place from which it cannot move.
requirement 3
Do not lose the signal
If the weights are small, the activations shrink layer by layer, the deep layers see nothing but the bias, and the gradient reaching the shallow layers is §09's cᴸ with c < 1. The forward signal and the backward signal die for the same reason and at the same rate.
requirement 4
Zero biases, almost always
The weights already break the symmetry, so the bias does not need to. Zero is compatible with every weight rule. The exceptions are deliberate and few: the output bias set to the base rate (§03), a small positive bias to keep rectifiers alive (§11), and a gate bias set so the gate starts open.

The derivation, forward

One layer, row convention: z = a W with a ∈ ℝ^{1×n} and W ∈ ℝ^{n×m}. Assume the entries of W are independent with mean 0 and variance σ_w², independent of a, and that the entries of a are identically distributed. Then for any output coordinate j:

The invariant is 𝔼[a²] = 1, not Var[a] = 1. Under the rectifier rule the fixed point has Var[z] = 2 and 𝔼[a²] = 1, but the variance of the post-activation settles at Var[relu(z)] = (½ − 1/2π)·Var[z] = 1 − 1/π = 0.6817, because a rectified Gaussian has a non-zero mean. Measure the activation variance at depth in a correctly initialised rectifier network and you will find 0.68, not 1.00, and that is the right answer. A sampled check at width 512, depth 30, gives 0.686. Anyone reporting that "the variance is preserved" is describing the pre-activation, or is describing tanh.

The derivation, backward

Run the identical argument on Δ⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾ = Δ⁽ˡ⁾ (W⁽ˡ⁾)ᵀ ⊙ g′. The only structural change is which dimension the sum runs over: forward, coordinate j of z sums over the n_in inputs; backward, coordinate i of Δ sums over the n_out outputs.

the two conditions, side by sideforward, preserve 𝔼[a²]: n_in · σ_w² · κ = 1 ⇒ σ_w² = 1/(κ·n_in) backward, preserve 𝔼[Δ²]: n_out · σ_w² · κ = 1 ⇒ σ_w² = 1/(κ·n_out) where κ = ½ for a rectifier and 1 for a linear or tanh unit. What every one of these rules assumes, and when the assumptions fail. Four assumptions, all of them false in a real network and none of them fatally so. (1) The weights and activations are independent — true at step 0, false immediately after, because training correlates them. (2) The activations are identically distributed — false as soon as any structure appears. (3) The activation is linear near the origin, or a clean rectifier — GELU and SiLU are neither, so the rectifier rule is an approximation for them, and a good enough one. (4) The width is infinite. All four are why every source says the initial scale should be treated as a hyperparameter with the formula as a starting point, and why the normalisation layers of §29 — which enforce the property at every step rather than hoping it survives — largely displaced the careful-initialisation research programme.

15 Gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, and the minibatch

The update rule is one line. Everything interesting is in which examples the gradient was computed from, because that choice trades arithmetic against noise, and the trade is not close: for essentially every problem the right batch size is far smaller than the dataset, and the reason is a square root.

The minibatch gradient is an average of B independent draws from the same distribution, so it is an unbiased estimate of the full-batch gradient, and its standard error falls as 1/√B. The deeper reason large batches are wasteful. Even the exact full-batch gradient of R̂ is only an estimate of the gradient of the thing you actually want, R — and that estimate's error is O(1/√N), fixed by the size of your dataset and unimprovable by any amount of arithmetic. Spending N/B times the compute to remove sampling noise from an estimate that already carries irreducible noise for a different reason is a bad trade twice over. This is §02's third bullet, and it is why the answer to "should I use the full dataset for each gradient" is essentially always no.

Where the noise is useful, and where it is not

useful
Escaping flat regions and saddles
At a saddle the exact gradient is zero and full-batch descent stops. A noisy gradient is not zero, so it perturbs off the ridge and continues. §17. This is not a small effect — it is one of the reasons the non-convexity of the problem turned out to be survivable in practice.
useful
A regularisation effect, for free
Gradient noise scales as η/B in the continuous-time picture, and the evidence is that a higher noise level biases the trajectory toward wider, flatter minima that generalise better. It is why simply increasing the batch size, holding everything else fixed, often worsens the test error while improving the training loss.
not useful
The last mile
Near a minimum the true gradient goes to zero but the sampling noise does not. The iterate then rattles around in a ball whose radius is set by η and B, and never settles. This is the entire reason a learning-rate schedule is necessary rather than optional (§24), and why full-batch descent can use a constant rate while SGD cannot.
the classical condition
Why the rate must go to zero, but not too fast
Convergence of SGD is guaranteed when ∑ ηₜ = ∞ and ∑ ηₜ² < ∞. The first says the steps must add up to enough total distance to reach anywhere; the second says the accumulated noise must be finite. ηₜ ∝ 1/t satisfies both; ηₜ ∝ 1/√t fails the second; a constant rate fails both. In practice nobody uses a schedule that satisfies these, because they are asymptotic and training is not.

Sampling matters, and the obvious way is wrong

The unbiasedness argument requires the batch to be an independent sample. Two ways people break it, both common:

  • Not shuffling. If the file is sorted by class, every batch contains one class, every gradient points at "predict this class", and consecutive updates fight each other. Shuffle once per epoch. Shuffling once, ever, is not enough: the same batches recur, so the same correlated noise recurs, and the trajectory can lock into a cycle.
  • Sampling without replacement and calling it i.i.d. Real implementations shuffle and walk the permutation, which is sampling without replacement. It is not i.i.d. — it is slightly better, because it guarantees coverage — and the difference matters only for the last, ragged batch of an epoch, which has fewer examples and therefore a noisier gradient with the same weight. Drop it or keep it, but know it is there.

The gradient tells you which way is downhill. It says nothing about how far you can go before downhill stops being downhill — that is curvature, and it is the second derivative. Once you look at the second-order picture, the learning rate stops being a knob you tune by feel and becomes a quantity with an explicit upper bound, an explicit optimum, and an explicit convergence rate, all of them functions of two eigenvalues.

17 Saddle points, plateaus, and cliffs: what the landscape is actually like

The standard mental picture of non-convex optimisation — a rugged surface full of local minima, in which gradient descent gets stuck — is essentially wrong for high-dimensional problems, and believing it leads people to worry about the wrong thing. The real obstacles are saddle points, flat regions, and cliffs, and each has a different fix.

Why local minima are rare and saddles are everywhere

At a critical point the gradient is zero and the type of the point is decided by the Hessian's eigenvalues: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle. Now count. In n dimensions there are n eigenvalues, and for a minimum every one of them must be positive. Treat the signs as roughly independent — which is what happens for many classes of random function — and the probability of a minimum is about 2⁻ⁿ:

The eigenvalues of a random symmetric matrix repel one another — they do not scatter independently, they spread out — so getting all n of them onto the same side of zero is much harder than n independent coin flips, and the true decay is faster than exponential in n. Rather than repeat the heuristic's arithmetic, this page reports the measurement and notes that the effect it is used to demonstrate is larger than the heuristic claims. In a million dimensions, either way, a critical point picked at random is a saddle.

None of this is a theorem about neural networks specifically — it is a statement about random functions of this type, and real loss surfaces are not random. But the direction is supported by both theory and measurement, and it comes with a second, more useful fact: for many random functions of this type the eigenvalues become more likely to be positive as the cost falls. Low-cost critical points are usually minima; high-cost critical points are usually saddles. So the local minima that do exist tend to be good ones, and the thing standing between you and them is a landscape of saddles.

obstacle 1
Saddle points
The gradient goes to zero as you approach, so progress slows. But descent is repelled from a saddle rather than attracted to it, and any noise at all breaks the symmetry, so first-order methods generally escape. The method that genuinely fails here is Newton's, which solves for a zero gradient and will happily converge onto a saddle — a large part of why second-order methods never displaced SGD.
obstacle 2
Plateaus and flat regions
Wide regions where both the gradient and the curvature are near zero. Nothing pulls you anywhere and there is no signal to follow. These are genuinely hard for every numerical method, and the practical fixes are structural: normalisation layers, residual paths, and a sensible initialisation, all of which are about not starting in one.
obstacle 3
Cliffs
Extremely steep walls, produced by the multiplication of several large weights (§09's product, in the forward direction). The gradient at the face of a cliff is enormous, so one ordinary step launches the parameters somewhere arbitrary and undoes hours of progress. This is the failure that gradient clipping exists for, and clipping is the correct fix precisely because the gradient's direction is still fine — it is only the magnitude that is meaningless.
not an obstacle
Reaching a critical point at all
Training usually does not converge to any critical point. Measured on real runs, the gradient norm frequently increases throughout training while the validation error steadily falls. Gradient descent is designed to go downhill, not to find a place where the gradient vanishes, and it is entirely normal for a successful run to end somewhere with a perfectly healthy gradient.
A consequence worth internalising: a small gradient norm is not a stopping criterion. Classical optimisation stops when ‖∇L‖ < ε. Deep learning stops when the validation metric stops improving, which is a completely different event and usually happens while the gradient is still large. If you find yourself waiting for the gradient to vanish, you will wait a long time and gain nothing by it.

18 Exponentially weighted moving averages, and bias correction

Every optimiser after plain SGD is built from one primitive: a running average of something, kept cheaply. Learn the primitive on its own — with numbers, and with the correction it needs at the start — and momentum, RMSProp, Adam and AdamW all become one-line variations rather than four algorithms to memorise.

the recursionv₀ = 0 v_t = β·v_{t−1} + (1 − β)·θ_t unrolled — this is what makes it an "exponentially weighted" averagev_t = (1 − β)·[ θ_t + βθ_{t−1} + β²θ_{t−2} + … + β^{t−1}θ₁ ] The weights decay geometrically into the past, they sum to 1 − βᵗ (not to 1 — that is the bug below), and the whole history is kept in ONE number. The bias, and its exact correction

Start the recursion at v₀ = 0 and the first estimate is v₁ = (1 − β)·θ₁. With β = 0.98 that is 0.02·θ₁ — the average of a single observation, reported as two per cent of it. The cause is visible in the unrolled form: the weights sum to 1 − βᵗ, not to 1, so the estimate is systematically shrunk toward the zero it was initialised at. Divide by the actual weight and the bias is gone exactly:

bias correction — one division, and it is exact, not approximatev̂_t = v_t / (1 − βᵗ) If every θ_t = c, then v_t = c(1 − βᵗ) exactly, so v̂_t = c exactly for every t including t = 1. The correction is not a heuristic warm-up; it is the exact normalisation the estimator was missing. 20 Momentum, and Nesterov's correction

Momentum exists because of §16's picture. In a badly conditioned ravine the gradient points mostly across the valley, not along it, so consecutive gradients largely cancel in the stiff direction and largely agree in the shallow one. Averaging them keeps what agrees and cancels what does not. That is the whole idea, and everything else is bookkeeping about the constant.

the two conventions — READ THIS BEFORE COPYING A LEARNING RATEACCUMULATION form (what most frameworks implement): v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv EWMA form (what most lecture material writes): v ← βv + (1 − β)g θ ← θ − ηv Under a CONSTANT gradient g the two settle at different velocities: accumulation v → g/(1 − β) = 10g at β = 0.9, 100g at β = 0.99 EWMA v → g always Verified numerically: 3000 steps at g = 1, η = 0.1, gives a final step of −1.0000 for the accumulation form at β = 0.9 and −0.1000 for the EWMA form. The accumulation form multiplies your effective learning rate by 1/(1 − β).

The practical consequence is worth stating as a rule: when you turn momentum on in the accumulation form, or raise β, you have raised the effective learning rate by 1/(1−β) and you should expect to lower η. The standard combination η = 0.1, β = 0.9 has an effective terminal step of 1.0, not 0.1. Moving from β = 0.9 to β = 0.99 without changing η multiplies the terminal step by ten, and a run that then diverges was not "unstable with high momentum" — it was running at ten times the learning rate you thought.

What momentum buys, in one number

terminal velocity, and how long it takes to get thereaccumulation form, constant g: v_t = g·(1 − βᵗ)/(1 − β) → g/(1 − β) steps to reach 90% of terminal velocity: β = 0.9 → 22 steps β = 0.99 → 230 steps So momentum is NOT an instantaneous speed-up. It takes tens to hundreds of steps to spin up, which is one reason it interacts with warmup (§24) and one reason very short runs see less benefit than long ones.
in the shallow direction
Consistent gradients accumulate
Every step's gradient points the same way, so the velocity builds toward g/(1−β) and the effective step grows tenfold at β = 0.9. Progress along the valley floor speeds up by exactly the factor the ravine was costing you.
in the stiff direction
Alternating gradients cancel
Successive gradients flip sign, so consecutive terms in the average subtract. The oscillation is damped rather than amplified. Momentum improves the conditioning of the effective problem: the achievable rate improves from (κ−1)/(κ+1) to roughly (√κ−1)/(√κ+1), which at κ = 100 is 0.980 against 0.818.
at a saddle
It carries you through the flat part
The velocity is non-zero even where the gradient is, so the iterate keeps moving through a plateau instead of stalling in it. This is the same mechanism as the physical analogy: a ball with speed does not stop at a level patch.
at the bottom
It overshoots
Arriving at the minimum with velocity means going past it. The iterate oscillates about the optimum with a period set by β and η. This is the specific problem Nesterov's variant addresses, and it is also why momentum and a decaying schedule work well together.

Nesterov: look before you leap

Standard momentum computes the gradient where it currently is, then adds the momentum step. Nesterov's variant does it the other way round: take the momentum step first, and evaluate the gradient there. Since you are going to move by −ηβv regardless, the gradient at the destination is more relevant than the gradient at the origin.

the difference, preciselyclassical g = ∇L(θ) v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv Nesterov g = ∇L(θ − ηβv) v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv ↑ evaluated at the LOOKAHEAD point The lookahead makes it a CORRECTION rather than an ANTICIPATION. When the velocity is about to carry the iterate past the minimum, the gradient at the lookahead point already points back, and the overshoot is damped a step earlier than it would otherwise be. Frameworks implement an algebraically equivalent rewriting that avoids evaluating the gradient anywhere except the current point, so it costs nothing extra.

The gain is real but modest, and it is largest exactly where momentum is most aggressive — high β, sharp turns, the approach to a minimum. On a convex problem it improves the theoretical rate; on a neural network it is a free improvement that is worth taking and not worth agonising over. Turn it on; do not expect a miracle.

21 AdaGrad and RMSProp: one learning rate per coordinate

§16's bound is set by the largest curvature over all coordinates, so every coordinate is forced to take the step size that suits the stiffest one. The adaptive methods break that coupling. They give each parameter its own effective step, inferred from the only per-coordinate signal available for free: the size of that coordinate's own gradients so far.

AdaGrad — accumulate every squared gradient, foreverr ← r + g ⊙ g element-wise θ ← θ − η · g / (√r + ε) element-wise A coordinate with consistently large gradients accumulates a large r and takes SMALL steps. A coordinate that has rarely been touched keeps a small r and takes LARGE ones. The per-coordinate effective rate is η/√rᵢ, and it depends on that coordinate's own history and nothing else. RMSProp — the same, with a moving average instead of a sumr ← β₂·r + (1 − β₂)·g ⊙ g the §18 primitive, applied to g² θ ← θ − η · g / (√r + ε)

Why AdaGrad had to be replaced

r is a sum of non-negative terms, so it is monotonically increasing and unbounded. Under a roughly constant gradient magnitude g, after t steps r ≈ t·g², so the effective learning rate is η/(g√t) — it decays like 1/√t no matter what the loss surface is doing. On a convex problem that is exactly the schedule the theory wants and AdaGrad's guarantees are excellent. On a non-convex problem trained for a long time it means the effective learning rate reaches zero while there is still progress to be made, and the run stalls for reasons that have nothing to do with the loss.

RMSProp fixes exactly that one thing, by replacing the sum with §18's exponentially weighted average. The window is 1/(1−β₂) steps: at the usual β₂ = 0.999 that is a thousand steps of history, long enough to be a stable estimate of the gradient scale and short enough to track a scale that genuinely changes as training proceeds. Note also what the table's first row shows — with the average started at zero, RMSProp's first step is 1/√(1−β₂) = 31.6 times larger than its steady-state step. That start-up transient is a real problem, and §22 is where it gets fixed.

What ε is actually for, and why its default is what it is. Nominally ε prevents division by zero. Its real job is to bound the maximum step: the update is at most η·|g|/ε, so ε is the floor on the denominator and therefore the ceiling on the step. Typical values run from 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁶. Raising it makes the method behave more like plain SGD, because a large ε dominates √r and the per-coordinate scaling disappears — which is occasionally the right emergency fix for an adaptive method that is behaving erratically, and is worth knowing as a knob rather than a magic constant. Note also that some implementations place ε inside the square root, √(r + ε), which is a different function and differs materially when r is small.

Where the per-coordinate idea genuinely wins

  • Sparse features. A feature that appears in one example in ten thousand contributes a gradient one time in ten thousand. Under SGD its weight is effectively never trained. Under an adaptive method its r stays small, so the rare gradient it does receive is amplified. This is the setting AdaGrad was designed for and it is a large effect.
  • Embeddings and output layers. Same argument: token embeddings for rare tokens, and the rows of a large softmax matrix, are the archetypal sparse-gradient parameters. It is not a coincidence that adaptive methods dominate in language models.
  • Parameters with genuinely different scales. Biases, gains of normalisation layers, and weights all have different natural magnitudes and different gradient magnitudes. A per-coordinate rate handles that automatically; a single global rate handles it by compromise.
  • Where it does not obviously win: a well-conditioned, dense, well-normalised convolutional network trained with a good schedule. That is precisely the setting in which SGD with momentum remains competitive, and the reason the "just use Adam" advice has an exception.

22 Adam: both moving averages, and the bias correction that makes them work

Adam is momentum and RMSProp in the same algorithm, plus §18's bias correction applied to both. The combination is unremarkable; the correction is the part that has a surprise in it, and it is a surprise in the opposite direction from the one everybody expects.

the algorithm, completem₀ = 0, v₀ = 0, t = 0 on each step: t ← t + 1 g ← ∇_θ L the minibatch gradient m ← β₁·m + (1 − β₁)·g 1st moment: the direction v ← β₂·v + (1 − β₂)·g ⊙ g 2nd moment: the scale m̂ = m/(1 − β₁ᵗ) bias correction v̂ = v/(1 − β₂ᵗ) θ ← θ − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) defaults β₁ = 0.9 β₂ = 0.999 ε = 10⁻⁸ η = 10⁻³ The two betas are averaging over ~10 and ~1000 steps respectively: the DIRECTION is smoothed over a short window and the SCALE over a long one, which is the right asymmetry — direction changes fast, scale does not.

The bias correction: the surprise

The standard story is that bias correction stops the moving averages from "starting off too low". That is true of each average individually — and it is not what determines Adam's first step, because the update is a ratio and both numerator and denominator are biased. Work out the ratio at t = 1, where m₁ = (1−β₁)g and v₁ = (1−β₂)g²:

the uncorrected first step, exactlym₁/√v₁ = (1 − β₁)·g / √((1 − β₂)·g²) = (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) · sign(g) At the DEFAULTS: (1 − 0.9)/√(1 − 0.999) = 0.1/0.0316228 = 3.1623 = √10 So the uncorrected first step is not too SMALL. It is √10 times too LARGE, and the correction makes it exactly 1. Verified numerically: with a constant gradient g = 0.5, corrected Adam's step is −1.0000000 for every t from 1 to 6; uncorrected, the first step is −3.162276. and the sign of the error depends on the betas, which is why it must be exactβ₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 → 3.1623× too LARGE β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.99 → 1.0000× exactly right, by coincidence β₁ = 0.5, β₂ = 0.999 → 15.811× too LARGE β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.9 → 0.3162× too SMALL β₁ = 0.99, β₂ = 0.999 → 0.3162× too SMALL

This matters, and not only as a curiosity. A three-fold oversized step on the very first update, before any structure exists in the network, is exactly the kind of event that kills rectified units (§11) and knocks a run into a region it spends a thousand steps climbing out of. It is also part of why Adam and warmup (§24) are so often used together: warmup is a second, cruder defence against the same failure, and it is needed because bias correction alone does not cover the whole start-up transient — only its first few steps.

The other thing the correction buys: a step size you can reason about. With both corrections in place, m̂/√v̂ is a ratio of a mean to a root-mean-square of the same quantity, so it is dimensionless and of order 1. The update is therefore about η in magnitude, per parameter, regardless of the scale of the loss or of the gradients. Measured over 5 000 steps of a deliberately non-stationary noisy gradient stream, the largest value of |m̂/(√v̂ + ε)| observed was 1.0546. That is why η = 10⁻³ is a usable default across wildly different problems, in a way that no SGD learning rate ever is — and it is the single most practically important property Adam has. Multiply your loss by 1000 and SGD needs its learning rate divided by 1000; Adam does not notice.

What Adam is not

  • It is not a second-order method. v is an average of squared gradients, not of curvature. On a quadratic the two are related but not equal, and there are surfaces on which they point in opposite directions. Adam approximates a diagonal preconditioner using first-order information only; that is a useful thing and it is not the Hessian.
  • It does not remove the need for a schedule. The per-coordinate scaling adapts to the gradient magnitude, not to the phase of training. Every serious Adam recipe is Adam plus warmup plus decay, and §24 is not optional.
  • It is not always better. There is credible evidence that adaptive methods find solutions that generalise slightly worse than SGD with momentum on some problems, particularly in vision. The effect is real, small, and problem-dependent.
  • Its β₂ is not a free parameter at small batch size. With very noisy gradients, v̂ is a poor estimate of the true gradient scale and the resulting step is erratic. Raising β₂ toward 0.999 or beyond averages over more steps and helps; lowering it makes an already-noisy method noisier. This is the one beta that is worth touching.

23 AdamW: why L2 and weight decay stopped being the same thing

Under plain gradient descent, adding λ/2·‖θ‖² to the loss and multiplying the weights by (1 − ηλ) each step are algebraically identical operations. Under any adaptive method they are not, and the gap is large enough that fixing it produced a measurably better optimiser. This section is that one derivation.

under SGD the two really are the same — this is the identity everyone remembersL̃(θ) = L(θ) + (λ/2)‖θ‖² ⇒ ∇L̃ = ∇L + λθ θ ← θ − η(∇L + λθ) = (1 − ηλ)θ − η∇L ↑ a MULTIPLICATIVE shrink of every weight, by the same factor, applied before the usual step under Adam the penalty goes through the 1/√v̂ machinery, and the identity breaksCOUPLED (L2 in the loss — what "weight_decay" means in a plain Adam): g ← ∇L + λθ then m, v, and the update ηm̂/(√v̂ + ε) the decay term is DIVIDED BY √v̂ along with everything else, so the effective decay on coordinate i is ηλθᵢ/√v̂ᵢ — different for every coordinate, and INVERSELY proportional to that coordinate's own recent gradient magnitude. DECOUPLED (AdamW): g ← ∇L the penalty is NOT in g θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√v̂ + ε) − ηλθ added separately every coordinate decays at the same rate ηλ, exactly as it does under SGD, and the adaptive machinery never sees it.

What the coupling actually does, stated as the bug it is

Under coupled L2, the amount by which a weight is shrunk depends on how large that coordinate's recent gradients have been. A parameter with large gradients has a large √v̂, so its decay is divided down and it is barely regularised. A parameter with small gradients has a small √v̂, so its decay is amplified and it is regularised hard. Read that again: the parameters that matter most are penalised least, and the ones that matter least are penalised most. That is the opposite of what the regulariser was supposed to do, and it is not a small effect — √v̂ varies over orders of magnitude across a real network.

consequence 1
The two hyperparameters are entangled
Under coupled L2 the effective decay is ηλ/√v̂, so changing η changes the amount of regularisation. Tune the learning rate and you have silently retuned the weight decay, so the two must be searched jointly. Decoupled, the effective decay is ηλ, which is still coupled to η — some implementations divide it out so that λ is genuinely independent, and it is worth checking which one you have.
consequence 2
The regularisation is applied backwards
Large-gradient coordinates escape the penalty; small-gradient coordinates absorb it. There is no principled reason to want this, and it is a large part of why "Adam generalises worse than SGD" was observed before the cause was identified.
the fix
Move the term out of the gradient
One line. The decay is a function of the current parameters, not of the loss, so it is not a gradient and does not belong in g. Applying it directly to θ restores the uniform multiplicative shrink that made weight decay work in the first place.
and while you are there
Do not decay everything
Biases and the gains and offsets of normalisation layers should generally be excluded from weight decay. There is no reason to shrink a normalisation gain toward zero — doing so fights the layer's entire purpose — and biases carry no capacity worth regularising. Most well-tuned recipes exclude both; most quick implementations do not, and the difference is measurable.
The practical instruction, in one line. Use the optimiser named AdamW and set its decay explicitly; do not use the weight_decay argument of a plain Adam and expect it to mean the same thing. It does not: one is L2 folded into the gradient, the other is a decoupled shrink, and they will give you different models. This is a case where two functions with the same argument name do genuinely different arithmetic, which is exactly the kind of thing that is invisible in a diff.

24 Learning-rate schedules, and why warmup exists

A constant learning rate is wrong at both ends of a run. Early on, the parameters are random and the gradients are large and uninformative, so a big step does damage. Late on, the true gradient has shrunk toward zero while the sampling noise has not, so a big step just rattles the iterate around the minimum without ever settling. The schedule is the fix at both ends, and it is worth more tuning effort than the choice of optimiser.

Warmup, and the three separate reasons for it

Warmup is not a schedule; it is a prefix that any schedule can carry — a linear ramp from zero (or near zero) to the peak over the first few hundred to few thousand steps. It looks like superstition until you notice that it is defending against three genuinely different failures at once.

reason 1
The adaptive optimiser's variance estimate is garbage at first
Adam's v̂ is an average over roughly 1/(1−β₂) = 1000 steps. In the first few dozen steps it is an estimate from almost no data, so the per-coordinate step it produces is erratic in exactly the period when the model is most fragile. §22 showed the corrected first step is exactly right in expectation and says nothing about its variance. Warmup keeps η small until the estimate has settled.
reason 2
Early gradients are large and point nowhere useful
At step 0 the network is random. The gradient is large — it is telling you the output is wrong, which you knew — and its direction encodes essentially nothing about the data. Taking a full-size step on it moves the parameters a long way for no information, and §11 measured the cost: at a learning rate high enough to matter, warmup took the first hidden layer's dead fraction from 22% to 0% with no loss in final performance.
reason 3
Post-norm residual stacks are unstable at initialisation
Where a normalisation layer sits after the residual addition, the gradient at the bottom of a deep stack at initialisation scales with the depth, so a deep post-norm transformer genuinely cannot be trained at its target learning rate from step 0. Warmup was found empirically before this was understood, and §30 is where the mechanism lives — along with the architectural fix that makes warmup less necessary.
and the rule of thumb
Longer warmup for larger batches
The larger the batch, the larger the learning rate you intend to use (§26), and the more damage a full-size early step does. Warmup lengths in the low thousands of steps are ordinary for large-batch training and unnecessary for small-batch. Quote warmup in steps, never in epochs — the two differ by N/B.

A cosine schedule is defined relative to T, so the same code on a dataset ten times larger decays ten times more slowly in wall-clock terms. And a schedule that decays to zero at T is wrong if you stop at T/2 — you will stop while the rate is still half its peak, and the model will be measurably worse than one that was scheduled for the budget you actually used.

25 Choosing η: the range test, and reading the cliff

The learning rate is the hyperparameter that matters most, and it is also the one that can be found in a few minutes without a search. The range test runs a single short training pass while raising the learning rate exponentially, and reads the answer off the resulting curve. It works because §16 guarantees the failure is a cliff and not a slope.

the procedure1 Start from the initial parameters and a very small rate, e.g. η = 10⁻⁸. 2 Take one minibatch step. Record the loss. 3 Multiply η by a constant factor (so that it covers ~10⁻⁸ to ~10 in a few hundred steps) and repeat. 4 Stop when the loss exceeds a few times its best value so far. 5 Plot the loss against log η. Read off: · the region where the loss is FLAT — η too small to do anything · the region where it FALLS steeply — this is the useful band · the point where it turns UP sharply — η_div, the divergence point 6 Choose η about an ORDER OF MAGNITUDE below η_div, or equivalently at the steepest point of the descent — NOT at the minimum of the curve, which is already too close to the cliff.
why it works
The failure is a cliff, and cliffs are easy to find
§16 measured the transition: one per cent below 2/λ_max the iteration converges, one per cent above it it diverges. On a log axis that appears as a near-vertical wall, and a wall is a much easier thing to locate than an optimum.
why not simply take the minimum
The steepest-descent reading is a deliberate safety margin
The lowest point of the range-test curve sits close to the edge of the cliff, and a rate that survives a few hundred steps there need not survive a few hundred thousand. The steepest-descent point is typically three to ten times below it, and that gap is the margin. On a short, easy problem the margin is pure loss — the figure below measures both readings against a ground-truth sweep and, with an adaptive optimiser, the curve minimum comes out closer. Read the steepest-descent point as the peak of a schedule with warmup and decay (§24), which is where the margin earns its keep, rather than as a constant rate.
the catch

27 Mixed precision, loss scaling, and what actually overflows

Half-precision arithmetic halves the memory for activations and runs several times faster on hardware built for it. It also has a dynamic range so narrow that ordinary gradients fall off the bottom of it. Mixed precision is the set of three specific compromises that make it work anyway, and understanding which quantity lives in which format is the whole of it.

the three formats, exactly exponent mantissa max min normal min subnormal ε float32 (fp32) 8 bits 23 bits 3.403e+38 1.175e−38 1.401e−45 1.19e−07 float16 (fp16) 5 bits 10 bits 6.550e+04 6.104e−05 5.960e−08 9.77e−04 bfloat16 (bf16) 8 bits 7 bits 3.390e+38 1.175e−38 9.184e−41 7.81e−03 Read the two 16-bit rows against each other. bfloat16 keeps float32's EXPONENT and throws away mantissa; float16 keeps more mantissa and throws away RANGE. That single choice is why bfloat16 needs no loss scaling and float16 does.

What breaks, precisely

Gradients in a trained network are small. Values of 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻¹⁰ are entirely ordinary, and float16's smallest subnormal is 5.96×10⁻⁸. So a large fraction of the gradient distribution flushes to exactly zero — not "loses precision", but becomes the number 0, which contributes nothing to any update. The forward pass usually survives; the backward pass is where the format runs out.

why skip rather than clip
An overflowed gradient carries no information
If a gradient became inf, its value is lost — there is nothing to clip to. The only correct response is to discard the step and lower S. Skipping a handful of steps out of thousands costs nothing; applying a garbage update costs the run.
why fp32 master weights
Small updates vanish into large weights
fp16 has about three decimal digits of precision. A weight of 1.0 and an update of 10⁻⁴ add to exactly 1.0 in fp16 — the update is silently discarded, every step, forever. The master copy exists so that the accumulation happens where there is enough mantissa to hold it.
what stays in fp32 regardless
Reductions, and anything that sums many terms
Softmax denominators, loss reductions, normalisation-layer statistics, and attention score sums are accumulated in fp32 even in a 16-bit model, because summing thousands of 16-bit values loses precision catastrophically. Every serious implementation does this; it is why "the model is in fp16" is never literally true.
bfloat16
Same range as fp32, so none of this is needed
No loss scaling, no overflow handling, no skipped steps — the exponent range is identical to fp32, so anything representable in fp32 is representable in bf16 to within its coarser mantissa. The cost is precision: ε = 7.8×10⁻³ against fp16's 9.8×10⁻⁴, eight times worse. In practice the range matters far more than the mantissa, which is why bf16 has largely displaced fp16 where the hardware supports it.

28 Gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing

Two techniques for the same situation: the batch size you want does not fit on the device you have. Both trade time for memory, both are single-device, and neither changes the mathematics. Anything that needs a second device — data, tensor or pipeline parallelism, sharded optimiser state — is Distributed Training's and not this page's.

Gradient accumulation: a large batch, one micro-batch at a time

the identity it rests onThe loss is a MEAN over the batch, and the gradient of a mean is the mean of the gradients: ∇[ (1/B) ∑_{i=1}^{B} ℓᵢ ] = (1/B) ∑_{i=1}^{B} ∇ℓᵢ So a batch of B can be split into k micro-batches of B/k, their gradients summed, and the result is EXACTLY the gradient of the batch of B — not an approximation, an identity. the loopzero the gradient buffer for each of k micro-batches: forward, backward, ADD into the buffer ← activations for B/k only divide the buffer by k ← or scale each micro-loss by 1/k one optimiser step Memory: activations for B/k instead of B. Parameters, gradients and optimiser state are unchanged, so the saving applies only to the activation term. Time: k forward-backward passes instead of 1, so essentially k× the wall clock for the same arithmetic — the per-step overhead is amortised over less work.
The mistake that makes accumulation subtly wrong. Divide by k and everything is exact — unless the model contains a batch-normalisation layer. BatchNorm's statistics are computed over whatever is in the current forward pass, so k micro-batches of B/k give k different sets of statistics, none of which is the statistic the batch of B would have produced. The gradient is then genuinely different, not just numerically. Dropout has the same property in a milder form. Accumulation is exact for a model whose forward pass is a function of each example independently, and only for such a model — which is one more entry in the long list of reasons sequence models use LayerNorm.

Activation checkpointing: recompute instead of store

§08 established that backpropagation is a schedule — store every node, or recompute some. Checkpointing takes the second option deliberately. Split the network into s segments, store only the activation at each segment boundary, and during the backward pass recompute a segment's interior from its boundary just before it is needed.

the arithmetic, for a chain of L layers split into s equal segmentsmemory s boundaries + the interior of ONE segment at a time = O(s + L/s) activations instead of O(L) minimised at s = √L, giving O(√L) time each segment's forward pass is done TWICE — once on the way forward and once during the backward sweep — so one extra forward pass over the whole network: 4 units of arithmetic per step instead of 3 (§01's rule), about a 33% slowdown. a concrete case: L = 64 layersstore everything 64 activations, 3.0× forward cost s = 8 segments 8 + 8 = 16 activations (a 4.0× saving), ~4.0× forward s = √64 = 8 the optimum for this L store nothing 1 activation, but recomputation is quadratic — do not

The trade is usually worth taking, because the constraint is almost never arithmetic. A 33% slowdown that lets you quadruple the batch size, or fit a model that otherwise does not fit at all, is a good deal. It also composes with everything else: checkpointing and accumulation together let a single device train at an effective batch size it could not hold by two independent factors.

The whole single-device memory toolkit, in the order to reach for it. (1) Reduce the batch size — free, and costs only gradient noise (§15). (2) Mixed precision (§27) — halves the activation term and usually makes the run faster. (3) Gradient accumulation — recovers the effective batch size, costs wall clock. (4) Activation checkpointing — buys a factor of √L on the activation term for a third more arithmetic. (5) A more frugal optimiser: momentum instead of Adam saves 4P bytes, plain SGD saves 8P (§19). (6) Only then: a second device, and Distributed Training.

29 Batch normalisation, and the train/eval asymmetry that causes most of its bugs

§12 and §13 chose an initialisation so that the activation statistics start in a useful range, and then hoped they would stay there. Normalisation layers stop hoping. They enforce the property at every layer at every step, and that one change made deep networks dramatically easier to train — at the cost of a layer that behaves differently in training and in inference, which is the single most common source of the bug where a model scores 99% during training and 50% when deployed.

bug 1 — the classic
Forgetting to switch to eval mode
Evaluate with the layer still in training mode and the statistics come from the evaluation batch. Metrics computed this way leak information between test examples, are wildly unstable at small batch, and collapse to β entirely at batch size 1. This single mistake is responsible for an enormous share of "my model works in the notebook and fails in production" reports.
bug 2
Small batches
The statistics are estimated from N samples, so their own noise scales as 1/√N. At N = 2 or 4 that noise is large enough to dominate, and the layer actively hurts. This is the reason gradient accumulation (§28) does not rescue a BatchNorm model, and the reason detection and segmentation models — which use tiny batches because their inputs are enormous — moved to other normalisations.
bug 3
A distribution shift the running statistics never saw
The running estimates describe the training distribution. Deploy on inputs whose statistics differ and the layer normalises by the wrong numbers, silently. Fine-tuning on a new domain without letting the statistics re-adapt is the same failure in a different costume.
and the free win
Fold it away after training
At inference the layer is affine with fixed coefficients, so it can be absorbed into the preceding linear layer: with W′ = γ⊙W/σ and b′ = γ⊙(b − μ)/σ + β, the pair computes exactly XW′ + b′. Verified numerically: maximum discrepancy 1.8×10⁻¹⁵, i.e. floating-point noise. The layer's entire inference cost disappears.

Why it works — and the explanation that turned out to be wrong

The original account was "internal covariate shift": each layer's input distribution keeps changing as the layers below it update, so each layer is chasing a moving target, and normalising holds it still. It is an appealing story and the evidence has not been kind to it. Later work found that networks with normalisation do not necessarily have more stable layer-input distributions, and that deliberately injecting distributional noise after the normalisation does not undo the benefit. The better-supported account is that normalisation smooths the loss landscape — it reduces the curvature and the variability of the gradients, which is §16's language for "raises the learning rate you are allowed to use". Three mechanisms that are easy to state and hold up:

  • It decouples the weight scale from the function. Multiply a layer's weights by c and the pre-activations scale by c — but the normalisation divides it straight back out, so the layer's output is unchanged. The gradient with respect to those weights, meanwhile, scales as 1/c. A normalised layer is therefore automatically protected from a badly scaled initialisation, which is why normalised networks are so much less sensitive to §14's failures.
  • It improves the conditioning directly. Zero-centring and unit-scaling every feature is precisely the input standardisation of §16 applied at every layer instead of only the first, and §16 showed that the condition number is what sets the achievable rate.
  • It regularises, as a side effect. Because a training-mode output depends on the other examples in the batch, each example's representation carries a noise term drawn from the batch composition. That is a stochastic perturbation of the same character as dropout, and it is why adding BatchNorm often lets you reduce other regularisation. It is also why the effect weakens as the batch grows.

31 Regularisation: what it actually is, and the whole map

A regulariser is any modification intended to reduce the generalisation error but not the training error. That definition is worth taking literally, because it covers a much wider set of things than the penalty terms — the early stop, the extra data, the noise, the architecture choice — and because it tells you the test: if it improved your training loss, whatever it did, it was not regularising.

the classical form, and what the λ is tradingJ̃(θ) = J(θ) + λ·Ω(θ) Ω encodes a PREFERENCE among the parameter settings that fit the data equally well. λ = 0 expresses no preference; λ → ∞ ignores the data entirely. Every useful value is in between, and the value is found on a VALIDATION set, because by construction the training loss is monotone increasing in λ.
The framing that makes the whole chapter make sense. Regularisation is not "making the model smaller". An over-parameterised, appropriately regularised model reliably beats a correctly-sized unregularised one, which is why the practical recipe is take a model that is too big and regularise it, rather than search for a model of exactly the right size. The reason is that "the right size" was never a real thing: the true data-generating process is not in your function class at any size, so there is no capacity at which the bias vanishes. 33 Dropout: an exponential ensemble, and the one line that makes it cheap

Dropout deletes a random subset of the units on every forward pass. Two readings of what that accomplishes, and both are useful: it trains an ensemble of exponentially many networks with shared weights, and it forbids any unit from relying on the presence of any other unit. The first reading explains the inference rule; the second explains why it works.

trainingsample a mask μ, one binary entry per unit, independently, P(μⱼ = 1) = p h ← (h ⊙ μ) / p ← INVERTED dropout typical p: 0.5 for hidden units, 0.8 for inputs (i.e. drop 20%) inferenceh ← h nothing at all The division by p at TRAINING time is what makes inference free. Without it, the expected activation during training is p·h and at inference it is h — a factor of 1/p mismatch that compounds through every layer. Two ways to fix it; inverted dropout puts the correction where it costs nothing at deployment.

Dropout works despite that because the gradient is an average over many masks and many examples, not because any individual forward pass is close to the truth.

Why the inference rule is an approximation, and why it is a good one

The exact ensemble prediction would average over all 2ⁿ masks, which is intractable — n = 100 units gives 10³⁰ networks. What the weight-scaling rule computes instead is the prediction of the single network with all units present and the weights scaled. For a model with no non-linearity that is exactly the geometric mean of the ensemble's predictions; with non-linearities it is an approximation with no error bound, and it is an approximation that works extremely well in practice. If you need the ensemble properly — for a calibrated uncertainty estimate rather than a point prediction — the honest method is to keep dropout on at inference and average over 10 to 20 sampled masks, which also gives you a spread to report. That is more expensive by a factor of 10 to 20 and it is a genuinely different, better-calibrated model.

what it prevents
Co-adaptation
Without dropout, unit A can learn to be useful only in combination with unit B — a brittle arrangement that fits the training set and does not transfer. With dropout, B is absent half the time, so A must be independently useful. Each unit is pushed toward a feature that stands on its own.
the interaction nobody mentions
Dropout and BatchNorm fight
Dropout changes the variance of its output between training and inference; BatchNorm's running statistics are estimated under the training-time variance and applied under the inference-time one. The mismatch degrades both. The common resolutions are to put dropout only after the last normalisation layer, or to use one and not the other — which is why modern convolutional networks with BatchNorm typically use little or no dropout.
where to put it
On the wide layers, not everywhere
Dropout is most useful where the parameters are: the large fully-connected layers, and the residual branches of a transformer block. Applying it to a narrow layer removes a large fraction of a small amount of information and mostly just adds noise. Applying it to the input is a mild form of data augmentation and usually wants a much smaller rate.
the cost
Slower training, and it is not free capacity
A network trained with p = 0.5 has, in effect, half the units available on each pass, so it typically needs to be wider and to train for longer to reach the same training loss. Turning dropout on and changing nothing else usually produces a worse model, not a better one.
The mask, the ensemble, and why the scaling factor is where it is the histogram is the whole argument for inverted dropout: with the division at training time the two distributions have the same mean, without it they are a factor of p apart

34 Early stopping, and why it is a regulariser rather than a shortcut

Stop when the validation error stops improving. It is the cheapest regulariser there is — it saves compute rather than costing it — it needs no hyperparameter search, and it has a precise relationship to L2 that turns "stopping early" from a heuristic into a statement about where in parameter space you end up.

the algorithm, with the details that matterbest ← ∞; patience_left ← P; θ_best ← θ₀ every k steps: v ← validation metric if v < best − δ: best ← v; θ_best ← copy of θ; patience_left ← P else: patience_left ← patience_left − 1 if patience_left = 0: stop, and RETURN θ_best The two things people get wrong: · returning the LAST parameters instead of the BEST ones — which throws away the entire point, since the last P checks were by construction worse; · a patience of 1, which stops on the first noisy uptick. Validation loss is an estimate and it fluctuates; P should be several checks' worth.

The equivalence to L2, and how far it goes

Run gradient descent on the quadratic model of §32 from w = 0. In the eigenbasis each coordinate evolves independently: wᵢ(τ) = [1 − (1 − ηλᵢ)^{τ}]·w*ᵢ. Compare with L2's w̃ᵢ = [λᵢ/(λᵢ + α)]·w*ᵢ. Both are the unregularised optimum multiplied by a factor between 0 and 1, and both shrink the low-curvature directions hardest — early stopping because those directions learn slowly, L2 because those directions cost little to give up. Matching the two factors gives the classical correspondence:

the advantage that matters
It tunes its own strength, in one run
Finding a good α for L2 means training the model several times over. Early stopping finds the equivalent amount of regularisation during the single run you were going to do anyway, by watching a validation curve. That is why it is used essentially always, and used alongside the other regularisers rather than instead of them.
the cost
A validation set, and periodic evaluation
Data held out of training, plus the compute for an evaluation pass every k steps. Both are small. The subtler cost is that the validation set is now being optimised against — through the stopping decision — so the final validation number is optimistically biased and is no longer a clean estimate of test performance.
the question everyone asks
Should I retrain on train + validation afterwards?
Two options, neither obviously right. Retrain from scratch for the number of steps the first run took — but that number was for a smaller dataset and is now wrong. Or continue training the stopped model on the combined data until its loss on the former validation set falls to the training loss it had — but that may never happen. Retraining from scratch on the same number of epochs is the usual compromise.
the geometric picture
A budget on distance travelled
Gradient descent from θ₀ can move at most ∑ηₜ‖g‖ in total, so stopping at step τ is a hard constraint confining θ to a ball around θ₀. That is why initialisation and early stopping interact: the initialisation is the centre of the ball that early stopping is drawing.

35 Data augmentation, noise injection, and label smoothing

The best way to make a model generalise is more data. The second best is to manufacture more data by applying transformations you know leave the label unchanged. That "you know" is the whole content: an augmentation is a piece of prior knowledge about the task, injected in the only form a network can absorb — examples.

what an augmentation assertsChoose a family of transformations T with the property f*( T(x) ) = f*( x ) for the TRUE function f* and train on (T(x), y) pairs. The network is being shown, empirically, an invariance that you could otherwise only build into the architecture. The condition is not decoration. Horizontal flip is label-preserving for "cat vs dog" and DESTROYS "b vs d", "6 vs 9" and any task involving text or chirality. Every augmentation is a claim about the task, and a wrong claim teaches the model something false with full confidence.
FamilyExamplesThe invariance it assertsWhere it goes wrong

Two rules that prevent most augmentation bugs. (1) Augment the training split only. A validation or test set that has been augmented is measuring performance on a distribution you will never see, and if the augmentation is random the number is not even reproducible. The one deliberate exception is test-time augmentation, where several transformed copies are predicted and averaged on purpose — and that is a different model, which must be reported as such. (2) Augment inside the training loop, not once beforehand. A fixed set of pre-generated variants is a slightly larger finite dataset; sampling a fresh transformation each epoch is an effectively infinite one, and it is the second that gives most of the benefit.

Noise injection, and where it lands

The three places noise can be added correspond to three different claims, and they are worth keeping distinct because they have different effects.

  • To the inputs. Says: the function should be flat around each training point. For a linear model under squared error this is exactly L2 on the weights, with the penalty proportional to the noise variance — one of the very few cases where two regularisers are provably the same thing.
  • To the weights. Says: the solution should be flat — a small perturbation of the parameters should barely change the function. This is a preference for wide minima, stated directly instead of hoped for, and it is the same preference that minibatch noise (§15) expresses by accident.
  • To the labels. Says: some of the labels are wrong, which is true of every real dataset. Label smoothing is the deterministic version — instead of flipping ε of the labels, it moves every target ε of the way toward uniform, which has the same effect on the expected loss with none of the variance.

36 Gradient clipping: capping the magnitude, keeping the direction

§17's cliffs produce gradients that are enormous and, crucially, correctly oriented. The direction is fine; it is the length that is meaningless, because the linear model the gradient represents is valid over a neighbourhood far smaller than the step it is asking for. Clipping caps the length and is the standard fix — and the choice between the two ways of doing it is not cosmetic.

clip by NORM — rescale the whole vector, preserving direction exactlyif ‖g‖₂ > c: g ← g · c/‖g‖₂ The direction is unchanged, bit for bit. The step is capped at c. clip by VALUE — clamp each coordinate independentlygᵢ ← clip(gᵢ, −c, +c) for every i Cheaper, needs no reduction across parameters, and CHANGES THE DIRECTION. the difference, on one gradientg = [0.9, 100.0], ‖g‖₂ = 100.00405 clip by value at 1.0: [0.9, 1.0] the two coordinates were 111× apart and are now nearly equal; the direction has rotated by 41.47°. clip by norm at 1.0: [0.00899964, 0.99995950] the ratio 0.009 : 1 is exactly the original 0.9 : 100; the direction has rotated by 0.00°.

Forty-one degrees is not a rounding error. Clipping by value on that gradient produces a step pointing roughly along the diagonal when the gradient was pointing almost entirely along the second axis. On a cliff — where the whole reason you are clipping is that one direction has become enormous — clipping by value redistributes the step into directions the gradient was not asking for at all. Clip by norm. Clipping by value exists because it is cheaper and needs no cross-parameter reduction, and there are settings where that matters; correctness is not one of them.

where it is essential
Recurrent networks
§09: the same weight matrix applied at every timestep makes the product λᵀ exactly, so the gradient really can grow by orders of magnitude within one sequence. Clipping is not a nicety here, it is the reason the model trains at all — and it was proposed for precisely this setting.
where it is standard
Large transformer training
A global norm clip at 1.0 is close to universal in large-model recipes. The gradients are almost never large enough to trigger it, and when they are — a bad batch, a numerical event, a rare token — the clip converts a destroyed run into an ordinary step.
how to choose c
Log the norm before you clip
Record ‖g‖ every step for a few hundred steps and look at the distribution. Set c somewhere around the high percentiles — so that it fires on the tail and not on the body. A clip that fires on most steps is not clipping, it is a learning-rate schedule with a strange shape, and it is silently normalising away all the magnitude information in the gradient.
what it does not fix
Vanishing gradients
Clipping bounds the gradient from above only. A gradient of 10⁻²⁰ is unaffected by any clip. Exploding and vanishing gradients are two faces of §09's product and they have completely different fixes: clipping for one, initialisation, normalisation and skip connections for the other.
Clipping by value against clipping by norm: what each one does to the direction the angle readout is the point — one of these two numbers is always exactly zero and the other is not

sample correctly
Log-uniform for anything multiplicative
Learning rate, weight decay and ε matter by ratio, not by difference: the gap between 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁴ is the same size as the gap between 10⁻² and 10⁻¹. Sample log₁₀η ~ U(−5, 0), not η ~ U(10⁻⁵, 1) — the second draws 90% of its samples above 0.1 and effectively never tests anything small.

coarse to fine
Wide and short, then narrow and long
First pass: a wide range, few configurations, short runs — you are locating the region, not the optimum. Second pass: resample around the best region, longer runs. Third if needed. This finds a good setting for a fraction of the cost of one exhaustive pass, and it is what successive-halving and its relatives automate.
the boundary check
If the best value is at the edge, the range was wrong
A search whose winner sits at the smallest or largest value tried has told you the optimum is outside the range. Extend and re-run rather than reporting the boundary as the answer — this is the single most common way a search reports a wrong number with a straight face.

40 What to remember, and the mistakes that cost the most

The compressed version of the optimisation, then the errors worth recognising on sight.

Twelve sentences

  1. Training minimises a surrogate of an estimate of what you want. Empirical risk stands in for risk, and a differentiable loss stands in for the metric. Both substitutions are deliberate and both have costs; the training loss is the one number guaranteed to fall and therefore the one that carries no information.
  2. Backpropagation is reverse-mode automatic differentiation: a specific dynamic-programming schedule on the chain rule, costing one sweep per output. Because the loss is one number and the parameters are millions, that is a factor of P cheaper than the alternative — and it buys the speed with memory, since every intermediate must be kept alive.
  3. The backward pass is a product of Jacobians, so it is multiplicative in depth. A per-layer factor of 0.9 becomes 2.7×10⁻⁵ over a hundred layers and 1.1 becomes 1.4×10⁴. There is no third option, and nothing in the architecture holds the factor at 1 by itself.
  4. Initialisation is the second-moment algebra that holds it at 1 at step 0. The rectifier rule is the fan-in rule times two, and the two is there because a rectifier throws away half the signal. The invariant preserved is 𝔼[a²] = 1, not the variance — a correctly initialised rectifier network has an activation variance of 1 − 1/π = 0.6817, and that is the right answer.
  5. Saturation is a property of the activation and the pre-activation distribution jointly. The honest per-layer figure of merit is c·𝔼[g′(z)²], which should be 1: it is 1.0004 for a rectifier at any scale, 0.46 for tanh at unit variance, and 0.045 for a sigmoid — which over twenty layers is 3×10⁻¹⁴ and is the whole vanishing-gradient story.
  6. A dead rectified unit in the first hidden layer is a fixed point: zero gradient and fixed inputs. Deeper dead units recover, because their inputs keep moving. Dying units are a symptom of the learning rate, and warmup fixes them almost completely for free.
  7. The learning rate is bounded by 2/λ_max and the speed is set by λ_min. One stiff direction caps the step for every direction — which is the entire motivation for per-coordinate methods — and the transition at the bound is a cliff, not a slope, which is what makes the range test possible.
  8. Minibatch noise falls as 1/√B, so every doubling of the batch buys 1.41× less noise for 2× the cost, forever. Large batches are not efficient; they are a memory-bandwidth compromise. And the noise is not purely a cost — it escapes saddles and biases the trajectory toward wider minima.
  9. Every optimiser after SGD is built from one moving average, and bias correction is exact rather than approximate. Adam's uncorrected first step is (1−β₁)/√(1−β₂) = √10 = 3.16 times too large at the default betas, not too small — and the sign of that error flips depending on the betas, which is why the correction must be the exact formula and not a warm-up hack.
  10. L2 and weight decay are the same operation under SGD and different operations under Adam. Coupled, the decay a coordinate receives is inversely proportional to its own gradient magnitude — measured across coordinates spanning four decades, a spread of 5.6×10³ — so the parameters that matter most are penalised least. Decoupled, it is flat. Use AdamW.
  11. BatchNorm is a different function in training and in inference. In training mode its output for one example depends on the other examples in the batch; at batch size 1 the output is exactly β and the input has been annihilated. LayerNorm reduces over features instead and has none of these properties, which is why sequence models use it.
  12. Before tuning anything, overfit ten examples to zero loss. If that fails it is a bug and no hyperparameter will fix it. If it succeeds, the model is wired correctly and everything else on this page is now applicable.

Pitfalls, in rough order of how much time they cost

  • Forgetting to switch to evaluation mode. BatchNorm uses batch statistics, dropout keeps dropping, and the deployed metric bears no relationship to the validation one. Costs days and always looks like something else (§29).
  • Tuning a hyperparameter on top of a data bug. The ladder in §39 exists for this. Check the initial loss against log K and overfit ten examples first.
  • Copying a learning rate across a momentum convention. v ← βv + g and v ← βv + (1−β)g differ by 1/(1−β) — a factor of ten at β = 0.9. The run that "diverges with momentum" was running at ten times the rate you thought (§20).
  • Copying a schedule without converting the units. Warmup and decay are quoted sometimes in steps and sometimes in epochs, and they differ by N/B. A cosine schedule stopped at half its budget stops at half its peak rate (§24).
  • Using Adam(weight_decay=…) and believing it means weight decay. It means coupled L2, which is a different algorithm with a different effect on every coordinate. Use AdamW (§23).
  • Changing the batch size without changing the learning rate. Two things moved silently: the gradient noise and the number of updates per epoch (§26).
  • Not shuffling, or shuffling once. A saw-tooth of period exactly one epoch is this and nothing else (§15).
  • Clipping by value instead of by norm. On the gradient [0.9, 100] a value clip rotates the update by 41.5°; a norm clip rotates it by 0°. The direction was never the problem (§36).
  • Weight-decaying the biases and the normalisation gains. Shrinking a normalisation gain toward zero fights the layer's entire purpose. Exclude them by name (§32).
  • Gradient accumulation with BatchNorm in the model. The micro-batches produce different statistics from the batch they are supposed to reconstitute, so the identity that makes accumulation exact does not hold (§28).
  • Reading the last checkpoint from an early-stopping run instead of the best one. The last P evaluations were, by construction, worse (§34).
  • Treating a small gradient norm as convergence. Successful runs routinely end with a large and even growing gradient. Stop on the validation metric (§17).
  • Adding regularisation to a model that is underfitting. The diagnosis comes first; §38's table separates the cases that look identical in the loss curve.
  • Consulting the test set forty times during a search. It is now a validation set and its number is optimistic. Hold one out and touch it once (§02).
