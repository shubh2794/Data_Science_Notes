09 Vanishing and exploding gradients, quantitatively

The backward recursion of §05 multiplies. Multiplying L numbers together does one of three things — it converges to zero, it diverges, or it stays put — and only the third is useful. Depth alone is enough to cause the problem, with no bad luck and no bad data, and the fact that it is multiplicative is why it is a cliff rather than a slope: fifty layers do not make training fifty times harder, they make the gradient 2⁵⁰ times wrong.

The product

unroll the recursion of §05 all the way down∂L/∂A⁽⁰⁾ = Δ⁽ᴸ⁾ · (W⁽ᴸ⁾)ᵀ · D⁽ᴸ⁻¹⁾ · (W⁽ᴸ⁻¹⁾)ᵀ · D⁽ᴸ⁻²⁾ · … · D⁽¹⁾ · (W⁽¹⁾)ᵀ where D⁽ˡ⁾ = diag( g′(Z⁽ˡ⁾) ) is the DIAGONAL gate the activation contributes. Each layer's Jacobian is J⁽ˡ⁾ = D⁽ˡ⁾ (W⁽ˡ⁾)ᵀ, and the gradient reaching the bottom is one long product of them. the bound that explains everything‖∂L/∂A⁽⁰⁾‖ ≤ ‖Δ⁽ᴸ⁾‖ · ∏_{l=1}^{L} ‖J⁽ˡ⁾‖₂ ‖·‖₂ is the SPECTRAL norm: the largest factor by which the matrix can stretch any vector, equal to its largest SINGULAR value. If every layer's spectral norm is c, the bound is cᴸ: c = 0.9, L = 100 → 2.66e−05 c = 1.1, L = 100 → 1.38e+04 c = 0.5, L = 100 → 7.89e−31 c = 2.0, L = 100 → 1.27e+30 There is no third option. c must be held at 1 to within a few per cent, and NOTHING in the architecture does that by itself.

The reason this is not a bound you can shrug off is that c is very nearly determined by two numbers you already chose. For an n × n weight matrix with independent entries of standard deviation σ, the spectral norm concentrates tightly at 2σ√n — a measured 1.99 for σ = 1/√n at width 1024, against a prediction of exactly 2. Multiply in a rectifier's gate, which zeroes about half the coordinates, and the typical per-layer factor lands near σ√n. Setting that to 1 gives σ = 1/√n… which is not the rule §13 arrives at, and the discrepancy is instructive: the spectral norm bounds the worst-case direction, while the variance argument tracks the typical one, and the typical direction is what actually matters for a gradient that is not adversarially aligned.

Why the recurrent case is worse, and why residual connections fix the feedforward one

A feedforward network uses a different matrix at each layer, so the log of the per-layer factor performs a random walk: it drifts, but the drift is slow and the variance grows only as √L. A recurrent network applies the same matrix at every step, so the factors are perfectly correlated and the product is a matrix power, growing like ρᴸ — governed by one number rather than by a random walk. (That is the asymptotic rate, not the norm at finite L: a non-normal matrix amplifies for a long transient even when ρ < 1, and RNNs & LSTMs measures a shear with ρ = 0.9 that still grows 35× before it begins to contract.) That is why recurrence hit this wall first and hardest, and why the gating mechanisms of an LSTM exist: they create a path along which the effective factor is 1 by construction.

The feedforward fix is the same idea with less machinery. If a block computes h + F(h) rather than F(h), its Jacobian is I + ∂F/∂h — the identity plus something small — and the product of L such Jacobians expands into a sum whose leading term is 1. The gradient reaches the bottom along the identity path regardless of what the residual branches do. That is Residual & Skip Connections, and it is the reason depth stopped being a research problem.

The tempting shortcut. "Vanishing gradients mean training is slow." They mean the early layers do not train at all while the late ones train normally. The loss does go down — the top of the network is perfectly healthy — so the run looks like it is working. What you actually have is a shallow network with a large randomly-initialised feature extractor bolted underneath it, and no amount of extra epochs will fix it, because the gradient reaching the bottom is smaller than the floating-point noise on the weights there. The diagnostic is the per-layer gradient norm, printed once per hundred steps; §39.

The row that deserves a second look is the sigmoid's. Its maximum derivative is 0.25, at z = 0, which is the best it will ever do. A ten-layer sigmoid network attenuates its gradient by 4¹⁰ ≈ 10⁶ in the most favourable case imaginable — every single unit sitting exactly at its most sensitive point. In any realistic network the units are spread out and the true factor is far worse. No initialisation scheme can rescue this, because the problem is not the scale of the weights, it is the shape of g′.

Where the z values actually sit is the whole story

Saturation is not a property of the activation function; it is a property of the joint state of the activation and the pre-activation distribution. A tanh unit with z ~ N(0, 1) has an expected derivative of 0.606 and is perfectly healthy. The same unit with z ~ N(0, 25) has an expected derivative of 0.157 — a factor of 3.9 worse per layer, which over twenty layers compounds to a factor of 5×10¹¹ (4×10⁻⁵ of the gradient survives in the first case and 8×10⁻¹⁷ in the second). The number that actually decides it

§12 derives the backward recursion in full; the one line worth having now is that the variance of the backward signal is multiplied, per layer, by n·σ_w²·𝔼[g′(z)²]. The weight rules of §13 exist to make the first two factors cancel the third: they set n·σ_w² = c, with c = 2 for the rectifier family and c = 1 for everything else. So the honest per-layer figure of merit is c·𝔼[g′(z)²], and it should equal 1. Integrated over z ~ 𝒩(0, sd²):

Four things fall out of that table that no amount of curve-staring gives you.

  • The rectifier is scale-free. Its factor is 1.0004 at every width of pre-activation distribution, because relu′ depends only on the sign of z. Rescale the whole network and a rectifier does not notice. Nothing else in the table has that property, and it is a large part of why rectifiers won.
  • The sigmoid is beyond rescuing by initialisation. Even at its best it is 0.056 per layer, and over twenty layers that is 3×10⁻¹⁴. You could fix it by using c = 1/0.056 ≈ 18 instead of 1 — nobody does, because the resulting weights are so large that the units immediately saturate and the analysis stops holding. This is the vanishing-gradient problem, and it is a property of the function.
  • The rectifier rule is only approximately right for the smooth units. GELU at 0.91 is slightly lossy, SiLU at 0.76 noticeably so, and ELU at 1.34 actually amplifies. All three are close enough that a normalisation layer or a residual path absorbs the difference, which is precisely why these units became usable only once those existed.
  • tanh needs a gain above 1, and everyone knows it. At c = 1 and sd = 1 the factor is 0.46. Frameworks ship a per-activation "gain" for exactly this reason, and the value quoted for tanh — around 5/3, so c ≈ 2.8 — is what makes the factor come out near 1. A directly measured version: a 30-layer tanh stack at c = 1 settles at an activation variance of 0.019, at c = 2 at 0.31, and at gain 5/3 at 0.42.

That drift is invisible in the loss and obvious in a histogram of z.

11 The dying ReLU, as an optimisation pathology

A rectified unit whose pre-activation is negative for every example in the training set outputs zero for every example, receives exactly zero gradient for every example, and is therefore frozen forever. It is not slow, it is not attenuated — it is removed from the model, and no optimiser will bring it back. This is the one failure mode on this page that is genuinely irreversible.

The mechanism, in three lines

why the freeze is permanentunit j is dead ⟺ wⱼ·xᵢ + bⱼ < 0 for every training example i ⟹ aᵢⱼ = 0 for all i (contributes nothing forward) ⟹ relu′(zᵢⱼ) = 0 for all i (receives nothing backward) ⟹ ∂L/∂wⱼ = 0 and ∂L/∂bⱼ = 0 exactly ⟹ the update is 0, so the hyperplane never moves, so the unit is dead at step t + 1 for exactly the same reason. It is a FIXED POINT of the training dynamics. Compare a saturated sigmoid, whose derivative is 1.8e−02 rather than 0: tiny, but not zero, so the unit drifts and can in principle recover. Zero is a different kind of number.
The step from "zero gradient" to "permanently dead" only closes in the FIRST hidden layer. The argument above needs both halves: the unit's weights stop moving, and its inputs stop moving. In the first hidden layer the inputs are the data, which never change, so both hold and the unit is finished. In any deeper layer the inputs are the previous layer's activations, which keep changing — so a unit with frozen weights can still find its pre-activation pushed back above zero by the layer below it. Measured on the figure at the end of this section: with a plain rectifier at a learning rate high enough to do damage, the first layer's dead count is monotone non-decreasing for the entire run, while the second layer's rises and falls repeatedly. Both are losing capacity right now; only the first loss is irreversible. This distinction is missing from most tellings, and it is why "dying ReLU" is a first-layer and input-layer problem far more than a deep-layer one.

How units die

Almost always the same way: one oversized update. The gradient ∂L/∂b_j is a sum over the batch of the deltas reaching that unit; if the learning rate is too large, a single step can push b_j far enough negative that z is below zero for the entire dataset. The unit was alive at step t, participated in a large update, and is dead at step t + 1. Two consequences that are worth stating plainly.

  • Dying ReLUs are a symptom of the learning rate, not of the activation. The activation determines that the failure is permanent; the learning rate determines whether it happens. Measured on the figure below, on one fixed problem: at η = 1 nothing dies, at η = 4 the first hidden layer ends 20% dead and the second 41%, and at η = 6.3 the network is 94% dead and the loss has snapped back to exactly log 2. The whole transition happens over a factor of six in one hyperparameter.
  • Warmup fixes it almost completely, and costs nothing. The same run at η = 4 with 200 steps of linear warmup ends with 0% of the first layer dead instead of 22%, and its final loss is 4.3×10⁻⁴ against 2.5×10⁻⁴ — statistically the same model, with none of the damage. §24.
  • In the first hidden layer they are cumulative and monotone. That layer's dead fraction never goes down under plain ReLU, which makes it a uniquely good diagnostic: it is a one-way ratchet, so a slow climb is visible long before the loss curve shows anything. Deeper layers are noisier for the reason in the callout above, so watch the first one.
first
Lower the learning rate, or add warmup
The oversized early updates are what kill units, and §24's warmup exists precisely to make the first few hundred steps small. This fixes the cause rather than the symptom, and it costs nothing.
second
Give the negative side a slope
Leaky ReLU (α = 0.01), PReLU (α learned), ELU, GELU, SiLU — all of them have a non-zero derivative below zero, so the freeze becomes an attenuation. The unit can climb back out. This makes the failure recoverable rather than preventing it.
third — and it did not work here
Initialise the bias slightly positive
The standard advice: set b = 0.1 instead of 0 so every unit starts on the active side of its own hyperplane. Measured on the figure below, it made things worse — at a learning rate high enough to kill units, going from b = 0 to b = 0.3 took the final dead fraction from 5.5% to 32%. The reason is not mysterious: starting every unit active means every unit takes the oversized step, so more of them are available to be killed by it. A positive bias helps when the problem is that units start dead. It does not help when the problem is that the learning rate kills them, and it is the second problem you almost always have.
and note
A little death is normal and useful
A trained rectifier network typically has some proportion of units inactive on any given input — that is the sparsity that makes rectifiers efficient, and part 1 §21's region counting depends on it. The pathology is a unit dead on all inputs, which is a different measurement. Measure "dead on the whole training set", not "zero on this example".

The tempting shortcut. "My network is not learning, so I will swap ReLU for something fancier." Measure the dead fraction first. If it is near zero, the activation is not your problem and swapping it will waste a day. If it is 40%, the activation swap will help — and lowering the learning rate would have helped more, because it would have prevented the loss of capacity instead of merely making it recoverable. Part 1 §13 makes the same point from the function-class side: it is almost never the activation.

12 Initialisation: the variance argument, derived

Deep networks are trained by an iterative algorithm, so somebody has to choose the starting point, and the choice determines whether the algorithm converges at all. The reasoning behind the standard rules is one paragraph of second-moment algebra, and it is worth doing rather than memorising, because the assumptions it makes are exactly the assumptions that a normalisation layer later removes.

What has to be true before anything else can be

requirement 1
Break the symmetry
Initialise every weight to the same value — zero, or anything — and two units in the same layer, reading the same inputs, compute the same function, receive the same gradient, and take the same step. They stay identical forever. A layer of n such units is a layer of one unit. This is why the weights must be random, and it is the one property nobody argues about.
requirement 2
Do not saturate
If the weights are large, the pre-activations are large, and a saturating unit lands where g′ ≈ 0. §10's table: tanh′(4) = 0.00134. The network starts in a place from which it cannot move.
requirement 3
Do not lose the signal
If the weights are small, the activations shrink layer by layer, the deep layers see nothing but the bias, and the gradient reaching the shallow layers is §09's cᴸ with c < 1. The forward signal and the backward signal die for the same reason and at the same rate.
requirement 4
Zero biases, almost always
The weights already break the symmetry, so the bias does not need to. Zero is compatible with every weight rule. The exceptions are deliberate and few: the output bias set to the base rate (§03), a small positive bias to keep rectifiers alive (§11), and a gate bias set so the gate starts open.

The derivation, forward

One layer, row convention: z = a W with a ∈ ℝ^{1×n} and W ∈ ℝ^{n×m}. Assume the entries of W are independent with mean 0 and variance σ_w², independent of a, and that the entries of a are identically distributed. Then for any output coordinate j:

one line of second momentszⱼ = ∑_{i=1}^{n} aᵢ W_{ij} 𝔼[zⱼ] = ∑ᵢ 𝔼[aᵢ]·𝔼[W_{ij}] = 0 (W has mean zero) Var[zⱼ] = 𝔼[zⱼ²] = ∑ᵢ ∑_{i'} 𝔼[aᵢ a_{i'}]·𝔼[W_{ij} W_{i'j}] = ∑ᵢ 𝔼[aᵢ²]·σ_w² (cross terms vanish: the W's = n · σ_w² · 𝔼[a²] are independent and mean-zero) Note carefully: the quantity that propagates is the SECOND MOMENT 𝔼[a²], not the variance. They coincide only when 𝔼[a] = 0, which is true for tanh and FALSE for every rectifier. Conflating the two is where most retellings of this argument go wrong.

Now close the loop by relating 𝔼[a²] at layer l to Var[z] at layer l, which is where the activation enters:

the activation's contribution to the recursionLINEAR or tanh near the origin: a ≈ z, so 𝔼[a²] ≈ Var[z] recursion Var[z⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾] = n·σ_w²·Var[z⁽ˡ⁾] fixed point at σ_w² = 1/n RECTIFIER: z is symmetric about 0, so relu keeps exactly half of it — 𝔼[a²] = 𝔼[max(z,0)²] = ½·𝔼[z²] = ½·Var[z] recursion Var[z⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾] = n·σ_w²·½·Var[z⁽ˡ⁾] fixed point at σ_w² = 2/n That factor of two is the WHOLE difference between the two famous rules, and it exists to undo the half that the rectifier throws away.
The invariant is 𝔼[a²] = 1, not Var[a] = 1. Under the rectifier rule the fixed point has Var[z] = 2 and 𝔼[a²] = 1, but the variance of the post-activation settles at Var[relu(z)] = (½ − 1/2π)·Var[z] = 1 − 1/π = 0.6817, because a rectified Gaussian has a non-zero mean. Measure the activation variance at depth in a correctly initialised rectifier network and you will find 0.68, not 1.00, and that is the right answer. A sampled check at width 512, depth 30, gives 0.686. Anyone reporting that "the variance is preserved" is describing the pre-activation, or is describing tanh.

The derivation, backward

Run the identical argument on Δ⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾ = Δ⁽ˡ⁾ (W⁽ˡ⁾)ᵀ ⊙ g′. The only structural change is which dimension the sum runs over: forward, coordinate j of z sums over the n_in inputs; backward, coordinate i of Δ sums over the n_out outputs.

the two conditions, side by sideforward, preserve 𝔼[a²]: n_in · σ_w² · κ = 1 ⇒ σ_w² = 1/(κ·n_in) backward, preserve 𝔼[Δ²]: n_out · σ_w² · κ = 1 ⇒ σ_w² = 1/(κ·n_out) where κ = ½ for a rectifier and 1 for a linear or tanh unit. BOTH conditions hold only when n_in = n_out. For any other layer shape you must choose, or compromise. That is not a defect of the analysis — it is a genuine impossibility, and it is why there are three rules and not one.

13 The three rules, what each assumes, and what each costs

Three names, one formula, one free choice — which fan to divide by — and one convention about whether to sample from a Gaussian or a uniform. Everything else in the literature is a gain factor bolted onto this.

the one formulaW_{ij} ~ 𝒩(0, σ_w²) or W_{ij} ~ U(−r, r) with r = √(3σ_w²) The uniform form matches the Gaussian's variance because U(−r, r) has variance r²/3. The two perform indistinguishably; the choice is a convention, not a finding. The fan-average rule does what it claims: it more than halves the worst-case error compared with committing to one direction. It is not, however, the optimal compromise — the geometric mean splits the discrepancy evenly and does better on this measure, at 2.84× against 4.55×. The field uses the arithmetic (fan-average) form because that is the form that was published and became the default, and the difference rarely matters because most layers in most modern architectures are square or nearly so. Where it does matter is the first layer of a network reading a high-dimensional input and the last layer of a classifier with many classes, and both of those are one layer out of dozens.

What every one of these rules assumes, and when the assumptions fail. Four assumptions, all of them false in a real network and none of them fatally so. (1) The weights and activations are independent — true at step 0, false immediately after, because training correlates them. (2) The activations are identically distributed — false as soon as any structure appears. (3) The activation is linear near the origin, or a clean rectifier — GELU and SiLU are neither, so the rectifier rule is an approximation for them, and a good enough one. (4) The width is infinite. That last one is not a formality: see below. All four are why every source says the initial scale should be treated as a hyperparameter with the formula as a starting point, and why the normalisation layers of §29 — which enforce the property at every step rather than hoping it survives — largely displaced the careful-initialisation research programme.

The infinite-width assumption is not free

The rules preserve 𝔼[a²] = 1 in expectation. At finite width the per-layer gain fluctuates, the fluctuations compound multiplicatively, and the distribution of the network's total gain becomes heavy-tailed: its mean stays at 1 while its median falls. A typical network is therefore quieter than the rule promises, and increasingly so as depth grows relative to width. The loss is finite and constant at log K, the gradient is zero, nothing errors, and the run appears to be training.

The scaling is exactly 100ˡ and 10⁻²ˡ, because the scale enters the variance squared and once per layer. A factor of ten in the standard deviation is a factor of a hundred per layer, so the correct scale is sharp to well under a factor of two once the network is deep. This is also why the failure is not a gentle degradation: there is no version of "slightly too small" that costs you slightly.

The quiet failure is the dangerous one

Overflow announces itself. Underflow does not. A network initialised at a tenth of the right scale produces a forward pass in which every deep activation is numerically zero, so the output is determined entirely by the output bias, so the loss is exactly the entropy of the label distribution — log K for K balanced classes, 2.303 for ten of them — and it stays there. Nothing raises an exception. The loss curve is a horizontal line, which is easy to misread as "needs more epochs" or "learning rate too low", and people have spent days on both.
