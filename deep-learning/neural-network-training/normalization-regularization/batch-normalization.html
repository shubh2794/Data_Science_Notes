01 The problem as the study framed it: internal covariate shift

Covariate shift is the classical name for a learning system whose input distribution changes between training and use. The study's move was to apply the idea inside a network. Every layer is a learner whose inputs are produced by the layers below, so whenever those layers update, the layer's input distribution changes under it.

the sub-network viewℓ = F₂( F₁(u, Θ₁), Θ₂ ) learning Θ₂ is exactly learning a stand-alone network ℓ = F₂(x, Θ₂) fed with inputs x = F₁(u, Θ₁). One SGD step with batch size m and rate α: Θ₂ ← Θ₂ − (α/m) ∑ᵢ ∂F₂(xᵢ, Θ₂)/∂Θ₂ ⇒ whatever makes training efficient for a stand-alone network — e.g. a FIXED input distribution — helps the sub-network too. But x moves whenever Θ₁ does. the definition used throughout the studyInternal Covariate Shift = the change in the distribution of network activations due to the change in network parameters during training.

Why it was thought to matter: saturation

Take a sigmoid layer z = g(Wu + b) with g(x) = 1/(1 + exp(−x)). As |x| grows, g′(x) → 0, so every dimension of x = Wu + b except the small ones sends almost no gradient down to u. Because x depends on W, b and every layer below, parameter updates keep pushing dimensions into the saturated regime, and the effect compounds with depth. The usual fixes at the time were ReLUs, careful initialisation and small learning rates (the mechanics are on gradient flow & initialisation). The study's claim was that if the distribution of nonlinearity inputs stayed stable, the optimiser would be less likely to get stuck in saturation and training would accelerate.

Hold this loosely. Internal covariate shift is the study's motivation, not something it proved to be the mechanism. Its own experiments show the method works. Whether it works because it reduces internal covariate shift was later contested (§13).

02 Two ways to fix the distribution that do not work

Whitening the inputs (zero mean, unit variance, decorrelated) was long known to speed up convergence, so the natural idea is to whiten every layer's inputs. The study rules out two versions of this before proposing its own: normalising outside the gradient step, and normalising with full whitening.

Failure 1 — normalisation the optimiser does not know about

Suppose a layer adds a learned bias and then centres the result using the mean over the training set, and the gradient step ignores the fact that the mean depends on b:

the bias blow-upx = u + b x̂ = x − E[x] E[x] = (1/N) ∑ᵢ xᵢ over the training set gradient step that treats E[x] as a constant: b ← b + Δb, Δb ∝ −∂ℓ/∂x̂ after re-computing the normalisation: u + (b + Δb) − E[u + (b + Δb)] = u + b − E[u + b] ⇒ the layer's output is UNCHANGED, so the loss is unchanged and the gradient points the same way next step. b grows without bound while ℓ stays fixed.

The update and the re-normalisation cancel exactly. The study reports that the effect is worse when the normalisation also scales, and that in its initial experiments the model blew up whenever the normalisation parameters were computed outside the gradient step. The lesson is that the normalisation has to be part of the function being differentiated. Written generally, x̂ = Norm(x, 𝒳) depends on the example and on the whole set 𝒳, so backpropagation needs both Jacobians ∂Norm/∂x and ∂Norm/∂𝒳. Dropping the second one is what causes the explosion above.

Failure 2 — full whitening, done properly, is too expensive

what proper whitening would need, every stepCov[x] = E[xxᵀ] − E[x]E[x]ᵀ (d × d, over the training set) x̂ = Cov[x]^(−1/2) · (x − E[x]) an inverse matrix square root + the derivatives of both through backprop.
  • Cost and differentiability. An inverse square root of a d × d covariance, plus its backward pass, at every layer and every step. It is also not differentiable everywhere.
  • Mini-batches cannot estimate it. The batch size is usually smaller than the number of activations being whitened, so a mini-batch covariance is singular and would need regularising. Per-dimension variances have no such problem, and that is what makes mini-batch statistics usable at all.
  • Per-example statistics are not the answer either. Normalising over a single example, or over feature maps at one location, discards the absolute scale of the activations and so changes what the network can represent. The study wanted each example normalised relative to the statistics of the training data.

This leads to two simplifications: (1) normalise each scalar feature independently instead of whitening jointly, and (2) estimate the statistics from the current mini-batch instead of the whole training set, so they "fully participate in the gradient backpropagation".

03 Algorithm 1: the Batch Normalizing Transform

For one activation x (the index k is dropped) over a mini-batch ℬ = {x₁…ₘ}, the transform BN_γ,β : x₁…ₘ → y₁…ₘ is four lines. The ε is a constant added to the variance for numerical stability.

Algorithm 1 — applied to activation x over a mini-batchinput: values of x over a mini-batch ℬ = {x₁ … xₘ}; learned parameters γ, β output: { yᵢ = BN_γ,β(xᵢ) } μ_ℬ ← (1/m) ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ xᵢ mini-batch mean σ²_ℬ ← (1/m) ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ (xᵢ − μ_ℬ)² mini-batch variance x̂ᵢ ← (xᵢ − μ_ℬ) / √(σ²_ℬ + ε) normalise yᵢ ← γ x̂ᵢ + β ≡ BN_γ,β(xᵢ) scale and shift why γ and β: the transform can represent the identitychoose γ⁽ᵏ⁾ = √Var[x⁽ᵏ⁾] and β⁽ᵏ⁾ = E[x⁽ᵏ⁾] ⇒ y⁽ᵏ⁾ = x⁽ᵏ⁾ (exactly, with full-data statistics and ε neglected)
  • Why the affine step is not optional. Normalising alone changes what a layer can represent. The study's example: normalising the inputs of a sigmoid to zero mean and unit variance would confine them to its linear regime. With γ and β learned, the network can restore any scale and offset it needs, so the change is to the parameterisation, not the function class. The study adds two parameters per activation.
  • What x̂ guarantees. Neglecting ε, ∑ᵢ x̂ᵢ = 0 and (1/m)∑ᵢ x̂ᵢ² = 1 within every batch. So if the batch elements are drawn from the same distribution, each x̂ has expected value 0 and variance 1. Each x̂⁽ᵏ⁾ is then the input to a sub-network (the affine step and everything after it) whose inputs have fixed first and second moments, although their joint distribution can still change.
  • Not per-example. BN_γ,β(xᵢ) depends on xᵢ and on the other examples in the batch. That has consequences for the backward pass (§04), for inference (§05) and for regularisation (§08).

The biased 1/m variance in the normalising step, and the unit-variance property it buys, are worked through numerically on the hub's §29, so they are not repeated here.

04 The backward pass, through the batch statistics

Because μ_ℬ and σ²_ℬ are functions of every xᵢ in the batch, the gradient with respect to one input has three paths: directly through x̂ᵢ, through the variance, and through the mean. The study writes the chain rule out before simplification. This is exactly what makes failure 1 of §02 impossible.

the study's chain rule (gᵢ ≡ ∂ℓ/∂yᵢ arriving from above)∂ℓ/∂x̂ᵢ = ∂ℓ/∂yᵢ · γ ∂ℓ/∂σ²_ℬ = ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ ∂ℓ/∂x̂ᵢ · (xᵢ − μ_ℬ) · (−½)(σ²_ℬ + ε)^(−3/2) ∂ℓ/∂μ_ℬ = ( ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ ∂ℓ/∂x̂ᵢ · (−1/√(σ²_ℬ + ε)) ) + ∂ℓ/∂σ²_ℬ · ( ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ −2(xᵢ − μ_ℬ) ) / m ∂ℓ/∂xᵢ = ∂ℓ/∂x̂ᵢ · 1/√(σ²_ℬ + ε) + ∂ℓ/∂σ²_ℬ · 2(xᵢ − μ_ℬ)/m + ∂ℓ/∂μ_ℬ · 1/m └── direct path ──────┘ └── via the variance ──┘ └── via the mean ┘ ∂ℓ/∂γ = ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ ∂ℓ/∂yᵢ · x̂ᵢ ∂ℓ/∂β = ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵐ ∂ℓ/∂yᵢ

What the three paths do together (this page's simplification)

The study stops at the expressions above. Collapsing them is a short exercise. Note that ∑ᵢ (xᵢ − μ_ℬ) = 0, so the second term of ∂ℓ/∂μ_ℬ vanishes. Write s = √(σ²_ℬ + ε):

collapsed form — derived on this page, not in the study∂ℓ/∂xᵢ = (γ / (m·s)) · ( m·gᵢ − ∑ⱼ gⱼ − x̂ᵢ · ∑ⱼ gⱼ x̂ⱼ ) consequences (ε neglected, so (1/m)∑ x̂ⱼ² = 1): ∑ᵢ ∂ℓ/∂xᵢ = 0 the gradient has no component along "shift the whole batch" ∑ᵢ x̂ᵢ · ∂ℓ/∂xᵢ = 0 … nor along "scale the batch about its mean"

This says the incoming gradient is projected away from the two directions the normalisation cancels. Shifting every xᵢ by a constant, or scaling them about their mean, cannot change x̂, so the loss cannot depend on them, and the gradient carries no signal along them. That is the formal version of "the optimiser now knows the normalisation is there". The training simulation in §09 uses exactly these formulas, term by term, and the scale-invariance figure in §07 was checked against finite differences to six significant figures. The general mechanics of backpropagation are on loss & backprop.

05 Algorithm 2: training a BN network, then freezing it for inference

Mini-batch dependence helps training but is "neither necessary nor desirable during inference". The output should depend on the input alone, deterministically. So after training, the study replaces the batch statistics with population statistics, and the layer becomes a fixed linear map.

Algorithm 2 — training a batch-normalised networkinput: network N with parameters Θ; a subset of activations {x⁽ᵏ⁾}ₖ₌₁ᴷ output: batch-normalised network for inference, N_BN^inf 1 N_BN^tr ← N 2 for k = 1 … K: 3 add y⁽ᵏ⁾ = BN_γ⁽ᵏ⁾,β⁽ᵏ⁾(x⁽ᵏ⁾) to N_BN^tr (Algorithm 1) 4 every layer that took x⁽ᵏ⁾ as input now takes y⁽ᵏ⁾ 6 train N_BN^tr to optimise Θ ∪ {γ⁽ᵏ⁾, β⁽ᵏ⁾}ₖ₌₁ᴷ 7 N_BN^inf ← N_BN^tr inference network, parameters frozen 8 for k = 1 … K: 10 process multiple training mini-batches ℬ of size m and average: E[x] ← E_ℬ[μ_ℬ] Var[x] ← (m / (m − 1)) · E_ℬ[σ²_ℬ] unbiased 11 in N_BN^inf replace y = BN_γ,β(x) with y = (γ / √(Var[x] + ε)) · x + ( β − γ·E[x] / √(Var[x] + ε) )
  • The m/(m − 1) factor. Each σ²_ℬ is a biased within-batch variance, so their average under-reports the population variance by (m − 1)/m. The factor undoes that. Neglecting ε, the inference-time x̂ then has the same mean 0 and variance 1 as during training.
  • Inference BN is one affine map per activation, y = a·x + c with a = γ/√(Var[x] + ε) and c = β − a·E[x]. The study notes that the normalisation "may further be composed with the scaling by γ and shift by β, to yield a single linear transform that replaces BN(x)". The step the hub makes explicit, absorbing it into the preceding weight matrix, follows directly (§29, "fold it away").
  • Moving averages instead of a post-hoc pass. Algorithm 2 averages over training batches after training. The study notes that moving averages can be used instead, which lets you track a model's accuracy as it trains. The simulation in §09 does this. The hub covers the running-average mechanics and the train/eval bugs.

Step 5 of the original listing is just end for. The step numbers above follow the original, so 5, 9 and 12 are missing: they are loop closings and a notational comment.

06 Where BN goes, and how it works in a convolutional layer

The study targets layers of the form z = g(Wu + b), which covers fully connected and convolutional layers. It places BN between the affine map and the nonlinearity, drops the bias, and in convolutional layers shares the statistics and the (γ, β) pair across every spatial location of a feature map.

placement, and the vanished biasz = g(Wu + b) becomes z = g( BN(Wu) ) b is dropped: BN subtracts the batch mean, which cancels any constant b exactly. Its role is taken over by β (Algorithm 1). convolutional BN — the effective mini-batchℬ = all values of ONE feature map, over every batch element AND every location batch size m, feature map p × q ⇒ m′ = |ℬ| = m · p · q one (γ, β) pair per feature map, not per activation example (this page's arithmetic): m = 32, a 28 × 28 map ⇒ m′ = 32 · 784 = 25 088
  • Why normalise Wu and not the layer input u. u is usually the output of another nonlinearity, so the shape of its distribution is likely to change during training, and fixing its first two moments would not remove the shift. Wu + b is more likely to be symmetric, non-sparse and "more Gaussian", so normalising it is more likely to produce a stable distribution. (The same argument is used in the conclusion to contrast BN with an earlier standardisation layer applied after the nonlinearity.)
  • Why share across locations. The convolutional property: elements of the same feature map at different locations should be normalised the same way. The inference transform of Algorithm 2 is modified the same way, so it is one affine map per feature map.
  • A side effect worth noticing. m′ is large even when m is small, so convolutional BN gets far better statistics than a fully connected BN at the same batch size. It does not fix the small-batch problem, though: the m · p · q values come from only m images and are strongly correlated (§13). For convolutions themselves, see convolutional networks.

07 Why BN permits higher learning rates: scale invariance

In an ordinary deep network, a learning rate that is too high can make gradients explode or vanish, or leave training stuck in poor minima. It can also grow the weights, which then amplify the gradients and lead to model explosion. With BN, backpropagation through a layer is unaffected by the scale of its weights.

the study's three identities, for a scalar aBN(Wu) = BN((aW)u) ∂BN((aW)u) / ∂u = ∂BN(Wu) / ∂u the layer Jacobian is unchanged ∂BN((aW)u) / ∂(aW) = (1/a) · ∂BN(Wu) / ∂W larger weights ⇒ smaller gradients consequence for an SGD step — this page's one-line corollary‖Δ(aW)‖ / ‖aW‖ = η · (1/a)‖∂ℓ/∂W‖ / (a‖W‖) ∝ 1/a² If a large step inflates the weights, the next steps become relatively smaller. The study's phrasing: BN will "stabilize the parameter growth".

The first identity holds because a scales xᵢ − μ_ℬ and σ_ℬ alike and cancels in x̂. The other two follow by differentiating it. Strictly, the cancellation is exact only when ε is neglected: once a²σ² is comparable to ε, the invariance breaks. The figure below shows this.

The conjecture about Jacobians

the study's heuristic, explicitly offered as a conjecturetwo consecutive normalised layers, z = F(x), with F(x) ≈ Jx (linear for fixed parameters) assume x and z Gaussian, uncorrelated ⇒ Cov[x] = Cov[z] = I I = Cov[z] = J Cov[x] Jᵀ = J Jᵀ ⇒ every singular value of J equals 1 ⇒ gradient magnitudes preserved in backprop

The study is explicit that reality violates every assumption: the map is not linear, and the normalised values are neither Gaussian nor independent. It still "expects" better-behaved gradient propagation, and calls the precise effect an area of further study. Singular values near 1 are the dynamical-isometry picture of initialisation. The broader link between curvature and the largest usable step size is on optimisers.

08 BN as a regulariser

A training example is seen together with the other examples in its mini-batch, so the training network "no longer produc[es] deterministic values for a given training example". The study found this noise helped generalisation, enough that dropout could be removed or reduced.

  • The noise is in the statistics. μ_ℬ and σ_ℬ change with batch composition, so the same example gets a slightly different x̂ each time it is seen. The hub measures this directly: the same input, three batches, three outputs (§29).
  • Indirect evidence from ImageNet (§10). Shuffling the training data more thoroughly, so the same examples do not keep landing in a batch together, gave about 1% better validation accuracy. The study reads this as consistent with the regulariser view: the randomisation helps most if it hits an example differently each time.
  • No separate analysis. The study offers this observation and no theory of the regularising effect. How it compares to dropout's own noise model is on the sibling page, Dropout.

What is simulated, honestly: the architecture is the study's (3 × 100 sigmoid hidden units, 10-way softmax, cross-entropy, batch 60, small Gaussian init, plain SGD), and it runs live in your browser. The data is not MNIST: there are ten random 7×7 binary prototypes, and each pixel is flipped with probability 0.25. Training runs 1 500 steps, not 50 000. Both nets start from identical weights and see identical batches. Percentiles are measured on 200 fixed held-out inputs, and the BN net is evaluated in inference mode, with running population estimates (momentum 0.1, unbiased m/(m−1) variance). Because this task tops out near 95% accuracy, the baseline eventually catches up. The point is the path, not the endpoint.

  • Why the baseline starts slowly here. With small weights every sigmoid sits near 0.5 and the backward signal is attenuated layer by layer. The pre-activations then drift as the lower layers learn, so each layer keeps re-adapting. With BN, every pre-activation is immediately rescaled to a useful range, whatever the weight scale (§07).
  • The BN band is stable but not frozen. γ and β are learned, so the distribution can move. It moves only when the loss asks it to, not as a by-product of updates in other layers. Try learning rate 2.0 to see β shift the band deliberately.

10 Experiment 2: ImageNet — the network, and what had to change

The main test was a new variant of the Inception network on 1000-class ImageNet classification. BN was applied to the input of every nonlinearity, in the convolutional way of §06, and the rest of the architecture was left unchanged. The important finding is that adding BN alone does not take full advantage of it. The training recipe has to change too.

"Accelerating BN networks": the seven changes

1
Increase the learning rate
A training speed-up "with no ill side effects" (§07). By 5× and 30× in the variants of §11.
2
Remove dropout
BN serves some of the same goals (§08). Removing it sped up training without increasing overfitting.
3
Reduce L2 weight regularisation by 5×
The weight of the L2 loss on the parameters was divided by 5, which improved held-out validation accuracy. Weight decay itself is on the hub.
4
Decay the learning rate 6× faster
Inception's rate was decayed exponentially. Since the BN network trains faster, it was lowered 6 times faster.
5
Remove local response normalisation
Inception and others benefit from it, but with BN it was found unnecessary.
6
Shuffle more thoroughly
Within-shard shuffling, so the same examples do not always share a batch: about 1% better validation accuracy (§08).
7
Reduce photometric distortions
BN networks train faster and see each example fewer times, so the trainer was allowed to focus on more "real" images by distorting them less.

Notice how many of the seven remove regularisation: dropout, most of the L2 penalty, augmentation strength. That is what you would do if the layer were itself a regulariser and the network now saw each example fewer times. The other three are about speed: a higher rate, faster decay, and dropping a normalisation that is now redundant.

The reported single-network numbers, three ways switch views; the speed-up factors in the steps view are this page's divisions of the reported step counts
  • BN alone halves the steps. BN-Baseline matches Inception "in less than half the number of training steps" (this page's arithmetic: 31.0 / 13.3 ≈ 2.3×), at the same learning rate and recipe.
  • The recipe does the rest. BN-x5 needs 14 times fewer steps than Inception to reach 72.2% (the division 31.0 / 2.1 is about 14.8; the study says 14×). The introduction puts the same fact as matching the baseline with "only 7% of the training steps" (2.1 / 31.0 ≈ 6.8%). This is the headline of the abstract.
  • Higher still: slower start, better finish. BN-x30 trains "somewhat slower initially" (2.7 × 10⁶ steps to 72.2%) but reaches 74.8% after 6 × 10⁶ steps. The study calls that 5 times fewer steps than Inception needed for 72.2% (31 / 6 ≈ 5.2).
  • The control that matters. The same 5× learning-rate increase applied to Inception without BN "caused the model parameters to reach machine infinity". So the higher rate is something BN makes possible, not just a better hyperparameter.
  • Sigmoids become trainable. BN-x5-Sigmoid reaches 69.8%. The original Inception with sigmoid stayed at chance and "never achieves better than 1/1000 accuracy". The study offers this as verification that removing internal covariate shift lets deep networks train with saturating nonlinearities.

13 What later work found (beyond the original study)

Everything above is the original method and its evidence. This section is general knowledge from later work. It is not part of the study's claims, it is summarised without its numbers, and parts of it are still debated.

the explanation
Internal covariate shift was challenged
A 2018 analysis trained BN networks and then deliberately injected time-varying, non-zero-mean, non-unit-variance noise after each BN layer, re-creating a severe distribution shift. The networks still trained about as well as ordinary BN networks and far better than the unnormalised ones. By a gradient-based measure of internal covariate shift, BN did not reliably reduce it either.
the replacement account
BN smooths the loss landscape
The same line of work argued that BN makes the loss and its gradients more Lipschitz, so gradients are more predictive and larger steps are safe. That fits §07's scale-invariance and §11's learning-rate results without needing internal covariate shift. The hub's §29 gives this account in the context of the layer's mechanics.
scale invariance, revisited
Weight decay becomes a learning-rate knob
Because BN((aW)u) = BN(Wu), an L2 penalty on W cannot change what a BN layer computes. It only shrinks ‖W‖, which by §07 raises the effective step (∝ 1/‖W‖²). Later analyses treat weight decay on BN networks as controlling an effective learning rate, not as capacity control.
the batch
Small batches break it
The statistics are estimated from m samples (or m images in a conv net), so at very small batches they become noisy and the layer hurts. Detection, segmentation and other memory-bound tasks run into this. Batch renormalisation corrects mini-batch statistics toward the running ones. Synchronised BN pools statistics across devices.
the alternatives
Normalise over another axis
LayerNorm normalises over the features of one example, so there is no batch dependence and no train/eval gap. It is the default in sequence models and transformers. GroupNorm normalises over groups of channels per example and is the small-batch choice for vision. RMSNorm drops the mean. See the hub's §30 and LayerNorm against RMSNorm in the transformer block.
and without it
Normaliser-free deep networks
Very deep residual networks were later trained without BN at all, using careful residual initialisation (scaling or zero-initialising each branch) and, at scale, adaptive gradient clipping. This suggests much of BN's value lies in keeping signal propagation healthy at initialisation. A related BN trick is zero-initialising the last γ in each residual block (residual initialisation tricks).

The original study's practical claims have held up well: faster training, higher usable learning rates, trainable saturating nonlinearities, and less need for dropout. Its proposed reason has not. The same pattern of a result outliving its first explanation shows up in the loss-landscape account of residual connections. BN and residual connections arrived in the same year and are usually used together.

14 Common misconceptions

"BN is just standardising the inputs at every layer — you could precompute the statistics." That is exactly the version the study shows failing. If the statistics sit outside the gradient step, the optimiser keeps pushing in directions the normalisation cancels, and parameters such as b grow without bound (§02). BN works because the batch statistics are inside the differentiated function and gradients flow through μ_ℬ and σ²_ℬ (§04).
"BN forces every layer's inputs to be zero-mean, unit-variance." Only x̂ is; the layer outputs γx̂ + β. With γ = √Var[x] and β = E[x] it can recover the identity (§03). What it removes is the accidental coupling between a layer's input moments and the parameters below.
"Keep the bias in the layer before BN." It does nothing. The batch mean subtraction cancels any constant exactly, and β takes over its role (§06). It is a wasted parameter. Its gradient through BN is exactly zero (§04's ∑ ∂ℓ/∂xᵢ = 0).
"The study showed BN works by reducing internal covariate shift." It assumed that and showed the method works. The MNIST figure shows more stable distributions alongside faster training. It does not show one causes the other, and later experiments that re-injected the shift found the benefit survived (§13).
"Adding BN to a network gives the 14× speed-up." Adding BN alone gave about 2.3×. The 14× needed the full recipe of §10: 5× the learning rate, no dropout, a fifth of the L2 penalty, 6× faster decay, no LRN, better shuffling and lighter distortions (§11).
"Convolutional BN has one γ, β per activation." One pair per feature map. Statistics are pooled over the batch and all p × q locations, for an effective batch of m · p · q (§06).

15 Takeaways

  • Motivation: internal covariate shift, the change in a layer's input distribution as earlier layers train, which pushes saturating units into flat regions.
  • Normalisation must be inside the gradient. Normalising outside the step lets the bias grow without bound. Full whitening is too costly and singular at mini-batch size. So: per-feature, per-mini-batch, differentiated through.
  • Algorithm 1: x̂ = (x − μ_ℬ)/√(σ²_ℬ + ε), y = γx̂ + β. γ, β can recover the identity, at two extra parameters per activation.
  • Backprop has three paths: direct, via σ²_ℬ and via μ_ℬ. Together they remove the gradient's shift and scale components.
  • Algorithm 2: train with batch statistics, then infer with E[x] = E_ℬ[μ_ℬ] and Var[x] = m/(m−1)·E_ℬ[σ²_ℬ]. Inference BN is one affine map and can be folded.
  • Conv nets: z = g(BN(Wu)) with the bias dropped. Statistics per feature map over m′ = m·p·q values.
  • Higher learning rates: BN((aW)u) = BN(Wu). The input Jacobian is unchanged and the weight gradient scales as 1/a, so weight growth self-limits.
  • Evidence: 72.2% in 2.1 × 10⁶ steps instead of 31.0 × 10⁶ (14×), 74.8% best single network, trainable sigmoids (69.8%), and a 6-network ensemble at 4.9% top-5 validation and 4.82% test.
  • Later: the results held up. The internal-covariate-shift explanation largely did not, and loss-landscape smoothing replaced it. Batch dependence pushed sequence models to LayerNorm and small-batch vision to GroupNorm.
