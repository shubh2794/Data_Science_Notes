Dropout

A paper-depth reading of the original dropout study (JMLR, 2014). On every training case, each unit is kept with probability p and otherwise deleted with all its connections; at test time the full network is used with its outgoing weights multiplied by p. This page goes past the mechanism: the motivation, the exact model and learning procedure, why it was paired with max-norm constraints and very high learning rates, how it combines with unsupervised pretraining, every benchmark it was run on (including the one where it barely helped), what it does to features and sparsity, how the retention rate and the data-set size interact with it, how good the weight-scaling shortcut really is, dropout RBMs, the closed-form marginalised version for linear regression, Gaussian multiplicative noise, and the stated cost.

01 Why: the ensemble you cannot afford, and the co-adaptation you do not want

Already covered on the hub. §33 of Normalization Layers & Training Regularization explains inverted dropout and why the rescaling must happen somewhere, measures it over 20 000 masks, reads dropout as a 2ⁿ-network ensemble with the weight-scaling approximation, and covers co-adaptation, the clash with BatchNorm and where to put the layer. This page does not repeat that; it follows the original study.

With unlimited compute, the best way to regularise a fixed-size model is to average the predictions of every parameter setting, each weighted by its posterior probability. The original study set out to approach that Bayesian gold standard far more cheaply. Its proposal is to approximate an equally weighted geometric mean of the predictions of exponentially many models that share parameters.

  • Why ordinary ensembles do not scale. Combining models helps most when the models differ, which means different architectures or different training data. Tuning many large architectures is a daunting job, large nets need large amounts of data that cannot easily be split, and running many big nets at test time is too slow where responses must be quick.
  • The evolutionary analogy. The study draws its motivation from a theory of the role of sex in evolution. Asexual reproduction would seem the better way to keep a well-adapted set of genes, yet sexual reproduction, which breaks such co-adapted sets apart, is how most advanced organisms evolved. One explanation: natural selection rewards the mix-ability of genes, their ability to work with a random set of partners. A gene that cannot rely on a large set of partners must do something useful on its own or with a few others. A hidden unit trained with dropout is in the same position: it has to work with a random sample of the other units.
  • Copies are not the answer. A net could in principle become robust to dropout by making many copies of each unit; the study calls this a poor solution, for the same reason replica codes are a poor way to deal with a noisy channel.
  • The conspiracy version. Ten conspiracies of five people each are a better way to create havoc than one conspiracy that needs fifty people to play their parts correctly. Big co-adaptations can be rehearsed to work on the training set; under changed conditions (new test data) many small ones are more likely to keep working.

Where it sits relative to earlier noise methods

Dropout can be read as adding noise to hidden units. Denoising autoencoders had already added noise to the inputs of an autoencoder and trained it to reconstruct the clean input. The study extends this in three ways: it applies the noise in hidden layers too, it interprets it as model averaging, and it uses it for supervised learning. On noise level, the study reports that about 5% noise typically works best for denoising autoencoders, whereas the test-time weight scaling lets dropout use far more. Dropping 20% of the input units and 50% of the hidden units was often found to be optimal.

02 The model: one Bernoulli retention mask per layer, per training case

Take a network with L hidden layers. Write z⁽ˡ⁾ for the input vector to layer l, y⁽ˡ⁾ for its output (y⁽⁰⁾ = x), and W⁽ˡ⁾, b⁽ˡ⁾ for its weights and biases. Dropout inserts one line into the forward pass.

standard network, for l ∈ {0, …, L − 1} and hidden unit izᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = wᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ · y⁽ˡ⁾ + bᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ yᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = f(zᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾) f any activation, e.g. f(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(−x)) dropout networkrⱼ⁽ˡ⁾ ~ Bernoulli(p) independent for every unit j ỹ⁽ˡ⁾ = r⁽ˡ⁾ ⊙ y⁽ˡ⁾ ⊙ = element-wise product: the thinned outputs zᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = wᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ · ỹ⁽ˡ⁾ + bᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ yᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = f(zᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾) test time: no dropout, scaled weightsW_test⁽ˡ⁾ = p · W⁽ˡ⁾
  • p is the probability of retaining a unit, not of dropping it. p = 1 means no dropout; small p means heavy dropout. Read every number on this page that way.
  • The mask multiplies a layer's outputs, so dropping unit j removes it together with all its outgoing connections for that pass. Applying it at every layer, including the input (l = 0), samples one sub-network from the full one.
  • The test-time rule. A unit present with probability p during training has its outgoing weights multiplied by p at test time. That makes the output of each unit at test time equal to its expected output under the training-time dropout distribution. The study states that the same effect is obtained by multiplying retained activations by 1/p during training and leaving the weights alone at test time (the "inverted" convention the hub uses), and that the two are equivalent with appropriate scaling of the learning rate and the weight initialisation at each layer.
  • How many networks. A net with n units is a collection of 2ⁿ thinned nets, all sharing weights, so the parameter count is still O(n²) or less. A fresh thinned net is sampled for every presentation of every training case, so each one is trained very rarely, if at all.

03 Learning: backpropagation through a different thinned net for every case

Training is ordinary minibatch SGD with one change: each training case in the minibatch gets its own sampled thinned network, and forward and backward passes for that case run only through it.

one minibatch of size mfor each case c = 1 … m: sample r⁽ˡ⁾ for every layer a new thinned net per case forward and backward through the thinned net only → gradient g_c(θ) with g_c(θ) = 0 for every weight touching a dropped unit update with (1/m) ∑_c g_c(θ) a weight used by few cases gets a correspondingly smaller averaged step

Momentum, annealed learning rates and L2 weight decay were all found to remain useful with dropout. The gradient each case computes is the gradient of a random sub-architecture, not of the network that will be used at test time — which is the root of the training cost discussed in §15. The loss and backprop page covers the backward pass itself.

04 The recipe that made it work: max-norm, huge decaying learning rates, high momentum

Dropout alone helps. Dropout together with a max-norm constraint, large decaying learning rates and high momentum was reported to give a significant further boost — and the reason given is about how the three interact.

max-norm regularisation, per hidden unitconstraint: ‖w‖₂ ≤ c w = the vector of weights INCOMING to one hidden unit enforced by projection after each update: if ‖w‖₂ > c : w ← c · w / ‖w‖₂ back onto the ball of radius c c is a hyperparameter chosen on a validation set
  • The stated justification. Constraining each weight vector to a ball of fixed radius makes it possible to use a huge learning rate without weights blowing up. The dropout noise then lets the optimiser explore regions of weight space it would otherwise have trouble reaching. As the learning rate decays, steps shorten, exploration falls, and training settles into a minimum.
  • Not only a dropout trick. Max-norm had earlier been used in collaborative filtering, and the study reports it typically improves SGD training of deep nets even without dropout (Table 9 in §08 bears this out: 1.35% with max-norm alone against 1.62% with L2).
  • Contrast with L2. An L2 penalty pulls every weight towards zero at all times; max-norm does nothing until a unit's incoming norm reaches c, then caps it. The general comparison is on the L2, L1 and weight decay section of the hub.

05 Fine-tuning pretrained nets with dropout: scale up by 1/p, and lower the learning rate

Nets pretrained as stacks of RBMs, as autoencoders or as Deep Boltzmann Machines can be fine-tuned with dropout. The pretraining procedure is unchanged; two things are not.

rescaling the pretrained weightsW_finetune-init = W_pretrained / p pretraining ran with every unit present. Under dropout, a unit's input is a p-fraction of its incoming units, so scaling the weights by 1/p keeps each unit's EXPECTED output equal to what it was during pretraining.
  • The worry, and when it came true. The study was initially concerned that dropout's noise would wipe out the information in the pretrained weights. That did happen when the fine-tuning learning rate was comparable to the best rate for randomly initialised nets.
  • The fix. With smaller learning rates the pretrained information was retained and dropout improved the final generalisation error over fine-tuning without dropout. On TIMIT, for example, fine-tuning the DBN with dropout needed a learning rate of about 0.01 (against 0.1 from random initialisation), and adding max-norm there gave no improvement.

The pretraining models themselves are on the Boltzmann machines & RBMs page (stacked RBMs and DBNs in its § on deep belief networks) and the autoencoders page.

09 What it does inside: features that stand alone, and sparse activations for free

Features (Figure 7)

In a standard net each parameter's gradient says how to change to reduce the loss given what all the other units are doing, so units can learn to fix up one another's mistakes — complex co-adaptations that do not generalise. The hypothesis is that dropout makes the presence of any other unit unreliable, so each unit must perform well in many different contexts. To see it directly, the study trained two single-hidden-layer autoencoders on MNIST with 256 rectified linear units, one without dropout and one with p = 0.5 in the hidden layer. Both reached similar test reconstruction error. Without dropout the features look co-adapted: no single unit appears to detect anything meaningful. With dropout the units detect edges, strokes and spots in different parts of the image. The study takes this as direct evidence that dropout breaks up co-adaptation, and calls that probably the main reason it lowers generalisation error.

Sparsity (Figure 8)

Using the same two autoencoders on a random test minibatch, the study plots two histograms per model: the mean activation of each hidden unit across the minibatch, and all activations. A good sparse code has few highly active units per case and low mean activation per unit.

So sparsity arrives as a side effect. It is worth noticing that Table 9 compared dropout against an explicit KL-sparsity penalty (1.55%) and dropout did better; whatever dropout does, it is more than a sparsity prior.

Data-set size (Figure 10): a sweet spot

The 784-1024-1024-2048-10 net, with p = 0.5 in hidden layers and 0.8 at the input, was trained on random MNIST subsets of 100, 500, 1K, 5K, 10K and 50K examples.

  • Tiny sets (100, 500): no improvement. The model has enough parameters to overfit the training data even through all the dropout noise.
  • Then the gain grows, and then it shrinks. For a given architecture and dropout rate there is a sweet spot: enough data that it cannot be memorised despite the noise, but not so much that overfitting stops being a problem anyway. The Reuters result in §07 is the large-data end of the same curve.

11 How good is the weight-scaling shortcut? Monte-Carlo model averaging

The more correct but expensive test-time procedure: sample k thinned nets per test case and average their predictions. As k → ∞ this approaches the true model average. The study measured how many samples it takes to match the single weight-scaled net.

two test-time predictorsMonte-Carlo: ŷ_MC(x) = (1/k) ∑ₛ₌₁ᵏ f(x; W, r⁽ˢ⁾) r⁽ˢ⁾ ~ Bernoulli(p), k forward passes weight scaling: ŷ_WS(x) = f(x; pW, r = 1) one forward pass
  • Result (Figure 11, MNIST): at around k = 50 the Monte-Carlo average becomes as good as weight scaling. Beyond that it is slightly better, but well within one standard deviation. The study concludes weight scaling is a fairly good approximation of the true model average.
  • Why it is exact in a special case (general knowledge, not from the study): for a single sigmoid output, the normalised geometric mean of the sub-models' predictions is σ(E[z]), the sigmoid of the mean logit. If everything before the output is linear in the masked units, E[z] is exactly the logit of the weight-scaled net. Non-linear hidden units break that equality — the simulation below shows by how much.

What the toy makes visible: a single thinned net is a poor predictor (the grey scatter), the Monte-Carlo mean needs tens of samples before its error falls below the small bias of the weight-scaled net, and that bias is set by the non-linearity — which is the qualitative shape of the study's k ≈ 50 finding. The hub's §33 covers keeping dropout on at inference for uncertainty estimates.

12 Dropout Restricted Boltzmann Machines

The central idea — take a large model that overfits easily and repeatedly sample and train smaller sub-models from it — is not specific to feed-forward nets. The study applies it to an RBM's hidden layer.

RBM, v ∈ {0,1}ᴰ, h ∈ {0,1}ᶠ, parameters θ = {W, a, b}P(h, v; θ) = (1 / Z(θ)) · exp(vᵀWh + aᵀh + bᵀv) dropout RBM: add r ∈ {0,1}ᶠ, one retention bit per hidden unitP(r, h, v; p, θ) = P(r; p) · P(h, v | r; θ) P(r; p) = ∏ⱼ₌₁ᶠ p^rⱼ (1 − p)^(1 − rⱼ) P(h, v | r; θ) = (1 / Z(θ, r)) · exp(vᵀWh + aᵀh + bᵀv) · ∏ⱼ₌₁ᶠ g(hⱼ, rⱼ) g(hⱼ, rⱼ) = 1(rⱼ = 1) + 1(rⱼ = 0) · 1(hⱼ = 0) a dropped unit is forced off conditionalsP(hⱼ = 1 | rⱼ, v) = 1(rⱼ = 1) · σ(aⱼ + ∑ᵢ Wᵢⱼ vᵢ) factorial over j P(vᵢ = 1 | h) = σ(bᵢ + ∑ⱼ Wᵢⱼ hⱼ) unchanged from the RBM

Bias letters here follow the energy function (a for hidden, b for visible). The study's printed conditionals use the opposite letters; the model is the same.

  • A mixture of exponentially many RBMs. Conditioned on r, the model is an ordinary RBM with the dropped hidden units removed, so a dropout RBM is a mixture of exponentially many RBMs with shared weights, each using a different subset of h.
  • Training is unchanged contrastive divergence (CD-1 in the experiments), except that r is sampled first — a new one for every training case in every minibatch — and only the retained hidden units are used. The CD procedure itself is on the RBM page's contrastive-divergence section.
  • Features (Figure 12, 256 hidden units, MNIST). With p = 0.5, the features are qualitatively different: coarser than the sharply defined stroke-like features of the standard RBM, and with very few dead units by comparison.
  • Sparsity (Figure 13). As in the feed-forward case, dropout RBMs learn much sparser hidden representations than standard RBMs, with no explicit sparsity regulariser.

The study's claim here is deliberately modest: dropout RBMs are "better than standard RBMs in certain respects". No classification benchmark of dropout-RBM features is reported.

13 Marginalising the noise: dropout in linear regression is a data-scaled ridge penalty

Instead of sampling the noise, take the expectation of the loss over it. The result is a deterministic regulariser that needs no random bits and has ordinary gradients. For linear regression the expectation has a closed form.

  • Ridge regression with a data-dependent scale. Each weight wⱼ is penalised in proportion to ∑ₙ Xₙⱼ², the energy of its input column. The study describes this as scaling the weight cost by the standard deviation of that data dimension (for a mean-centred column, √(∑ₙ Xₙⱼ²) is √N times the standard deviation). A dimension that varies a lot has its weight squeezed harder.
  • The strength is explicit in the second form. The regularisation constant is (1 − p)/p: near zero when p → 1 (almost no dropout), growing without bound as p → 0. At p = 0.5 it is 1; at p = 0.8, 0.25 (this page's arithmetic).
  • Logistic regression and deep nets have no closed form. Earlier work showed dropout in logistic regression can be marginalised approximately by treating the input to the logistic unit and the gradients as Gaussian, which beat Monte-Carlo dropout in both training time and generalisation; the assumptions grow weaker with each added layer, so the result does not carry over directly to deep networks.

Compare with plain ridge on the regularization page: ridge charges every weight the same rate, dropout's marginal charges each weight by how large its input is — invariant, in effect, to rescaling an input feature.

14 Multiplicative Gaussian noise: same mean and variance, maximum entropy

Dropout multiplies activations by Bernoulli variables. The study generalises the multiplier to other distributions and finds a Gaussian works as well, perhaps slightly better.

two multipliers with matched momentsBernoulli, inverted form: r_b = 1/p with probability p, 0 otherwise E[r_b] = 1 Var[r_b] = (1 − p) / p Gaussian: r_g ~ N(1, σ²) set σ² = (1 − p) / p E[r_g] = 1 Var[r_g] = (1 − p) / p hᵢ ← hᵢ · r equivalently hᵢ + hᵢ·ε, ε ~ N(0, σ²) consequencesE[r] = 1 ⇒ no weight scaling at test time p = 0.5 ⇒ σ² = 1 ⇒ r ~ N(1, 1) the case the study first tried

For given first and second moments, the Gaussian has the highest entropy and the Bernoulli the lowest. Both extremes work; the preliminary results suggest the high-entropy one may be slightly better.

15 The stated cost: 2–3× longer training, and the trade it buys

training time
2–3× longer
A dropout network typically takes 2–3 times longer to train than a standard network of the same architecture.
why
Gradients of the wrong network
Every case trains a different random architecture, so the gradients are not gradients of the network used at test time; the updates are very noisy.
the trade
Noise is the regulariser
The same stochasticity is likely what prevents overfitting. With more training time one can use stronger dropout and overfit less.
open direction
Marginalise it away
A deterministic regulariser with dropout's effect in expectation would avoid the noise; for linear regression it is a modified L2 (§13), for deeper models no equivalent was known. Speeding up dropout is named as future work.

Limits the evidence itself shows: little gain on a large text corpus (§07), none on very small data sets (§10), and a measurable loss against full Bayesian averaging where that is affordable (§07).

16 Common misconceptions

"Dropout with p = 0.8 drops 80% of the units." In the original study p is the retention probability. p = 0.8 keeps 80% and drops 20%. Many library APIs instead take the drop rate as their argument, so the same number can mean opposite things; check which convention you are reading.
"Weight scaling computes the exact ensemble average." It is an approximation; exact only in special cases. On MNIST, Monte-Carlo averaging needed about 50 sampled nets to match it and was only slightly better beyond that (§11). It is exact for the normalised geometric mean when the masked part of the network is linear; with non-linear hidden units it is close, not equal.
"Convolutional layers have few parameters, so dropout there is pointless." Not in the reported results. On SVHN, adding dropout to the convolutional layers took the error from 3.02% to 2.55%, because noisier low-level features keep the fully connected layers above them from overfitting (§06).
"Dropout always gives a big improvement." It depends on how much overfitting there is to remove. It helped on every data set tried, but on Reuters-RCV1 (over 200 000 training documents) the gain was 31.05% → 29.62%, and on MNIST subsets of 100 or 500 examples it gave none (§07, §10).
"Just add dropout layers to an existing recipe." The original recipe changed several other knobs with it. Wider layers (n/p), a 10–100× larger learning rate, momentum 0.95–0.99 and a max-norm constraint (§04). Dropout + max-norm beat dropout + L2 on the same net, 1.05% vs 1.25% (§08).
"Gaussian dropout needs the same test-time rescaling as Bernoulli dropout." No — its multiplier already has mean 1. With r ~ N(1, σ²) the expected activation is unchanged, so the test-time network is used as is (§14).

17 Takeaways

  • Model: rⱼ⁽ˡ⁾ ~ Bernoulli(p), ỹ⁽ˡ⁾ = r⁽ˡ⁾ ⊙ y⁽ˡ⁾, a fresh mask per training case; at test time W_test = pW, with p the retention probability (0.5 hidden, 0.8 input as defaults).
  • Recipe: dropout + max-norm (‖w‖₂ ≤ c, c ≈ 3–4) + very high decaying learning rate + momentum 0.95–0.99, and layers at least n/p wide.
  • Pretrained nets: scale the pretrained weights by 1/p and fine-tune with a smaller learning rate.
  • Evidence: MNIST 1.60% → 0.95% (0.79% with DBM pretraining), SVHN 3.95% → 2.55%, CIFAR-10 14.98% → 12.61%, CIFAR-100 43.48% → 37.20%, ILSVRC-2012 top-5 ≈ 26% → 16.4%, TIMIT 22.7% → 19.7%; small on Reuters (31.05% → 29.62%); below a Bayesian net on alternative splicing (567 vs 623 bits) but above everything else.
  • Mechanism evidence: stand-alone edge/stroke/spot features instead of co-adapted ones, and sparse activations (mean ≈ 0.7 vs ≈ 2.0) without a sparsity penalty.
  • p and data: with width fixed, p in 0.4–0.8 is flat; with pn fixed, small p stops being harmful. Gains peak at intermediate data-set sizes.
  • Theory corners: weight scaling ≈ the Monte-Carlo average at k ≈ 50; in linear regression dropout is ridge with penalty ((1 − p)/p)‖Γw̃‖²; Gaussian noise with σ² = (1 − p)/p matches Bernoulli's moments and works as well or slightly better.
  • Cost: 2–3× longer training, because every gradient belongs to a different random sub-network.
