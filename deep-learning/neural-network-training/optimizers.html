15 Gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, and the minibatch

The update rule is one line. Everything interesting is in which examples the gradient was computed from, because that choice trades arithmetic against noise, and the trade is not close: for essentially every problem the right batch size is far smaller than the dataset, and the reason is a square root.

the ruleθ_{t+1} = θ_t − η · ĝ_t ĝ_t = (1/B) ∑_{i ∈ batch} ∇_θ ℓ(f(xᵢ; θ_t), yᵢ) Move in the direction of steepest LOCAL decrease. That direction is a statement about an infinitesimal neighbourhood and about nothing else, which is why η exists and why §16 puts a hard bound on it. three names for three values of BB = N batch (or "full-batch") gradient descent — one update per epoch 1 < B < N MINIBATCH gradient descent — what everyone actually does, and confusingly what everyone calls "SGD" B = 1 stochastic gradient descent, strictly — N updates per epoch

The estimator, and the square root that decides everything

The minibatch gradient is an average of B independent draws from the same distribution, so it is an unbiased estimate of the full-batch gradient, and its standard error falls as 1/√B. That is the whole argument, and its consequences are worth spelling out because the square root makes the trade extremely lopsided.

The constant is problem-specific; the law is not. The third column is flat, which is the point: the error is exactly c/√B, with c ≈ 7.05 here, over three orders of magnitude of B. Now read the last column. Going from B = 16 to B = 1024 costs 64 times the arithmetic and buys a factor of 8 in gradient accuracy. Every doubling of the batch buys √2 ≈ 1.41× less noise for 2× the cost — and it keeps getting worse, forever. There is no batch size at which this trade becomes good.

The deeper reason large batches are wasteful. Even the exact full-batch gradient of R̂ is only an estimate of the gradient of the thing you actually want, R — and that estimate's error is O(1/√N), fixed by the size of your dataset and unimprovable by any amount of arithmetic. Spending N/B times the compute to remove sampling noise from an estimate that already carries irreducible noise for a different reason is a bad trade twice over. This is §02's third bullet, and it is why the answer to "should I use the full dataset for each gradient" is essentially always no.

Where the noise is useful, and where it is not

useful
Escaping flat regions and saddles
At a saddle the exact gradient is zero and full-batch descent stops. A noisy gradient is not zero, so it perturbs off the ridge and continues. §17. This is not a small effect — it is one of the reasons the non-convexity of the problem turned out to be survivable in practice.
useful
A regularisation effect, for free
Gradient noise scales as η/B in the continuous-time picture, and the evidence is that a higher noise level biases the trajectory toward wider, flatter minima that generalise better. It is why simply increasing the batch size, holding everything else fixed, often worsens the test error while improving the training loss.
not useful
The last mile
Near a minimum the true gradient goes to zero but the sampling noise does not. The iterate then rattles around in a ball whose radius is set by η and B, and never settles. This is the entire reason a learning-rate schedule is necessary rather than optional (§24), and why full-batch descent can use a constant rate while SGD cannot.
the classical condition
Why the rate must go to zero, but not too fast
Convergence of SGD is guaranteed when ∑ ηₜ = ∞ and ∑ ηₜ² < ∞. The first says the steps must add up to enough total distance to reach anywhere; the second says the accumulated noise must be finite. ηₜ ∝ 1/t satisfies both; ηₜ ∝ 1/√t fails the second; a constant rate fails both. In practice nobody uses a schedule that satisfies these, because they are asymptotic and training is not.

Sampling matters, and the obvious way is wrong

The unbiasedness argument requires the batch to be an independent sample. Two ways people break it, both common:

  • Not shuffling. If the file is sorted by class, every batch contains one class, every gradient points at "predict this class", and consecutive updates fight each other. Shuffle once per epoch. Shuffling once, ever, is not enough: the same batches recur, so the same correlated noise recurs, and the trajectory can lock into a cycle.
  • Sampling without replacement and calling it i.i.d. Real implementations shuffle and walk the permutation, which is sampling without replacement. It is not i.i.d. — it is slightly better, because it guarantees coverage — and the difference matters only for the last, ragged batch of an epoch, which has fewer examples and therefore a noisier gradient with the same weight. Drop it or keep it, but know it is there.

16 Curvature, the condition number, and why the learning rate has a hard ceiling

The gradient tells you which way is downhill. It says nothing about how far you can go before downhill stops being downhill — that is curvature, and it is the second derivative. Once you look at the second-order picture, the learning rate stops being a knob you tune by feel and becomes a quantity with an explicit upper bound, an explicit optimum, and an explicit convergence rate, all of them functions of two eigenvalues.

what a step actually does, to second orderL(θ − ηg) ≈ L(θ) − η gᵀg + ½η² gᵀHg ↑ the gain you wanted ↑ the correction curvature charges you The step HELPS only while ½η² gᵀHg < η gᵀg, i.e. η < 2·gᵀg / gᵀHg. Notice what that implies: it is entirely possible for the gradient to be LARGE and for every step to increase the loss, because the curvature term is growing faster than the linear term. "Big gradient" and "making progress" are different statements, and the second one requires H. on a quadratic, exactlyL(w) = ½ wᵀHw, H symmetric with eigenvalues 0 < λ_min ≤ … ≤ λ_max gradient descent: w ← (I − ηH) w in the eigenbasis: each coordinate is multiplied by (1 − ηλᵢ) every step. CONVERGES ⟺ |1 − ηλᵢ| < 1 for every i ⟺ 0 < η < 2/λ_max OPTIMAL η = 2/(λ_min + λ_max), and the per-step contraction is then exactly (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) where κ = λ_max/λ_min is the CONDITION NUMBER.

Three readings that transfer directly to a real network.

  • The ceiling is set by the largest eigenvalue and the speed by the smallest. One stiff direction — a single sharply curved coordinate — caps η for every coordinate, including the thousands that would happily take a step a hundred times larger. This is the entire motivation for the per-coordinate optimisers of §21–§23.
  • Ill-conditioning is a speed problem, not a stability problem. At κ = 6 the contraction is 0.72; at κ = 100 it is 0.980; at κ = 10⁴ it is 0.9998, which needs 11 513 steps to reduce the error tenfold. The optimiser is behaving perfectly; the problem is just shaped badly. Normalising the inputs, or inserting a normalisation layer, changes κ directly and is worth more than any amount of optimiser tuning.
  • The transition at the bound is sharp. One per cent below it the run converges; one per cent above it the run diverges. There is no gentle degradation, which is why a learning-rate sweep shows a cliff rather than a slope, and why the range test of §25 looks for exactly that cliff.
The tempting shortcut. "The loss went up, so the learning rate is too high — I'll halve it." Often right, and worth knowing why it is right. A loss that rises monotonically and fast is |1 − ηλ_max| > 1 and halving η is exactly the fix. A loss that oscillates while trending down is η slightly past the ideal for the stiffest direction and fine for everything else, and halving it will make the run slower rather than better. A loss that rises sharply once and recovers is a cliff (§17), and the fix is gradient clipping (§36), not the learning rate. Three different diagnoses, three different fixes, one symptom.

17 Saddle points, plateaus, and cliffs: what the landscape is actually like

The standard mental picture of non-convex optimisation — a rugged surface full of local minima, in which gradient descent gets stuck — is essentially wrong for high-dimensional problems, and believing it leads people to worry about the wrong thing. The real obstacles are saddle points, flat regions, and cliffs, and each has a different fix.

Why local minima are rare and saddles are everywhere

At a critical point the gradient is zero and the type of the point is decided by the Hessian's eigenvalues: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle. Now count. In n dimensions there are n eigenvalues, and for a minimum every one of them must be positive. Treat the signs as roughly independent — which is what happens for many classes of random function — and the probability of a minimum is about 2⁻ⁿ:

The coin argument is right about the conclusion and wrong about the rate — in the direction that makes the conclusion stronger. Measured above: at n = 1 the heuristic is exact, and by n = 6 it overstates the probability of a minimum by a factor of five hundred. The independence assumption is what fails. The eigenvalues of a random symmetric matrix repel one another — they do not scatter independently, they spread out — so getting all n of them onto the same side of zero is much harder than n independent coin flips, and the true decay is faster than exponential in n. Rather than repeat the heuristic's arithmetic, this page reports the measurement and notes that the effect it is used to demonstrate is larger than the heuristic claims. In a million dimensions, either way, a critical point picked at random is a saddle.

None of this is a theorem about neural networks specifically — it is a statement about random functions of this type, and real loss surfaces are not random. But the direction is supported by both theory and measurement, and it comes with a second, more useful fact: for many random functions of this type the eigenvalues become more likely to be positive as the cost falls. Low-cost critical points are usually minima; high-cost critical points are usually saddles. So the local minima that do exist tend to be good ones, and the thing standing between you and them is a landscape of saddles.

obstacle 1
Saddle points
The gradient goes to zero as you approach, so progress slows. But descent is repelled from a saddle rather than attracted to it, and any noise at all breaks the symmetry, so first-order methods generally escape. The method that genuinely fails here is Newton's, which solves for a zero gradient and will happily converge onto a saddle — a large part of why second-order methods never displaced SGD.
obstacle 2
Plateaus and flat regions
Wide regions where both the gradient and the curvature are near zero. Nothing pulls you anywhere and there is no signal to follow. These are genuinely hard for every numerical method, and the practical fixes are structural: normalisation layers, residual paths, and a sensible initialisation, all of which are about not starting in one.
obstacle 3
Cliffs
Extremely steep walls, produced by the multiplication of several large weights (§09's product, in the forward direction). The gradient at the face of a cliff is enormous, so one ordinary step launches the parameters somewhere arbitrary and undoes hours of progress. This is the failure that gradient clipping exists for, and clipping is the correct fix precisely because the gradient's direction is still fine — it is only the magnitude that is meaningless.
not an obstacle
Reaching a critical point at all
Training usually does not converge to any critical point. Measured on real runs, the gradient norm frequently increases throughout training while the validation error steadily falls. Gradient descent is designed to go downhill, not to find a place where the gradient vanishes, and it is entirely normal for a successful run to end somewhere with a perfectly healthy gradient.
A consequence worth internalising: a small gradient norm is not a stopping criterion. Classical optimisation stops when ‖∇L‖ < ε. Deep learning stops when the validation metric stops improving, which is a completely different event and usually happens while the gradient is still large. If you find yourself waiting for the gradient to vanish, you will wait a long time and gain nothing by it.

18 Exponentially weighted moving averages, and bias correction

Every optimiser after plain SGD is built from one primitive: a running average of something, kept cheaply. Learn the primitive on its own — with numbers, and with the correction it needs at the start — and momentum, RMSProp, Adam and AdamW all become one-line variations rather than four algorithms to memorise.

the recursionv₀ = 0 v_t = β·v_{t−1} + (1 − β)·θ_t unrolled — this is what makes it an "exponentially weighted" averagev_t = (1 − β)·[ θ_t + βθ_{t−1} + β²θ_{t−2} + … + β^{t−1}θ₁ ] The weights decay geometrically into the past, they sum to 1 − βᵗ (not to 1 — that is the bug below), and the whole history is kept in ONE number. Cost: one multiply-add and one slot of memory per parameter, regardless of how far back the average reaches.

How far back does it look

The weight on the observation k steps ago is βᵏ. Ask when that has fallen to 1/e: solve βᵏ = 1/e, which for β near 1 gives k ≈ 1/(1 − β). That is the standard "averaging over 1/(1−β) steps" rule of thumb, and it is worth seeing how good it is:

The bias, and its exact correction

Start the recursion at v₀ = 0 and the first estimate is v₁ = (1 − β)·θ₁. With β = 0.98 that is 0.02·θ₁ — the average of a single observation, reported as two per cent of it. The cause is visible in the unrolled form: the weights sum to 1 − βᵗ, not to 1, so the estimate is systematically shrunk toward the zero it was initialised at. Divide by the actual weight and the bias is gone exactly:

bias correction — one division, and it is exact, not approximatev̂_t = v_t / (1 − βᵗ) If every θ_t = c, then v_t = c(1 − βᵗ) exactly, so v̂_t = c exactly for every t including t = 1. The correction is not a heuristic warm-up; it is the exact normalisation the estimator was missing. The factor 1/(1 − βᵗ) is 1/(1 − β) at t = 1 and decays to 1: at β = 0.9 it is 10.0 at t = 1, 1.30 at t = 10, 1.0000 by t ≈ 44. It matters at the START and nowhere else — which is exactly where a badly sized step does the most damage (§11).

The moving average, its lag, and what bias correction fixes turn the correction off at β = 0.98 and watch the first fifty steps crawl up from zero — that is the whole phenomenon
A distinction worth being pedantic about, because it changes the learning rate by 10×. There are two conventions for momentum in circulation and they are different algorithms with the same name. The EWMA form is v ← βv + (1−β)g; the accumulation form is v ← βv + g. Under a constant gradient the first settles at v = g and the second at v = g/(1−β) — a factor of ten at β = 0.9, verified numerically. Frameworks predominantly implement the accumulation form; lecture material predominantly writes the EWMA form. Copy a learning rate from one into the other and it is off by 1/(1−β). §20 keeps them separate and the figure there lets you switch between them.

The memory bill, which is not a footnote

Each running average is one full copy of the parameters. For a model with P parameters in 32-bit floats, the optimiser state alone is 4P bytes per buffer, on top of 4P for the weights and 4P for the gradients. Concretely, for P = 7×10⁹:

Adam costs twice the memory of plain SGD before a single activation is stored. That is a real constraint at scale, and it is why optimiser-state sharding is the first thing a distributed training system does — see Distributed Training, which owns that. On one device the levers are §27 and §28.

Which one to actually use. The honest, boring answer: AdamW with the default betas is the right first choice for essentially everything, and for transformer-shaped models it is not seriously contested. SGD with Nesterov momentum remains competitive and sometimes better for convolutional vision models trained to convergence with a good schedule, and there is real evidence that adaptive methods can generalise slightly worse on some problems. What is not true is that the choice of optimiser is where the gains are. The learning rate matters more than the optimiser, the schedule matters more than the betas, and the data pipeline matters more than all of them. Spend the tuning budget accordingly (§37).

20 Momentum, and Nesterov's correction

Momentum exists because of §16's picture. In a badly conditioned ravine the gradient points mostly across the valley, not along it, so consecutive gradients largely cancel in the stiff direction and largely agree in the shallow one. Averaging them keeps what agrees and cancels what does not. That is the whole idea, and everything else is bookkeeping about the constant.

the two conventions — READ THIS BEFORE COPYING A LEARNING RATEACCUMULATION form (what most frameworks implement): v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv EWMA form (what most lecture material writes): v ← βv + (1 − β)g θ ← θ − ηv Under a CONSTANT gradient g the two settle at different velocities: accumulation v → g/(1 − β) = 10g at β = 0.9, 100g at β = 0.99 EWMA v → g always Verified numerically: 3000 steps at g = 1, η = 0.1, gives a final step of −1.0000 for the accumulation form at β = 0.9 and −0.1000 for the EWMA form. The accumulation form multiplies your effective learning rate by 1/(1 − β).

The practical consequence is worth stating as a rule: when you turn momentum on in the accumulation form, or raise β, you have raised the effective learning rate by 1/(1−β) and you should expect to lower η. The standard combination η = 0.1, β = 0.9 has an effective terminal step of 1.0, not 0.1. Moving from β = 0.9 to β = 0.99 without changing η multiplies the terminal step by ten, and a run that then diverges was not "unstable with high momentum" — it was running at ten times the learning rate you thought.

What momentum buys, in one number

terminal velocity, and how long it takes to get thereaccumulation form, constant g: v_t = g·(1 − βᵗ)/(1 − β) → g/(1 − β) steps to reach 90% of terminal velocity: β = 0.9 → 22 steps β = 0.99 → 230 steps So momentum is NOT an instantaneous speed-up. It takes tens to hundreds of steps to spin up, which is one reason it interacts with warmup (§24) and one reason very short runs see less benefit than long ones.
in the shallow direction
Consistent gradients accumulate
Every step's gradient points the same way, so the velocity builds toward g/(1−β) and the effective step grows tenfold at β = 0.9. Progress along the valley floor speeds up by exactly the factor the ravine was costing you.
in the stiff direction
Alternating gradients cancel
Successive gradients flip sign, so consecutive terms in the average subtract. The oscillation is damped rather than amplified. Momentum improves the conditioning of the effective problem: the achievable rate improves from (κ−1)/(κ+1) to roughly (√κ−1)/(√κ+1), which at κ = 100 is 0.980 against 0.818.
at a saddle
It carries you through the flat part
The velocity is non-zero even where the gradient is, so the iterate keeps moving through a plateau instead of stalling in it. This is the same mechanism as the physical analogy: a ball with speed does not stop at a level patch.
at the bottom
It overshoots
Arriving at the minimum with velocity means going past it. The iterate oscillates about the optimum with a period set by β and η. This is the specific problem Nesterov's variant addresses, and it is also why momentum and a decaying schedule work well together.

Nesterov: look before you leap

Standard momentum computes the gradient where it currently is, then adds the momentum step. Nesterov's variant does it the other way round: take the momentum step first, and evaluate the gradient there. Since you are going to move by −ηβv regardless, the gradient at the destination is more relevant than the gradient at the origin.

the difference, preciselyclassical g = ∇L(θ) v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv Nesterov g = ∇L(θ − ηβv) v ← βv + g θ ← θ − ηv ↑ evaluated at the LOOKAHEAD point The lookahead makes it a CORRECTION rather than an ANTICIPATION. When the velocity is about to carry the iterate past the minimum, the gradient at the lookahead point already points back, and the overshoot is damped a step earlier than it would otherwise be. Frameworks implement an algebraically equivalent rewriting that avoids evaluating the gradient anywhere except the current point, so it costs nothing extra.

The gain is real but modest, and it is largest exactly where momentum is most aggressive — high β, sharp turns, the approach to a minimum. On a convex problem it improves the theoretical rate; on a neural network it is a free improvement that is worth taking and not worth agonising over. Turn it on; do not expect a miracle.

21 AdaGrad and RMSProp: one learning rate per coordinate

§16's bound is set by the largest curvature over all coordinates, so every coordinate is forced to take the step size that suits the stiffest one. The adaptive methods break that coupling. They give each parameter its own effective step, inferred from the only per-coordinate signal available for free: the size of that coordinate's own gradients so far.

AdaGrad — accumulate every squared gradient, foreverr ← r + g ⊙ g element-wise θ ← θ − η · g / (√r + ε) element-wise A coordinate with consistently large gradients accumulates a large r and takes SMALL steps. A coordinate that has rarely been touched keeps a small r and takes LARGE ones. The per-coordinate effective rate is η/√rᵢ, and it depends on that coordinate's own history and nothing else. RMSProp — the same, with a moving average instead of a sumr ← β₂·r + (1 − β₂)·g ⊙ g the §18 primitive, applied to g² θ ← θ − η · g / (√r + ε)

Why AdaGrad had to be replaced

r is a sum of non-negative terms, so it is monotonically increasing and unbounded. Under a roughly constant gradient magnitude g, after t steps r ≈ t·g², so the effective learning rate is η/(g√t) — it decays like 1/√t no matter what the loss surface is doing. On a convex problem that is exactly the schedule the theory wants and AdaGrad's guarantees are excellent. On a non-convex problem trained for a long time it means the effective learning rate reaches zero while there is still progress to be made, and the run stalls for reasons that have nothing to do with the loss.

effective rate η/√rᵢ under a constant gradient of magnitude 1, η = 0.01: step AdaGrad RMSProp (β₂ = 0.999) 1 0.01000 0.31623 ← the ε-free ratio is 1/√(1−β₂) 100 0.00100 0.03241 ← still warming up: r = 1 − β₂¹⁰⁰ = 0.0952 10 000 0.00010 0.01000 ← r has reached 1; the average has forgotten 10⁶ 0.00001 0.01000 AdaGrad's rate falls by 10× per hundredfold increase in t, forever. RMSProp's settles at η/g and stays there, because the average FORGETS.

RMSProp fixes exactly that one thing, by replacing the sum with §18's exponentially weighted average. The window is 1/(1−β₂) steps: at the usual β₂ = 0.999 that is a thousand steps of history, long enough to be a stable estimate of the gradient scale and short enough to track a scale that genuinely changes as training proceeds. Note also what the table's first row shows — with the average started at zero, RMSProp's first step is 1/√(1−β₂) = 31.6 times larger than its steady-state step. That start-up transient is a real problem, and §22 is where it gets fixed.

What ε is actually for, and why its default is what it is. Nominally ε prevents division by zero. Its real job is to bound the maximum step: the update is at most η·|g|/ε, so ε is the floor on the denominator and therefore the ceiling on the step. Typical values run from 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁶. Raising it makes the method behave more like plain SGD, because a large ε dominates √r and the per-coordinate scaling disappears — which is occasionally the right emergency fix for an adaptive method that is behaving erratically, and is worth knowing as a knob rather than a magic constant. Note also that some implementations place ε inside the square root, √(r + ε), which is a different function and differs materially when r is small.

Where the per-coordinate idea genuinely wins

  • Sparse features. A feature that appears in one example in ten thousand contributes a gradient one time in ten thousand. Under SGD its weight is effectively never trained. Under an adaptive method its r stays small, so the rare gradient it does receive is amplified. This is the setting AdaGrad was designed for and it is a large effect.
  • Embeddings and output layers. Same argument: token embeddings for rare tokens, and the rows of a large softmax matrix, are the archetypal sparse-gradient parameters. It is not a coincidence that adaptive methods dominate in language models.
  • Parameters with genuinely different scales. Biases, gains of normalisation layers, and weights all have different natural magnitudes and different gradient magnitudes. A per-coordinate rate handles that automatically; a single global rate handles it by compromise.
  • Where it does not obviously win: a well-conditioned, dense, well-normalised convolutional network trained with a good schedule. That is precisely the setting in which SGD with momentum remains competitive, and the reason the "just use Adam" advice has an exception.

22 Adam: both moving averages, and the bias correction that makes them work

Adam is momentum and RMSProp in the same algorithm, plus §18's bias correction applied to both. The combination is unremarkable; the correction is the part that has a surprise in it, and it is a surprise in the opposite direction from the one everybody expects.

the algorithm, completem₀ = 0, v₀ = 0, t = 0 on each step: t ← t + 1 g ← ∇_θ L the minibatch gradient m ← β₁·m + (1 − β₁)·g 1st moment: the direction v ← β₂·v + (1 − β₂)·g ⊙ g 2nd moment: the scale m̂ = m/(1 − β₁ᵗ) bias correction v̂ = v/(1 − β₂ᵗ) θ ← θ − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) defaults β₁ = 0.9 β₂ = 0.999 ε = 10⁻⁸ η = 10⁻³ The two betas are averaging over ~10 and ~1000 steps respectively: the DIRECTION is smoothed over a short window and the SCALE over a long one, which is the right asymmetry — direction changes fast, scale does not.

The bias correction: the surprise

The standard story is that bias correction stops the moving averages from "starting off too low". That is true of each average individually — and it is not what determines Adam's first step, because the update is a ratio and both numerator and denominator are biased. Work out the ratio at t = 1, where m₁ = (1−β₁)g and v₁ = (1−β₂)g²:

the uncorrected first step, exactlym₁/√v₁ = (1 − β₁)·g / √((1 − β₂)·g²) = (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) · sign(g) At the DEFAULTS: (1 − 0.9)/√(1 − 0.999) = 0.1/0.0316228 = 3.1623 = √10 So the uncorrected first step is not too SMALL. It is √10 times too LARGE, and the correction makes it exactly 1. Verified numerically: with a constant gradient g = 0.5, corrected Adam's step is −1.0000000 for every t from 1 to 6; uncorrected, the first step is −3.162276. and the sign of the error depends on the betas, which is why it must be exactβ₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 → 3.1623× too LARGE β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.99 → 1.0000× exactly right, by coincidence β₁ = 0.5, β₂ = 0.999 → 15.811× too LARGE β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.9 → 0.3162× too SMALL β₁ = 0.99, β₂ = 0.999 → 0.3162× too SMALL

This matters, and not only as a curiosity. A three-fold oversized step on the very first update, before any structure exists in the network, is exactly the kind of event that kills rectified units (§11) and knocks a run into a region it spends a thousand steps climbing out of. It is also part of why Adam and warmup (§24) are so often used together: warmup is a second, cruder defence against the same failure, and it is needed because bias correction alone does not cover the whole start-up transient — only its first few steps.

The other thing the correction buys: a step size you can reason about. With both corrections in place, m̂/√v̂ is a ratio of a mean to a root-mean-square of the same quantity, so it is dimensionless and of order 1. The update is therefore about η in magnitude, per parameter, regardless of the scale of the loss or of the gradients. Measured over 5 000 steps of a deliberately non-stationary noisy gradient stream, the largest value of |m̂/(√v̂ + ε)| observed was 1.0546. That is why η = 10⁻³ is a usable default across wildly different problems, in a way that no SGD learning rate ever is — and it is the single most practically important property Adam has. Multiply your loss by 1000 and SGD needs its learning rate divided by 1000; Adam does not notice.

What Adam is not

  • It is not a second-order method. v is an average of squared gradients, not of curvature. On a quadratic the two are related but not equal, and there are surfaces on which they point in opposite directions. Adam approximates a diagonal preconditioner using first-order information only; that is a useful thing and it is not the Hessian.
  • It does not remove the need for a schedule. The per-coordinate scaling adapts to the gradient magnitude, not to the phase of training. Every serious Adam recipe is Adam plus warmup plus decay, and §24 is not optional.
  • It is not always better. There is credible evidence that adaptive methods find solutions that generalise slightly worse than SGD with momentum on some problems, particularly in vision. The effect is real, small, and problem-dependent.
  • Its β₂ is not a free parameter at small batch size. With very noisy gradients, v̂ is a poor estimate of the true gradient scale and the resulting step is erratic. Raising β₂ toward 0.999 or beyond averages over more steps and helps; lowering it makes an already-noisy method noisier. This is the one beta that is worth touching.

23 AdamW: why L2 and weight decay stopped being the same thing

Under plain gradient descent, adding λ/2·‖θ‖² to the loss and multiplying the weights by (1 − ηλ) each step are algebraically identical operations. Under any adaptive method they are not, and the gap is large enough that fixing it produced a measurably better optimiser. This section is that one derivation.

under SGD the two really are the same — this is the identity everyone remembersL̃(θ) = L(θ) + (λ/2)‖θ‖² ⇒ ∇L̃ = ∇L + λθ θ ← θ − η(∇L + λθ) = (1 − ηλ)θ − η∇L ↑ a MULTIPLICATIVE shrink of every weight, by the same factor, applied before the usual step under Adam the penalty goes through the 1/√v̂ machinery, and the identity breaksCOUPLED (L2 in the loss — what "weight_decay" means in a plain Adam): g ← ∇L + λθ then m, v, and the update ηm̂/(√v̂ + ε) the decay term is DIVIDED BY √v̂ along with everything else, so the effective decay on coordinate i is ηλθᵢ/√v̂ᵢ — different for every coordinate, and INVERSELY proportional to that coordinate's own recent gradient magnitude. DECOUPLED (AdamW): g ← ∇L the penalty is NOT in g θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√v̂ + ε) − ηλθ added separately every coordinate decays at the same rate ηλ, exactly as it does under SGD, and the adaptive machinery never sees it.

What the coupling actually does, stated as the bug it is

Under coupled L2, the amount by which a weight is shrunk depends on how large that coordinate's recent gradients have been. A parameter with large gradients has a large √v̂, so its decay is divided down and it is barely regularised. A parameter with small gradients has a small √v̂, so its decay is amplified and it is regularised hard. Read that again: the parameters that matter most are penalised least, and the ones that matter least are penalised most. That is the opposite of what the regulariser was supposed to do, and it is not a small effect — √v̂ varies over orders of magnitude across a real network.

consequence 1
The two hyperparameters are entangled
Under coupled L2 the effective decay is ηλ/√v̂, so changing η changes the amount of regularisation. Tune the learning rate and you have silently retuned the weight decay, so the two must be searched jointly. Decoupled, the effective decay is ηλ, which is still coupled to η — some implementations divide it out so that λ is genuinely independent, and it is worth checking which one you have.
consequence 2
The regularisation is applied backwards
Large-gradient coordinates escape the penalty; small-gradient coordinates absorb it. There is no principled reason to want this, and it is a large part of why "Adam generalises worse than SGD" was observed before the cause was identified.
the fix
Move the term out of the gradient
One line. The decay is a function of the current parameters, not of the loss, so it is not a gradient and does not belong in g. Applying it directly to θ restores the uniform multiplicative shrink that made weight decay work in the first place.
and while you are there
Do not decay everything
Biases and the gains and offsets of normalisation layers should generally be excluded from weight decay. There is no reason to shrink a normalisation gain toward zero — doing so fights the layer's entire purpose — and biases carry no capacity worth regularising. Most well-tuned recipes exclude both; most quick implementations do not, and the difference is measurable.
The practical instruction, in one line. Use the optimiser named AdamW and set its decay explicitly; do not use the weight_decay argument of a plain Adam and expect it to mean the same thing. It does not: one is L2 folded into the gradient, the other is a decoupled shrink, and they will give you different models. This is a case where two functions with the same argument name do genuinely different arithmetic, which is exactly the kind of thing that is invisible in a diff.
