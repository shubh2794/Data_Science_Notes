Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization

The optimisers page already derives the update, the defaults and the bias-correction surprise. This page reads the original study itself: how it frames the method, what its analysis of the effective step Δₜ = α·m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ actually claims (a trust region, a signal-to-noise ratio, automatic annealing, scale invariance), where the bias-correction term comes from and why sparse gradients make it essential, what the convergence theorem assumes and proves — and, clearly separated, how later work showed that proof to be wrong. 01 What the original study set out to do

A first-order method for noisy objectives in high-dimensional parameter spaces, where second-order methods are ruled out by cost. The name is from adaptive moment estimation: every parameter gets its own step size, computed from running estimates of the first and second moments of its gradient.

  • Two parents, one strength each. The method is explicitly designed to combine AdaGrad, which works well with sparse gradients, and RMSProp, which works well in on-line and non-stationary settings. §08 makes both relationships exact.
  • Noise is the setting, not a nuisance. The stochasticity is taken to come from minibatch subsampling, from inherent function noise, or from stochastic regularisers such as dropout — the last one matters in §09, where a quasi-Newton competitor fails exactly there.
  • The objective is E[f(θ)]; f₁, …, f_T are its realisations at successive steps and gₜ = ∇θ fₜ(θₜ₋₁).
claimed property 1
Scale-invariant step
The magnitude of the update is invariant to rescaling the gradient (§04).
claimed property 2
Step ≈ bounded by α
The step size hyperparameter is approximately an upper bound on each coordinate's step (§03).
claimed property 3
No stationarity needed
Exponential forgetting lets the moment estimates track an objective whose gradient statistics drift.
claimed property 4
Sparse gradients
It works with gradients that are zero most of the time — provided the bias correction is in place (§05).
claimed property 5
Built-in annealing
Steps shrink automatically as the gradient's signal-to-noise ratio falls near an optimum (§04).

The study appeared as a conference paper in 2015; the public preprint went through nine versions, the last in January 2017, and its acknowledgements credit a reader with catching an error in the original AdaMax derivation. The convergence proof of §06 survived all nine versions; §07 is about that.

02 Algorithm 1, compactly — and its "more efficient" form

The update is restated here only as the study writes it. The derivation of each line, the defaults' ~10- and ~1000-step windows, and the exact first-step ratio √10 are on the optimisers page, §22; the moving-average primitive and its correction are its §18.

Algorithm 1, as given (all operations element-wise)require α (stepsize), β₁, β₂ ∈ [0, 1), f(θ), θ₀ m₀ ← 0, v₀ ← 0, t ← 0 while θₜ not converged: t ← t + 1 gₜ ← ∇θ fₜ(θₜ₋₁) mₜ ← β₁·mₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₁)·gₜ biased first moment vₜ ← β₂·vₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₂)·gₜ² biased second RAW moment (uncentred) m̂ₜ ← mₜ / (1 − β₁ᵗ) v̂ₜ ← vₜ / (1 − β₂ᵗ) θₜ ← θₜ₋₁ − α · m̂ₜ / (√v̂ₜ + ε) defaults "for the tested machine learning problems": α = 0.001 β₁ = 0.9 β₂ = 0.999 ε = 10⁻⁸ the reordering the study offers "at the expense of clarity"αₜ = α · √(1 − β₂ᵗ) / (1 − β₁ᵗ) θₜ ← θₜ₋₁ − αₜ · mₜ / (√vₜ + ε̂)

The second form folds both corrections into a per-step learning rate and never forms m̂ or v̂. Note the hat on ε̂: it is not the same constant. Our own arithmetic: the two forms agree exactly only if ε̂ = ε·√(1 − β₂ᵗ), so a framework that uses the reordered form with a fixed ε runs a slightly different algorithm during the first few thousand steps at β₂ = 0.999 — its effective ε is up to 1/√(1 − β₂) ≈ 31.6 times larger at t = 1. It is a small difference, and it is one reason two "Adam" implementations can disagree early in training.

03 The effective step and its two bounds (section 2.1)

The study calls its "careful choice of stepsizes" an important property and analyses it directly. With ε = 0, the step actually taken in parameter space on coordinate i is Δₜ = α·m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ, and its size is controlled by α — not by the gradient.

the two upper bounds stated in the study|Δₜ| ≤ α · (1 − β₁) / √(1 − β₂) in the case (1 − β₁) > √(1 − β₂) |Δₜ| ≤ α otherwise which case the defaults fall in — our arithmeticβ₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999: 1 − β₁ = 0.1 > √0.001 = 0.0316 → FIRST case bound = 0.1 / 0.0316 = 3.162 · α β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.99: 1 − β₁ = 0.1 = √0.01 = 0.1 → second case, |Δₜ| < α β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.9999: 1 − β₁ = 0.1 > √0.0001 = 0.01 → first case, bound 10 · α
  • The first case is the extreme of sparsity. The study says it "only happens in the most severe case of sparsity": the gradient has been zero at every step except the current one. Then mₜ = (1 − β₁)gₜ and vₜ = (1 − β₂)gₜ², and the ratio is exactly (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) before correction. With the correction factors it is that times √(1 − β₂ᵗ)/(1 − β₁ᵗ), which tends to the same limit as t grows.
  • Less sparse means smaller. When (1 − β₁) = √(1 − β₂), |m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ| < 1 and so |Δₜ| < α. In the common case the study argues m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ ≈ ±1, because |E[g]| / √E[g²] ≤ 1: a mean can never exceed a root-mean-square.
  • So the summary claim is |Δₜ| ⪅ α — "approximately bounded". Keep the "approximately": it is a statement about typical gradient streams, not a worst case over all of them (see the growing-gradient stream in the figure, and §10).
The effective step |Δₜ|/α on a chosen gradient stream — a simulation of one coordinate start with "one spike after a long silence" at β₂ = 0.9999 and compare the corrected and uncorrected curves; then try the growing stream, which pushes Adam past the stated bound

A simulation, not data from the study: one scalar coordinate, ε = 10⁻⁸, 3000 steps of a synthetic stream; noise is a seeded Gaussian. What it reproduces exactly is algebra — the uncorrected spike step equals (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) because the zeros before it leave m and v at zero.

What the figure shows that the bound statement does not

  • Spike at t = 1500, defaults: uncorrected 3.162α, exactly the bound; corrected 2.79α. At β₂ = 0.9999: uncorrected 10.0α, corrected 3.73α — the correction is far from cosmetic when β₂ is close to 1, which is §05's point. (Simulation values.)
  • Growing gradients exceed the stated bound. If each gradient is larger than the last, m (short memory) is dominated by the large recent values while v (long memory) still remembers smaller ones, and the ratio climbs. With gradients growing ×1.11 per step, the simulated corrected step peaks near 5.9α at the defaults, and the uncorrected one near 7.27α. Our own arithmetic gives the true worst case by Cauchy–Schwarz: |mₜ/√vₜ| ≤ (1 − β₁) / √((1 − β₂)(1 − β₁²/β₂)), which is 7.27 at the defaults. That is the honest ceiling; the study's 3.16 is the ceiling for the sparse pattern it analyses.
  • AdaMax's step at a lone spike is (1 − β₁)α = 0.1α, because its denominator is the gradient's own size rather than a shrunken average of its square (§10).

04 Three readings of the same ratio: trust region, signal-to-noise, scale invariance

Section 2.1 then interprets m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ three ways. Each one is a reason the default α transfers across problems.

A trust region of radius about α

Because |Δₜ| ⪅ α, each step stays within a region of size about α around the current parameters, "beyond which the current gradient estimate does not provide sufficient information." The practical consequence the study draws: you can often choose α in advance. If you know roughly how far good optima lie from θ₀ — many models have a prior over their parameters — then α sets the order of magnitude of the distance covered per step, and you can pick α so that the optimum is reachable in some number of iterations. Under SGD the same reasoning is impossible, because the step is α·g and depends on the unknown gradient scale.

The signal-to-noise ratio, and automatic annealing

"with a slight abuse of terminology"SNR = m̂ₜ / √v̂ₜ ≈ E[g] / √E[g²] E[g²] = E[g]² + Var[g] ⇒ |SNR| ≈ |E[g]| / √(E[g]² + Var[g]) ∈ [0, 1]

The second line is our own expansion, to show why the name fits. When the gradient's mean dominates its spread, the ratio is near ±1 and Adam takes full steps of about α. When the mean is small compared with the noise, the ratio is small and so is the step. The study calls this desirable: a small SNR means more uncertainty about whether m̂ₜ points the way the true gradient does. And because the SNR "typically becomes closer to 0 towards an optimum" — the mean gradient vanishes there while minibatch noise does not — the steps shrink on their own: a form of automatic annealing. Choose "approaching an optimum" in the figure above to watch it happen. (It is not a substitute for a schedule; see the misconceptions in §11 and the schedules page.)

Invariance to diagonal rescaling of the gradient

rescale every gradient by c (per coordinate, so "diagonal")g → c·g ⇒ m̂ₜ → c·m̂ₜ , v̂ₜ → c²·v̂ₜ (c · m̂ₜ) / √(c² · v̂ₜ) = m̂ₜ / √v̂ₜ the step does not change

Because the moments are kept per coordinate, c can be a different positive number on every coordinate — a diagonal rescaling — and the steps are still unchanged. That is the abstract's "invariant to diagonal rescaling of the gradients". One caveat the derivation hides by setting ε = 0: the invariance holds only while √v̂ₜ ≫ ε. Set the figure's gradient scale to ×10⁻⁹ and ε = 10⁻⁸ dominates the denominator, the steps collapse, and Adam has silently turned into momentum SGD with learning rate α/ε. §09 records the study itself observing this in its convolutional network.

05 Where the bias-correction term comes from (section 3)

The study derives the correction for the second moment; the first is "completely analogous". The point of the derivation is the ζ term — it says when the correction is exact and when it is only approximately right.

unroll the moving average, with v₀ = 0 and gₜ ~ p(gₜ)vₜ = (1 − β₂) · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ β₂ᵗ⁻ⁱ · gᵢ² (1) take expectationsE[vₜ] = E[ (1 − β₂) · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ β₂ᵗ⁻ⁱ · gᵢ² ] (2) = E[gₜ²] · (1 − β₂) · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ β₂ᵗ⁻ⁱ + ζ (3) = E[gₜ²] · (1 − β₂ᵗ) + ζ (4) ζ = 0 if the true second moment E[gᵢ²] is stationary ζ small otherwise, because the average puts little weight on old gradients

Step (3) pulls out the current second moment and puts all the drift into ζ; step (4) is the finite geometric sum (1 − β₂)·(1 + β₂ + … + β₂ᵗ⁻¹) = 1 − β₂ᵗ. What is left, (1 − β₂ᵗ), is caused purely by starting at zero, so Algorithm 1 divides by it. (The study's text names the first decay rate in the sentence about keeping ζ small; the moving average in question is the β₂ one.)

Why sparse gradients force large β₂, and why that makes the correction essential

  • The estimation problem. A gradient that is non-zero one step in a hundred gives v one informative sample per hundred steps. A reliable second-moment estimate needs many of those samples, so it must average over a long window — 1 − β₂ small, β₂ close to 1. (The study's sentence says "a small value of β₂"; §5 and §6.4 of the study make clear the intended meaning is β₂ close to 1, i.e. small 1 − β₂.)
  • The trap. Large β₂ is exactly the case where the initialisation bias is largest and lasts longest: 1 − β₂ᵗ stays far below 1 for thousands of steps. Without the correction, v is underestimated, the denominator is too small, and the early steps are too large. Our arithmetic for the uncorrected first step at β₁ = 0.9: 3.16α at β₂ = 0.999 and 10α at β₂ = 0.9999; the hub's §22 has the general table.
  • The experiment that tests it is §09's VAE ablation: the prediction is instability without correction when β₂ is near 1, worst in the early epochs.

06 The convergence analysis, as the study states it (section 4)

The analysis uses the online convex optimisation framework: an arbitrary, unknown sequence of convex losses f₁, f₂, …, f_T; at each step the algorithm commits to θₜ and is then charged fₜ(θₜ). Performance is measured by regret against the best fixed point in hindsight. The general background on convexity and step-size conditions is on the optimisation page.

regret, and the notationR(T) = ∑ₜ₌₁ᵀ [ fₜ(θₜ) − fₜ(θ*) ] θ* = argmin over θ ∈ 𝒳 of ∑ₜ₌₁ᵀ fₜ(θ) gₜ = ∇fₜ(θₜ), gₜ,ᵢ its i-th coordinate, g₁:ₜ,ᵢ = [g₁,ᵢ, g₂,ᵢ, …, gₜ,ᵢ] ∈ ℝᵗ γ = β₁² / √β₂ Theorem 4.1 — assumptions‖∇fₜ(θ)‖₂ ≤ G, ‖∇fₜ(θ)‖∞ ≤ G∞ bounded gradients ‖θₙ − θₘ‖₂ ≤ D, ‖θₘ − θₙ‖∞ ≤ D∞ bounded iterates β₁, β₂ ∈ [0, 1) with γ = β₁²/√β₂ < 1 αₜ = α / √t decaying step size β₁,ₜ = β₁ · λᵗ⁻¹, λ ∈ (0, 1) decaying momentum ("typically close to 1, e.g. 1 − 10⁻⁸") Theorem 4.1 — the bound, for all T ≥ 1R(T) ≤ D² / (2α(1 − β₁)) · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵈ √(T · v̂_T,ᵢ) + α(1 + β₁)G∞ / ( (1 − β₁) · √(1 − β₂) · (1 − γ)² ) · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵈ ‖g₁:T,ᵢ‖₂ + ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵈ D∞² G∞ √(1 − β₂) / ( 2α(1 − β₁)(1 − λ)² ) Corollary 4.2 — average regretusing ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵈ ‖g₁:T,ᵢ‖₂ ≤ d·G∞·√T : R(T) / T = O(1/√T) so lim over T → ∞ of R(T)/T = 0

Reading the three terms

  • Why the whole thing is O(√T) — our arithmetic. Each v̂_T,ᵢ ≤ G∞², so the first term is at most d·D²·G∞·√T / (2α(1 − β₁)); each ‖g₁:T,ᵢ‖₂ ≤ G∞·√T, so the second is O(d√T) too; the third does not depend on T. Dividing by T gives the corollary.
  • Where the adaptivity pays. With sparse features and bounded gradients the two sums can be much smaller than their worst cases: ∑ᵢ ‖g₁:T,ᵢ‖₂ ≪ d·G∞·√T and ∑ᵢ √(T·v̂_T,ᵢ) ≪ d·G∞·√T. Borrowing AdaGrad's analysis of such problem classes, the study states that adaptive methods like Adam and AdaGrad can reach O(log d · √T), against O(√(dT)) for a non-adaptive method.
  • Decaying β₁ is load-bearing. The study notes that decaying β₁,ₜ toward zero is important to the analysis, and links it to an earlier empirical finding that reducing the momentum coefficient at the end of training can improve convergence.
  • What λ = 1 − 10⁻⁸ means in practice — our arithmetic. After a million steps λ¹⁰⁶ = e^(−0.01) ≈ 0.990, so β₁,ₜ has moved from 0.9 to about 0.891: the decay is real in the proof and invisible in a training run. Meanwhile (1 − λ)² = 10⁻¹⁶ sits in the denominator of the third term, which is therefore constant in T but astronomically large. The bound is an asymptotic statement, not a usable estimate.
  • The scope is convex. The study says plainly (in §6.2) that the analysis does not apply to non-convex problems such as multilayer networks; the evidence there is empirical.

07 Later work: the proof is flawed, and Adam can fail on a one-dimensional convex problem

not in the original study — everything in this section comes from a later analysis published in 2018, which studied the convergence of Adam-type methods. It is included because a reader of §06 needs it.

The regret proof assumes a quantity is non-negative that Adam does not keep non-negative. With that assumption removed, there is a simple online convex problem on which Adam, with constant β₁ and β₂, has average regret that does not go to zero.

the step the proof gets wrong (per coordinate)Γₜ₊₁ = √v̂ₜ₊₁ / αₜ₊₁ − √v̂ₜ / αₜ The proof telescopes a sum of terms (θₜ,ᵢ − θ*ᵢ)² · Γₜ,ᵢ and bounds it by a multiple of D∞² · √(T·v̂_T,ᵢ)/α. That is valid only if every Γₜ,ᵢ ≥ 0 — the "learning rate" αₜ/√v̂ₜ never increases. SGD and AdaGrad satisfy it. Adam does not: v̂ can shrink faster than αₜ = α/√t does, and then the effective rate GROWS. the counterexample (feasible set 𝒳 = [−1, 1])fₜ(x) = C·x if t mod 3 = 1 −x otherwise with C > 2 over each block of three steps the gradients are C, −1, −1 (sum C − 2 > 0) ⇒ the best fixed point is x* = −1 with β₁ = 0 and β₂ = 1/(1 + C²), Adam converges to x = +1, the WORST point: R(T) ≥ (2C − 4)·T / 3 so R(T)/T does not go to 0

The intuition given there: Adam sees the large, correct gradient C once every three steps and the small, wrong gradient −1 twice. With this short-memory β₂, v̂ jumps up whenever C arrives, so the big step is divided down by almost C, while the two small steps are divided by a v̂ that has already decayed. Net drift is toward +1. Plain SGD, which scales all three gradients equally, drifts correctly. The same analysis then extends the result: for any constant β₁, β₂ with β₁ < √β₂ there is an online convex problem on which Adam's average regret stays bounded away from zero, and also a stochastic convex problem on which it fails to converge.

the fix proposed there: AMSGradvₜ = β₂·vₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₂)·gₜ² v̂ₜ = max(v̂ₜ₋₁, vₜ) never let the denominator shrink θₜ₊₁ = Π_𝒳( θₜ − αₜ · mₜ / √v̂ₜ ) ⇒ Γₜ ≥ 0 whenever αₜ is non-increasing
The later counterexample, simulated: Adam vs AMSGrad vs SGD on fₜ(x) = C·x once in three steps, −x otherwise with β₂ = 1/(1 + C²) Adam walks to the wrong end of the interval and its average regret flattens on the red floor; switch β₂ to 0.999 and it recovers on this particular sequence

A simulation of the published construction. Both adaptive methods here apply Algorithm 1's bias correction to v (with β₁ = 0, m̂ₜ = gₜ); at β₂ = 1/(1 + C²) it changes only the first few steps. At C = 3, α = 0.5 the simulated Adam average regret after 6000 steps is 0.672, against the proven floor (2·3 − 4)/3 = 0.667; AMSGrad's is 0.017.

  • What the counterexample does and does not show. It shows the theorem as stated is false, and that no constant pair (β₁, β₂) is safe on every convex problem. It does not show Adam fails on typical deep-learning objectives — the β₂ that breaks this sequence is about 0.1, far from anything used in practice, and the general construction for other betas uses a period and a gradient ratio tuned to those betas.
  • The fix did not displace the original. In widespread later practice, Adam and AdamW with constant betas remain the defaults and AMSGrad is an option rather than the standard. (General knowledge about later practice; not a claim of either paper.)

08 Related work: what Adam is, relative to its neighbours (section 5)

The two parents get precise statements. RMSProp with momentum is "Adam without bias correction", but not exactly; AdaGrad is a limiting special case of Adam, exactly — and only because of the correction.

RMSProp with momentum: two differences

schematically — the order of operations differsRMSProp + momentum: momentum applied to the RESCALED gradient gₜ / √vₜ Adam: running averages of g and g² kept separately, then divided: m̂ₜ / √v̂ₜ
  • Where momentum sits. In RMSProp with momentum, each gradient is rescaled by the current √vₜ before it enters the velocity; in Adam, the first and second moments are estimated directly and only their ratio is used.
  • No bias correction. RMSProp has none. The study says this matters most when β₂ is close to 1 — "required in case of sparse gradients" — because the uncorrected early steps are then very large and "often" cause divergence, which it tests in §6.4 (here §09).

AdaGrad as the β₁ = 0, β₂ → 1, annealed-α corner of Adam

AdaGrad's basic updateθₜ₊₁ = θₜ − α · gₜ / √( ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ gᵢ² ) take Adam with β₁ = 0, β₂ infinitesimally close to 1, and αₜ = α · t^(−1/2)β₁ = 0 ⇒ m̂ₜ = gₜ β₂ → 1 from below ⇒ lim v̂ₜ = t⁻¹ · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ gᵢ² θₜ − α·t^(−1/2) · m̂ₜ / √(lim v̂ₜ) = θₜ − α·t^(−1/2) · gₜ / √( t⁻¹ · ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ gᵢ² ) = θₜ − α · gₜ / √( ∑ᵢ₌₁ᵗ gᵢ² ) AdaGrad, exactly

Why the limit is the mean — our step: v̂ₜ = (1 − β₂)·∑ β₂ᵗ⁻ⁱgᵢ² / (1 − β₂ᵗ); as β₂ → 1, every β₂ᵗ⁻ⁱ → 1 and (1 − β₂)/(1 − β₂ᵗ) → 1/t. The study's punchline is the converse: without the correction the correspondence breaks. The uncorrected vₜ tends to 0 as β₂ → 1, so an RMSProp-style method in that limit has infinitely large bias and infinitely large updates. The hub's §21 has AdaGrad's 1/√t decay and RMSProp's fix in numbers.

Natural gradient, and the other neighbours

  • A conservative diagonal natural gradient. vₜ approximates the diagonal of the Fisher information matrix, so Adam, like natural gradient descent, uses a preconditioner that adapts to the geometry of the data. But it preconditions with the square root of the inverse diagonal Fisher, not the inverse itself — more conservative than plain natural gradient, as AdaGrad is.
  • Curvature-from-first-order methods — vSGD, AdaDelta and a natural Newton method — set step sizes by estimating curvature from first-order information.
  • The sum-of-functions optimiser (SFO) is a minibatch quasi-Newton method whose memory is linear in the number of minibatch partitions of the dataset — often infeasible on a GPU. It is the second-order baseline in §09.

The CNN finding is a small surprise worth keeping

In the CNN, the study notes that v̂ₜ vanishes to zero after a few epochs and is dominated by ε. The second-moment estimate is then a poor approximation to the cost function's geometry, so what drives the speed-up is the first moment — reducing minibatch variance — not the per-coordinate scaling. That is also why AdaGrad, which has only the second-moment idea, is slow here. Our reading: once √v̂ ≪ ε, the update is α·m̂ₜ/ε, which is momentum SGD with an effective learning rate α/ε; §04's scale invariance has quietly switched off. The figure in §03 reproduces this with the gradient scale at ×10⁻⁹.

The VAE ablation: what bias correction buys

  • Prediction (from §05): values of β₂ close to 1, which robustness to sparse gradients needs, produce larger initialisation bias, so the correction should matter exactly there.
  • Result: with β₂ close to 1, training without the correction was unstable, especially in the first few epochs. The best results came from small 1 − β₂ with the correction, and this was more apparent towards the end of optimisation, when gradients tend to become sparser as hidden units specialise.
  • Summary as stated: Adam performed equal to or better than RMSProp, regardless of hyperparameter setting.

The model itself — the evidence lower bound, the reparameterisation trick, and why its gradient is noisy — is on the VAE page.

10 AdaMax, and temporal averaging (section 7)

Adam divides each gradient by a scaled L2 norm of that coordinate's current and past gradients. Generalise to an Lp norm and the variants become numerically unstable for large p — except in the limit p → ∞, where "a surprisingly simple and stable algorithm emerges".

Temporal averaging (section 7.2)

average the parameters, not just the gradientsPolyak–Ruppert: θ̄ₜ = (1/t) · ∑ₖ₌₁ᵗ θₖ exponential: θ̄ₜ ← β₂ · θ̄ₜ₋₁ + (1 − β₂) · θₜ , θ̄₀ = 0 corrected: θ̂ₜ = θ̄ₜ / (1 − β₂ᵗ)

The last iterate is noisy because of the stochastic approximation, so averaging iterates often generalises better; Polyak–Ruppert averaging was already known to improve SGD's convergence. The exponential version weights recent parameters more and costs one line in either algorithm — and since it starts at zero it needs the same correction as everything else (§18 of the hub). The study writes the decay as β₂; nothing ties it to the second-moment rate, and it can be its own constant.

11 Common misconceptions

"The original study proved Adam converges on convex problems." It claimed to; the proof was later shown to be wrong. The non-negativity of Γₜ it relies on does not hold for Adam, and an explicit one-dimensional convex sequence makes Adam's average regret stay above (2C − 4)/3 (§07). The theorem also assumed αₜ = α/√t and a decaying β₁,ₜ, neither of which is how Adam is normally run.
"Adam's step can never exceed α." Only approximately, and the study says so. At the default betas the stated first-case bound is 3.16α (a lone spike after silence), and an adversarially growing gradient goes beyond even that — up to 7.27α by our Cauchy–Schwarz bound (§03). AdaMax is the variant with a tight α bound (§10).
"Bias correction is a cosmetic warm-up tweak." It is what keeps large β₂ usable. Without it, the early steps scale like (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) — 10× α at β₂ = 0.9999 — and the VAE ablation shows instability exactly in that regime (§05, §09). It is also what makes AdaGrad a special case of Adam (§08).
"Adam is invariant to the scale of the gradient, full stop." Only while √v̂ ≫ ε. When gradients are tiny, ε dominates the denominator and Adam becomes momentum SGD with learning rate α/ε; the study observed v̂ falling to the ε level in its CNN (§04, §09).
"Automatic annealing means Adam needs no learning-rate schedule." It shrinks steps only as far as the SNR falls. If minibatch noise is low or the mean gradient stays large, the step stays near α. The study's own logistic-regression runs used α/√t decay; modern recipes add warmup and decay (schedules).
"RMSProp with momentum is just Adam without bias correction." Close, not identical. Removing the correction gives "a version of" RMSProp with momentum; the real RMSProp-with-momentum applies momentum to the already-rescaled gradient, while Adam rescales the averaged gradient (§08).

12 Takeaways

  • Designed as AdaGrad's sparse-gradient strength plus RMSProp's non-stationary strength, for noisy, high-dimensional, first-order optimisation.
  • The effective step Δₜ = α·m̂ₜ/√v̂ₜ is approximately bounded by α: exactly α·(1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) (3.16α at the defaults) for a lone sparse spike, below α in common cases — a trust region whose size you can choose in advance.
  • m̂/√v̂ is a signal-to-noise ratio: steps shrink as the mean gradient vanishes near an optimum, and the ratio is invariant to per-coordinate rescaling of the gradient — while √v̂ ≫ ε.
  • E[vₜ] = E[gₜ²]·(1 − β₂ᵗ) + ζ: the correction divides out the zero-initialisation factor; it matters most for the large β₂ that sparse gradients need.
  • The stated O(√T) regret bound assumed αₜ = α/√t, β₁,ₜ = β₁λᵗ⁻¹ and β₁²/√β₂ < 1; later work showed the proof wrong and gave a convex problem where Adam fails; AMSGrad's running max of v̂ restores the guarantee.
  • AdaGrad = Adam with β₁ = 0, β₂ → 1, αₜ = α/√t — only with bias correction.
  • Experiments: logistic regression (MNIST, sparse IMDB), MLPs with and without dropout (beating SFO, which fails under dropout), a CIFAR-10 CNN, and a VAE ablation where removing the correction destabilises training at large β₂.
  • AdaMax: uₜ = max(β₂uₜ₋₁, |gₜ|), no correction on u, |Δₜ| ≤ α, default α = 0.002. And an exponential average of the parameters themselves is one more line.
