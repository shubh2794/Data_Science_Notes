36 Gradient clipping: capping the magnitude, keeping the direction

§17's cliffs produce gradients that are enormous and, crucially, correctly oriented. The direction is fine; it is the length that is meaningless, because the linear model the gradient represents is valid over a neighbourhood far smaller than the step it is asking for. Clipping caps the length and is the standard fix — and the choice between the two ways of doing it is not cosmetic.

clip by NORM — rescale the whole vector, preserving direction exactlyif ‖g‖₂ > c: g ← g · c/‖g‖₂ The direction is unchanged, bit for bit. The step is capped at c. clip by VALUE — clamp each coordinate independentlygᵢ ← clip(gᵢ, −c, +c) for every i Cheaper, needs no reduction across parameters, and CHANGES THE DIRECTION. the difference, on one gradientg = [0.9, 100.0], ‖g‖₂ = 100.00405 clip by value at 1.0: [0.9, 1.0] the two coordinates were 111× apart and are now nearly equal; the direction has rotated by 41.47°. clip by norm at 1.0: [0.00899964, 0.99995950] the ratio 0.009 : 1 is exactly the original 0.9 : 100; the direction has rotated by 0.00°.

Forty-one degrees is not a rounding error. Clipping by value on that gradient produces a step pointing roughly along the diagonal when the gradient was pointing almost entirely along the second axis. On a cliff — where the whole reason you are clipping is that one direction has become enormous — clipping by value redistributes the step into directions the gradient was not asking for at all. Clip by norm. Clipping by value exists because it is cheaper and needs no cross-parameter reduction, and there are settings where that matters; correctness is not one of them.

where it is essential
Recurrent networks
§09: the same weight matrix applied at every timestep makes the product a matrix power growing like ρᵀ, so the gradient really can grow by orders of magnitude within one sequence. Clipping is not a nicety here, it is the reason the model trains at all — and it was proposed for precisely this setting.
where it is standard
Large transformer training
A global norm clip at 1.0 is close to universal in large-model recipes. The gradients are almost never large enough to trigger it, and when they are — a bad batch, a numerical event, a rare token — the clip converts a destroyed run into an ordinary step.
how to choose c
Log the norm before you clip
Record ‖g‖ every step for a few hundred steps and look at the distribution. Set c somewhere around the high percentiles — so that it fires on the tail and not on the body. A clip that fires on most steps is not clipping, it is a learning-rate schedule with a strange shape, and it is silently normalising away all the magnitude information in the gradient.
what it does not fix
Vanishing gradients
Clipping bounds the gradient from above only. A gradient of 10⁻²⁰ is unaffected by any clip. Exploding and vanishing gradients are two faces of §09's product and they have completely different fixes: clipping for one, initialisation, normalisation and skip connections for the other.
Clipping by value against clipping by norm: what each one does to the direction the angle readout is the point — one of these two numbers is always exactly zero and the other is not

37 Hyperparameters: what matters, and why random search beats grid search

A hyperparameter is anything the training procedure does not learn. There are dozens of them and they are emphatically not equally important — the difference between the most and least important is several orders of magnitude of effect on the final result. Knowing the ordering is worth more than any search algorithm.

RankHyperparameterEffect and how to set itSearch over
1learning rateBy far the largest single effect. Too high and the run diverges or destroys units (§11, §16); too low and it never gets anywhere. Find it with the range test (§25), then decay it (§24).log-uniform, 10⁻⁵ … 10⁰
2the schedule and its warmupWorth more than the choice of optimiser. A good peak with a bad schedule loses more than a mediocre optimiser with a good one.shape (a few options) × warmup length
3batch sizeUsually fixed by memory (§26). When it is a free choice, remember it is coupled to the learning rate and change both together.powers of two
4model size — width and depthMore capacity plus more regularisation beats less capacity, up to what you can afford. Part 1 §23 owns the shape question.a small ladder
5weight decay λThe main explicit regulariser. Its useful range is narrow and problem-dependent. Use the decoupled form (§23) and exclude biases and norm gains (§32).log-uniform, 10⁻⁵ … 10⁻¹
6dropout rateMatters where there are big dense layers and hardly at all where there are not. Often zero is correct in a normalised convolutional net (§33).uniform, 0 … 0.5
7the optimiserAdamW unless you have a reason. The gap between reasonable choices is small compared with everything above it.two or three options
8β₁, β₂, ε, initialisation scaleLeave them at the defaults. The one worth touching is β₂, upward, if the batch is small and the run is noisy (§22).don't

Why random search beats grid search, with the argument

The usual justification is a hand-wave about coverage. The real argument is short and worth having exactly, because it explains why the advantage grows with the number of hyperparameters.

the counting argumentSearch d hyperparameters on a grid with k values each: kᵈ runs. Suppose ONE of the d actually matters and the rest barely do. GRID: every one of the kᵈ runs uses one of only k distinct values of the important one. You paid kᵈ and bought k samples of it. RANDOM: kᵈ runs give kᵈ DISTINCT values of every dimension, including the important one. d = 2, k = 5: 25 runs → 5 values (grid) vs 25 values (random) d = 3, k = 4: 64 runs → 4 values vs 64 values d = 4, k = 3: 81 runs → 3 values vs 81 values The waste GROWS with d, and the "one dimension dominates" premise is not a convenient fiction — it is the ordering in the table above. how many random draws you needP(at least one of n uniform draws lands in the best 5% of the space) = 1 − 0.95ⁿ n = 10: 0.4013 n = 20: 0.6415 n = 60: 0.9539 n = 100: 0.9941 Sixty random configurations give a 95% chance of hitting the top 5%, in ANY number of dimensions — the count does not depend on d at all. That is the sentence to remember.
sample correctly
Log-uniform for anything multiplicative
Learning rate, weight decay and ε matter by ratio, not by difference: the gap between 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁴ is the same size as the gap between 10⁻² and 10⁻¹. Sample log₁₀η ~ U(−5, 0), not η ~ U(10⁻⁵, 1) — the second draws 90% of its samples above 0.1 and effectively never tests anything small.
for β near 1
Sample 1 − β log-uniformly
What matters about β is the window 1/(1−β) (§18), so 0.9 and 0.99 are one "step" apart while 0.9 and 0.91 are almost identical. Sample the complement, on a log scale.
coarse to fine
Wide and short, then narrow and long
First pass: a wide range, few configurations, short runs — you are locating the region, not the optimum. Second pass: resample around the best region, longer runs. Third if needed. This finds a good setting for a fraction of the cost of one exhaustive pass, and it is what successive-halving and its relatives automate.
the boundary check
If the best value is at the edge, the range was wrong
A search whose winner sits at the smallest or largest value tried has told you the optimum is outside the range. Extend and re-run rather than reporting the boundary as the answer — this is the single most common way a search reports a wrong number with a straight face.
Grid against random on the same budget, when one dimension dominates watch the projections on the axes: the grid's samples pile up on a handful of values of the important dimension no matter how many runs you buy

38 Reading a training curve

The pair of curves — training loss and validation loss against step — is the primary instrument, and most of the diagnoses on this page can be made from their shape alone. Here is the table, and it is worth reading before the next run rather than during it.

What you seeWhat it meansWhat to do
Both fall, validation flattens then risesTextbook overfitting. The model has capacity to spare and is spending it memorising.Stop at the minimum (§34). Then add regularisation (§31–§35) or more data, and train longer.
Both fall and both are still falling at the endUnder-trained. Nothing is wrong.Train longer. Check the schedule is not decaying to nothing early (§24).
Both plateau high, close togetherUnderfitting: too little capacity, too low a rate, or a broken pipeline. This is the one people misdiagnose as overfitting.First: overfit ten examples (§39). If that fails, the bug is upstream. If it succeeds, raise capacity or the learning rate, and remove regularisation.
Training loss flat at exactly log KThe model is predicting the class prior and nothing else. Almost always a dead forward pass (§14), a detached graph, or a zeroed learning rate.Print the gradient norm at the first layer. §14's table: it is the only column that separates "converged" from "nothing is reaching me".
Loss oscillates violently but trends downThe rate is slightly past ideal for the stiffest direction and fine for everything else (§16).It is often fine. If it bothers you, decay sooner, or raise the batch size. Halving η will make it slower without making it better.
Loss rises monotonically from step 1η > 2/λ_max. Divergence, exactly (§16).Lower η. Add warmup. Check the loss sign.
Loss falls, then spikes to a huge value, then recoversA cliff (§17). One batch produced an enormous gradient and the step launched the parameters somewhere arbitrary.Gradient clipping by norm (§36). Also check that batch for corrupt data.
Loss becomes NaN and stays thereAn infinity or a zero-division propagated. Usually a naive log or exp (§03), a division by a zero denominator, or fp16 overflow (§27).Switch to the fused stable loss. Turn on NaN detection and find the first bad step. Under mixed precision, lower the loss scale.
Validation loss rises while validation ACCURACY still improvesNot a bug (§02). The model is becoming more confident, and cross-entropy punishes confident errors much more than accuracy does.Nothing, unless you need calibrated probabilities. Then: label smoothing (§35), or temperature scaling afterwards.
Validation loss is BELOW training lossUsually not a miracle. Either the training loss includes regularisation and dropout noise and the validation loss does not, or the validation split is easier, or — the bad case — the splits overlap.Compare like with like: evaluate the training set in eval mode. Then check for leakage between splits.
A sharp step down exactly at a schedule milestoneNormal and healthy for a step schedule: the smaller rate lets the iterate settle into a basin it was rattling around in (§15).Nothing. If it is very large, the previous rate was too high for too long.
A saw-tooth with period exactly one epochThe data is not being shuffled, so the same ordered batches recur and the same correlated gradients recur with them.Shuffle every epoch (§15).
What to log, and it is not just the loss. Six series, all cheap, all diagnostic. (1) Training and validation loss, on the same axes. (2) The gradient norm, globally and for the first layer — it separates §14's two flat curves. (3) The learning rate, actually read from the optimiser rather than from what you believe the schedule is doing. (4) The fraction of dead units (§11), which is a one-way ratchet in the first layer and therefore an early warning. (5) Activation statistics — mean and standard deviation per layer — every few hundred steps, which catches the drift of §10 long before the loss does. (6) The ratio of update size to parameter size, ‖Δθ‖/‖θ‖: a healthy value is around 10⁻³, and orders of magnitude away from that in either direction is a learning rate that is wrong.
Nine failure modes, generated rather than drawn — and the diagnosis each of these is a real run of a real network with one thing deliberately broken; the diagnostic panel shows the auxiliary series that identifies it

39 Debugging a run that will not learn

The single most valuable habit in this domain: before you touch a hyperparameter, prove the model can memorise ten examples. That one test separates "the optimisation is badly configured" from "there is a bug", and those two situations have entirely disjoint fixes. People spend days tuning learning rates on top of a broken data loader.

the ladder — do these IN ORDER and stop at the first failure0 CHECK THE INITIAL LOSS. Before any training, one forward pass. Balanced K-class cross-entropy should start at ≈ log K K = 2 → 0.6931 K = 10 → 2.3026 K = 100 → 4.6052 K = 1000 → 6.9078 Far ABOVE: the output scale is wrong, or the labels are misaligned. Far BELOW: the labels have leaked into the inputs. This is a data bug and it will look like a wonderful result until it is deployed. 1 OVERFIT ONE BATCH. Take 10 examples. Turn OFF augmentation, dropout, and weight decay. Train on those 10 alone. You must reach a loss of essentially zero within a few hundred steps. If you CANNOT: it is a BUG, not a hyperparameter. Go to §39's bug list. If you CAN: the model, loss and backward pass are wired correctly. The problem is optimisation, data, or generalisation. 2 GRADIENT-CHECK any hand-written backward pass. Central differences on a tiny random input, h ≈ 10⁻⁵ in float64 (§07). Compare with rel = |exact − numeric| / max(|exact|, |numeric|, 10⁻⁸) PASS is rel < 10⁻⁷ for a smooth function; anything above 10⁻³ is a bug. Check at a point where NO kink is active — a ReLU exactly at zero will fail the check for a correct implementation, and that is not the bug. 3 SCALE UP ONE AXIS AT A TIME. 10 examples → 1000 → all. Then turn the regularisation back on, one piece at a time. A failure that appears when you add a piece belongs to that piece. 4 ONLY NOW tune the learning rate (§25), then the schedule, then the rest in §37's order.

The bug list, roughly in order of how often it is the answer

SymptomLikely causeConfirm it by
Cannot overfit ten examplesThe labels do not correspond to the inputs — a shuffle applied to one and not the other, an off-by-one in the index, a transform that reorders.Print five (input, label) pairs and look at them with your eyes. Every time.
Loss barely moves; gradients are exactly zeroThe graph is detached: a .detach(), a no_grad block, a conversion to a plain array, or the parameter was never registered with the optimiser.Print ‖∂L/∂θ‖ for the first and last layers. Exactly zero is a wiring bug, not a small number.
Loss decreases far too slowlyThe optimiser was constructed over the wrong parameter list, so some layers are frozen without being marked as frozen.Count the parameters the optimiser holds against the parameters the model has.
Loss is finite but nonsense; accuracy near chanceSoftmax applied twice, because the loss expects logits and was given probabilities (part 1 §28).Read the loss function's signature. If the name says with_logits, believe it.
Loss is suspiciously low from step 1Leakage: the target is derivable from a feature. A row index, a timestamp, an identifier, a leftover column.Train on the features alone with the label column removed and see if it still works.
Shapes are fine but the loss is meaninglessA silent broadcast: predictions (N, 1) against targets (N,) broadcast to (N, N).Assert the shape of the per-example loss before reducing it.
Works in training, fails in deploymentThe model was never switched to evaluation mode, so BatchNorm is using batch statistics and dropout is still dropping (§29, §33).Run the identical input through in both modes and diff the outputs.
Metrics differ between two runs of the same codeUnseeded randomness — initialisation, shuffling, augmentation, dropout, and non-deterministic kernels are five independent sources.Seed all of them, and accept that some kernels are non-deterministic by design.
The first epoch is fine and then it degradesThe learning rate is too high and units are dying (§11), or a schedule is doing something you did not intend.Log the dead fraction and the actual learning rate. Both are three lines.
NaN after a while, not immediatelyA rare input triggers an overflow: an empty sequence, a zero-norm vector, a log of zero, an fp16 spike (§27).Catch the first non-finite loss, save that batch, and inspect it.
Validation is much better than trainingNot a miracle — the training loss includes dropout noise and the penalty term; or the splits overlap.Evaluate the training set in eval mode and compare with the validation number.
The gradient check, done properly. Use float64 — in float32 the check has about four good digits (§07) and you will not be able to tell a bug from rounding. Use central differences, not forward. Check the relative error, not the absolute one, since the gradients themselves span orders of magnitude. Check a random subset of parameters rather than all of them, since each one costs two forward passes. Avoid points where a piecewise-linear activation is exactly at its kink, and avoid checking with dropout or any other randomness active — the two evaluations must be of the same function. And sweep h: a correct implementation traces §07's valley, while a buggy one is flat and wrong at every h. The shape of that curve tells you which you have.
