09 The forward pass in tensors: shapes, batching, broadcasting

Everything above was one example at a time. Real code never does that. The forward pass is a short sequence of matrix products over a batch × features tensor, and if you can write down the shape chain you can find the bug.

The batch dimension N is a spectator: it passes through every product untouched. Nothing in the layer definition depends on N, which is why a model trained with batches of 256 runs unchanged on a batch of 1.

Broadcasting, stated precisely

The bias is a vector of length dₗ and the pre-activation is a matrix of shape (N × dₗ). Adding them is undefined in linear algebra; the convention used by every framework is to align the shapes from the right and stretch any axis of length 1 or missing to match. So b is treated as (1 × dₗ) and tiled down all N rows. Each unit's bias is added to that unit's output for every example — which is what you want, and the reason the operation is defined this way rather than some other way.

The broadcasting bug that does not raise an error. If your targets have shape (N,) and your predictions have shape (N, 1), then pred − target broadcasts to (N, N): every prediction is subtracted from every target. The loss becomes a mean over N² numbers, most of them meaningless. Nothing crashes. The loss is finite, decreases slowly, and the model learns almost nothing. Always reshape or squeeze to a shape you have stated out loud, and print the shape of the loss's input at least once per project.

Beyond a matrix: what the batch axis actually is

The batch axis generalises. An image batch is (N, C, H, W); a token batch is (N, T, d). In both cases a dense layer applies to the last axis only, and every axis before it is a spectator that gets folded into the batch. A dense layer applied to a (N, T, d) tensor is exactly a matrix product on a (N·T, d) matrix, reshaped back. That is precisely how the position-wise feedforward network inside a transformer works (§26): the same small MLP, applied independently to each of the N·T positions, with the positions treated as a batch. Recognising this saves a great deal of confusion later — that block is not a new kind of layer.

the same dense layer, three input rankstabular X (N, d) → XW one product sequence X (N, T, d) → reshape to (N·T, d), one product, reshape back image X (N, H, W, C) → reshape to (N·H·W, C), one product, reshape back — this is exactly a 1 × 1 convolution In all three the parameter count is the same: d·dₒᵤₜ + dₒᵤₜ. The COMPUTE differs by the number of positions, because the same matrix is applied at each one.

10 Counting parameters and arithmetic, exactly

Two numbers characterise any dense architecture, and both are one line of arithmetic. Neither is difficult; both are routinely got wrong by a factor of two, and the factor of two is always the same one.

Parameters

one layerweights dᵢₙ · dₒᵤₜ biases dₒᵤₜ total dᵢₙ·dₒᵤₜ + dₒᵤₜ = (dᵢₙ + 1)·dₒᵤₜ a chain with widths d₀, d₁, …, d_LP = ∑_{l=1}^{L} ( dₗ₋₁·dₗ + dₗ ) = ∑_{l=1}^{L} (dₗ₋₁ + 1)·dₗ The dominant term is the sum of ADJACENT-WIDTH PRODUCTS. Biases are a rounding error: for a layer of width 512 fed by width 512 they are 0.2% of the layer.

Arithmetic: MACs, and the factor of two

A multiply-accumulate is one multiply plus one add. Computing one output of a layer with dᵢₙ inputs takes dᵢₙ MACs. A whole layer takes dᵢₙ·dₒᵤₜ MACs per example — numerically equal to the weight count, because each weight is used exactly once per example. That coincidence is worth memorising: for a dense layer, MACs per example = weights.

the factor of two, stated once so it stops causing arguments1 MAC = 1 multiply + 1 add = 2 floating-point operations FLOPs (forward, one example) = 2 · ∑ dₗ₋₁·dₗ ≈ 2P and the other factor, for trainingbackward ≈ 2× the forward cost — one pass to propagate the error to the layer below, one to accumulate the weight gradients — so training step ≈ 3 × forward ≈ 6P FLOPs per example This 2:1 backward-to-forward ratio is derived on the training page; it is quoted here only because no parameter budget is meaningful without it.
Where the "6ND" rule of thumb comes from. Training a dense model of P parameters on D tokens or examples costs roughly 6PD floating-point operations: 2 for the forward multiply-add, 4 for the backward. Every compute-budget estimate in the field is that arithmetic. It ignores attention's quadratic term, normalisation, activation functions and data movement — all of which are real, and none of which change the order of magnitude for a dense network.

Two counting errors that are always the same two. First, quoting FLOPs when you computed MACs — a factor of 2. Second, quoting the forward cost when you meant the training cost — a factor of about 3. Together they are a factor of 6, which is exactly the gap between "my model is 0.6 GFLOPs" and "my model is 3.8 GFLOPs" for the same model. Say which one you mean, every time.

What the parameter count does and does not tell you

  • It tells you the memory. Weights, gradients and optimiser state all scale with P. For a method that keeps two moment estimates in float32, training memory for parameters alone is about 16P bytes.
  • It roughly tells you the compute for a dense network — but only roughly, and not at all once attention or convolution is involved, where the same weight is applied at many positions and compute scales with positions × parameters.
  • It does not tell you the capacity in any useful sense. Two networks with identical parameter counts can have wildly different expressive power depending on how those parameters are arranged — §21 is the counting argument, and §24 is the structural one.
  • It does not tell you the speed. Wall-clock time is governed by memory bandwidth and kernel efficiency at least as much as by arithmetic. A model with fewer FLOPs and worse memory access patterns is routinely slower.

11 Hidden units I — the rectified family

The choice of g is a choice about the shape of the function class: how many linear pieces it has, whether it is bounded, whether it is monotone, whether it is zero-centred. Those are architectural facts. Whether a given choice trains well is a separate question and belongs to the training page; this section and the next two are about the function.

The rectifier and why it won

the default recommendation for hidden units, and it is not closeg(z) = max{0, z} g′(z) = 1 if z > 0, else 0 Two linear pieces, one kink at the origin. Not differentiable at z = 0: the left derivative is 0 and the right derivative is 1. In practice every framework returns one of the one-sided values and this causes no trouble, because a gradient method is never at exactly z = 0 — and if a float rounded to 0 it was probably some tiny ε anyway.

The properties that made it the default are worth separating, because they are usually run together.

  • It is nearly linear. On each side it is linear, with slope exactly 1 or exactly 0. It therefore inherits most of what makes linear models easy to optimise while still breaking the collapse of §05.
  • Its derivative is constant, not merely large. Where the unit is active, g′ = 1 exactly. Not 0.9, not decaying with |z|. Products of many such factors do not shrink, which matters enormously once L is large.
  • Its second derivative is zero almost everywhere. There are no curvature effects for an optimiser to fight; the gradient direction is more informative than it is for a curved activation.
  • It is sparse. Roughly half the units are exactly zero for a typical input, so the representation is genuinely sparse rather than merely small — and exact zeros propagate as exact zeros.
  • It is a fold. Every rectified unit contributes one hyperplane to the arrangement that §21 counts. Two pieces per unit is the minimum, and it is enough.
Complicated components are not needed for a complicated system. A Turing machine's tape cell only has to store 0 or 1. A universal function approximator only needs a function with two linear pieces. In both cases the power comes from composition, not from the richness of the component — and the historical belief that an activation had to be smooth and sigmoidal delayed the field by roughly a decade.

The family, and what each variant changes

All the rectified variants have the form g(z) = max(0, z) + α·min(0, z); they differ only in α.

What this section deliberately does not claim. Nothing here says ReLU trains better, or that a dying unit is a disaster, or that leaky ReLU fixes anything. Those are claims about optimisation dynamics and they live at Neural Network Training §10. What this section claims is narrower and firmer: ReLU is the smallest departure from linearity that breaks the collapse, it contributes exactly one fold per unit, and its derivative is exactly 1 or exactly 0 — three facts about a function, independent of any optimiser.

12 Hidden units II — smooth, saturating, and exotic

Everything that is not a rectifier. Two families matter in practice — the smooth gated units that large models actually use, and the saturating units that they replaced — plus a short list of the ones worth knowing exist.

The saturating pair, and the exact relationship between them

the logistic sigmoid and the hyperbolic tangent are the same curveσ(z) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᶻ) range (0, 1), σ(0) = ½, max σ′ = ¼ at z = 0 tanh(z) = (eᶻ − e⁻ᶻ)/(eᶻ + e⁻ᶻ) range (−1, 1), tanh(0) = 0, max tanh′ = 1 at z = 0 the identity connecting them — worth knowing, it comes up constantlytanh(z) = 2σ(2z) − 1 So tanh is the sigmoid rescaled to be ZERO-CENTRED with UNIT SLOPE at the origin. Near 0, tanh(z) ≈ z, so a shallow tanh network with small activations behaves almost like a linear model — which is exactly why tanh is the better of the two as a hidden unit whenever a saturating unit is required at all.

Both saturate on both sides: the derivative decays to zero as |z| grows, and the function is only meaningfully sensitive to its input in a band around the origin. As an architectural fact this means a saturating network is bounded and smooth — which is occasionally exactly what is wanted, in a gate that must produce a number in (0, 1), in a recurrent cell that must not blow up over many steps, in a model whose output must be a probability. As hidden units in a deep feedforward network, they are discouraged, and the training page gives the reason.

The smooth gated units: GELU and SiLU

The modern default in large transformer-class models is not a rectifier but a smooth, non-monotone function that multiplies the input by a soft gate depending on the input itself.

The reflex. "Loss is not going down — I will try a different activation." It is almost never the activation. In practice the data pipeline, the learning rate, and a mismatched output-unit-and-loss pairing (§17) explain the overwhelming majority of failures. The activation is one of the last things to change and one of the smallest effects when you do — with the single historical exception of moving off two-sided saturating units in a deep network, which was worth a great deal.

13 Hidden units III — what actually makes one good

There is no theory that predicts which hidden unit will work. There are four properties that separate the ones that survived from the ones that did not, and all four are statements about the function, checkable without training anything.

PropertyWhy it matters to the function classWho has it
Breaks the collapse without compoundingThe composition must not be affine (§05) and must not blow up with depth (§05's near-miss). A piecewise-linear unit gives a composition that is still piecewise linear — a function of the same kind, with more pieces. A polynomial gives one of degree 2ᴸ.Every rectifier; hard tanh. GELU and SiLU are smooth but stay within a constant factor of the identity for large x, so they compound just as gently.
Nearly linearLinear models are easy to fit and generalise predictably. The closer a unit is to linear while still non-linear, the more of that inheritance survives. This is why "more non-linear" is not a virtue.ReLU is the extreme case: literally linear on each side. The smooth gated units are next. Sigmoids are furthest away.
Derivative near 1 on the active setThe composition's Jacobian is a product of per-layer factors. If each factor is systematically below 1 the product shrinks geometrically with depth; if above, it grows. A unit whose derivative is exactly 1 where it is active contributes a factor of exactly 1.ReLU (exactly 1 or 0), hard tanh (1 or 0), leaky ReLU (1 or α). Sigmoid's derivative never exceeds 0.25, so a stack of them shrinks by at least 4× per layer even at its best point.
CheapThe activation is evaluated once per unit per example per step. At trillions of unit-evaluations a transcendental function is not free — although on modern hardware it is usually memory-bound anyway, which is why GELU is affordable at all.max(0, z) is a single comparison. erf is not, which is exactly why the tanh surrogate of §12 exists.
Where the pathologies live, and why they are not here. "A ReLU unit whose input is always negative receives zero gradient and can never recover." "A sigmoid deep in a stack saturates and the gradient vanishes." Both are true, both matter enormously, and both are statements about gradient flow, not about the function class — they depend on the initialisation, the learning rate, and the data distribution, none of which this page has. They are Neural Network Training §10. What §11–§13 establish is the prior: which functions are worth trying, and why the shortlist is as short as it is.
A tempting inference. "Softplus is everywhere differentiable and saturates less completely than a sigmoid, so it should beat ReLU." Measured, it does not, and it is generally discouraged. This is the clearest available warning that intuition about activation functions is unreliable and that the design space is genuinely empirical. Published activations are, as a rule, the ones that were demonstrated to help; the far larger set that performs identically to ReLU is never written up.
