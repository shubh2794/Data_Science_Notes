14 Output units, derived from maximum likelihood

The output layer and the loss are one decision, not two. Choose a probability distribution for the target, let the network output its parameters, and take the negative log-likelihood — the loss then follows with no further choices, and every standard loss in deep learning drops out of that one recipe.

The recipe, once

the general construction — the only thing to remember1. Choose a family of distributions p(y | ω) appropriate to the TARGET. 2. Let the network compute the PARAMETERS: ω = f(x; θ). 3. Take the loss to be the negative log-likelihood: L = −log p(y ; ω(x)). 4. Choose the output non-linearity so that step 3 is well-behaved — that is, so the log UNDOES whatever squashing step 2 needed. the reframing that makes step 2 make senseThe network does NOT predict y. It predicts the PARAMETERS OF A DISTRIBUTION over y. "The prediction" is then a summary of that distribution — its mean, its mode, a sample. Everything about calibration, uncertainty and multimodality follows from taking that sentence seriously.

Linear output → Gaussian
ŷ = hW + b, no non-linearity. Read as p(y|x) = N(y; ŷ, I). Maximising the log-likelihood is exactly minimising mean squared error. §15.

Why the choice of output unit is really a choice about gradients

Any function that maps ℝ into [0, 1] defines a valid Bernoulli parameter. Clipping would do it: P(y = 1|x) = clip(w·h + b, 0, 1) is a perfectly valid probability. It is also untrainable: whenever w·h + b strays outside [0, 1] the gradient with respect to every parameter is exactly zero, and there is no signal telling the model which way to move. A zero gradient is not a small gradient; it is the absence of a direction.

So the requirement is stronger than validity. The output unit must give a strong gradient precisely when the model is wrong. The exponential-family construction does this automatically, and the mechanism is worth naming because it is the same in all three cases: the output unit involves an exp, the loss involves a log, and the log undoes the exp. Whatever saturation the squashing introduced is removed by the logarithm before any gradient is taken. Pair the same output unit with a loss that has no logarithm — squared error, most obviously — and the saturation survives into the gradient, with the consequences §17 measures.

the result that makes all three cases look the samefor the (linear, MSE), (sigmoid, BCE) and (softmax, cross-entropy) pairings: ∂L/∂z = ŷ − y The gradient at the LOGITS is the prediction minus the target — nothing else, no derivative of the output non-linearity anywhere in it. Wrong by a lot ⇒ a large gradient. Wrong by a little ⇒ a small one. Right ⇒ zero. This is not three coincidences; it is a property of matching an exponential-family output unit with its own negative log-likelihood, and it is the single best reason to derive losses this way rather than choosing them by taste.
The practical consequence, and the bug it explains. Because the clean gradient lives at the logits, every mature framework's cross-entropy takes logits and applies the softmax or sigmoid internally, where it can be fused with the log for stability (§16). Passing already-normalised probabilities into such a function applies the squashing twice. Nothing raises an error. The model trains, more slowly, to a worse optimum. It is among the most common silent bugs in this domain and the reason the function is usually named something like …_with_logits.

15 Linear output, Gaussian target, squared error

The simplest case, and the one that shows the derivation is not a formality: mean squared error is not an arbitrary choice of distance. It is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian with fixed variance, and every property it has follows from that.

the modelŷ = hW + b no output non-linearity — "linear units" p(y | x) = N(y ; ŷ, σ²I) the network predicts the MEAN; σ² is fixed the negative log-likelihood, written out−log p(y|x) = ½‖y − ŷ‖²/σ² + (d/2)·log(2πσ²) └─── depends on θ ───┘ └──── constant in θ ────┘ Drop the constant and the fixed 1/σ², and MINIMISING the negative log-likelihood IS minimising ‖y − ŷ‖². With σ² = 1 they are equal up to an additive constant. the gradient at the output∂L/∂ŷ = ŷ − y for the ½‖·‖² form Linear units do not saturate, so this gradient is available everywhere and grows without bound as the error grows.

Three consequences that are usually attributed to "MSE being bad"

  • It fits the conditional mean. The minimiser of expected squared error is E[y | x]. If the true conditional distribution is bimodal — say the target is equally often −1 or +1 — the squared-error optimum is 0, a value the target never takes. This is not a failure of optimisation. It is what "predict the mean" means, and §19 is the fix.
  • It is sensitive to outliers, because the Gaussian has thin tails and assigns an implausible target a very large negative log-likelihood. Wanting robustness means wanting heavier tails, which means a different distribution: absolute error is the Laplace log-likelihood, and the Huber loss is a smooth interpolation between the two.
  • It assumes constant variance. A single fixed σ² weights every example equally. If the noise level genuinely varies with x, that assumption is wrong and the fix is to predict the variance as well (§19), not to change the distance function.
The naming that causes confusion. Mean squared error and Gaussian negative log-likelihood are the same objective; so are mean absolute error and Laplace negative log-likelihood; so are binary cross-entropy and Bernoulli negative log-likelihood. The "loss function" vocabulary and the "distribution" vocabulary describe one object. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model, whether or not you meant to.

16 Sigmoid output, Bernoulli target, and the softplus identity

The binary case, derived rather than asserted — and then the numerically stable form, which is not a detail. Writing this loss the obvious way produces infinities on inputs the stable form handles perfectly.

Where the sigmoid comes from

The target is binary, so the distribution is Bernoulli, defined by a single number in [0, 1]. Rather than constrain the network's output directly, assume the unnormalised log-probabilities are linear in the network's scalar output z, then exponentiate and normalise.

the construction, four lineslog P̃(y) = y·z an unnormalised log-probability, linear in y and z P̃(y) = exp(y·z) P(y) = exp(y·z) / ∑_{y′∈{0,1}} exp(y′·z) P(y) = σ( (2y − 1)·z ) with σ(t) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᵗ) Setting y = 1 gives P(y = 1) = σ(z), which is the sigmoid output unit. The quantity z is called a LOGIT — a log-odds, since z = log( P(y=1) / P(y=0) ). The sigmoid was not chosen for its shape. It is what normalising a linear log-probability over two outcomes produces.

The loss, and the softplus identity that makes it readable

negative log-likelihood, in three equivalent formsL(θ) = −log P(y | x) = −log σ( (2y − 1)·z ) = ζ( (1 − 2y)·z ) ζ(t) = log(1 + eᵗ), the softplus and the familiar form, which is the same thingL = −[ y·log ŷ + (1 − y)·log(1 − ŷ) ] ŷ = σ(z) The softplus form is the one to think with, because everything about the loss's behaviour is visible in it: it is a SOFTPLUS of a single scalar, (1 − 2y)·z.
reading the softplus form — this is the whole argument for cross-entropylet t = (1 − 2y)·z so t = −z when y = 1, t = +z when y = 0 t very NEGATIVE ⇒ ζ(t) → 0 the model is confidently RIGHT: loss ≈ 0, gradient ≈ 0 — correctly, nothing to fix t very POSITIVE ⇒ ζ(t) → t the model is confidently WRONG: loss grows LINEARLY in |z|, and dζ/dt → 1, so the gradient does not shrink AT ALL The loss saturates ONLY when the model is already right. That one asymmetry is why cross-entropy is the correct partner for a saturating output unit, and §17 measures what happens when it is replaced by squared error.

The numerically stable form, and why the obvious code is wrong

Written literally, −log(σ(z)) computes σ(z) first. For z = −800, σ(z) underflows to exactly 0.0 in double precision, and log(0) is −∞; the loss is +∞, the gradient is NaN, and the whole run is destroyed. For z = +800, computing log(1 + eᶻ) directly overflows eᶻ to +∞ and returns ∞ where the answer is 800.000….

the stable identities — implement these, not the definitionssoftplus(z) = max(z, 0) + log(1 + e^{−|z|}) log σ(z) = −softplus(−z) BCE(z, y) = softplus( (1 − 2y)·z ) = max(z, 0) − z·y + log(1 + e^{−|z|}) The exponential is now ALWAYS of a non-positive number, so it lies in (0, 1] and can never overflow. If it underflows to 0 the log term is 0, which is the right answer to full precision. Every framework's "…_with_logits" function is this.

Why this is an architecture question and not merely an implementation one. The stability of the loss determines what range of logits the model can safely occupy. If −log σ(z) is computed naively, the model cannot be allowed to become very confident — the loss detonates — so the effective function class is silently restricted to small logits. Implemented stably, the model can express a probability of e⁻⁸⁰⁰ and the loss is simply 800. The numerics decide what the model is allowed to say.

17 Why cross-entropy pairs with a saturating output and squared error does not

The single most consequential pairing decision in the whole page, and it is settled by one derivative. Squared error on a sigmoid output produces a gradient that vanishes exactly when the model is most badly wrong.

The two gradients, side by side

cross-entropy on a sigmoid outputL = ζ((1 − 2y)z) ∂L/∂z = σ(z) − y = ŷ − y Bounded in (−1, 1), and its MAGNITUDE IS THE ERROR ITSELF. Confidently wrong (y = 1, z = −10): ∂L/∂z ≈ −0.99995. Nearly the largest gradient available. squared error on the SAME sigmoid outputL = ½(σ(z) − y)² ∂L/∂z = (σ(z) − y) · σ′(z) = (ŷ − y)·ŷ(1 − ŷ) The extra factor σ′(z) = ŷ(1 − ŷ) is at most ¼, and it goes to ZERO at both ends. Confidently wrong (y = 1, z = −10): ŷ = 4.54e−5, so ∂L/∂z = (4.5397869e−5 − 1)·4.5397869e−5·(1 − 4.5397869e−5) = −4.5393749e−5. the comparison, at the point where it matters mosty = 1, z = −10 cross-entropy |∂L/∂z| = 9.99954602e−01 squared error |∂L/∂z| = 4.53937490e−05 RATIO = 22 028.4658 The ratio has a closed form. Dividing the two gradients cancels (ŷ − y), leaving ratio = 1 / ( ŷ(1 − ŷ) ) = 1/σ′(z) = 2 + eᶻ + e⁻ᶻ so it grows like e^{|z|}. At z = −10 that is 22 028.47; at z = −20 it is 485 165 197.41; at z = −5, still only mildly wrong, it is already 150.42. The squared-error model is not learning slowly from a confidently wrong example — it is not learning from it AT ALL, and that is the example carrying the most information in the whole batch.

The mechanism, in one sentence: cross-entropy contains a logarithm which cancels the exponential inside the sigmoid, and squared error does not, so the sigmoid's saturation survives into the gradient. The same argument applies verbatim to softmax with squared error, where a confidently wrong prediction likewise produces almost no gradient.

Loss and gradient against the logit, for every output-unit and loss pairing look at the left end — that is a confidently wrong model, and the two gradient curves are orders of magnitude apart there
Why this survived as the single biggest algorithmic change of the 1990s. Squared error was the default loss for neural networks into the 1990s, and it was gradually replaced by the cross-entropy family as the maximum-likelihood framing spread from statistics. The improvement was large and it was concentrated exactly where this section predicts: models with sigmoid and softmax outputs, which had previously suffered from saturation and slow learning, started to train. The other change of comparable size was replacing sigmoid hidden units with piecewise-linear ones (§27).
The plausible-sounding defence. "Squared error is fine for classification — accuracy is what I care about, not calibration." The problem is not calibration, it is that the model does not learn. The examples that receive no gradient under squared error are the confidently-wrong ones, which are precisely the examples carrying the most information. The model converges — to a worse optimum, more slowly, with a loss curve that looks deceptively smooth because the hardest examples have stopped contributing to it.

18 Softmax: the multinoulli output and everything it implies

The K-class generalisation of §16, and the most reused function in the domain — it is the output of every classifier, the routing weight in a mixture of experts, and the weight vector inside attention. Five properties are worth knowing exactly.

The construction, identical to §16 with K outcomes instead of 2

definitionz = hW + b ∈ ℝᴷ zᵢ = log P̃(y = i | x), an unnormalised log-probability softmax(z)ᵢ = e^{zᵢ} / ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ} the loss — the stable form is the DEFINITION, not an optimisationlog softmax(z)ᵢ = zᵢ − log ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ} L = −log softmax(z)ₖ = log ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ} − zₖ (k the true class) The first term rises with EVERY logit; the second falls with the TRUE one. So the loss pushes zₖ up and all of z down, and the balance point is the correct distribution. Crucially zₖ appears LINEARLY and cannot saturate. the gradient∂L/∂zᵢ = softmax(z)ᵢ − 1[i = k] = ŷ − y again, prediction minus target

Property 1 · shift invariance, and the max-subtraction trick

the invariancesoftmax(z + c·1) = softmax(z) for ANY scalar c proof: e^{zᵢ+c}/∑ⱼe^{zⱼ+c} = eᶜ·e^{zᵢ}/(eᶜ·∑ⱼe^{zⱼ}) = e^{zᵢ}/∑ⱼe^{zⱼ} therefore the numerically stable evaluation, which is exact, not approximatesoftmax(z) = softmax(z − maxᵢ zᵢ) After the shift the largest argument is exactly 0, so every exponential lies in (0, 1] and NOTHING can overflow. Underflow of a tiny term is harmless: it was going to be negligible in the sum anyway. the same trick, for the losslog ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ} = m + log ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ − m} m = maxⱼ zⱼ This is "log-sum-exp". Naively, softmax([1000, 999, 1001]) is NaN/NaN in every language; with the shift it is (0.244728, 0.090031, 0.665241), exactly right.

A second consequence of shift invariance is worth naming: the softmax is over-parameterised. Only K − 1 numbers are needed to specify a distribution over K outcomes, yet the layer outputs K. Fixing z_K = 0 would remove the redundancy, and doing so with K = 2 reproduces the sigmoid exactly: softmax([z, 0])₁ = σ(z). Both parameterisations describe the same set of distributions and differ only in learning dynamics; the over-parameterised one is simpler to implement and is what everyone uses.

Property 2 · it responds to differences, so its scale is meaningless

Because only differences matter, "the logits are large" says nothing. What matters is the gap between the largest logit and the rest. Logits of (1000, 999) give the same output as (1, 0): (0.731, 0.269).

Property 3 · saturation, and why it is not the sigmoid's saturation

the two saturating limitssoftmax(z)ᵢ → 1 when zᵢ = maxⱼ zⱼ AND zᵢ − max_{j≠i} zⱼ → +∞ softmax(z)ᵢ → 0 when maxⱼ zⱼ − zᵢ → +∞ A sigmoid saturates on the value of ONE number. A softmax saturates on the GAPS between numbers. Both flatten the gradient of any loss that does not undo the exponential — which is exactly why squared error on a softmax output can fail to move a model that is confidently and completely wrong (§17).

Property 4 · temperature

definition and the two limitssoftmax(z / T)ᵢ = e^{zᵢ/T} / ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ/T} T → 0⁺ → a one-hot vector at the arg max — hard, deterministic, zero entropy T = 1 → the model's own distribution T → ∞ → the uniform distribution, maximum entropy, log K nats Temperature does NOT change the ranking — softmax is monotone in each zᵢ, so the arg max is the same for every T > 0. It changes only the CONFIDENCE. That is why it is the sampling control in a language model, the softening knob in knowledge distillation, and the sharpness parameter in contrastive objectives.

Property 5 · the name is wrong, and the wrong reading causes real errors

It is a smooth relaxation of arg max, not of max. Arg max returns a one-hot vector and is neither continuous nor differentiable; softmax returns a nearby smooth thing. A better name would have been softargmax. The smooth version of max itself is a different function — log ∑ⱼ e^{zⱼ}, which is why max(z) ≈ log ∑ e^{zⱼ} is the approximation used in reading the loss above. There is also a genuine competition built in: the outputs sum to 1, so one unit rising forces the others down, which is why the softmax is used as a router and a gate as well as a classifier head.

Softmax, live: shift it, heat it, and break it the shift slider changes the logits by hundreds and the output bars do not move at all — until the naive implementation is asked to compute it
Softmax appears three times in a transformer and it is the same function each time. Once as the classifier head over the vocabulary, where the logits are scores for tokens. Once inside attention, where the logits are scaled query–key dot products and the output is a set of mixing weights over positions. Once inside a mixture-of-experts router, where the logits are scores for experts. The shift invariance, the temperature and the saturation behave identically in all three, which is why understanding it once pays three times.

19 Beyond the three: variances, mixtures, and everything else

The maximum-likelihood recipe of §14 does not stop at three cases. Any distribution you can write down and differentiate can be an output layer, and two extensions are worth doing properly because they fix failure modes that look like model failures and are not.

Predicting the spread as well as the centre

A network with a linear output and squared error implicitly assumes a constant noise level everywhere. When the noise genuinely varies with the input — the model is heteroscedastic — that assumption throws away real information: the model has no way to say "I am confident here and not there".

predict the precision, not the variance — and here is whyp(y | x) = N(y ; μ(x), diag(β(x))⁻¹) β = 1/σ², the PRECISION −log p = ½ ∑ᵢ [ βᵢ·(yᵢ − μᵢ)² − log βᵢ ] + const Only MULTIPLICATION by β and the LOG of β appear. Both have well-behaved gradients. Parameterise by the variance instead and the log-likelihood contains a DIVISION, whose gradient is arbitrarily steep near zero; parameterise by the standard deviation and you get a division AND a square, whose gradient VANISHES near zero. The precision parameterisation is chosen for its gradients, not its elegance. and it must stay positiveβ = ζ(a) = log(1 + eᵃ) softplus of a raw linear output — smooth, positive, and ≈ a for large a so it does not saturate

Read the loss again: β·(y − μ)² is a squared error weighted by the model's own confidence, and −log β is the penalty that stops the model from declaring infinite confidence everywhere. Learned attenuation of hard examples and honest error bars come out of the same two terms.

Mixture density outputs: when the mean is the wrong answer

If p(y | x) genuinely has several peaks, the conditional mean sits between them, at a value the target may never take. A network with a linear output and squared error will confidently predict that impossible value. The fix is to let the network output the parameters of a mixture.

a Gaussian mixture output with n componentsp(y | x) = ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ πᵢ(x) · N( y ; μ⁽ⁱ⁾(x), Σ⁽ⁱ⁾(x) ) three groups of outputs, three different constraintsπ(x) mixing weights, ≥ 0 and summing to 1 → a SOFTMAX over n logits μ⁽ⁱ⁾(x) the n component means, unconstrained → LINEAR units, n × d of them Σ⁽ⁱ⁾(x) covariances, positive definite → usually DIAGONAL, each entry a softplus, to avoid determinants and inverses of a full matrix the loss, and the responsibility weighting it produces for freeL = −log ∑ᵢ πᵢ·N(y; μ⁽ⁱ⁾, Σ⁽ⁱ⁾) Differentiate it and each component's mean is pulled toward y in proportion to that component's POSTERIOR RESPONSIBILITY for y. Nobody wrote that rule down — it falls out of the negative log-likelihood of a mixture. Gradient descent does the right thing automatically, provided the specification is right.

A distinction that costs real accuracy. Multi-class (exactly one of K labels is correct) uses one softmax over K logits. Multi-label (any subset can be correct) uses K independent sigmoids. Using softmax for a multi-label problem forces the outputs to sum to 1, so the model cannot say "both of these are present" — it is structurally prevented from being right, and the loss curve gives no hint that anything is wrong.
Where the gradients go wrong in a mixture, since it is the one case that is genuinely fragile. The mixture log-likelihood involves division by a component's variance, so a component that collapses onto a single data point drives its variance toward zero and its gradient toward infinity. In practice this is handled by clipping gradients, by flooring the predicted variance, or by rescaling — and it is the reason mixture density outputs, though the correct answer to multimodality, have a reputation for being awkward to train.
