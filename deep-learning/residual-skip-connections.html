Deep Learning · architecture pattern

Residual / Skip Connections

The one-line idea that made very deep networks trainable: instead of asking a block of layers to compute a mapping H(x), ask it to compute a correction F(x) = H(x) − x and add the input back, y = x + F(x). This page works through why that matters: the degradation problem that motivated it, the identity-mapping argument, the exact gradient algebra of x + F(x) and the ensemble-of-paths view it implies, the concrete block designs (basic, bottleneck, projection shortcuts, pre-activation, ResNeXt, DenseNet), the gated precursor (highway networks), the long skips of U-Net, the residual stream of every Transformer, and the training tricks that ride on it: stochastic depth, zero-initialised branches (zero-γ, Fixup, ReZero) and the smoother loss landscape. Every figure computes what it draws.

01 The degradation problem: deeper plain nets train worse

The motivating observation is not "deep nets overfit". It is stranger: past some depth, a plain stack of layers reaches a higher training error than a shallower one — even with good initialisation and batch normalisation.

By 2015 the two classical obstacles to depth had largely been dealt with. Variance-preserving initialisation kept activations and gradients at a sane scale at step zero, and batch normalisation kept them there during training. Networks of 20–30 layers trained fine. Yet when He et al. (2015) stacked more of the same layers, something unexpected happened:

Setting (He et al. 2015)Shallower plain netDeeper plain netSame depths, with residual shortcuts
CIFAR-1020 layers56 layers: higher training error and higher test error56 layers beats 20; 110 layers better still
ImageNet18 layers34 layers: higher training error than 18ResNet-34 beats ResNet-18; ResNet-152 best single model

Three things rule out the usual suspects:

not overfitting
Training error is worse
Overfitting lowers training error and raises test error. Here the deeper model is worse on the data it was trained on. More capacity, less fit: an optimisation failure.
not (only) vanishing gradients
The nets used BatchNorm
The authors checked that forward activations and backward gradients had healthy norms. The solver was receiving a signal; it just could not find good weights in reasonable time.
an optimisation-geometry problem
The loss surface is hard
A deep plain composition is a product of many Jacobians; its loss surface is rougher, its gradients less informative (§15). "Deeper" has to be made easy to optimise, not just possible.

Before 2015 the record ImageNet architectures had ~20 weight layers (VGG-19, GoogLeNet-22). After, ResNet-152 won ILSVRC 2015 at 3.57% top-5 error (ensemble) with fewer FLOPs than VGG-19 (11.3 vs 19.6 GFLOPs), and a 1001-layer pre-activation ResNet trained on CIFAR-10. The whole difference is one addition per block.

02 The identity-mapping argument

A constructive proof that depth should never hurt — and an explanation of why plain stacks nevertheless fail to realise it.

Take a trained shallow network with k layers. Build a deeper one by copying those k layers and appending m extra layers that compute the identity. The deeper net computes exactly the same function, so its training error is identical. Therefore a solution at least as good as the shallow one exists in the deeper net's hypothesis space. The degradation result says SGD does not find it.

Why is the identity hard to find for a plain layer? A layer computes φ(Wx + b). To be the identity it needs W = I, b = 0 and a non-linearity that is linear on the whole range of its inputs — ReLU is the identity only on the positive orthant, so a plain ReLU layer cannot represent the identity on signed inputs at all, and a stack of them approximates it only by carefully coordinated weights. Starting from a random initialisation, the identity is a very specific, far-away point.

reparameterise what the block learnsplain block: learn H(x) identity requires H = I (hard) residual block: learn F(x) = H(x) − x, output y = x + F(x) identity requires F = 0 (easy: shrink the weights)

Weight decay, small initialisations and the implicit bias of SGD all pull weights toward zero, so F ≈ 0 is the default a residual block drifts to when it has nothing useful to add. An unhelpful block becomes a near no-op instead of a scrambler. The hypothesis — borne out empirically — is that the optimal mappings in a deep net are often close to the identity, so learning the small perturbation F is better-conditioned than learning H from scratch. He et al. measured the layer responses (the std of each block's F output) and found residual branches produce generally small outputs, smaller still in deeper nets: each block makes a modest edit.

Precondition, not guarantee. The argument shows the deeper residual net can represent the shallow solution and that it sits near initialisation. It does not prove a deeper ResNet always generalises better: on CIFAR-10 the 1202-layer original ResNet trained to similar training error as the 110-layer one but tested worse (7.93% vs 6.43%) — overfitting, now that optimisation is no longer the bottleneck.

03 The residual formulation, precisely

a residual unity = h(x) + F(x, {Wᵢ}) F = the residual branch (2–3 conv/linear layers) x′ = f(y) h = shortcut (identity, or a projection W_s x) f = post-addition activation (ReLU in 2015; identity in pre-act) the ideal case (identity shortcut, f = identity)x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l, W_l)

Four properties of this definition matter everywhere below:

  • Shapes must match for the addition: F(x) must have the same shape as x. When a block changes channel count or spatial resolution, the shortcut must change too (§07).
  • Zero extra parameters, near-zero extra compute for an identity shortcut — one element-wise add. That is why plain vs residual comparisons are fair: same depth, width, parameter count and FLOPs.
  • The branch must be at least two layers. With one linear layer, y = x + Wx = (I + W)x is still just a linear layer and nothing was gained; the original paper observed no advantage for single-layer branches.
  • Addition, not concatenation. Addition keeps width constant and forces the block to write into the same representation it read from. DenseNet (§09) makes the other choice.
# PyTorch — a basic residual block (post-activation, as in the 2015 paper) class BasicBlock(nn.Module): def __init__(self, c_in, c_out, stride=1): super().__init__() self.conv1 = nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 3, stride, 1, bias=False); self.bn1 = nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out) self.conv2 = nn.Conv2d(c_out, c_out, 3, 1, 1, bias=False); self.bn2 = nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out) self.short = nn.Identity() if stride != 1 or c_in != c_out: # projection shortcut (option B) self.short = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 1, stride, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out)) def forward(self, x): out = F.relu(self.bn1(self.conv1(x))) out = self.bn2(self.conv2(out)) return F.relu(out + self.short(x)) # add, THEN ReLU

In Keras functional style it is the same four moves: save residual = x, run the block on x, project residual with a 1×1 conv if the channel count or stride changed, then layers.add([x, residual]). Use padding="same" inside the block so the spatial size only changes where you intend it to.

04 Gradient flow through x + F(x)

Backpropagation through a sum splits the gradient; the identity term carries it through untouched. Unrolled over many blocks, that turns a product of Jacobians into a sum of terms, one of which is always 1.

one blocky = x + F(x) ⇒ ∂L/∂x = ∂L/∂y · (I + ∂F/∂x) = ∂L/∂y + ∂L/∂y · ∂F/∂x └ direct ┘ └ through the branch ┘ unrolled from block l to block L (identity shortcuts, no post-add activation)x_L = x_l + ∑_{i=l}^{L−1} F(x_i, W_i) ∂L/∂x_l = ∂L/∂x_L · ( I + ∂/∂x_l ∑_{i=l}^{L−1} F(x_i, W_i) ) compare the plain stackx_L = f_{L−1}(…f_l(x_l)) ⇒ ∂L/∂x_l = ∂L/∂x_L · ∏_{i=l}^{L−1} J_i (J_i = ∂f_i/∂x_i)

The plain gradient is a product of L − l Jacobians; its norm behaves like ∏‖Jᵢ‖ and so goes to zero or infinity geometrically unless every factor is held at exactly 1. The residual gradient contains an additive ∂L/∂x_L term that reaches every earlier block unchanged, whatever the weights. For the whole bracket to cancel, the branch derivatives would have to sum to exactly −I for every minibatch — generically, that does not happen. This is the gradient highway.

Two precise caveats, both visible in the figure:

  • The highway only exists if the shortcut is clean. If anything multiplies the shortcut — a scale λ, a ReLU after the add, a gate — the direct term becomes ∏λᵢ or ∏ diag(relu′) and the product problem is back (§08).
  • Sums can grow. Without normalisation, each block adds its own variance: with Var[F(x)] ≈ Var[x], the forward signal doubles in variance per block, so ‖x_L‖ ∼ 2^{L/2} and the gradient reaching the input grows the same way. Residual nets without BatchNorm need the branch scaled down — 1/√L, or zero-initialised (§14).
‖∂L/∂hₗ‖ at initialisation, plain stack vs residual stack (width 48, no normalisation) a real forward + backward pass through random networks; y-axis is log₁₀ of the gradient norm at each layer's input, loss L = r·h_L with a random unit r

Things to try: at g = 1 with ReLU, the plain stack's gradient is roughly preserved in expectation (that is what He initialisation buys) but wanders multiplicatively; move g to 0.8 or 1.2 and it vanishes or explodes geometrically in depth. The residual stack with a = 1/√L stays within a small band for any gain. Switch to a = 1 and watch the residual line grow toward the input — the variance-doubling caveat. Switch the wiring to "original" with tanh and the post-add non-linearity reintroduces a product of derivatives on the highway.

05 The unrolled view: a ResNet as an ensemble of paths

Expand the product of (I + ∂F/∂x) factors and a residual network turns out to be a collection of 2ⁿ paths of different lengths — most of them short.

three blocks, expanded (Veit, Wilber & Belongie 2016)x₃ = x₂ + F₃(x₂) = x₁ + F₂(x₁) + F₃(x₁ + F₂(x₁)) = x₀ + F₁(x₀) + F₂(x₀ + F₁(x₀)) + F₃(x₀ + F₁(x₀) + F₂(x₀ + F₁(x₀))) the Jacobian, as a sum over paths∂x_n/∂x₀ = ∏_{i=1}^{n} (I + Jᵢ) = ∑ over subsets S ⊆ {1…n} ∏_{i∈S} Jᵢ number of paths through exactly k branches = C(n, k) total = 2ⁿ

Each input-to-output path chooses, at every block, either the skip or the branch. A plain net has exactly one path, of length n; a residual net with n blocks has 2ⁿ, with length distribution Binomial(n, ½). Veit et al. drew three conclusions by experiment on ResNets:

  • Lesioning is graceful. Deleting a single block from a trained ResNet at test time barely changes error (removing one block halves the paths but leaves 2ⁿ⁻¹ intact); deleting a layer from VGG destroys it. Error rises smoothly as more blocks are removed — ensemble-like behaviour.
  • Paths are not strongly dependent on each other — the network behaves like a collection of shallower networks rather than one deep one.
  • Gradient comes from short paths. Each branch traversed shrinks the gradient by some factor β < 1. Weighting path counts by βᵏ, the gradient in a 110-layer ResNet (54 blocks) was dominated by paths only 5–17 blocks long — the "effective depth" is far below the nominal depth.
How many paths of each length exist, and how much gradient each length carries bars = share of paths with k branches, C(n,k)/2ⁿ; line = share of gradient, ∝ C(n,k)·βᵏ; defaults reproduce the 54-block, β≈0.23 case
closed forms the readout usesmean path length = n/2 gradient-weighted length ~ Binomial(n, β/(1+β)) ⇒ mean n·β/(1+β) n = 54, β = 0.23: mean = 54 × 0.187 = 10.1 blocks, sd = 2.9 ⇒ ≈ 5–16 blocks carry most of it

Caveat: the ensemble picture is a description of trained ResNets, not a derivation from first principles — the paths share weights, so they are not independent models. But it explains two practical facts: why ResNets tolerate stochastic depth (§13) and block dropping at inference, and why "a 152-layer network" is less exotic than it sounds.

06 Basic vs bottleneck blocks, with shapes and parameters

The two canonical ResNet blocks trade a pair of 3×3 convolutions for a 1×1 → 3×3 → 1×1 "bottleneck" that does its expensive spatial work on a quarter of the channels.

The paper chose the bottleneck width so that a 256-channel bottleneck block costs about the same as a 64-channel basic block (69,632 vs 73,728 weights) — four times the representation width for the same compute, which is why ResNet-50 has 3.8 GFLOPs while ResNet-34 has 3.6 despite being far wider at its outputs. Two design notes: the identity shortcut matters most for bottlenecks, because it connects the two wide ends — a projection there would roughly double the block's cost; and in the widely used "ResNet v1.5" the stride-2 downsampling sits in the 3×3 conv rather than the first 1×1, which avoids a 1×1 stride-2 conv that silently discards three-quarters of the input positions.

Block builder: tensor shapes and parameter counts, layer by layer counts are conv weights (no bias — each conv is followed by BatchNorm) plus BN's 2 parameters per channel; MACs = weights × output positions

Check the arithmetic on "ResNet-50 conv2_1": the input has 64 channels but the block outputs 256, so the shortcut must project (64·256 = 16,384 weights) — the only block in the stage that pays for one. The following two blocks in the stage (conv2_2, conv2_3) take 256 channels in and out and use free identity shortcuts.

07 Projection shortcuts and downsampling

When a block changes the number of channels or the spatial size, x and F(x) no longer have the same shape. Something must reshape the shortcut — and the cheaper that something is, the better.

projection shortcuty = F(x, {Wᵢ}) + W_s x W_s = 1×1 conv, stride s (+ BN), maps C_in → C_out
Option (He et al. 2015)What happens when shapes changeElsewhereImageNet ResNet-34 top-1 err
Aidentity, extra channels zero-padded, spatial subsampled by stride 2identity25.03%
B1×1 projectionidentity24.52%
Cprojectionprojection everywhere24.19%

All three dwarf the plain-vs-residual gap (the plain 34-layer net was at 28.54%), so projections are not essential to the effect; B is the standard compromise. C's small gain comes from extra parameters, and it breaks the clean identity path that §04 relies on — so projections are used only where unavoidable, typically once per stage.

  • Stride-2 1×1 projections throw away information: they read one pixel in four. The "ResNet-D" tweak (He et al. 2019, "Bag of Tricks") replaces it with a 2×2 average-pool followed by a stride-1 1×1 conv — a free ~0.5–1% top-1 improvement.
  • In a Transformer the width is constant across all layers, so every shortcut is an identity; projections appear only at the embedding and unembedding.
  • In Keras/TF: when a block includes MaxPooling2D(2), project the residual with Conv2D(filters, 1, strides=2) so the two tensors match.

08 Pre-activation ResNets: make the highway truly clean

The 2015 block applies a ReLU after the addition. That means the "identity" path is not an identity. Moving the normalisation and activation inside the branch fixes it.

original (post-activation)x_{l+1} = ReLU( x_l + BN(W₂·ReLU(BN(W₁ x_l))) ) pre-activation (He et al. 2016)x_{l+1} = x_l + W₂·ReLU(BN(W₁·ReLU(BN(x_l)))) nothing after the add why scaling the shortcut hurtsif x_{l+1} = λ_l x_l + F(x_l): x_L = (∏_{i=l}^{L−1} λᵢ) x_l + … ∂L/∂x_l contains (∏ λᵢ) · ∂L/∂x_L — explodes if λ > 1, vanishes if λ < 1

He et al. ran the ablation: shortcuts scaled by 0.5, gated shortcuts, 1×1-conv shortcuts and dropout on the shortcut all trained worse than the pure identity — even though each is strictly more expressive. The lesson generalises: do not put anything on the main path. With pre-activation, the 1001-layer ResNet reached 4.92% error on CIFAR-10 against 7.61% for the post-activation 1001-layer net in the controlled comparison (4.62% in the best reported run), and its training loss fell faster from the first epoch.

benefit 1
Clean highway
Every block's output is x_l + F; the stack's output is x₀ + ∑F. Gradient reaches every block unmodified.
benefit 2
Better regularised
Every branch input is normalised (BN first), so each branch sees well-scaled inputs regardless of how large the stream has grown.
cost
Needs a final norm
The stream is never normalised on the main path, so its norm grows with depth; add a BN-ReLU (or LayerNorm, in Transformers) before the head.

This is exactly the post-norm vs pre-norm debate in Transformers: the original Transformer put LayerNorm after the addition (LN(x + Sublayer(x))); nearly every modern LLM uses pre-norm (x + Sublayer(LN(x))) plus a final LayerNorm — see Transformers §12.

09 The family: ResNeXt, Wide ResNets, DenseNet

ResNeXt — split, transform, aggregate

aggregated transformations (Xie et al. 2017)y = x + ∑_{i=1}^{C} Tᵢ(x) C = cardinality (number of parallel paths) each Tᵢ = 1×1 (→ d) → 3×3 (d → d) → 1×1 (→ out) ≡ one bottleneck whose 3×3 is a GROUPED conv with C groups

ResNeXt-50 (32×4d) uses 32 paths of width 4: 1×1 256→128 (32,768), grouped 3×3 128→128 with 32 groups (9·128·128/32 = 4,608), 1×1 128→256 (32,768) ≈ 70k weights — the same budget as the ResNet-50 bottleneck, but with a wider (128) inner representation. At equal complexity it beats ResNet-50 (≈22.2% vs 23.9% top-1). The finding: increasing cardinality was a more effective use of budget than going deeper or wider.

Wide ResNets

Zagoruyko & Komodakis (2016) argued depth past ~50 layers gives diminishing returns per FLOP; a 16-layer ResNet widened 8× (WRN-16-8) matched or beat 1000-layer thin ones on CIFAR, trained several times faster on GPUs (wide convs parallelise better), and benefited from dropout inside the branch.

DenseNet — concatenate instead of add

dense connectivity (Huang et al. 2017)x_l = H_l([x₀, x₁, …, x_{l−1}]) [·] = channel concatenation channels into layer l of a block: k₀ + k·(l − 1) k = growth rate (e.g. 32) H_l = BN → ReLU → 1×1 conv (→ 4k) → BN → ReLU → 3×3 conv (→ k)

Each layer reads all previous feature maps in its block and adds only k new channels to the "collective knowledge". A block of l layers has l(l+1)/2 connections. Because features are reused rather than re-learned, DenseNets are very parameter-efficient (DenseNet-201, with 20M parameters, matched the ImageNet accuracy of ResNet-101 with 44.5M). Between blocks, transition layers (BN, 1×1 conv with compression θ = 0.5, 2×2 average pool) shrink the channel count and resolution, since concatenation cannot cross a resolution change.

10 Highway networks: the gated precursor

Months before ResNets, highway networks trained 100-layer plain-ish nets by borrowing the LSTM's trick: let a learned gate decide how much of the input to carry through unchanged.

highway layer (Srivastava, Greff & Schmidhuber 2015)y = H(x, W_H) ⊙ T(x, W_T) + x ⊙ (1 − T(x, W_T)) T(x) = σ(W_T x + b_T) transform gate; C = 1 − T is the carry gate Jacobian along the carry path∂y/∂x = diag(1 − T) + (terms through H and through T) carry product over L layers: ∏_{l} (1 − T_l)

The lineage is direct. The LSTM cell state update c_t = f_t ⊙ c_{t−1} + i_t ⊙ g_t is an additive, gated path through time — the "constant error carousel" that lets gradients survive hundreds of steps (RNNs & LSTMs). A highway network is that idea turned 90°: an additive gated path through depth. A residual block is the highway layer with both gates fixed open: T = 1 on the branch and carry weight 1 on the input.

  • Negative gate bias at init (b_T ∈ [−1, −3]) biases every layer toward carrying, so the deep net starts close to the identity — the same prior that ResNets hard-wire.
  • The gate is data-dependent and can close the highway. The carry factor is 1 − T < 1, so across many layers the product still decays; ResNets fix the carry at exactly 1. Empirically, ungated identity shortcuts won — the gates cost parameters and reintroduced the multiplicative path.
What the carry path passes back through L highway layers, vs a residual identity path each layer's gate is T = σ(b_T + ε), ε ~ N(0, s²) per unit (64 units); y-axis log₁₀ of the mean carry product ∏(1 − T) — the gradient the carry path alone delivers

Worked number: at b_T = −2 the mean gate is σ(−2) = 0.119, so the carry factor per layer is 0.881 and over 50 layers 0.881⁵⁰ ≈ 0.0018. The highway only works because gradient also flows through H, and because training pushes many gates toward fully-carry in layers that are not needed. Push the bias to −4 and the carry product stays near 1 over 100 layers — at which point the layer is doing almost nothing, which is what an identity shortcut gives for free.

11 Long skips: U-Net and encoder–decoder shortcuts

A different kind of skip: not around a block of the same resolution, but across the whole network, from an encoder stage to the decoder stage that works at the same resolution.

U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer & Brox 2015)encoder: e₁ (H×W) → e₂ (H/2) → e₃ (H/4) → e₄ (H/8) → bottleneck (H/16) decoder: d₄ = Conv(concat[Up(bottleneck), e₄]) d₃ = Conv(concat[Up(d₄), e₃]) … d₁ → per-pixel output

The purpose is information, not primarily gradient flow. The encoder's downsampling discards where things are in exchange for what they are; a per-pixel output (segmentation masks, denoised images) needs both. The long skip hands the decoder the high-resolution encoder features directly, so the bottleneck only has to carry global context. Consequences:

  • Concatenation, usually — the decoder learns how to fuse coarse semantics with fine detail. (Feature Pyramid Networks use addition after a 1×1 projection instead.)
  • Sharp boundaries: without the skips, upsampled predictions are blurry; with them, edges are precise. See Segmentation.
  • Diffusion models: the denoising network in most image diffusion models is a U-Net whose blocks are themselves residual blocks with time-embedding injection — long skips across, short skips within. See Diffusion Models.
  • A failure mode: in autoencoders meant to learn a compressed code, long skips let information bypass the bottleneck entirely — the reconstruction is perfect and the code is useless. Skips and bottlenecks have opposite goals; see Autoencoders.

12 Everywhere now: the residual stream in Transformers

Every Transformer layer is two residual blocks. Unrolled, the whole model is an embedding plus a sum of writes by attention and MLP sub-layers into one shared vector per token — the residual stream.

pre-norm Transformer layerx ← x + Attn(LN(x)) x ← x + MLP(LN(x)) unrolled over L layersx_final = x_embed + ∑_{l=1}^{L} [ Attn_l(·) + MLP_l(·) ] logits = W_U · LN(x_final)
  • Nothing is overwritten. Each sub-layer reads a normalised view of the stream and adds its output. Information written by layer 3 is still present, linearly, at layer 40 unless some later layer writes its negation. This is the view underlying "logit lens" analyses and much interpretability work (Transformers §11).
  • Depth is trainable because of the same algebra as §04 — 96-layer stacks train with pre-norm and warmup; post-norm stacks of that depth diverge without special care (DeepNet's scaled shortcut LN(αx + F(x)) with α > 1, 2022, is one such care).
  • Output projections are often scaled down at init by 1/√(2L) (GPT-2) — the same "shrink the branch so the sum does not blow up" fix as Fixup (§14).
  • Mixture-of-experts layers, adapters and LoRA all plug into the residual stream as extra additive writes — see Mixture of Experts and PEFT.

13 Stochastic depth (drop-path)

If a ResNet behaves like an ensemble of paths, you can train it like one: randomly skip whole residual branches during training.

training (Huang et al. 2016)x_{l+1} = x_l + b_l · F(x_l) b_l ~ Bernoulli(p_l) inference — the expected branchx_{l+1} = x_l + p_l · F(x_l) (or: divide by p_l during training, "inverted" drop-path) linear decay rulep_l = 1 − (l / L)·(1 − p_L) p_L = 0.5 at the last block E[active blocks] = ∑ p_l = L − (1 − p_L)(L + 1)/2

Worked numbers: a 110-layer CIFAR ResNet has L = 54 blocks; with p_L = 0.5, E = 54 − 0.5·55/2 = 40.25 blocks per forward pass — about 25% less training compute — and test error improved from 6.41% to 5.25%. Early blocks are kept almost always because they compute low-level features that everything else depends on. Stochastic depth made the 1202-layer ResNet generalise better than the 110-layer one. Today it is standard as drop-path in Vision Transformers and ConvNeXt (rates 0.1–0.5, larger for bigger models).

14 Initialisation tricks: start every block at the identity

§04's caveat — the sum's variance grows with depth — has one family of fixes: make each residual branch output (nearly) zero at initialisation, so the network starts as the identity and the blocks switch on as training needs them.

TrickWhat it doesWhy it works
Zero-γ (Goyal et al. 2017)initialise the last BatchNorm's γ to 0 in every residual branchevery block outputs x + 0 at step 0: the net behaves like a shallow one and trains stably at large batch sizes / learning rates. A one-line default in modern ResNet training recipes.
Fixup (Zhang, Dauphin & Ma 2019)no normalisation: zero-init the last layer of each branch, scale the other branch layers by L^{−1/(2m−2)} (m = layers per branch), add scalar biases and a multiplierbounds the function update per SGD step independent of depth; trained 10,000-layer nets and matched BN ResNets on ImageNet with good regularisation

Try all three settings in the gradient figure in §04. Zero-init does not kill learning: with a = 0 (or γ = 0), the gradient w.r.t. a (or γ) is nonzero, the branches switch on in the first few steps, and the gradient w.r.t. everything upstream flows through the identity. What it prevents is a random, depth-amplified function at step 0.

15 Loss-landscape smoothing and shattered gradients

Two empirical lines of evidence explain why residual nets are easier to optimise, beyond "the gradient does not vanish".

  • Visualised landscapes (Li et al. 2018): plotting the loss on a 2-D slice through the trained weights, along random filter-normalised directions, ResNet-56 has a smooth, nearly convex basin; the same architecture with skips removed has a chaotic surface with many small local minima, and it gets worse with depth (ResNet-110-noshort is dramatically chaotic). Width also smooths the landscape.
  • Shattered gradients (Balduzzi et al. 2017): in a deep plain ReLU net, the gradients at nearby inputs decorrelate exponentially with depth — the gradient looks like white noise, so it carries little usable information about how to change the function. In a residual net the correlation decays only like 1/√L. BatchNorm alone does not fix this; skips do.
filter-normalised direction (Li et al. 2018)L(α, β) = L(θ* + α·d₁ + β·d₂), d ~ N(0, I) then, for every filter / neuron j: d_j ← d_j · ‖θ*_j‖ / ‖d_j‖

Filter normalisation removes the scale ambiguity (a ReLU net's function is unchanged by scaling a layer up and the next down), so slices of different networks are comparable.

A 2-D slice of the loss around a random initialisation: plain vs residual (toy regression, width 10) each pixel is a full forward pass on 32 samples; colour = log₁₀ loss; directions filter-normalised; this is a toy at init, not the published trained-network plots, but the mechanism is the same

Why it happens mechanically: in the plain net, a perturbation of every layer multiplies through the chain, so the loss is a high-degree polynomial in (α, β) — roughly (1 + α)^D in scale — giving steep walls and a huge dynamic range. In the residual net, each block's perturbation adds to the stream, so the loss is much closer to low-order in (α, β). The "roughness" readout is the mean absolute second difference of log-loss across the grid.

16 Practice and pitfalls

Myth: "Residual connections solve vanishing gradients." Better: they give the gradient an identity path, but the degradation problem appeared with BatchNorm and healthy gradient norms. Residuals chiefly make deep nets easier to optimise — better-conditioned, smoother, near-identity at init.
Bug: adding a ReLU, dropout or a scale on the main path "for regularisation". Fix: keep the shortcut untouched; put dropout / drop-path inside or on the branch.
Bug: shape mismatch errors at stage boundaries. Fix: whenever channels or stride change, project the shortcut (1×1 conv, stride s) — and in "same"-padded convs make sure the branch does not change size unintentionally.
Bug: an un-normalised residual net that diverges at depth. Fix: scale branches (1/√L), zero-init the last layer / γ, or use ReZero/Fixup; with pre-norm add a final norm.
  • Where to put the skip: around every 2–3 layers, never around a single linear layer; in Transformers around every sub-layer.
  • Memory: the skip keeps x alive until the add; negligible for ResNets, large for DenseNets and for U-Nets (encoder activations are held until the decoder uses them).
  • Fine-tuning: residual architectures are the backbones people fine-tune; LoRA and adapters are themselves small residual branches added to frozen layers — see Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning.
  • Pruning and early exit: the ensemble view (§05) is why whole residual blocks can be dropped at inference with small loss — the basis of layer-dropping and early-exit schemes.

17 Takeaways

  • Degradation: deeper plain nets train worse — an optimisation problem, not overfitting.
  • y = x + F(x): learn the residual; identity is F = 0, the easy default.
  • Gradient: ∂L/∂x = ∂L/∂y·(I + ∂F/∂x) — a sum, with an always-present identity term, instead of a product.
  • Ensemble view: 2ⁿ paths, Binomial lengths; gradient comes mostly from short paths.
  • Blocks: basic (2×3×3) vs bottleneck (1×1–3×3–1×1, ~same cost at 4× width); project the shortcut only where shapes change.
  • Keep the highway clean: pre-activation; nothing on the main path; final norm at the end.
  • Relatives: ResNeXt (grouped branches), DenseNet (concatenate), highway (gated, LSTM-like), U-Net (long skips for spatial detail), Transformer residual stream.
  • Training tricks: stochastic depth / drop-path; start at the identity (zero-γ, Fixup, ReZero, 1/√L branch scaling).
  • Builds on Neural Network Training and CNNs; used by Transformers.
