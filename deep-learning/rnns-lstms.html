RNNs & LSTMs

Part 3 constrained the weight matrix so that a layer would share its parameters across space. This page does the same thing across time, and the two constraints look almost identical on paper — yet one of them produced a decade of stable, easy training and the other produced a decade of research into why the gradient would not travel. The difference is a single structural fact: a convolution applies its kernel to disjoint windows, while a recurrence feeds its own output back into the same matrix, so the Jacobian that carries the gradient backwards is not a product of different matrices but a matrix power. A power has exactly one thing that decides its fate, and that thing is the spectral radius.

The centre of this page is a hand derivation of backpropagation through time — all five gradients, from the unrolled graph, in one convention, checked twice: once against a completely independent path-sum expansion of the same chain rule, and once against central differences. From there the page follows the consequence, which is that the gradient decays or grows like the spectral radius raised to the distance; measures that claim rather than asserting it; and then derives the LSTM and the GRU as the two answers that stuck. It ends where attention begins: at the fixed-length context vector of a sequence-to-sequence model, whose capacity limit this page measures exactly.

Every number printed here was recomputed against an independent implementation before it was printed — the gradients, the truncation bias, the spectral radii, the parameter counts, the decoding statistics and the capacity curves. Where a common presentation of the vanishing-gradient criterion is wrong, the page shows the counterexample rather than repeating the claim.

01 What a recurrent layer is, and the conventions this page never departs from

One sentence first: a recurrent layer is a dense layer applied once per time step, whose input at each step is the concatenation of the current observation and its own previous output. Nothing else is new. The same affine map, the same non-linearity, the same loss, the same backward pass. What changes is that the layer's output is wired back into its own input, so the computation has a length that the data chooses rather than the architecture.

the layer, in the form part 1 would recognisedense layer z = x·W + b x is one row, W is (dᵢₙ × dₒᵤₜ) recurrent layer zₜ = hₜ₋₁·W + xₜ·U + b and hₜ = g(zₜ) ───────── ───── its own the new last output observation which is ONE dense layer of width dₕ reading a (dₕ + dₓ)-dimensional input — the concatenation [hₜ₋₁, xₜ] — with its weight matrix split into the two named blocks W and U for readability. Frameworks store it either way.

The consequences all come from the feedback edge, and they arrive in a fixed order. Because the same W is used at every step, the model has a size that does not grow with the sequence (§03). Because hₜ depends on hₜ₋₁, the forward pass is serial and the backward pass reaches back through every intervening step (§09–§14). And because the matrix carrying the gradient backwards is always the same matrix, the composition is a power (§17), which either collapses or detonates unless one number is close to one (§18). The gates of §24 onwards exist to make that number close to one by construction.

02 Why a feedforward network cannot do this: three failures, counted

A dense network takes a fixed-size input and produces a fixed-size output. The honest way to see why that is fatal for sequences is not to say "sequences are variable length" but to try the obvious workaround — pad or crop everything to a fixed window and feed it in — and then count what it costs.

Failure 1 · The input does not have a fixed size

Translation, transcription, tagging, summarisation and forecasting all have inputs whose length varies from example to example, and outputs whose length varies too, and usually varies independently of the input's. A dense layer has a weight matrix of shape (dᵢₙ × dₒᵤₜ); dᵢₙ is baked into the parameter tensor at construction time. There is no way to feed it a longer input. The workaround is to fix a maximum window Tₘₐₓ, pad shorter sequences and truncate longer ones — which means committing, before seeing the data, to a hard ceiling on how far back the model can ever look.

Failure 2 · The parameter count grows linearly with the window, and every parameter is position-specific

This is the failure that can be counted, so count it. Flatten a window of T steps of dₓ features into a single vector of length T·dₓ and feed it to a hidden layer of width dₕ:

That is the entire difference and it is worth being precise about WHY: the window model does not have MORE capacity in exchange for those parameters, because every one of them is nailed to an absolute position.

Failure 3 · No sharing of statistical strength across positions

Take a tagger that must recognise a name. In the flattened-window model, the weights that read position 3 and the weights that read position 17 are disjoint sets of numbers. A name that appeared at position 3 in every training sentence teaches the model nothing whatever about a name at position 17. To learn the rule everywhere it must see the rule everywhere, and the sample complexity multiplies by the window length.

This is exactly the argument part 3 made for images, transposed from space to time, and it is worth holding the two side by side because the parallel is exact and the consequences are not:

03 Parameter sharing across time as the inductive bias, stated exactly

Part 1's table of inductive biases gives the recurrent network one row: sequential structure, and that the rule relating one step to the next is the same at every step. That row is correct and it is worth unpacking, because the second half of it is a much stronger assumption than the first and is the one that actually fails.

the assumption, written as a constraint on the function classA general model of a length-t prefix is some function gₜ: hₜ = gₜ( xₜ, xₜ₋₁, …, x₁ ) a DIFFERENT g for every length t A recurrent model asserts that every one of those functions factorises through the SAME two-argument function f, applied over and over: hₜ = f( hₜ₋₁, xₜ ; θ ) ONE f, ONE θ, for every t so that g₃(x₃,x₂,x₁) = f( f( f(h₀,x₁), x₂), x₃). the two things that buys, and they are different things1 · f has a FIXED input size (dₕ + dₓ) whatever t is, so ONE model runs on every length, including lengths never seen in training. 2 · θ is shared across positions, so evidence about the rule collected at step 3 is evidence about the rule at step 300. The sample complexity does not multiply by the sequence length.

The name for the second assumption is stationarity: the conditional relationship between one step and the next does not depend on where in the sequence you are. That is a strong claim about the world and it is often false. The first word of a sentence behaves differently from the fortieth; the first minute of a recording is not statistically the minute after an hour. A recurrent network cannot express position-dependence in θ at all — it can only express it through the state, by learning to keep a counter in hₜ and condition on it. That works, and it costs capacity, and it is a real limitation rather than a technicality. (A transformer, by contrast, has no notion of position at all until one is injected explicitly, which is the opposite failure mode.)

04 The cell, the state, and what "unrolling" actually produces

The recurrent cell is three lines of arithmetic. The interesting object is not the cell but the state — a fixed-size vector that has to stand in for the entire past, and whose size is therefore a hard ceiling on how much of the past can be represented at all.

the vanilla (Elman) cell, completegiven h₀ and x₁, …, xₜ for t = 1 … T: zₜ = hₜ₋₁·W + xₜ·U + b (1 × dₕ) hₜ = tanh( zₜ ) (1 × dₕ) the optional read-out, at whichever steps the task has a target sₜ = hₜ·V + c (1 × d_y) aₜ = ŷₜ = out( sₜ ) out = softmax | σ | identity

The state is a lossy summary, and the loss is forced

Written as a function, hₜ = gₜ(xₜ, …, x₁) maps a variable-length prefix to a fixed vector of dₕ real numbers. That map cannot be injective once the prefix carries more information than dₕ numbers can hold — a fact that has nothing to do with training and everything to do with counting. §36 measures exactly how much survives, and the answer is a clean dₕ/(T·dₓ) in the linear case. What the state chooses to keep is decided by the training criterion: a language model needs only enough of the past to predict the next word, an autoencoder needs enough to reconstruct the whole input, and those are very different demands on the same vector.

Unrolling, and what it is not

Unrolling replaces the cyclic circuit with an acyclic graph: one copy of the cell per time step, all copies sharing the same parameter tensors. That graph is an ordinary feedforward network of depth T with tied weights, and part 2's backpropagation applies to it unmodified. Three things about it are worth being explicit about, because each is a place people go wrong:

it is not an approximation
The unrolled graph is exact
Unrolling is a change of drawing, not of model. Every value the cyclic version computes appears somewhere in the unrolled one. The approximation happens only when the graph is cut short for the backward pass, which is §15.
it is not a deep network
The depth is data-dependent and the weights are tied
A 60-layer feedforward network has 60 independent weight matrices. A 60-step unrolled RNN has one, used 60 times. Everything in §17–§20 follows from that single difference.
it is not free
Every intermediate state must be kept
hₜ is needed in the backward pass, so all T of them stay in memory during the forward pass. Memory is O(T·dₕ) per sequence in the batch, and that — not arithmetic — is what usually sets the maximum sequence length (§14).
The state, step by step: what actually happens inside hₜ every cell of the heat map is a real computed activation; the ρᵗ reference line is drawn only for the two undriven inputs, because a continuously driven state does not decay at all
Why tanh and not ReLU. Part 2's activation table gives the rectifier a derivative of exactly 1 on the active side, which is precisely what a deep feedforward stack wants. A recurrent stack wants something else: the same unit is applied to its own output T times, so an unbounded activation with unit gain lets the state grow without limit, and a positive feedback loop with gain above one diverges within a few dozen steps. tanh is bounded, which caps the forward dynamics no matter what W does, and is symmetric about zero, which keeps the state centred. The price is a derivative that is at most 1 and is usually much less — the vanishing side of §18. Rectified recurrent networks do exist and they need careful initialisation (identity W) and aggressive clipping to be stable; they are the exception, not the default.

The bug that does not raise an error. W is square. The one that is universal, and the one that is not. The first wiring — hidden-to-hidden recurrence with a read-out at each step — is Turing complete in the sense that a finite such network with rational weights and unbounded precision can compute anything a Turing machine can, reading its answer after a number of steps linear in the machine's. The second wiring, with recurrence only from the output, is strictly weaker: it cannot simulate a universal machine, because the only channel from the past to the future is a vector that has been explicitly trained to equal the target. If the target does not contain the information, the information is gone. That is a clean statement about representational power and it has an equally clean practical corollary — the weaker model trains faster and in parallel, so it is the right choice exactly when the target really is a sufficient summary.

07 The loss over a sequence, and where the gradient enters the graph

Before any derivative: the loss of a sequence is the sum of the per-step losses, and that single word is doing more work than it looks. Sum, not mean; and the sum is over the steps that have a target, which need not be all of them.

the total loss for one sequence T L = ∑ Lₜ with Lₜ = 0 at any step that carries no target t=1 the per-step loss, for the three read-outs this page usessoftmax + cross-entropy Lₜ = − ∑ⱼ yₜ[j] · log aₜ[j] aₜ = softmax(sₜ) σ + binary cross-entropy Lₜ = − ∑ⱼ [ y log a + (1−y) log(1−a) ] identity + squared error Lₜ = ½ ∑ⱼ ( aₜ[j] − yₜ[j] )² and the point of choosing them: all three have the SAME derivative at sₜ∂Lₜ / ∂sₜ = aₜ − yₜ This is not a coincidence; it is part 1's exponential-family construction, where the log in the loss cancels the exp in the output unit. It means the derivation in §08–§10 never has to name which read-out is in use, and it means the FINITE-DIFFERENCE AUDIT in §13 is a real test of that claim rather than an assumption baked into the code.

Sum against mean, and the bug it hides

Divide by T and you have the mean per-step loss, which is the number worth reporting because it is comparable across sequence lengths. Divide by T in the objective and you have silently made the gradient of a long sequence smaller than the gradient of a short one by a factor of the length ratio — which is either what you want or a serious bug, depending on whether your batch mixes lengths. With padded batches the trap is sharper still: divide by the padded length and every example's gradient is scaled by how much padding it happened to receive. Mask the padding, sum over real steps, and divide by the number of real steps in the batch, not by the tensor's shape.

The silent one. Padding a batch to a common length and then computing the loss over the whole padded tensor. The padded steps have targets — usually zeros or a pad token — and the model dutifully learns to predict them. Nothing errors, the reported loss looks lower than it should (the pad token is trivially predictable), and the model wastes capacity. The same applies to the state: an unmasked recurrence keeps running over the padding, so the "final" state of a short sequence in a padded batch is the state after the padding, not after the sentence.

Where the gradient enters

The loss touches the graph only at the read-out nodes. Everything else — every hidden state, every weight — receives gradient only through those entry points, routed backwards along the edges of the unfolded graph. The number and placement of those entry points is what §06's fifth column was about, and it is the single best predictor of how hard a recurrent model will be to train: a sequence-to-vector model has one entry point at the very end, and the only route to step 1 is a chain of T − 1 recurrent Jacobians.

09 Backpropagation through time, part 2: ∂L/∂W, the sum over every path

This is the derivation the page exists for. W is used at every step, and — unlike V — every use of it feeds the next use. So a change in W changes h₁, which changes h₂ both directly through W and indirectly through h₁, and so on. The gradient is a sum over every route from every loss back to every use.

Step 1 · Give every use of W its own name

The clean way to handle a parameter that appears many times is the standard one: pretend it does not. Introduce T dummy matrices W⁽¹⁾, …, W⁽ᵀ⁾, all numerically equal to W, with W⁽ᵗ⁾ used only at step t. Now every dummy appears exactly once, the graph is an ordinary feedforward graph, and the total derivative is recovered by summing:

Look at the last line of the T = 3 block, because it is the entire second half of this page. The route from the loss at step 3 back to the use of W at step 1 contains Wᵀ twice. From step t back to step i it contains Wᵀ exactly t − i times, interleaved with tanh gates. That is a power of a single matrix, and §17 is about what powers of a matrix do.

A notational hazard in the standard presentation, worth flagging precisely. The compact form of this result is usually written with a single symbol ∂zₜ/∂hᵢ standing in for the whole travel bracket:
the compact form, as it is normally printed∂Lₜ/∂W = ∑ᵢ (∂Lₜ/∂aₜ)(∂aₜ/∂sₜ)(∂sₜ/∂hₜ)(∂hₜ/∂zₜ)(∂zₜ/∂hᵢ)(∂hᵢ/∂zᵢ)(∂zᵢ/∂W)
Two things about it. First, ∂zₜ/∂hᵢ must be read as a total derivative — it is Wᵀ Dₜ₋₁ Wᵀ ⋯ D_{i+1} Wᵀ, not the single graph edge W, which only exists when i = t − 1. Read as an edge, every term with i < t − 1 is zero and the formula collapses to nonsense. Second, the i = t term of that sum contains the factors (∂zₜ/∂hₜ)(∂hₜ/∂zₜ), and ∂zₜ/∂hₜ does not exist: zₜ is computed from hₜ₋₁, and hₜ is computed from zₜ, so the compact formula is ill-formed at its own first term. The expanded three-line versions above are unambiguous and correct; the compact one needs both repairs before it means anything. Note what is identical in all three: the entire middle — δₜVᵀ ∏(DⱼWᵀ) Dᵢ — does not mention U, W or b. It is the gradient of the loss with respect to the pre-activation zᵢ, and it is computed once and reused three times. That observation is not an optimisation; it is the whole content of §11.

Three consequences that decide how a recurrent network behaves in practice.
  • Every parameter gradient is a sum of T terms, so its magnitude scales with the sequence length. A batch that mixes a 10-step sequence with a 500-step one delivers gradients differing by roughly fifty times before any dynamics enter. This is the same effect part 3 §25 noted for a shared kernel over a feature map, and it is why the learning rate that worked at one sequence length often does not survive a change of length.
  • U's gradient is exactly as long-ranged as W's. It is tempting to think the input matrix is "closer to the data" and therefore safe. It is not: the term reaching x₁ from L_T travels the same T − 1 recurrent factors. If W's gradient vanishes then so does U's, and the early inputs stop mattering.
  • The bias gradient is the same chain with the outer-product factor removed, so it is a sum of T row vectors rather than T matrices. It is usually the largest-magnitude gradient in the model, because it is the one term with no small multiplier in front of it.

15 Truncated BPTT — and the two different algorithms that share the name

§14's memory table says the honest algorithm does not fit. The universal response is to cut the backward pass short after k steps. That is called truncated BPTT, and the phrase names two genuinely different algorithms with different costs and different biases. Conflating them is common and it matters, because one of them is much worse than the other and it is the one the frameworks implement.

form A
Per-loss truncation
Each loss Lₜ is back-propagated through the k most recent steps and stopped. Every loss gets the same k steps of history. Cost O(T·k), memory O(k). This is the form textbooks derive, and it is what the notation TBPTT(k) usually means in a paper.
form B
Chunked truncation
The sequence is cut into non-overlapping windows of k. Within a window, full BPTT. At a boundary the state is carried forward but the gradient is not — the state is "detached". Cost O(T), memory O(k). This is what every framework's tutorial does, because it is one line of code.
the asymmetry
Form B is unfair by position
A loss that lands at the end of a window sees k steps of history; a loss that lands at the start of one sees one. Averaged over a sequence a loss sees (k+1)/2 steps, not k, and the amount of history it gets depends on where the window boundaries happened to fall.
form A, exactly∂L T t ⎡ j = t ↓ i+1 ⎤ ──── ≈ ∑ ∑ hᵢ₋₁ᵀ δₜ Vᵀ ⎢ Dⱼ Wᵀ ⎥ Dᵢ ∂W t=1 i=t−k+1 ⎣ ∏ in THAT order ⎦ §09's double sum with the INNER range clipped. Every term with i ≤ t − k is simply dropped. Nothing else changes. form B, exactlyfor each window [w, w+k): forward from h₍w₋₁₎ as usual — the STATE crosses the boundary backward with ∂L/∂h₍w₋₁₎ DISCARDED — the GRADIENT does not so a loss at step t reaches back only to the start of ITS OWN window: history available = t − w + 1, which ranges over 1 … k

What truncation is, and is not, an approximation of

Both forms leave the forward pass exact. The model computes the same states and the same loss; the sequence is not shortened. What is approximated is only the gradient, and the approximation is not a small perturbation — it is the deliberate deletion of every term whose travel exceeds k. That means truncation makes a substantive modelling commitment: the model will not learn any dependency longer than k. It can still represent one, and if it somehow acquired one it would still use it, but gradient descent will never build one, because no gradient ever tells it to.

The claim to be careful with. "Truncation is fine, because the terms you dropped were vanishing anyway." That is circular, and it is exactly backwards for the model you are trying to train. If the recurrence is contracting then the dropped terms really are negligible and truncation costs nothing — but then the model has no long-range memory to learn with in the first place. If the recurrence is near the edge of stability, which is where a model that can remember has to sit (§18), the dropped terms are the same order as the kept ones and truncation removes real signal. The two cases are exactly the ones you cannot distinguish without measuring, so §16 measures.

Everything from §09 hangs on one object, so isolate it. The factor that carries gradient from step t back to step k is a product of d = t − k one-step Jacobians, and every one of them contains the same matrix.

The eigenvalues are complex, and it matters

The consequence for what you can learn

There is a result here that is unpleasant and important, and it is not a statement about optimisation but about the model class. In order to store information robustly — so that a small perturbation of the state does not lose it — the recurrence must be contracting near the stored state, and a contraction has ρ < 1, which is exactly the vanishing condition. The two things you want, robust memory and a gradient that reaches back, pull in opposite directions for a vanilla cell. The gradient of a long-range interaction is therefore exponentially smaller than the gradient of a short-range one whenever the model can actually remember anything, and it is not that learning is impossible but that the long-range signal is buried under short-range noise. Experiments on this go back to the early 1990s and they reported the probability of successfully training a vanilla recurrent network by gradient descent falling to near zero for dependencies of only ten to twenty steps. §31 reproduces that failure and measures where it starts.

The gates of §24 won, and the field mostly stopped talking about the alternatives. Initialisation: where to put ρ(W) at step zero

Part 2's variance argument gives the general rule and it is not repeated. The recurrent-specific finding is narrower: initialise the recurrent matrix with a spectral radius slightly above one. The reasoning is §18's — below one and the model provably cannot remember, at exactly one you are on a boundary you will immediately drift off — and the reason "slightly above" is safe rather than catastrophic is that the tanh's own gate contributes a factor well below one at any realistic operating point (§17 measured 0.605 at a typical scale), so a spectral radius of, say, 1.2 gives a net per-step factor comfortably under one in the forward pass while leaving the backward pass much healthier than a contraction would. Values around 1.1 to 1.2, combined with a sparse rather than dense random matrix and with clipping switched on, are the standard recipe; an orthogonal initialisation, which sets every singular value and every eigenvalue modulus to exactly one, is the other common choice and is what most frameworks default to for the recurrent block.

a linear self-connection with a FIXED weighthₜ = α hₜ₋₁ + (1 − α) · tanh( hₜ₋₁W + xₜU + b ) α ∈ [0, 1) ∂hₜ/∂hₜ₋₁ = α I + (1 − α) DₜWᵀ ← an identity path with a GUARANTEED coefficient α so the gradient decays no faster than αᵈ, and α is YOUR choice: α = 0.9 a time constant of about 10 steps α = 0.99 about 100 steps α = 0.999 about 1000 steps This is a running average, and it is exactly the exponential moving average that part 2 built the momentum optimiser out of. Same object, different place in the model.

Two things to notice, because both come back in §24. First, this is a residual connection — αI plus something — and it works for the same reason. Second, α is a constant, which means the unit's time scale is fixed at design time and the unit can never clear its state: whatever it accumulated decays at a rate you chose and cannot be discarded on demand. A model built from leaky units with a spectrum of different α values covers a range of time scales and is genuinely useful, but the moment the task needs "remember this until the sentence ends, then forget it", the constant is the wrong shape of object. Replace the constant with a sigmoid of the input and you have the forget gate.

Skip connections through time, and removed connections

Add a direct edge from hₜ₋ₘ to hₜ for some fixed delay m. A dependency of length d now has a graph path of length d/m, so a gradient that decayed as ρᵈ decays as ρd/m along the new route. It is a real improvement and a bounded one: the exponent shrinks by a constant factor and stays exponential, and — worth noting — explosion is not helped at all, because the short path is still there and the long one is still exponential. The related idea of removing the length-one connections instead of adding long ones forces a group of units onto a coarse time scale rather than merely permitting it, and organising a network into several such groups, each updating at a different frequency, is a clean way to build a multi-scale model. All of these are structural commitments made in advance, which is their strength (no learning required) and their weakness (the scales are yours, not the data's).

Echo state networks: do not train the recurrence at all

The most radical answer, and the one that deserves more attention than it gets. Fix U and W at random — with ρ(W) set deliberately large, values around 3 are advocated, relying on the tanh's saturation to keep the forward dynamics bounded — and train only the read-out V. Because the state is then a fixed non-linear function of the input history, and the read-out is linear, the training problem is a linear regression: convex, solvable in closed form, no BPTT, no vanishing, no exploding, no clipping. The recurrent layer is a "reservoir" — a bank of temporal features — and the analogy to a kernel method is exact: an arbitrary-length history is mapped to a fixed vector and a linear predictor is fitted on top.

What reservoir computing gets right, and where it stops. It is the cleanest demonstration that the representation a recurrent network needs is often not very task-specific: a random high-dimensional non-linear echo of the input history contains enough for a linear read-out to do well on a great many time-series problems. Where it stops is where the features must be task-specific — language, in particular, where the useful summary of a prefix is exactly the thing that has to be learned. The idea also had a second life as an initialisation: set the weights the way a reservoir would and then train them anyway with BPTT, which is where §23's "ρ ≈ 1.2, sparse" recipe came from.

Here is the whole idea in one sentence, before any equations. Add a second state vector whose only update is "multiply by a number near one, then add something", and never pass it through a matrix or a squashing function. Everything else in the LSTM exists to control the two numbers in that sentence.

25 Each gate, derived from the job it has to do

The four are not interchangeable and the design is not arbitrary. Each answers a question the previous section's accumulator raises, and the order below is the order in which the questions arise.

gate 1 · the one that matters most
Forget — fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁
A leaky unit (§23) has a fixed α. It can hold and it can never clear. The question "when should this accumulator be reset?" has an input-dependent answer — a full stop, a new speaker, a scene change — so make the coefficient a function of the input: fₜ = σ(…). Ablation studies across many architectural variants agree that this is the single most important component; remove it and performance collapses on most tasks.
gate 2
Input — iₜ ⊙ gₜ
Without it the accumulator absorbs every step's candidate, and a hundred steps of irrelevant input drown whatever was being held. The input gate makes writing conditional: iₜ ≈ 0 means "nothing here is worth recording". Note that the candidate gₜ is tanh, not σ — it is a value to be written and can be negative, whereas a gate is a proportion and cannot.
gate 3
Output — oₜ ⊙ tanh(cₜ)
Answers "should the thing I am holding affect my behaviour right now?". Separating holding from acting is what lets a unit carry a fact silently. The tanh around cₜ is a squashing step whose job is to bound what leaves the cell — cₜ itself is unbounded, and feeding an unbounded value into the next layer's affine map is how a network diverges.
not a gate
The candidate gₜ
This is the ordinary recurrent cell — exactly the vanilla tanh(hₜ₋₁W + xₜU + b) of §04, unchanged. Everything the LSTM adds is plumbing around that computation. Seeing this makes the parameter count obvious: four affine maps of the same shape rather than one.

Why sigmoids for the gates and tanh for the values

a gate is a proportion; a value is a quantitygate σ(z) ∈ (0, 1) "keep this fraction" multiplicative value tanh(z) ∈ (−1, 1) "write this amount" additive, signed Swap them and the model breaks in specific ways: · a tanh forget gate can be NEGATIVE, so the accumulator flips sign every step — an oscillator, not a memory. · a σ candidate can never write a negative value, so the cell can only ever count upwards. and the reason the sigmoid's small derivative is not a problem HEREPart 2's table notes max σ′ = 0.25 and calls the sigmoid a bad hidden unit for exactly that reason. A gate is not a hidden unit. Its output is MULTIPLIED into a path rather than passed along one, and the long-range gradient travels through the value fₜ itself, not through σ′. A saturated gate — fₜ ≈ 1 with σ′ ≈ 0 — is a gate that has DECIDED, and it passes the memory gradient perfectly while receiving almost none itself. That is a feature: it makes the decision stable.
The intuition that misleads. "The gates learn to open and close like switches, so I can read the model by looking at them." Some units do that; most do not. In a trained LSTM a minority of units have forget gates that genuinely saturate near 0 or 1 and behave like latches, and those are interpretable and often correspond to something nameable. The majority sit at intermediate values and act as leaky integrators with learned, mildly input-dependent time constants — much closer to §23's leaky unit than to a switch. Both behaviours are useful and only the first is the story usually told.

Why 1 and not 5

Because a forget gate at σ(5) = 0.993 is a cell that cannot clear, and §25 argued that clearing is the forget gate's actual job. 29 Peephole connections, and the variants that were tried

The LSTM as it is used today is not the LSTM as it was first published: the forget gate was added three years later, and the peephole connections a year after that. A great many further variants have been proposed and systematically compared, and the result of those comparisons is worth stating plainly because it saves a lot of time.

Peephole connections

The three gates decide what to do with the cell state, and in the plain cell they cannot see it — they read only hₜ₋₁, which is the cell state after the output gate has filtered it. If the output gate is closed, hₜ₋₁ ≈ 0 and the gates are deciding blind. A peephole adds a direct connection from the cell state to each sigmoid gate:

peephole LSTM — the three sigmoid gates gain one term eachfₜ = σ( hₜ₋₁Wf + xₜUf + cₜ₋₁Zf + bf ) iₜ = σ( hₜ₋₁Wi + xₜUi + cₜ₋₁Zi + bi ) oₜ = σ( hₜ₋₁Wo + xₜUo + cₜ Zo + bo ) ← note cₜ, not cₜ₋₁ The candidate gₜ gets NO peephole: it is a value, not a decision. the ordering detail that is easy to get wrongfₜ and iₜ are needed BEFORE cₜ exists, so they peep at cₜ₋₁. oₜ is needed AFTER, so it peeps at the FRESH cₜ — which is the whole point, since the output gate's job is to decide how much of the state it is about to expose, and it should see the state it is exposing. the cost, and the two forms of ZZ DIAGONAL (the original) 3m parameters each unit peeps only at ITSELF. This is the form that is normally meant. Z FULL (m × m) 3m² parameters rarely used. At m = 128 that is 384 extra parameters against 219 648 — under 0.2%. The intervention is essentially free, which is why it was worth trying.

Peepholes were introduced to let the cell learn precise timing — counting a fixed number of steps, measuring an interval — which needs the gates to react to the state's actual value rather than to a gated view of it. On tasks with that character they help clearly. On language and most sequence labelling they do not, and modern implementations mostly omit them.

30 The GRU: the same idea with one state and two gates

The gated recurrent unit asks which parts of the LSTM are load-bearing and removes the rest. It drops the separate cell state, couples the input and forget gates into one, and drops the output gate, leaving two gates and three matrix pairs instead of four.

the GRU, completezₜ = σ( hₜ₋₁Wz + xₜUz + bz ) UPDATE gate rₜ = σ( hₜ₋₁Wr + xₜUr + br ) RESET gate cₜ = tanh( (rₜ ⊙ hₜ₋₁)Wc + xₜUc + bc ) CANDIDATE └─────┬─────┘ the reset gate acts on the state BEFORE the matrix, not on the result — this is the one place the GRU is not just a simplified LSTM hₜ = zₜ ⊙ cₜ + (1 − zₜ) ⊙ hₜ₋₁ a CONVEX COMBINATION the carousel, for the GRU∂hₜ/∂hₜ₋₁ = diag( 1 − zₜ ) + (everything through cₜ and the gates) └─────┬─────┘ the additive path. Same structure as the LSTM's diag(fₜ), with the SAME consequence: diagonal, so no matrix power, and per-unit time constants. So the GRU's "forget gate" is 1 − zₜ, and holding a value means zₜ → 0.
A polarity trap that costs an afternoon. Two opposite conventions for zₜ are both in wide use. Above, zₜ is how much new, so carrying the old state means zₜ → 0. Several standard references and most framework implementations write hₜ = zₜ ⊙ hₜ₋₁ + (1 − zₜ) ⊙ cₜ instead, in which zₜ is how much old and carrying means zₜ → 1. The two describe the same model — the gate simply learns the opposite sign — but every statement about gate values, every initialisation bias and every interpretability plot flips. This page uses the first convention throughout and says so at every point where a gate value is printed. Check which one your code uses before you set a bias.

32 Bidirectional RNNs: legitimate, and the one way they are not

Everything so far has been causal: hₜ depends on the past and the present. For many tasks that is an artificial handicap — the right interpretation of a sound depends on the sounds that follow it, and the right tag for a word depends on the rest of the sentence. If the whole input is available before any output is required, there is no reason to look only backwards.

two independent recurrences, opposite directions, concatenatedforward hₜ = f( hₜ₋₁, xₜ ) t = 1 → T, h₀ = 0 backward gₜ = f′( gₜ₊₁, xₜ ) t = T → 1, gₜ₊₁ = 0 output oₜ = [ hₜ , gₜ ] · V + c the CONCATENATION, so V is (2dₕ × d_y) f and f′ have SEPARATE parameters. Nothing is shared between the two directions and nothing needs to be: they are two models reading the same input, and only the read-out sees both. what it costs, exactlyparameters 2× the cell, and the read-out grows from (dₕ × d_y) to (2dₕ × d_y) arithmetic 2× latency the two directions are INDEPENDENT, so on parallel hardware the wall-clock cost is ≈ 1× — but the backward pass cannot start until the LAST input has arrived, which is the real cost and it is not a compute cost.

When it is legitimate

Whenever the entire input sequence is in hand before any output is needed. Sequence tagging, sentence classification, offline transcription, the encoder half of a translation model — all fine, and all substantially better with both directions. The gain is not marginal: on tasks where the disambiguating evidence is genuinely to the right, a unidirectional model is being asked to guess.

The failure that produces a beautiful validation curve and a useless model. Using a bidirectional layer in a setting where the future is not available at inference time. The model has read the answer. Three specific cases, all common:
  • Autoregressive generation. Generating yₜ requires gₜ, which requires y values you have not generated yet. Not a leak so much as an impossibility — but people build it, because it trains fine with teacher forcing (§35) and only fails at sampling time.
  • Streaming or real-time inference. Trains offline on complete sequences, deploys on a live stream, and the backward direction has nothing to read. What usually happens is that someone feeds it a buffer, so the model silently sees a truncated and differently-distributed future from the one it trained on.
  • Forecasting. A bidirectional layer over a time series that includes the prediction window is reading the future it is being asked to predict. The validation loss is superb. This is the most common form of leakage in applied time-series work and the bidirectional flag is one of the easiest ways to introduce it.
The test is one question, and it has nothing to do with the model: at inference time, will step t's output be required before step T's input has arrived? If yes, the layer cannot be bidirectional.
Extensions worth knowing. The idea generalises to grids: four recurrences over an image, one per direction, giving each position a summary of everything above, below, left and right. That is more expensive than a convolution and buys long-range lateral interaction within a feature map. The idea also stacks — a bidirectional layer's concatenated output is a perfectly good input to another bidirectional layer (§33) — and this was the standard architecture for speech recognition and sequence tagging for several years, until the transformer encoder replaced it with something that reaches both directions in one hop rather than in T.

33 Deep and stacked recurrent networks

A recurrent layer is already deep in time. Stacking adds depth in the ordinary sense: the sequence of states produced by one layer becomes the input sequence of the next. The two kinds of depth are independent and they do different things.

What each layer ends up doing

The empirical finding is consistent and matches the convolutional analogy: lower layers respond to short-range, fast-changing structure and higher layers to longer-range, slower structure. In a character-level language model the first layer tracks something close to spelling and the higher ones something closer to syntax; in speech the first layer is near-phonetic and the upper ones carry word- and phrase-level context. The mechanism is not mysterious — layer 2's input is already a summary, so its own recurrence is integrating summaries rather than raw observations, and the effective time constant compounds.

what to use
Two to four layers
The gain from one layer to two is usually large and from two to three moderate; beyond four it is rarely worth the cost. This is a much shallower sweet spot than a convolutional network's, and the reason is the previous formula: every extra layer lengthens the path a long-range gradient has to travel.
what to add with it
Skip connections between layers
Exactly the residual idea: hₜ⁽ˡ⁾ = f(…) + hₜ⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾. It shortens the vertical path in the same way the gates shortened the horizontal one, and it is what makes five or more layers trainable when they are needed.
where the normalisation goes
LayerNorm, not BatchNorm

LayerNorm reduces across the features of one example and does not care about time at all.
the dropout trap
Not on the recurrent connection, or not naively
Sampling a fresh dropout mask at every time step injects noise that compounds through the recurrence and destroys the memory. 34 Sequence to sequence: two recurrences and one vector between them

Every wiring in §06 except the last requires the input and output sequences to have the same length, and translation does not. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple, which is part of why it worked: use two recurrent networks, and let the first one's final state be the second one's starting point.

Five details that are easy to get wrong

1
The encoder produces no outputs
It has no read-out and no per-step loss. Its only gradient arrives from the decoder, through C. That single channel is what §36 is about.
2
The two halves need not match
Different widths, different depths, different cell types — nothing requires them to agree. If the widths differ, a small projection matrix sits on C.
3
The decoder needs an end symbol
Nothing about the architecture says when to stop. A dedicated end-of-sequence token is added to the vocabulary and inserted at the end of every training target; generation halts when it is emitted. Without it, generation does not terminate.
4
Reversing the source often helped
Feeding the input backwards was a real and reproducible improvement in early translation systems. The reason is §26's: it puts the first source words closest to C, and the first target words are usually their translations, so the shortest and most important dependencies become short.
5
The encoder is usually bidirectional
And legitimately so (§32) — the whole source is available before any target word is produced. The decoder can never be bidirectional, because its own future does not exist yet.
The observation that reversing the source helps is the whole argument for attention in miniature. If the model's behaviour improves when you reorder the input so that important dependencies become short, then the model is not handling long dependencies well, and the fixed-length channel is the reason. Reversal is a trick that helps one language pair with one word order; it does not generalise. What generalises is removing the constraint that forced the trick, and that is attention.

35 Teacher forcing, and the exposure bias it creates

The decoder is autoregressive: yₜ is generated from yₜ₋₁. At training time there are two choices for what to feed in as yₜ₋₁ — the model's own previous prediction, or the true previous target. Feeding the truth is called teacher forcing, it is what everyone does, and it is not a heuristic: it falls out of maximum likelihood.

why maximum likelihood REQUIRES itThe objective factorises by the chain rule of probability: log P( y₁, y₂ | x ) = log P( y₂ | y₁, x ) + log P( y₁ | x ) ───── the TRUE y₁, from the data. The second factor is a conditional probability given the OBSERVED y₁. Substituting the model's own prediction for y₁ would be computing a different quantity — the likelihood of a sequence the data does not contain. Teacher forcing is not an approximation of the correct objective; it IS the correct objective. and what it buys computationallyEvery target yₜ₋₁ is known in advance, so all T decoder inputs are known in advance, so the whole decoder input sequence can be prepared as ONE tensor and the input-to-hidden products can be batched across time. In a model with NO hidden-to-hidden recurrence, the time steps decouple entirely and training parallelises completely.

The bias it creates

two different distributions, and the model only ever sees oneTRAINING the decoder's input at step t is drawn from the DATA: yₜ₋₁ ∼ the true target sequence INFERENCE the decoder's input at step t is drawn from ITS OWN OUTPUT: ŷₜ₋₁ ∼ the model's prediction at the previous step These are the same distribution only if the model is perfect. Any error at step t moves step t+1's input OFF the training distribution, where the model has no guarantees, which makes another error more likely, and the process compounds. The name for the gap is EXPOSURE BIAS: the model was never exposed to its own mistakes.

The failure mode is characteristic and recognisable: generation that starts well and degrades, repeats, or falls into a loop after a few dozen tokens. It is worth being precise about the cause, because it is often mis-attributed. The model has not "forgotten" anything and its parameters are fine; it has been handed a prefix that no training sequence resembled, and it is extrapolating.

A trained decoder defines a probability distribution over whole sequences. Producing an output means searching that distribution, and the search is not free — the number of candidate sequences is |V| to the power of the length. Taking the most probable token at each step is the obvious approximation, and it is wrong far more often than people expect.

38 Recursive networks: the same cell on a tree instead of a chain

A recurrent network composes one function along a chain. A recursive network composes the same kind of function along a tree. It is the natural generalisation, it has one clear structural advantage, and it is not widely used — all three facts are worth understanding.

A naming warning. Do not abbreviate "recursive neural network" as RNN. The abbreviation is taken and the two models are different. This page writes "recursive network" in full, every time.

39 Where recurrence lost, where it still wins, and where it came back

Recurrent networks were the default for sequences for roughly two decades and then stopped being, quickly. It is worth being precise about what replaced them and why, because the reason is not the one usually given, and because the property that lost is not the property that matters in every setting.

Where training throughput is the binding constraint and the data is effectively unlimited, the parallel model wins decisively. It is not that the recurrent inductive bias is wrong; it is that it cannot be fitted fast enough to compete.

The return: recurrence that trains in parallel

The interesting recent development is that the one disadvantage — the serial training chain — turns out to be removable if the recurrence is made linear in the state. A recurrence of the form hₜ = aₜ ⊙ hₜ₋₁ + bₜ, where aₜ and bₜ depend on xₜ but not on hₜ₋₁, is an associative scan, and an associative scan over T elements can be evaluated in O(log T) parallel depth rather than O(T). That is precisely the LSTM's cell-state line — cₜ = fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + iₜ ⊙ gₜ — with the gates' dependence on hₜ₋₁ removed. This is the family that includes the modern state-space models and the simplified gated variants: constant-memory inference like a recurrent network, parallel training like a transformer, at the price of the gates no longer being able to react to the model's own current state. State Space Models owns that story in full; §26's diagonal-Jacobian argument is why it was possible.

The line worth carrying away. The property that made the LSTM work — a state whose update is elementwise, additive and gated, so that its Jacobian is diagonal — is the same property that makes a recurrence parallelisable, because a diagonal linear recurrence is an associative scan. The gates were introduced to fix the gradient and turned out, twenty-five years later, to also be the thing that lets recurrence be trained at scale. The part that had to be given up in the meantime was the gates reading hₜ₋₁, which is exactly the non-linearity that made the LSTM sequential.

The eight things this page would keep if it could keep only eight

  1. Parameter sharing across time is the whole idea, and it has one consequence the convolutional version does not have. A convolution's shared applications are independent; a recurrence's feed one another. That single difference turns a product of distinct Jacobians into a matrix power, and a power has exactly one number that decides its fate.
  2. BPTT is ordinary backpropagation on the unrolled graph, plus one rule: a parameter used T times gets a gradient that is the sum of T terms. The double sum over (loss, use) pairs and the O(T) backward recursion are the same computation in different orders — verified here to 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ — and the recursion is T times cheaper.
  3. The criterion for vanishing is spectral, not entrywise. A matrix whose every entry is 0.5 has ρ = 2 and explodes; a matrix with an entry of 9 can have ρ = 0.9 and contract — after amplifying by 35× for sixty steps first. ‖W‖₂ < 1 gives the guarantee, ρ(W) gives the rate, and the gap between them is transient behaviour that neither number describes.
  4. Truncation is a modelling decision disguised as an optimisation. It bounds memory and it also decides that the model will never learn a dependency longer than the window. The two named algorithms behind the one name behave differently, and the one the frameworks implement is both more biased and not monotone in the window size.
  5. Clipping is mandatory and it only fixes half the problem. A recurrent loss surface is genuinely cliff-shaped because ∂(wᵈ)/∂w = d·wd−1, and one unclipped step on a cliff destroys a training run. Nothing about clipping helps a gradient that is too small, because the problem there is a ratio and rescaling preserves ratios.
  6. The gates work because the memory path is elementwise. cₜ = fₜ ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + … has a diagonal Jacobian, so there is no matrix power, no spectral radius, and each unit gets its own learned time constant. That is the structural statement; the residual connection is the same idea with an unconditional coefficient, and the reason recurrence needs a learned one is that a sequence model must be able to forget.
  7. The constant error carousel is a capability, not a guarantee — and the initialisation switches it on. Measured at a distance of sixty steps, an LSTM with b_f = 0 passes 3.5 × 10⁻¹³, 2.8 million times less than a vanilla cell at ρ = 0.9; with b_f = 3 it passes 4.5 × 10⁻¹ and does not decay at all. One number in the initialiser.
  8. The fixed-length context vector has an exact capacity, and it is dₕ/(L·dₓ). That is a rank argument, unaffected by training or by the weights, and it is why the encoder's memory of the beginning of its input goes to zero while its memory of the end stays near one. Removing that constraint is what attention does, and it is where this series goes next.

The mistakes that cost the most

  • Not printing ρ(W), the truncation window and σ(b_f) before debugging anything else. Three numbers, one line, and they explain most "the model will not learn long-range structure" reports (§18, §16, §27).
  • Believing the gradient check because it passed on a square toy. With dₓ = dₕ = d_y and a symmetric W, a transposed recurrent matrix passes (§13).
  • Using a bidirectional layer in a causal setting. The validation curve is beautiful and the model has read the answer (§32).
  • Comparing cells at equal hidden width. That gives the LSTM four times the parameters and is a comparison of sizes, not architectures (§28, §31).
  • Comparing cells at a single training budget. The LSTM fails at T = 50 after 600 steps and solves it after 1500. A benchmark at one step count measures convergence speed and reports it as capability (§31).
  • Computing the loss over padding, or reading "the last state" out of a padded batch. Both silent, both common (§07, §40).
  • Assuming the GRU's update gate has the polarity you expect. Both conventions are in wide use and every gate value, bias and plot flips between them (§30).
  • Taking "the LSTM solves the vanishing gradient" literally. It provides a path that can carry gradient. Whether it does is decided by the forget gate, and at zero bias it does not (§26).
  • Reaching for a different gate topology when a gated cell will not learn. Large systematic searches found no variant that clearly beats both the LSTM and the GRU; the forget-gate bias closes most of the gap to the best thing they found (§29).
  • Widening the beam and expecting better output. Past a moderate width the search gets better and the output gets worse, because the objective — not the search — prefers bland continuations (§37).
