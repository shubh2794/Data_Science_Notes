32 Bidirectional RNNs: legitimate, and the one way they are not

Everything so far has been causal: hₜ depends on the past and the present. For many tasks that is an artificial handicap — the right interpretation of a sound depends on the sounds that follow it, and the right tag for a word depends on the rest of the sentence. If the whole input is available before any output is required, there is no reason to look only backwards.

two independent recurrences, opposite directions, concatenatedforward hₜ = f( hₜ₋₁, xₜ ) t = 1 → T, h₀ = 0 backward gₜ = f′( gₜ₊₁, xₜ ) t = T → 1, gₜ₊₁ = 0 output oₜ = [ hₜ , gₜ ] · V + c the CONCATENATION, so V is (2dₕ × d_y) f and f′ have SEPARATE parameters. Nothing is shared between the two directions and nothing needs to be: they are two models reading the same input, and only the read-out sees both. what it costs, exactlyparameters 2× the cell, and the read-out grows from (dₕ × d_y) to (2dₕ × d_y) arithmetic 2× latency the two directions are INDEPENDENT, so on parallel hardware the wall-clock cost is ≈ 1× — but the backward pass cannot start until the LAST input has arrived, which is the real cost and it is not a compute cost.

When it is legitimate

Whenever the entire input sequence is in hand before any output is needed. Sequence tagging, sentence classification, offline transcription, the encoder half of a translation model — all fine, and all substantially better with both directions. The gain is not marginal: on tasks where the disambiguating evidence is genuinely to the right, a unidirectional model is being asked to guess.

The failure that produces a beautiful validation curve and a useless model. Using a bidirectional layer in a setting where the future is not available at inference time. The model has read the answer. Three specific cases, all common:
  • Autoregressive generation. Generating yₜ requires gₜ, which requires y values you have not generated yet. Not a leak so much as an impossibility — but people build it, because it trains fine with teacher forcing (§35) and only fails at sampling time.
  • Streaming or real-time inference. Trains offline on complete sequences, deploys on a live stream, and the backward direction has nothing to read. What usually happens is that someone feeds it a buffer, so the model silently sees a truncated and differently-distributed future from the one it trained on.
  • Forecasting. A bidirectional layer over a time series that includes the prediction window is reading the future it is being asked to predict. The validation loss is superb. This is the most common form of leakage in applied time-series work and the bidirectional flag is one of the easiest ways to introduce it.
The test is one question, and it has nothing to do with the model: at inference time, will step t's output be required before step T's input has arrived? If yes, the layer cannot be bidirectional.
Extensions worth knowing. The idea generalises to grids: four recurrences over an image, one per direction, giving each position a summary of everything above, below, left and right. That is more expensive than a convolution and buys long-range lateral interaction within a feature map. The idea also stacks — a bidirectional layer's concatenated output is a perfectly good input to another bidirectional layer (§33) — and this was the standard architecture for speech recognition and sequence tagging for several years, until the transformer encoder replaced it with something that reaches both directions in one hop rather than in T.
One direction against two, and the leak, on a task whose answer is to the right the disambiguating token is placed by a slider — move it past the query position and watch the causal model lose the information the bidirectional one still has

33 Deep and stacked recurrent networks

A recurrent layer is already deep in time. Stacking adds depth in the ordinary sense: the sequence of states produced by one layer becomes the input sequence of the next. The two kinds of depth are independent and they do different things.

stacking, and the shapeslayer 1 hₜ⁽¹⁾ = f⁽¹⁾( hₜ₋₁⁽¹⁾, xₜ ) U⁽¹⁾ is (dₓ × dₕ) layer 2 hₜ⁽²⁾ = f⁽²⁾( hₜ₋₁⁽²⁾, hₜ⁽¹⁾ ) U⁽²⁾ is (dₕ × dₕ) ⋮ read-out sₜ = hₜ⁽ᴸ⁾ V + c Layer 2 runs over the SAME time axis. It is not a second pass over the sequence; it is a second representation of it, computed simultaneously. the three places depth can be added, and what each does to the path length1 · STACK the layers shortest path from xₜ to hₜ₊ᵈ⁽ᴸ⁾: d + L (the standard, and what everyone means by a deep RNN) 2 · Deepen the TRANSITION — an MLP for the state-to-state map shortest path between two adjacent steps: × (depth of that MLP). A single hidden layer in the transition DOUBLES the length of every long-range path, which is why this is used sparingly. 3 · Deepen the INPUT-to-hidden or hidden-to-OUTPUT maps costs nothing in path length, because neither is on the recurrence. This is the cheap one and it is usually where the depth should go.

What each layer ends up doing

The empirical finding is consistent and matches the convolutional analogy: lower layers respond to short-range, fast-changing structure and higher layers to longer-range, slower structure. In a character-level language model the first layer tracks something close to spelling and the higher ones something closer to syntax; in speech the first layer is near-phonetic and the upper ones carry word- and phrase-level context. The mechanism is not mysterious — layer 2's input is already a summary, so its own recurrence is integrating summaries rather than raw observations, and the effective time constant compounds.

what to use
Two to four layers
The gain from one layer to two is usually large and from two to three moderate; beyond four it is rarely worth the cost. This is a much shallower sweet spot than a convolutional network's, and the reason is the previous formula: every extra layer lengthens the path a long-range gradient has to travel.
what to add with it
Skip connections between layers
Exactly the residual idea: hₜ⁽ˡ⁾ = f(…) + hₜ⁽ˡ⁻¹⁾. It shortens the vertical path in the same way the gates shortened the horizontal one, and it is what makes five or more layers trainable when they are needed.
where the normalisation goes
LayerNorm, not BatchNorm
Part 2 §30 owns the reason: BatchNorm's statistics are per-timestep, so a variable-length batch has a different number of examples contributing at each step and the running estimates are meaningless past the shortest sequence. LayerNorm reduces across the features of one example and does not care about time at all.
the dropout trap
Not on the recurrent connection, or not naively
Sampling a fresh dropout mask at every time step injects noise that compounds through the recurrence and destroys the memory. Apply dropout to the vertical connections between layers, and if you want it on the recurrent path use the same mask at every step of a sequence. This is a real and well-documented failure of the naive application.

34 Sequence to sequence: two recurrences and one vector between them

Every wiring in §06 except the last requires the input and output sequences to have the same length, and translation does not. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple, which is part of why it worked: use two recurrent networks, and let the first one's final state be the second one's starting point.

the architecture, completeENCODER reads x₁ … x₍ₙₓ₎, produces NO output at all hₜᵉ = fᵉ( hₜ₋₁ᵉ, xₜ ) t = 1 … nₓ C = h₍ₙₓ₎ᵉ THE CONTEXT VECTOR a FIXED-LENGTH summary of an ARBITRARY-LENGTH input DECODER generates y₁ … y₍ₙᵧ₎, conditioned on C h₀ᵈ = C (or C enters at every step, hₜᵈ = fᵈ( hₜ₋₁ᵈ, yₜ₋₁, C ) or both — all three are used) ŷₜ = softmax( hₜᵈ V + c ) TRAINED JOINTLY, one objective: maximise log P( y₁…ₙᵧ | x₁…ₙₓ ) = ∑ₜ log P( yₜ | y<ₜ, x )

Five details that are easy to get wrong

1
The encoder produces no outputs
It has no read-out and no per-step loss. Its only gradient arrives from the decoder, through C. That single channel is what §36 is about.
2
The two halves need not match
Different widths, different depths, different cell types — nothing requires them to agree. If the widths differ, a small projection matrix sits on C.
3
The decoder needs an end symbol
Nothing about the architecture says when to stop. A dedicated end-of-sequence token is added to the vocabulary and inserted at the end of every training target; generation halts when it is emitted. Without it, generation does not terminate.
4
Reversing the source often helped
Feeding the input backwards was a real and reproducible improvement in early translation systems. The reason is §26's: it puts the first source words closest to C, and the first target words are usually their translations, so the shortest and most important dependencies become short.
5
The encoder is usually bidirectional
And legitimately so (§32) — the whole source is available before any target word is produced. The decoder can never be bidirectional, because its own future does not exist yet.
The observation that reversing the source helps is the whole argument for attention in miniature. If the model's behaviour improves when you reorder the input so that important dependencies become short, then the model is not handling long dependencies well, and the fixed-length channel is the reason. Reversal is a trick that helps one language pair with one word order; it does not generalise. What generalises is removing the constraint that forced the trick, and that is attention.
The encoder-decoder pair, and the single edge everything must pass through the context edge is drawn at a width proportional to its actual dimension against the input's total size — that ratio is §36's subject

35 Teacher forcing, and the exposure bias it creates

The decoder is autoregressive: yₜ is generated from yₜ₋₁. At training time there are two choices for what to feed in as yₜ₋₁ — the model's own previous prediction, or the true previous target. Feeding the truth is called teacher forcing, it is what everyone does, and it is not a heuristic: it falls out of maximum likelihood.

why maximum likelihood REQUIRES itThe objective factorises by the chain rule of probability: log P( y₁, y₂ | x ) = log P( y₂ | y₁, x ) + log P( y₁ | x ) ───── the TRUE y₁, from the data. The second factor is a conditional probability given the OBSERVED y₁. Substituting the model's own prediction for y₁ would be computing a different quantity — the likelihood of a sequence the data does not contain. Teacher forcing is not an approximation of the correct objective; it IS the correct objective. and what it buys computationallyEvery target yₜ₋₁ is known in advance, so all T decoder inputs are known in advance, so the whole decoder input sequence can be prepared as ONE tensor and the input-to-hidden products can be batched across time. In a model with NO hidden-to-hidden recurrence, the time steps decouple entirely and training parallelises completely.

The bias it creates

two different distributions, and the model only ever sees oneTRAINING the decoder's input at step t is drawn from the DATA: yₜ₋₁ ∼ the true target sequence INFERENCE the decoder's input at step t is drawn from ITS OWN OUTPUT: ŷₜ₋₁ ∼ the model's prediction at the previous step These are the same distribution only if the model is perfect. Any error at step t moves step t+1's input OFF the training distribution, where the model has no guarantees, which makes another error more likely, and the process compounds. The name for the gap is EXPOSURE BIAS: the model was never exposed to its own mistakes.

The failure mode is characteristic and recognisable: generation that starts well and degrades, repeats, or falls into a loop after a few dozen tokens. It is worth being precise about the cause, because it is often mis-attributed. The model has not "forgotten" anything and its parameters are fine; it has been handed a prefix that no training sequence resembled, and it is extrapolating.

RemedyWhat it doesHonest assessment
Scheduled samplingDuring training, flip a coin at each step and feed either the true token or the model's own sample, with the probability of the model's own sample rising over training.Directly attacks the distribution gap and does help on some tasks. It also makes the objective no longer a proper likelihood — the model can reduce the loss by making its own predictions easier to continue rather than more accurate — and that inconsistency is a real objection, not a quibble.
Multi-step targetsPredict several steps ahead through the model's own outputs and take the loss there, so the model learns to return to a good state after drifting.Expensive and effective. It is the same idea as the above with a fixed rather than sampled schedule.
Better decoding (§37)Beam search, or sampling with a truncated distribution, instead of taking the highest-probability token at every step.Attacks the symptom rather than the cause, and is by far the cheapest intervention. It also addresses a genuinely separate problem, which §37 is about.
More data and a bigger modelNothing clever.What actually happened. Exposure bias is a statement about the model's error rate compounding; drive the per-step error rate low enough and the compounding takes long enough not to matter over the lengths people generate. Large autoregressive language models are trained with pure teacher forcing and no correction, and generate coherent text for thousands of tokens. This is worth saying plainly, because exposure bias was expected to be a fundamental barrier and it turned out to be a symptom of models that were not good enough.

36 The fixed-context bottleneck, measured — the argument attention answers

This is the last section of the recurrent story and the first section of the next page's. The claim is that a single fixed-length vector cannot summarise an arbitrarily long input, and that translation quality degrades with source length as a result. The claim is usually asserted. Here it is measured, in a form that admits an exact answer.

The question, posed so that it has an exact answer

"Can the context vector summarise the input?" is too vague to measure. Here is a sharp version: how much of the input is still linearly recoverable from the context vector? Take a linear encoder — drop the tanh — feed it random inputs, and ask, for each source position k, what fraction of the variance of xₖ the best possible linear read-out of C can explain. That quantity has a closed form and no training is involved.

the linear encoder, written as one matrixhₜ = hₜ₋₁W + xₜU ⟹ C = hₗ = ∑ₖ xₖ · ( U Wᴸ⁻ᵏ ) └────┬────┘ Mₖ, a (dₓ × dₕ) block Stack the blocks: C is a LINEAR MAP from an (L·dₓ)-dimensional input to a dₕ-dimensional output. So rank ≤ min( L·dₓ, dₕ ) and the moment L·dₓ > dₕ the map has a NON-TRIVIAL NULL SPACE. There exist two different inputs with the SAME context vector, and no decoder however clever can tell them apart. This is a counting argument; it does not depend on the weights, the training, or the task. the best linear reconstruction, in closed formΣ = ∑ⱼ MⱼᵀMⱼ the covariance of C when xⱼ ∼ N(0, I) R²(k) = trace( Mₖ Σ⁺ Mₖᵀ ) / dₓ the fraction of xₖ recoverable

The input contains L·dₓ. The average fraction that survives is the ratio. It is a RANK argument and nothing else, and the recurrent weights cannot change it — note that the ρ = 0.9 and ρ = 1.0 columns have IDENTICAL means.

Where the surviving information sits, which is the part that hurts

The mean is only half the story. Look at the last two columns: at L = 64 the final source position is still 97.8% recoverable while the first is 0.0%. The context vector does not degrade gracefully into a blurry summary of the whole input; it becomes a sharp record of the end and an empty record of the beginning. The spectral radius controls how sharply. At L = 32 the first position retains 1.80% at ρ = 1.0 and 0.03% at ρ = 0.9 — a factor of 60 — while the two have identical means, because the rank argument fixes the total and only the distribution is free. That is exactly why §23 recommends initialising near the edge: it does not buy more capacity, it redistributes the capacity you have towards the beginning of the input.

Saturation costs information that the counting argument alone does not charge for.
Why this is the argument for attention, stated precisely. Every number above is a consequence of one design decision: C has a fixed dimension that does not depend on the input length. The counting argument says the average recoverable fraction is dₕ/(L·dₓ), so doubling the source halves it, and no amount of training changes a rank. The two available responses are to make dₕ grow with L — which means abandoning the fixed-size summary — or to let the decoder read the encoder's states directly rather than through a summary. The second is attention: replace the single vector C with the whole sequence of encoder states, and give the decoder a learned, per-step mechanism for deciding which of them to read. The rank bound then becomes min(L·dₓ, L·dₕ) and the length dependence disappears. Part 5 starts here. This page has built the argument and stops at it.

37 Decoding: why the greedy sequence is not the most probable sequence

A trained decoder defines a probability distribution over whole sequences. Producing an output means searching that distribution, and the search is not free — the number of candidate sequences is |V| to the power of the length. Taking the most probable token at each step is the obvious approximation, and it is wrong far more often than people expect.

the object being searchedP( y₁…yₗ | x ) = ∏ₜ P( yₜ | y<ₜ, x ) argmax over all sequences → |V|ᴸ candidates. At |V| = 30 000 and L = 20 that is 10⁸⁹. Not searchable. GREEDY: at each step take argmaxⱼ P( yₜ = j | y<ₜ, x ). Cost L·|V|. BEAM k: keep the k highest-scoring PREFIXES at each step. Cost L·k·|V|.

The smallest counterexample, exactly

two steps, two symbols, and greedy already failsP(y₁ = A) = 0.60 P(y₂ | A) = (0.55, 0.45) P(y₁ = B) = 0.40 P(y₂ | B) = (0.90, 0.10) all four sequences: P(AA) = 0.3300 P(AB) = 0.2700 P(BA) = 0.3600 P(BB) = 0.0400 GREEDY step 1: A, because 0.60 > 0.40 step 2: A, because 0.55 > 0.45 → AA, P = 0.3300 TRUE ARGMAX → BA, P = 0.3600 Greedy took the locally better first symbol and lost 9.1% of the probability mass. B is worse NOW and leads somewhere better.

Beam search is not exact either, and it has its own pathology. A beam of width k keeps k prefixes and can still discard the true argmax's prefix if it looks bad early — the table shows beam 2 missing a quarter of the time at L = 10. And there is a second, subtler problem: longer sequences have lower probability, because every extra factor is below one. A beam search that scores by total log-probability therefore has a systematic preference for short outputs, and translation systems produce truncated translations unless the score is length-normalised — divided by the length, or by a tuned power of it. That normalisation is a decoding heuristic with no probabilistic justification and it is essential.
The one that surprises people who fix the search. "So use a wider beam and get better output." Past a moderate width, wider beams make translation and summarisation output worse by human judgement, even as they reliably find higher-probability sequences. The search is working; the objective is the problem. Maximum likelihood on a finite corpus assigns a great deal of probability to bland, high-frequency, generic outputs, and a strong enough search finds them. This is why sampling-based decoders — restricting to the top few tokens or to the smallest set whose probability exceeds a threshold, and then sampling — are standard for open-ended generation, and why beam search survives mainly where the output is tightly constrained by the input, as in translation and transcription.
Greedy, beam and exhaustive search on a tree small enough to enumerate the whole tree is drawn and every leaf's exact probability computed, so "the argmax" is a fact here rather than an estimate

38 Recursive networks: the same cell on a tree instead of a chain

A recurrent network composes one function along a chain. A recursive network composes the same kind of function along a tree. It is the natural generalisation, it has one clear structural advantage, and it is not widely used — all three facts are worth understanding.

A naming warning. Do not abbreviate "recursive neural network" as RNN. The abbreviation is taken and the two models are different. This page writes "recursive network" in full, every time.
the recursive cell — the same shape, two children instead of one predecessorrecurrent hₜ = f( hₜ₋₁ , xₜ ; θ ) one predecessor recursive hnode = f( hleft , hright ; θ ) two children = tanh( [hleft, hright] · W + b ) W is (2dₕ × dₕ) Leaves are the inputs. The root is the representation of the whole sequence. The SAME θ is used at every internal node — parameter sharing, over tree positions instead of time positions. the structural advantage, which is the entire argument for itDEPTH between a leaf and the root: recurrent chain O(T) every step is a composition balanced binary tree O(log T) halves at every level At T = 1024 that is 1024 compositions against 10. Everything in §17–§18 is exponential in that number, so the difference is not a constant factor — it is the difference between ρ¹⁰²⁴ and ρ¹⁰.

Which tree?

Choice of treeWhere it comes fromTrade
A balanced binary treeNothing — it is fixed by the sequence length alone.Guarantees the O(log T) depth and encodes no knowledge whatever. The splits fall wherever they fall, usually mid-phrase.
The syntactic parse treeAn external parser, run over the sentence before the model sees it.The composition boundaries are linguistically meaningful, so the model is composing phrases rather than arbitrary spans. Requires a parser, inherits its errors, and gives every example a different shape — which is the practical killer.
A learned treeThe model infers the structure.The right answer in principle. The structure is discrete, so the objective is not differentiable, and the training methods available are much weaker than backpropagation.
Why they did not win, stated honestly. Three reasons, and only the first is about accuracy. Batching. Every example has a different tree, so the computation graph differs per example and the matrix products cannot be batched without substantial machinery — a fatal inefficiency in an era when throughput decided what got trained. The parser dependency. A pipeline that begins with a parser inherits its errors and its languages. They were overtaken. Transformers reach every position in one hop, which beats O(log T), and they learn whatever structure they need from the data rather than being told it. The recursive network's central insight — that composition should follow structure rather than sequence order — is not refuted by any of that; it is the same intuition that motivates graph neural networks, where the structure is given by the data and the batching problem has been solved.
