08 Backpropagation through time, part 1: ∂L/∂V, the gradient that is not special

The next four sections derive every gradient of the vanilla recurrent network from the unrolled graph. Part 2 owns backpropagation as an algorithm and is not repeated; what is derived here is the one thing recurrence changes, which is what happens when the same parameter appears at every one of T places in the graph. V is the warm-up, because it is the one matrix for which nothing changes at all.

the model, restated so the next four sections need nothing elsezₜ = hₜ₋₁W + xₜU + b hₜ = tanh(zₜ) sₜ = hₜV + c aₜ = out(sₜ) L = ∑ₜ Lₜ δₜ ≡ ∂Lₜ/∂sₜ = aₜ − yₜ (§07 — all three read-outs) Dₜ ≡ diag( 1 − hₜ ⊙ hₜ ) the tanh Jacobian at step t, diagonal, entries in (0, 1]

Why V is easy: it is outside the loop

Look at the unrolled graph and ask which nodes V touches. It appears in sₜ for every t — so it is still a shared parameter and its gradient is still a sum over T terms — but sₜ is a leaf of the recurrence. Nothing downstream of sₜ feeds back into any hₖ. Changing V changes the outputs and does not change a single hidden state. The graph below the read-out is untouched.

the sum over the T places V appears, and nothing more∂L ∑ ∂Lₜ ∂sₜ ────── = ───── · ────── ∂V[a][j] t ∂sₜ[j] ∂V[a][j] and from sₜ[j] = ∑ₐ hₜ[a]·V[a][j] + c[j], the second factor is hₜ[a]. so, in matrix form∂L/∂V = ∑ₜ hₜᵀ δₜ (dₕ × 1)(1 × d_y) = (dₕ × d_y) ✓ ∂L/∂c = ∑ₜ δₜ (1 × d_y) ✓ An OUTER PRODUCT per time step, accumulated. Exactly what part 2's dense layer gives — the only recurrent feature is the ∑ₜ.
A shape slip worth naming, because it appears in print. A common presentation gives this gradient as ∂Lᵢ/∂V = (aᵢ − yᵢ) hᵢ, with the two vectors simply written next to each other. As written that expression has no defined shape. (aᵢ − yᵢ) is d_y-dimensional and hᵢ is dₕ-dimensional; their juxtaposition is neither an inner product (the dimensions do not match) nor a scalar multiple. The intended object is the outer product — hᵢᵀ(aᵢ − yᵢ) in the row convention, (aᵢ − yᵢ)hᵢᵀ in the column convention — and the missing transpose is exactly the kind of thing that is harmless on a slide and fatal in code, because dₕ and d_y are often accidentally equal in a toy example.

The gradient arriving at the state, which is where the interesting part starts

While we are here, record what the read-out hands to the recurrence. This is the only channel by which the loss reaches any hidden state, and everything in §09 onwards routes it backwards:

the gradient the read-out delivers to hₜ, from ITS OWN loss only(∂Lₜ/∂hₜ)[a] = ∑ⱼ δₜ[j] · V[a][j] i.e. ∂Lₜ/∂hₜ = δₜ Vᵀ Row-convention rule, used everywhere below: if a row covector u is the gradient with respect to the OUTPUT of the map v ↦ v·M, then the gradient with respect to the INPUT is u·Mᵀ, and the gradient with respect to M is vᵀu. Those two lines are the whole of the chain rule in this convention.
∂L/∂V: the sum of T outer products, accumulated one step at a time step the slider forward and the accumulator fills in; the finite-difference column is recomputed live and never hard-coded

09 Backpropagation through time, part 2: ∂L/∂W, the sum over every path

This is the derivation the page exists for. W is used at every step, and — unlike V — every use of it feeds the next use. So a change in W changes h₁, which changes h₂ both directly through W and indirectly through h₁, and so on. The gradient is a sum over every route from every loss back to every use.

Step 1 · Give every use of W its own name

The clean way to handle a parameter that appears many times is the standard one: pretend it does not. Introduce T dummy matrices W⁽¹⁾, …, W⁽ᵀ⁾, all numerically equal to W, with W⁽ᵗ⁾ used only at step t. Now every dummy appears exactly once, the graph is an ordinary feedforward graph, and the total derivative is recovered by summing:

the tied-parameter rule, which is the ONLY thing recurrence adds∂L ∑ᵀ ∂L ──── = ──────── one term per USE of the matrix ∂W i=1 ∂W⁽ⁱ⁾ Same rule as a convolution's shared kernel (part 3 §25), same rule as a weight-tied autoencoder. Nothing recurrent about it yet.

Step 2 · One loss, one use: the chain from Lₜ back to W⁽ⁱ⁾

Fix a loss at step t and a use at step i ≤ t. Read the path off the unrolled graph, right to left:

the chain, with every factor named and its shape givenLₜ ← aₜ ← sₜ ← hₜ ← zₜ ← hₜ₋₁ ← ⋯ ← hᵢ ← zᵢ ← W⁽ⁱ⁾ ∂Lₜ/∂sₜ = δₜ = aₜ − yₜ (1 × d_y) ∂sₜ/∂hₜ = Vᵀ as a right-multiplication (d_y × dₕ) ∂hⱼ/∂zⱼ = Dⱼ = diag(1 − hⱼ ⊙ hⱼ) (dₕ × dₕ) ∂zⱼ/∂hⱼ₋₁ = Wᵀ as a right-multiplication (dₕ × dₕ) ∂zᵢ/∂W⁽ⁱ⁾ = hᵢ₋₁ as an outer-product factor (dₕ × 1) multiplied out, one loss and one use∂Lₜ ⎡ j = t ↓ i+1 ⎤ ──────── = hᵢ₋₁ᵀ · δₜ Vᵀ · ⎢ Dⱼ Wᵀ ⎥ · Dᵢ ∂W⁽ⁱ⁾ ⎣ ∏ in THAT order ⎦ └───┬──┘ └──┬───┘ └──────┬──────┘ └┬┘ the use the loss the TRAVEL back the tanh site enters through t − i gate at here recurrent steps the use The bracket is EMPTY when i = t (a product over an empty range is the identity), which is the term where the loss and the use are at the same step and no travel is needed. ORDER MATTERS and the arrow means it: the factors are written left to right with j DECREASING, DₜWᵀ first and Dᵢ₊₁Wᵀ last, because the gradient travels backwards through the graph. Reversing them gives a different matrix, and the T = 3 expansion below is the unambiguous form.

Step 3 · Sum over both

the complete gradient of the recurrent matrix∂L T t ⎡ j = t ↓ i+1 ⎤ ──── = ∑ ∑ hᵢ₋₁ᵀ δₜ Vᵀ ⎢ Dⱼ Wᵀ ⎥ Dᵢ ∂W t=1 i=1 ⎣ ∏ in THAT order ⎦ T(T+1)/2 terms. At T = 100 that is 5 050 matrix chains — which is why nobody computes it this way, and §11 turns it into T terms instead. the three smallest cases, written out — this is the whole derivationT = 1 ∂L₁/∂W = h₀ᵀ δ₁Vᵀ D₁ T = 2 ∂L₂/∂W = h₁ᵀ δ₂Vᵀ D₂ (i = 2) + h₀ᵀ δ₂Vᵀ D₂Wᵀ D₁ (i = 1) T = 3 ∂L₃/∂W = h₂ᵀ δ₃Vᵀ D₃ (i = 3) + h₁ᵀ δ₃Vᵀ D₃Wᵀ D₂ (i = 2) + h₀ᵀ δ₃Vᵀ D₃Wᵀ D₂Wᵀ D₁ (i = 1) and ∂L/∂W is the sum of all six of those lines.

Look at the last line of the T = 3 block, because it is the entire second half of this page. The route from the loss at step 3 back to the use of W at step 1 contains Wᵀ twice. From step t back to step i it contains Wᵀ exactly t − i times, interleaved with tanh gates. That is a power of a single matrix, and §17 is about what powers of a matrix do.

A notational hazard in the standard presentation, worth flagging precisely. The compact form of this result is usually written with a single symbol ∂zₜ/∂hᵢ standing in for the whole travel bracket:
the compact form, as it is normally printed∂Lₜ/∂W = ∑ᵢ (∂Lₜ/∂aₜ)(∂aₜ/∂sₜ)(∂sₜ/∂hₜ)(∂hₜ/∂zₜ)(∂zₜ/∂hᵢ)(∂hᵢ/∂zᵢ)(∂zᵢ/∂W)
Two things about it. First, ∂zₜ/∂hᵢ must be read as a total derivative — it is Wᵀ Dₜ₋₁ Wᵀ ⋯ D_{i+1} Wᵀ, not the single graph edge W, which only exists when i = t − 1. Read as an edge, every term with i < t − 1 is zero and the formula collapses to nonsense. Second, the i = t term of that sum contains the factors (∂zₜ/∂hₜ)(∂hₜ/∂zₜ), and ∂zₜ/∂hₜ does not exist: zₜ is computed from hₜ₋₁, and hₜ is computed from zₜ, so the compact formula is ill-formed at its own first term. The expanded three-line versions above are unambiguous and correct; the compact one needs both repairs before it means anything. This page verified that the repaired expansion reproduces the recursion of §11 to 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁵, so the underlying chain is right — it is the abbreviation that is broken.

10 Backpropagation through time, part 3: ∂L/∂U, ∂L/∂b, and what changes (nothing)

U also feeds the recurrence — it contributes to zₜ, hence to hₜ, hence to everything after. So its gradient has exactly the same structure as W's, and differs in precisely one factor: the last one.

the same chain, with the final factor swapped∂zᵢ/∂W⁽ⁱ⁾ = hᵢ₋₁ the PREVIOUS STATE was what W multiplied ∂zᵢ/∂U⁽ⁱ⁾ = xᵢ the INPUT is what U multiplied ∂zᵢ/∂b⁽ⁱ⁾ = 1 the bias multiplied nothing so, term for term∂L T t ⎡ j = t ↓ i+1 ⎤ ──── = ∑ ∑ xᵢᵀ δₜ Vᵀ ⎢ Dⱼ Wᵀ ⎥ Dᵢ (dₓ × dₕ) ∂U t=1 i=1 ⎣ ∏ in THAT order ⎦ ∂L T t ⎡ j = t ↓ i+1 ⎤ ──── = ∑ ∑ δₜ Vᵀ ⎢ Dⱼ Wᵀ ⎥ Dᵢ (1 × dₕ) ∂b t=1 i=1 ⎣ ∏ in THAT order ⎦

Note what is identical in all three: the entire middle — δₜVᵀ ∏(DⱼWᵀ) Dᵢ — does not mention U, W or b. It is the gradient of the loss with respect to the pre-activation zᵢ, and it is computed once and reused three times. That observation is not an optimisation; it is the whole content of §11.

Three consequences that decide how a recurrent network behaves in practice.
  • Every parameter gradient is a sum of T terms, so its magnitude scales with the sequence length. A batch that mixes a 10-step sequence with a 500-step one delivers gradients differing by roughly fifty times before any dynamics enter. This is the same effect part 3 §25 noted for a shared kernel over a feature map, and it is why the learning rate that worked at one sequence length often does not survive a change of length.
  • U's gradient is exactly as long-ranged as W's. It is tempting to think the input matrix is "closer to the data" and therefore safe. It is not: the term reaching x₁ from L_T travels the same T − 1 recurrent factors. If W's gradient vanishes then so does U's, and the early inputs stop mattering.
  • The bias gradient is the same chain with the outer-product factor removed, so it is a sum of T row vectors rather than T matrices. It is usually the largest-magnitude gradient in the model, because it is the one term with no small multiplier in front of it.

11 The whole thing as one O(T) recursion — and why it is the same numbers

§09's double sum has T(T+1)/2 terms and each contains a matrix chain, which makes the naive cost O(T³dₕ³). The actual algorithm is O(T dₕ²), and getting from one to the other is a single observation: the inner travel bracket for i is the bracket for i+1 with one more factor on the end. Compute it once, going backwards, and carry it.

the recursion — six lines, and this is what every framework runsinitialise gₜ₊₁ ← 0 (1 × dₕ) ∂L/∂U, ∂L/∂W, ∂L/∂V, ∂L/∂b, ∂L/∂c ← 0 for t = T down to 1: δₜ = aₜ − yₜ the read-out error ∂L/∂V += hₜᵀ δₜ ∂L/∂c += δₜ ∂L/∂hₜ = δₜ Vᵀ + gₜ₊₁ Wᵀ ← the ONLY recurrent line ───── ──────── from its from the FUTURE, arriving own loss through the shared matrix gₜ = ∂L/∂zₜ = (∂L/∂hₜ) ⊙ (1 − hₜ ⊙ hₜ) ∂L/∂W += hₜ₋₁ᵀ gₜ ∂L/∂U += xₜᵀ gₜ ∂L/∂b += gₜ finally ∂L/∂h₀ = g₁ Wᵀ (needed only if h₀ is learned, or if the sequence is a continuation)

Why this is the same thing

Unrolling the recursion by hand for T = 3 gives back §09's six lines exactly. Expand ∂L/∂h₁:

the recursion, unrolled at t = 1 for a T = 3 sequence∂L/∂h₁ = δ₁Vᵀ + g₂Wᵀ = δ₁Vᵀ + ( ∂L/∂h₂ ) D₂ Wᵀ = δ₁Vᵀ + ( δ₂Vᵀ + g₃Wᵀ ) D₂ Wᵀ = δ₁Vᵀ + δ₂Vᵀ D₂Wᵀ + δ₃Vᵀ D₃Wᵀ D₂Wᵀ └──┬──┘ └──────┬──────┘ └───────────┬───────────┘ t = 1, no t = 2, one t = 3, two travels travel travel which are precisely the i = 1 terms of the three losses in §09. The double sum was never wrong; it was just being computed in the expensive order.

Reassurance is not evidence, so this page implemented both — the literal double sum with its explicit matrix chains, and the recursion — and ran them against each other. Over sequences of length 3, 7, 12 and 20 with hidden widths 3 to 6, the largest disagreement in any entry of any of the five gradients was:

the path-sum expansion against the O(T) recursion, max absolute differenceT = 3, dₕ = 3 ∂L/∂U 5.55e−17 ∂L/∂W 2.78e−17 ∂L/∂V 0.00e+00 T = 7, dₕ = 4 ∂L/∂U 4.44e−16 ∂L/∂W 2.22e−16 ∂L/∂V 0.00e+00 T = 12, dₕ = 5 ∂L/∂U 7.77e−16 ∂L/∂W 4.44e−16 ∂L/∂V 0.00e+00 T = 20, dₕ = 6 ∂L/∂U 3.55e−15 ∂L/∂W 1.78e−15 ∂L/∂V 0.00e+00 ────────────────── WORST 3.55e−15 That is the accumulated rounding of a few thousand double-precision multiplications, and nothing else. The two computations are the same computation. ∂L/∂V is EXACTLY equal because it involves no travel at all.

The zero that teaches something. h₀ = 0, so the t = 1 contribution to ∂L/∂W is the outer product of a zero vector — exactly zero. The first step's use of W contributes nothing to W's gradient, whatever the loss is. That is not an artefact of this example: with a zero initial state it is always true, and it means a length-1 sequence carries no information about the recurrent matrix at all. The recurrent matrix is only ever learned from steps 2 onwards, which is one more reason a sequence-to-vector model on short inputs is a hard place to learn recurrence.

Why these numbers are bigger than part 3's, and why that is not a red flag

Part 3's convolution audit reported a worst relative error of 2.9 × 10⁻⁹, two orders of magnitude tighter than this page's. The difference is real and it is not a defect in either derivation. It is a property of the test, not of the code under test:

the error budget of a central differencetotal error ≈ (h²/6)·|L‴| + ε·|L| / h ──────── ──────── truncation cancellation shrinks with h GROWS as h shrinks and here is the difference between the two pagesA convolution layer's loss is a LOW-degree polynomial in its kernel entries — for ∂L/∂K the output is LINEAR in K — so |L‴| is tiny and the truncation term nearly vanishes. Both terms can be made small at once. A T-step recurrent loss is a composition of T tanh layers through the SAME matrix. Its third derivative with respect to a recurrent weight is large and grows with T. The truncation term dominates, and no choice of h removes it. Richardson extrapolation — combining h and h/2 to cancel the h² term — buys back roughly two decades, and the table above is the EXTRAPOLATED figure. Plain central differences at h = 10⁻⁵, which is what the live figure computes, give a worst relative error of 8.88e−06 and a worst normalised residual ‖Δg‖/‖g‖ of 2.74e−08.

The right reading of a gradient check is therefore not "is it below 10⁻⁹" but "is it at the level the finite-difference formula can achieve on this function". A genuine bug does not produce 10⁻⁶; it produces 10⁻¹ or 10⁰. There is no ambiguous middle ground in practice, which is why the check is worth running even when its own precision is modest.

A gradient check that passes for the wrong reason. Test with dₓ = dₕ = d_y and a symmetric W, and a transposed recurrent matrix, a swapped U and V, and several sign errors all pass. Use three different dimensions and an asymmetric matrix. Every configuration in the table above has dₓ ≠ dₕ ≠ d_y for exactly this reason, and the interactive figure below lets you turn the transpose bug on deliberately to see how loudly it fails.

14 What BPTT costs: time, memory, and the parallelism that is not there

Two of the three costs are ordinary. The third is the one that shaped the field: the forward pass is inherently serial, and no amount of hardware removes that.

ResourceCostCompared with a feedforward network of the same width
Arithmetic, forwardT · (dₓdₕ + dₕ² + dₕd_y) MACsIdentical to T dense layers. Nothing is saved and nothing extra is spent.
Arithmetic, backward≈ 2× the forwardPart 2's 1 : 2 : 3 rule holds exactly, for the same reason: every step is a dense layer, and a dense layer's backward pass is two matrix products of the same size as its forward one.
MemoryO(N · T · dₕ) statesThis is the binding constraint. Every hₜ must survive until the backward sweep reaches it. A gated cell stores its gates too, which multiplies the figure by five or six.

The serial chain, made concrete. Take a 200-step sequence and a hidden width of 1024. The forward pass is 200 dependent matrix products, each of which must finish before the next begins. On a device that can do the 1024 × 1024 product in a microsecond, the sequence still takes 200 microseconds of latency no matter how many such products the device could have done in parallel — and it will not be doing anything else while it waits. A transformer layer computes all 200 positions in one batched product. That is not a constant factor; it is a different shape of computation, and it is the reason the same accuracy became reachable in a fraction of the wall-clock time. 15 Truncated BPTT — and the two different algorithms that share the name

§14's memory table says the honest algorithm does not fit. The universal response is to cut the backward pass short after k steps. That is called truncated BPTT, and the phrase names two genuinely different algorithms with different costs and different biases. Conflating them is common and it matters, because one of them is much worse than the other and it is the one the frameworks implement.

form A
Per-loss truncation
Each loss Lₜ is back-propagated through the k most recent steps and stopped. Every loss gets the same k steps of history. Cost O(T·k), memory O(k). This is the form textbooks derive, and it is what the notation TBPTT(k) usually means in a paper.
form B
Chunked truncation
The sequence is cut into non-overlapping windows of k. Within a window, full BPTT. At a boundary the state is carried forward but the gradient is not — the state is "detached". Cost O(T), memory O(k). This is what every framework's tutorial does, because it is one line of code.
the asymmetry
Form B is unfair by position
A loss that lands at the end of a window sees k steps of history; a loss that lands at the start of one sees one. Averaged over a sequence a loss sees (k+1)/2 steps, not k, and the amount of history it gets depends on where the window boundaries happened to fall.

What truncation is, and is not, an approximation of

Both forms leave the forward pass exact. The model computes the same states and the same loss; the sequence is not shortened. What is approximated is only the gradient, and the approximation is not a small perturbation — it is the deliberate deletion of every term whose travel exceeds k. That means truncation makes a substantive modelling commitment: the model will not learn any dependency longer than k. It can still represent one, and if it somehow acquired one it would still use it, but gradient descent will never build one, because no gradient ever tells it to.

The claim to be careful with. "Truncation is fine, because the terms you dropped were vanishing anyway." That is circular, and it is exactly backwards for the model you are trying to train. If the recurrence is contracting then the dropped terms really are negligible and truncation costs nothing — but then the model has no long-range memory to learn with in the first place. If the recurrence is near the edge of stability, which is where a model that can remember has to sit (§18), the dropped terms are the same order as the kept ones and truncation removes real signal. The two cases are exactly the ones you cannot distinguish without measuring, so §16 measures.

Three things that table says, and one that surprises

1
The bias is not small at small k
At k = 1 the truncated gradient is 33 degrees away from the true one and misses more than half its magnitude. That is not a perturbation; it is a different descent direction, and a model trained on it converges somewhere else.
2
Form A converges fast, and the rate is ρᵏ
At ρ = 0.95 the error falls by roughly a factor of three per doubling of k and reaches 10⁻⁵ by k = 20. That is exactly the geometric decay of the dropped terms: what truncation deletes is what the recurrence was going to attenuate anyway, at this spectral radius.
3 — the surprise
Form B is not monotone in k
k = 5 is worse than k = 3 (0.409 against 0.336), and k = 20 is worse than k = 12. Increasing the window in the chunked form moves the boundaries as well as widening them, and a boundary that lands in a bad place costs more than the extra width buys. There is no guarantee that a larger window gives a better gradient.

That third point is worth being blunt about because it contradicts the intuition everyone brings: with chunked truncation, doubling the window is not guaranteed to improve anything. The bias does trend downwards, but it is noisy in k, and it stays an order of magnitude worse than form A at the same window for every k in the table. The reason is structural, not statistical: form A gives every loss k steps of history; form B gives the average loss (k+1)/2 and gives the unlucky ones one.

What to do about it, and what practitioners actually do. Two mitigations are cheap and both are standard. Random offsets: shift the window boundaries by a random amount each epoch, so that no step is permanently unlucky and the bias averages out over training rather than accumulating in one direction. Overlapping windows: run windows of length k that start every k/2 steps and only take the gradient from the second half — the first half is a warm-up whose only job is to give the second half a real state and a real gradient path. Overlapping doubles the arithmetic and recovers most of form A's accuracy. Neither is exotic and neither is in the tutorial version.
