17 The repeated Jacobian: why this is a matrix power, and why that changes everything

Everything from §09 hangs on one object, so isolate it. The factor that carries gradient from step t back to step k is a product of d = t − k one-step Jacobians, and every one of them contains the same matrix.

the one-step Jacobian, and the d-step oneone step ∂hⱼ/∂hⱼ₋₁ acts on a row covector as u ↦ u Dⱼ Wᵀ d steps Jₜ←ₖ = Dₜ Wᵀ · Dₜ₋₁ Wᵀ · ⋯ · Dₖ₊₁ Wᵀ d factors of Wᵀ with Dⱼ = diag(1 − hⱼ ⊙ hⱼ), every entry in (0, 1]. the simplification that makes the behaviour legibleRemove the non-linearity — set every Dⱼ = I — and the product collapses to Jₜ←ₖ = (Wᵀ)ᵈ A MATRIX POWER. Not a product of d different matrices: the same matrix, d times. Everything about its behaviour is decided by its eigenvalues, because if Wᵀ = Q Λ Q⁻¹ then (Wᵀ)ᵈ = Q Λᵈ Q⁻¹ and Λᵈ = diag( λ₁ᵈ, λ₂ᵈ, … ) Every eigenvalue is raised to the power d. Any |λ| < 1 decays to nothing; any |λ| > 1 grows without bound; and after enough steps the LARGEST one is the only one left, which is why a single number governs the outcome.

The eigenvalues are complex, and it matters

W is real and square, and nothing forces it to be symmetric. A real non-symmetric matrix generally has complex eigenvalues, in conjugate pairs. A complex λ = r eiφ raised to the power d gives rd ei d φ: the modulus behaves exactly as before, and the argument rotates. So a recurrent network's free dynamics can oscillate while decaying or growing — which is genuinely useful (a decaying oscillation is a clock, and a network that needs to count uses one) and is also the reason the naive tool for "the biggest eigenvalue", power iteration, fails here. Power iteration converges to a dominant real eigenvalue and stalls on a conjugate pair, so this page uses a shifted QR iteration with deflation, verified against a reference implementation to 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ relative on the spectral radius over twenty-four matrices including rotations, a defective Jordan block and a companion matrix.

the quantity that governs the rate, defined onceρ(W) = maxᵢ |λᵢ(W)| the SPECTRAL RADIUS (a complex modulus, not an absolute value) and the identity that the whole of §18 rests on: ρ(Wᵈ) = ρ(W)ᵈ EXACTLY, for every d and every square W checked here to a relative error of 1.4e−14 at d = 40.

Where the tanh gate goes

Putting Dⱼ back does not change the structure; it multiplies the effective factor by a number that is at most 1. The clean statement is a bound and a rate:

Three numbers, and they are not the same number. max |Wᵢⱼ| is the largest entry and it tells you nothing (§19). ‖W‖₂ is the largest singular value; it upper-bounds the one-step amplification and gives you the vanishing guarantee. ρ(W) is the largest eigenvalue modulus; it gives the asymptotic rate. They satisfy ρ(W) ≤ ‖W‖₂ always, with equality exactly when W is normal. The gap between them is transient behaviour — §19's second half — and it can be enormous.

19 "The weights are less than one, so the gradient vanishes" — the counterexamples

A very common statement of the vanishing-gradient criterion is that it happens "because the weight matrices are less than 1". Taken literally — every entry below one — that criterion is wrong in both directions, and the counterexamples are small enough to check by hand.

four matrices, and the three numbers that might be the criterionmatrix max|entry| ‖A‖₂ ρ(A) ‖A²⁰‖₂ 4×4, every entry 0.5 0.500 2.0000 2.000000 1.049e+06 4×4, every entry 0.3 0.300 1.2000 1.200000 3.834e+01 8×8, every entry 0.2 0.200 1.6000 1.600000 1.209e+04 2×2, 0.9 · rotation by 60° 0.779 0.9000 0.900000 1.216e−01 2×2, ⎡0.9 9⎤ (a shear) 9.000 9.0891 0.900000 2.432e+01 ⎣ 0 0.9⎦ read the first column against the lastRows 1–3: EVERY ENTRY is well below 1, and the 20-step Jacobian has a norm of a million. The gradient EXPLODES. The entries never mattered; the matrix is rank-one with all-positive entries, so its one non-zero eigenvalue is n·0.5 = 2 (or n·0.3 = 1.2, or n·0.2 = 1.6). Row 5: an ENTRY OF NINE, and ρ = 0.9, so the gradient eventually vanishes. The entries never mattered here either.

The arithmetic behind rows 1 to 3 is worth doing once because it shows how easily this happens. A matrix in which every entry equals a is a times the all-ones matrix, which has rank one and eigenvalues {n·a, 0, 0, …}. So an n × n matrix of entries all equal to a has ρ = n·a: at n = 128 — an unremarkable hidden width — entries of 0.01 already give ρ = 1.28. Sums of many small numbers are not small. That is the whole of it, and it is why the criterion has to be a spectral quantity: only a spectral quantity knows how many terms are being added and whether they reinforce.

The other half: ρ < 1 does not mean the gradient is small now

Row 5 is the more interesting case and it is the one that catches people who have learned the correct criterion. The shear [[0.9, 9], [0, 0.9]] has both eigenvalues equal to 0.9, so ρ = 0.9 < 1 and ‖Aᵈ‖ → 0 — eventually. Measured:

‖Aᵈ‖₂ for the shear, which has ρ = 0.9d = 1 9.089 d = 5 29.536 d = 9 34.872 ← the PEAK d = 10 34.871 d = 20 24.316 d = 40 5.912 d = 61 < 1 ← the first d at which it has finally contracted d = 80 0.175 A contraction with ρ = 0.9 that AMPLIFIES BY 35× for the first sixty steps. The asymptotic rate is not the behaviour on the sequences you actually train on. This is transient growth, it is entirely a non-normality effect, and a defective matrix like this one is the extreme case: ‖Aᵈ‖ ≈ 9d · 0.9ᵈ⁻¹, a polynomial times the geometric decay, peaking at d ≈ −1/ln 0.9 = 9.49.
So what is the criterion? There are three statements and they answer different questions.
  • Will it definitely vanish? Yes if ‖W‖₂ · γ < 1, where γ = sup|g′| is 1 for tanh and 0.25 for the logistic sigmoid. This is a sufficient condition and it uses the spectral norm. It is the only statement here that is a theorem about every sequence.
  • At what rate, over long distances? Like ρ(W)ᵈ, times a bounded non-normality constant (§18 measured it at 1.26 to 1.56 for a typical matrix), times the accumulated gate. This uses the spectral radius and it is an asymptotic statement.
  • What happens over the next twenty steps? Neither number tells you. Measure it. The gap between ρ and ‖W‖₂ is precisely how much transient behaviour the matrix can hide, and for a defective matrix that gap is unbounded.
And a fourth statement, which is the one worth remembering: ρ(W) ≤ ‖W‖₂ always, so if the norm is below one you are safe from explosion and doomed to forget, and if the radius is above one you will explode eventually. Everything interesting happens in the band between them.

20 Why this is a different problem from vanishing gradients in a deep feedforward net

Part 2 §09 owns the general product-of-Jacobians argument and shows the same exponential behaviour with depth. It is tempting to conclude that a 60-step RNN and a 60-layer MLP have the same problem. They do not, and the difference is worth stating precisely because it explains why residual connections fixed one and not the other.

What fixes itResidual connections. A block computing h + F(h) has Jacobian I + ∂F/∂h, and the product of L of those expands with leading term 1. See Residual & Skip Connections.Gates. The same idea — an additive path whose Jacobian is close to the identity — but the multiplier on that path must be learned and data-dependent, because the model has to be able to forget. §24 onwards.
The deepest version of the same point. A residual connection makes the identity path unconditional: it is always there, at full strength, and the block can only add to it. That is exactly right for a feedforward stack, where every layer should refine what the last one produced. It is exactly wrong for a sequence, where the model must sometimes clear the state — a new sentence begins, a new speaker starts, the subject of the sentence changes and the old one is no longer the thing being agreed with. A recurrent network with an unconditional identity path is a perfect integrator that can never reset. So the recurrent answer is an identity path with a learned, input-dependent multiplier in [0, 1] sitting on it, and that multiplier is the forget gate. The LSTM is a residual connection whose skip weight is itself a small network.

The consequence for what you can learn

There is a result here that is unpleasant and important, and it is not a statement about optimisation but about the model class. In order to store information robustly — so that a small perturbation of the state does not lose it — the recurrence must be contracting near the stored state, and a contraction has ρ < 1, which is exactly the vanishing condition. The two things you want, robust memory and a gradient that reaches back, pull in opposite directions for a vanilla cell. The gradient of a long-range interaction is therefore exponentially smaller than the gradient of a short-range one whenever the model can actually remember anything, and it is not that learning is impossible but that the long-range signal is buried under short-range noise. Experiments on this go back to the early 1990s and they reported the probability of successfully training a vanilla recurrent network by gradient descent falling to near zero for dependencies of only ten to twenty steps. §31 reproduces that failure and measures where it starts.

21 Gradient clipping: not a nicety in a recurrent network

Part 2 §36 derives clipping, shows that clipping by value rotates the update by tens of degrees while clipping by norm does not, and is not repeated. This section covers only the recurrent-specific fact: clipping was proposed for this setting, and here it is the difference between a model that trains and a model that does not.

clip by norm, the form to use — from part 2if ‖g‖ > v: g ← g · v / ‖g‖ One scaling factor for the WHOLE parameter vector, so the direction is preserved exactly. Clipping each entry to ±v instead changes the direction, and on a recurrent cliff — where one coordinate has become enormous and the rest have not — that is precisely the worst case.

Why the loss surface of a recurrent network has cliffs

This follows directly from §17 and it is the whole argument in three lines. The loss depends on W through Wᵈ. Differentiate a scalar version: ∂(wᵈ)/∂w = d·wd−1. At d = 100 and w = 1.05 that derivative is 100 × 1.05⁹⁹ ≈ 1.25 × 10⁴, and at w = 1.1 it is 100 × 1.1⁹⁹ ≈ 1.25 × 10⁶. A five per cent change in one weight has multiplied the gradient by a hundred. The surface is flat almost everywhere and then, in a narrow band, nearly vertical — and the band is exactly where the model becomes able to remember.

what a cliff does to one SGD step, without clippingSuppose ‖g‖ is 10 000 × its usual value for one batch, and the learning rate was tuned for the usual value. Δθ = −η g is 10 000 × the intended step. The parameters land somewhere unrelated to where they were. Every hour of prior training is gone in one update. With Adam the damage is smaller — the normalisation caps the step at roughly η per coordinate — but the second-moment estimate is now poisoned by one enormous sample and the effective learning rate stays wrong for O(1/(1−β₂)) ≈ 1000 steps. and what it does WITH clipping at v Δθ = −η v · g/‖g‖ a step of length exactly ηv, in the SAME direction the gradient asked for. The step still goes uphill on the cliff face — clipping does not know that — but it goes a bounded distance, so the next step can recover.
what to set
Clip by global norm, at 1.0
One threshold across every parameter in the model, applied after the gradients are averaged over the batch and before the optimiser sees them. 1.0 is close to universal; if the typical gradient norm of your model is far from that, set v to roughly the median observed norm rather than picking a number from a paper.
what it costs
A biased gradient, deliberately
Clipping the average of a batch is not the average of the clipped per-example gradients, so the minibatch estimate is no longer unbiased. Examples with large gradients — and their unlucky batch-mates — have their contribution shrunk. This is a heuristic bias that is known empirically to help, and it should be named as such rather than pretended away.
how to know it is working
Log the pre-clip norm
Not the post-clip norm, which is constant by construction and tells you nothing. The pre-clip norm's histogram is the diagnostic: a healthy run has a tight distribution with occasional spikes into the clip, and a run whose norm is above the threshold most of the time is telling you the threshold is wrong or the model is diverging.
The one that wastes a week. Clipping so aggressively that the clip fires on nearly every step. You have not stabilised training; you have replaced gradient descent with sign descent at a fixed step size. Every update now has length exactly ηv, the magnitude information is entirely discarded, and the model plateaus at a loss that no learning-rate schedule will move — because the schedule multiplies a step whose length is being reset every time. The tell is that the pre-clip norm's median sits above the threshold.

22 Why clipping cannot fix vanishing, and what the alternatives actually are

Clipping bounds a gradient from above. Vanishing is a gradient that is too small. There is no rescaling that fixes it, and the reason is not that nobody has tried the obvious thing — it is that the obvious thing does not mean anything.

why "just scale it up" is not a fixSuppose ∂L/∂W has a short-range part of magnitude 1 and a long-range part of magnitude 10⁻⁸. They are ADDED, not stored separately: g = gshort + glong ‖gshort‖ = 1, ‖glong‖ = 10⁻⁸ Multiplying g by 10⁸ multiplies BOTH by 10⁸. The ratio is unchanged, and the ratio is the entire problem: the long-range signal is not too small in absolute terms, it is too small RELATIVE to the short-range signal it is buried in. In float32, with a machine epsilon of about 1.2e−07, a long-range term below 10⁻⁷ of the short-range one does not even survive the addition — it is rounded away before any optimiser sees it.

So the fix has to change the architecture or the objective, not the update rule. Here is the honest survey, with what each one buys and what it costs.

A note on second-order methods, since they get suggested. The reasoning is that a second-order method divides the gradient by the curvature, and if both vanish at the same rate the ratio survives. That is true, and it was investigated seriously, with promising results. It was superseded — not by a better optimiser but by a better model. The pattern is worth naming because it recurs throughout the field: it is usually easier to design a model that is easy to optimise than an optimiser that can handle a difficult model. The LSTM is the canonical instance, and residual connections are the other one.

23 The remedies that are not gates: initialisation, leaky units, delays, reservoirs

The gates of §24 won, and the field mostly stopped talking about the alternatives. Three of them are still worth knowing: one because it is free and you should be doing it anyway, one because it is the direct ancestor of the gate, and one because it is a completely different and occasionally better answer.

Initialisation: where to put ρ(W) at step zero

Part 2's variance argument gives the general rule and it is not repeated. The recurrent-specific finding is narrower: initialise the recurrent matrix with a spectral radius slightly above one. The reasoning is §18's — below one and the model provably cannot remember, at exactly one you are on a boundary you will immediately drift off — and the reason "slightly above" is safe rather than catastrophic is that the tanh's own gate contributes a factor well below one at any realistic operating point (§17 measured 0.605 at a typical scale), so a spectral radius of, say, 1.2 gives a net per-step factor comfortably under one in the forward pass while leaving the backward pass much healthier than a contraction would. Values around 1.1 to 1.2, combined with a sparse rather than dense random matrix and with clipping switched on, are the standard recipe; an orthogonal initialisation, which sets every singular value and every eigenvalue modulus to exactly one, is the other common choice and is what most frameworks default to for the recurrent block.

the three initialisations, and what each fixesGaussian, sd = s/√dₕ ρ ≈ s, eigenvalues fill a disc of radius s. §18's table: at s = 1 you are on the edge with better than even odds of being over it. Orthogonal every |λ| = 1 AND every singular value = 1, so ρ = ‖W‖₂ = 1 and there is no non-normality gap and no transient at all. The cleanest start. Identity · α ρ = α and the recurrence is initially a pure leak. Pairs with a rectifier instead of tanh; needs clipping, because nothing bounds it.

Leaky units: the gate before it could be learned

a linear self-connection with a FIXED weighthₜ = α hₜ₋₁ + (1 − α) · tanh( hₜ₋₁W + xₜU + b ) α ∈ [0, 1) ∂hₜ/∂hₜ₋₁ = α I + (1 − α) DₜWᵀ ← an identity path with a GUARANTEED coefficient α so the gradient decays no faster than αᵈ, and α is YOUR choice: α = 0.9 a time constant of about 10 steps α = 0.99 about 100 steps α = 0.999 about 1000 steps This is a running average, and it is exactly the exponential moving average that part 2 built the momentum optimiser out of. Same object, different place in the model.

Two things to notice, because both come back in §24. First, this is a residual connection — αI plus something — and it works for the same reason. Second, α is a constant, which means the unit's time scale is fixed at design time and the unit can never clear its state: whatever it accumulated decays at a rate you chose and cannot be discarded on demand. A model built from leaky units with a spectrum of different α values covers a range of time scales and is genuinely useful, but the moment the task needs "remember this until the sentence ends, then forget it", the constant is the wrong shape of object. Replace the constant with a sigmoid of the input and you have the forget gate.

Skip connections through time, and removed connections

Add a direct edge from hₜ₋ₘ to hₜ for some fixed delay m. A dependency of length d now has a graph path of length d/m, so a gradient that decayed as ρᵈ decays as ρd/m along the new route. It is a real improvement and a bounded one: the exponent shrinks by a constant factor and stays exponential, and — worth noting — explosion is not helped at all, because the short path is still there and the long one is still exponential. The related idea of removing the length-one connections instead of adding long ones forces a group of units onto a coarse time scale rather than merely permitting it, and organising a network into several such groups, each updating at a different frequency, is a clean way to build a multi-scale model. All of these are structural commitments made in advance, which is their strength (no learning required) and their weakness (the scales are yours, not the data's).

Echo state networks: do not train the recurrence at all

The most radical answer, and the one that deserves more attention than it gets. Fix U and W at random — with ρ(W) set deliberately large, values around 3 are advocated, relying on the tanh's saturation to keep the forward dynamics bounded — and train only the read-out V. Because the state is then a fixed non-linear function of the input history, and the read-out is linear, the training problem is a linear regression: convex, solvable in closed form, no BPTT, no vanishing, no exploding, no clipping. The recurrent layer is a "reservoir" — a bank of temporal features — and the analogy to a kernel method is exact: an arbitrary-length history is mapped to a fixed vector and a linear predictor is fitted on top.

What reservoir computing gets right, and where it stops. It is the cleanest demonstration that the representation a recurrent network needs is often not very task-specific: a random high-dimensional non-linear echo of the input history contains enough for a linear read-out to do well on a great many time-series problems. Where it stops is where the features must be task-specific — language, in particular, where the useful summary of a prefix is exactly the thing that has to be learned. The idea also had a second life as an initialisation: set the weights the way a reservoir would and then train them anyway with BPTT, which is where §23's "ρ ≈ 1.2, sparse" recipe came from.
