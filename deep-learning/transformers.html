Transformers

A Transformer is a stack of identical blocks — an attention sub-layer and a position-wise feedforward network, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation — with token embeddings and positional information in at the bottom and a language-modelling head out at the top. It is built around the attention mechanism, which the attention page owns and which that page ends on two proofs rather than on a summary. The first: self-attention is exactly permutation-equivariant — shuffle the tokens and the output is the same set of vectors, shuffled the same way, measured at 5.6e−16 — so any model that is to know that "dog bites man" differs from "man bites dog" must have position injected, and the mechanism itself says nothing about how. The second: attention mixes tokens and does nothing else — with the weights held fixed the map from input features to output features is one matrix, at any depth — so a per-token non-linearity must be added, and the mechanism says nothing about that either. This page is what happens when you take both proofs seriously. The block has the shape it has because of them: a token-mixing sub-layer and a per-token sub-layer, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation, with position injected once at the bottom or at every attention.

The centre of the page is the architecture as a set of decisions, each derived and each measured: where position goes and what each choice can and cannot extrapolate to (sinusoidal, learned, RoPE, ALiBi, relative buckets, none — trained live and tested past the training length); the residual stream as a shared bus and why the order of the normalisation decides whether the model trains without warmup (the gradient scale through the stack, measured per layer); the position-wise FFN as two-thirds of the parameters and as a key–value memory; the three stacks; the embedding in and the unembedding out, with the √d_model scale and weight tying done right; exact parameter and compute accounting for five real configurations and the 6·N·D rule derived in MACs and in FLOPs; the training recipe as it is actually set — teacher forcing, label smoothing's loss floor, the warmup-then-decay schedule, β₂, clipping, bf16 against fp16; decoding; and a two-layer decoder trained live in the browser from the same primitives every figure uses.

Every number printed here was recomputed before it was printed. Every backward pass on the page — LayerNorm, RMSNorm, the ungated and gated FFN, attention with RoPE and per-head biases, the pre-LN and post-LN block, and the whole tiny model with every positional scheme — was audited against central differences, and §17 prints the worst relative error of each. 01 What a Transformer is, and why the block has the shape it has

One sentence first: a Transformer is a stack of identical blocks, each an attention sub-layer followed by a position-wise feedforward network, with every sub-layer wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation. Token embeddings, with positional information added to them or rotated into the attention, go in at the bottom; a normalisation and one matrix onto the vocabulary — the language-modelling head — come out at the top; nothing in between changes shape, every block mapping (n × d) to (n × d). Whether the attention is unmasked, causally masked, or followed by a second attention that reads another stack decides the three variants — encoder-only, decoder-only and encoder–decoder — and nothing else about the block differs between them.

the whole model, in the row convention of this page — one TOKEN per ROWtokens t₁ … tₙ integers in [0, V) embed X⁰ = √d · E[t] (+ P) (n × d), one ROW per token; E (V × d); P (n × d) is position if it is ADDED here (§04–§05), absent if it is rotated in inside Attn (§06) or added to the scores (§08) block ℓ = 1 … L, identical in shape, separate in weights, pre-norm form: X₁ = X + Attn( N₁(X) ) token mixing — the ONLY place rows talk to each other Y = X₁ + FFN( N₂(X₁) ) per-token computation — the same MLP on every row Xˡ = Y (n × d) in, (n × d) out; nothing changes shape head logits = N_f(Xᴸ) · W_out (n × V), W_out (d × V), often W_out = Eᵀ (§21) N = LayerNorm or RMSNorm, applied to each ROW independently FFN = act(X·W₁ + b₁)·W₂ + b₂ W₁ (d × d_ff), W₂ (d_ff × d) Attn = multi-head self-attention, and the mask decides the family (§18–§19): encoder-only no mask — every row reads every row decoder-only causal mask — row i reads rows 0 … i encoder–decoder causal self-attention, then a second attention whose keys and values are the encoder stack's output

That is the whole architecture, and every section from §02 on is one of its decisions taken apart and measured. The block has exactly these two sub-layers, and not one, because of two theorems about the mechanism — both handed over by the attention page rather than resolved there. Each one forces a component. Put the two components together with the only wiring that lets them stack, and the Transformer block is what you get.

Why the block has two sub-layers: the two hand-offs from the attention page

Restate the two results exactly as the attention page's permutation-equivariance section and its token-mixing section leave them, because the whole architecture is a response to them.

hand-off 1 — attention is permutation-equivariant, quotedfor any (n × n) permutation matrix Π, Attention(Π·X) = Π·Attention(X) (Π, not P — P is the positional matrix everywhere on this page) so a stack of attention layers, each followed by any PER-ROW operation (a norm, an FFN, an output head), is a SET function of its tokens: it cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog" at any depth. Measured residual on the attention page's figure: 5.6e−16 — round-off. Position is not "weak" in the mechanism; it is ABSENT, and must be injected. hand-off 2 — attention mixes tokens and does nothing to features, quotedY = ∑ⱼ Aⱼ · X · (W_Vʲ W_Oʲ) with the Aⱼ held fixed, X ↦ Y is LINEAR with one token (n = 1) there is nothing to mix, A = [1], and L stacked attention layers are ONE matrix: Y = x · ∏ₗ (W_Vˡ W_Oˡ). No depth of attention creates a per-token non-linearity. One must be added.

From the two hand-offs to the block

Hand-off 2 says a block needs a second sub-layer that acts on each token's feature vector alone — a small MLP applied to every row, the same weights at every position, which the feedforward page already owns and which §14 names the position-wise feedforward network. Hand-off 1 says something about position has to enter, and in every scheme in wide use it enters at one of three places: added to the input rows before the first block, rotated into the queries and keys inside every attention, or added to the score matrix as a distance-dependent bias. §03 lays the three out and §04–§10 take each apart. What neither hand-off says, and what turns two sub-layers into a trainable stack of dozens of them, is the wiring: every sub-layer adds its output to its input rather than replacing it, and every sub-layer's input is normalised first. The residual makes depth trainable (§11); the normalisation's position decides whether the model needs warmup (§12). That is the whole block, and it has not changed since 2017 except in the order of those two operations, in the choice of norm, activation and positional scheme, and in the attention variant inside the first sub-layer (grouped-query and multi-latent heads, both on the attention page).

the block, pre-norm form, in the row convention of the attention page — one TOKEN per ROWinput X (n × d) d = d_model, one row per token X₁ = X + Attn( N₁(X) ) token mixing: hand-off 1's object, plus position Y = X₁ + FFN( N₂(X₁) ) per-token computation: hand-off 2's repair N = LayerNorm or RMSNorm, applied to each ROW independently Attn = multi-head self-attention, exactly the attention page's DL.mhaForward, causal mask or not FFN = act(X·W₁ + b₁)·W₂ + b₂ W₁ (d × d_ff), W₂ (d_ff × d) every operation is either PER ROW (N, FFN) or ACROSS ROWS (Attn); nothing is both, and that separation is the architecture's one real idea.

Every quantity on this page obeys the attention page's layout law and nothing here re-declares it: a sequence is X (n × d) with one token per row, a weight is (d_in × d_out) and sits on the right, heads split the last axis into adjacent blocks, masks are additive and −∞. 02 The block, sub-layer by sub-layer, with a real matrix pushed through it

§01 wrote the block in two lines. This section pushes an actual (n × d) matrix through those two lines, prints what every intermediate looks like, and names the one template every sub-layer obeys — because once the template is visible, pre-norm against post-norm, LayerNorm against RMSNorm and gated against ungated FFNs are each a one-symbol edit.

The sub-layer template

Both halves of the block are instances of one pattern. A sub-layer is a function f from (n × d) to (n × d); it is wrapped as x ↦ x + f(N(x)) in the pre-norm form or as x ↦ N(x + f(x)) in the post-norm form. The residual addition is what lets f be small — at initialisation f(N(x)) has a norm comparable to x's or smaller, so the block starts close to the identity and depth costs nothing to optimise through (§11). The norm is what keeps the input to f at a fixed scale whatever has been accumulated on the stream below it. Neither operation has anything to do with attention; they are the same two operations a deep residual CNN uses, in the same roles.

the two wirings, side by side — the ONLY difference between them is where N sitsPRE-NORM (the form nearly every large model since 2019 uses; "pre-LN" when the norm is LayerNorm) X₁ = X + Attn(N₁(X)) Y = X₁ + FFN(N₂(X₁)) the residual path X → X₁ → Y is an UNBROKEN sum: Y = X + Attn(·) + FFN(·) POST-NORM (the 2017 form; "post-LN") X₁ = N₁(X + Attn(X)) Y = N₂(X₁ + FFN(X₁)) the residual path passes THROUGH a norm twice per block: the stream is re-scaled after every sub-layer, and the identity path is not an identity a pre-norm stack of L blocks needs ONE more norm at the very top, N_f, before the output head — because the last thing written to the stream was never normalised. A post-norm stack does not.

What the trace shows

The figure below builds one block from DL.blockInit — the attention page's attention block plus this page's FFN and norms — and pushes a seeded random X through it, recording the row norms and one token's feature vector at every stage. Three things are visible at once. First, in pre-norm wiring the norm of the stream grows through the block: ‖Y‖ > ‖X₁‖ > ‖X‖ for a typical row, because two things were added and nothing rescaled the sum; §11 measures the growth across a whole stack. Second, in post-norm wiring the output rows all have the same norm — √d at unit γ — whatever the input was; the wiring erases the stream's scale twice per block. Third, and easy to miss, the FFN's contribution is larger than attention's at initialisation for the default weight scale, which is a consequence of its width: d_ff = 4d hidden units each contributing to every output coordinate. Both wirings compute a legitimate function; what differs is what the gradient sees on the way back, and that is §12.

The description to avoid. "The residual connection passes the input around the sub-layer so the network can skip it if it wants." It is an addition, not a bypass, and the sub-layer cannot be skipped. Y = X + f(N(X)) always includes f's output; what the residual guarantees is that the identity is always present in the sum, so that ∂Y/∂X = I + ∂f/∂X has a term of norm 1 no matter how small ∂f/∂X is. That is a statement about the gradient, and §11 measures exactly that Jacobian. A gate that could set f's contribution to zero is a different design — a highway layer — and the Transformer block does not have one.

03 Positional encoding as an architectural choice: the three places position can enter

Hand-off 1 said position must be injected. There are three places in the block where a signal can be injected without breaking the per-row / across-row separation of §01, and every positional scheme in wide use is one of the three. Name the three first; then each scheme is a short derivation. (Vocabulary: a positional encoding is a fixed formula — sinusoids, a rotation, a bias — and a positional embedding is a learned table; the two words are used interchangeably in practice, and this page keeps the distinction only where it matters.)

The three injection points

Trace the data flow of §01 and ask where a quantity that depends on the position index i (or on the pair (i, j)) can be introduced. The rows of X are the only per-token quantity before attention; the projected Q and K are the only per-token quantities that meet each other; and the score matrix S is the only per-pair quantity in the block. Those are the three doors. (A fourth exists — adding a position-dependent term to the values, so that what is retrieved also depends on the offset — and some early relative-position schemes used it; the schemes that survived did not, and it is not opened here.)

04 The sinusoidal table, derived from one requirement, and its relative-offset property measured

The sinusoidal table gives position p a row of sines and cosines, (sin p·ωᵢ, cos p·ωᵢ) pair by pair, at wavelengths spaced geometrically from 2π to 2π·base; every row is distinct, and moving k positions is a fixed rotation of the row that does not depend on p. That second fact is the one that matters. The table is usually presented as a formula to be accepted; it is better presented as the solution to a requirement — that a fixed linear map should turn "position p" into "position p + k", for every p, so that a head can learn to look a fixed distance away without knowing where it is.

The requirement, and why it forces rotations

An attention head scores a query row against a key row with a bilinear form, score(p, p') = P[p]·(W_Q W_Kᵀ)·P[p']ᵀ when the rows carry only position. For the head to prefer "the token k places from me" (k negative for "before") it needs the score to peak at p' = p + k for every p, with the same W_Q W_Kᵀ. The cleanest way to guarantee that is to ask for a matrix M_k, depending on k alone, with P[p + k] = P[p]·M_k: then setting W_Q W_Kᵀ = M_k makes the score P[p+k]·P[p']ᵀ, which is largest when p' = p + k if the rows are most similar to themselves. What functions of p have that property — a shift in the argument is a fixed linear map on the value? Continuous solutions of f(p + k) = f(p)·M_k are matrix exponentials, f(p) = f(0)·exp(p·A). Among them, the ones that stay bounded at every position — the only usable coordinates — are the rotations, A skew-symmetric, which are sinusoid pairs; the unbounded ones (growing exponentials, and polynomial families such as f(p) = (1, p) with M_k = [[1, k], [0, 1]]) satisfy the identity too and are useless. A sine needs its cosine alongside for the map to be linear (a shift of sin alone is not a multiple of sin). So each coordinate pair is (sin ωp, cos ωp), and the shift is a 2-D rotation.

What the table learns, and what it cannot

Nothing in the scheme says that P[i] and P[i + 1] should be similar; that has to be learned from the fact that neighbouring positions play similar roles. In practice the learned Gram matrix P·Pᵀ of a well-trained model is banded — rows close in position are close in the embedding — with additional structure the sinusoids do not have, such as a distinctive first row and a periodicity that matches the corpus's typical sentence length. On a ten-token task there is not much for the table to learn, and that is the honest picture: the learned table is exactly as structured as the data forced it to be, no more. The sinusoidal Gram is banded before any training happens; the learned one becomes whatever separates the positions the task needed separated.

The wall is the property that matters architecturally. A learned table is indexed, not evaluated: there is no rule producing P[n_max], so a sequence of length n_max + 1 raises an index error. Every other scheme on this page at least runs past the training length (§09 measures how well). This is why context extension for models with learned tables means retraining the table, while for RoPE models it means rescaling the angles. The trade the scheme makes is flexibility for structure: given enough data the table can represent any position-dependent prior, including ones no fixed formula would guess; given little data it represents noise, which is why the usual reasoning is that a learned table earns its flexibility only on a large corpus. (The work that introduced the sinusoids compared the two on translation and found them "nearly identical", choosing the formula for its chance of extrapolating; §09 measures how far that chance goes.) The length of the table, n_max, is what a model card calls its context window — for a learned table it is a hard limit, for the other schemes a training length.

What RoPE changes relative to the input table, and what it does not

Compare with §04. There, relative offset was available to a head that learned a rotation for W_Q W_Kᵀ, and the signal had to survive the residual stream from the bottom of the stack. Why a model needs both regimes

A pair that turns many times over the context is a fine ruler: the score's dependence on cos((m − m′)θᵢ) distinguishes offset 3 from offset 4 sharply, and it is periodic — offset 3 and offset 3 + λᵢ look the same to that pair, which is harmless because the other pairs disambiguate. A pair whose angle never wraps inside the context (λᵢ > n) is a coarse ruler: the pair (cos, sin) of its angle is a one-to-one function of the offset, so "far" and "near" are never confused, and if it has turned less than half a turn (λᵢ > 2n) the cosine alone is monotone in the offset. And because the offset to the first token is the absolute position (§06), a head that reads such a pair through the first token gets a monotone function of m — the nearest thing to an absolute coordinate a RoPE model has. Both are needed: one for local syntax, one for "this is the start of the document". When the context is extended to a length at which every pair has wrapped, the coarse rulers are gone and the model has to be told how to rescale them; that is exactly the subject of Context-Length Extension, which owns position interpolation and its NTK-aware and YaRN refinements and is not repeated here.

The base is the knob that trades the two. Raising it stretches every wavelength except the first (λ₀ = 2π always), so more pairs stay unwrapped over a given context: the move from 10⁴ to 5·10⁵ in the newer long-context decoders amounts to a decision to keep about a quarter of the pairs (15 of 64) as coarse rulers at 128k tokens instead of none. The price is resolution in the middle of the spectrum — pairs that used to complete a few turns over 4k tokens now complete a fraction of one, and the head has fewer sharp rulers at intermediate distances. There is no free choice here; the figure shows the count on each side of the line for any triple, and the count is the design decision.

What a monotone bias can and cannot address

The ALiBi bias is monotone in distance, and that has a consequence §09 measures. A head whose content scores carry no positional information — which is every first-layer head, since nothing positional was added to X; deeper layers can carry whatever earlier heads derived (§10) — sees a score profile that is the content term plus a straight line falling with distance. Its softmax puts the most weight on the nearest key whose content matches, and it has no way to prefer "exactly two back" over "one back" unless content distinguishes them. "The previous token" is reachable by a trick — the head can learn to make a token's key repel its own query, so that the diagonal is suppressed and the nearest remaining key wins — but "two back" is not, within one head, because the token one back is a random symbol with no reason to be suppressed. ALiBi is therefore a recency prior, not a positional encoding in the sense of §04 or §06: it tells each head how quickly to forget, and leaves the model to derive anything sharper from the composition of heads with different slopes across layers. That is also why it extrapolates so well — nothing it uses at distance 5 000 is new to it, the penalty is simply larger — and why a model that needs precise long-range offsets does not use it.

Learned buckets are the other extreme: every offset up to ±7 has its own scalar per head, so "two back" is one parameter. The cost is that the bias is a lookup, not a formula: it cannot be extended past the last bucket except by sharing the last value, and it is added to every layer's scores, which for a long sequence means materialising an (n × n) bias per head unless the kernel fuses it. Both schemes have a property the door-1 schemes lack and RoPE shares: the score bias is the same at every depth, because it is recomputed from (i, j) in every layer rather than read off a residual stream that has been written to thirty times.

Reading the measurement

  • Learned absolute stops. Not "degrades" — the evaluation past 10 raises an error, and the line ends. This is the property, not a limitation of the toy.
  • Sinusoidal runs and fails. It is the best or near-best in-distribution and the worst out of it, because the head's W_Q W_Kᵀ was fitted to the geometry of ten rows and the eleventh row's slow coordinates are new. The same model at 64 tokens has lost about two-thirds of its accuracy and is within a few points of the model with no positional encoding at all.
  • RoPE degrades gracefully. The offset property guarantees the form of the score at any length; what changes is that the slow pairs are evaluated at offsets — hence angles — never seen, and the head's projections were not fitted for them. (At dₖ = 4 there are two pairs, with wavelengths 6.3 and 628 positions; the first wrapped within the training length of 10 and is safe, the second is the one that meets new angles.) The curve falls, but from a high start and slowly — this is the curve that position interpolation is designed to flatten, and Context-Length Extension owns that.
  • ALiBi is flat and lower. Flat because nothing new happens at a new length; lower because the "previous token" is reachable only through the self-suppression trick of §08, which this budget learns partially. Switch the task to "two back" and ALiBi falls at the training length to the level of the model with no positional encoding (about 40% against 100% for the addressable schemes — above the 12.5% chance level only because a recency window over the last few tokens lets it guess among them): a monotone recency bias cannot address an exact offset, and no amount of training fixes what the function class cannot represent.
  • None is weak but not at chance. The causal mask alone lets a two-layer model derive a rough position (§10); it is enough to beat chance and not enough to solve the task, and it does not transfer.
The claim to be careful with. "ALiBi extrapolates better than RoPE, so it is the better positional encoding." It extrapolates better because it encodes less. The measurement above is the whole argument: on the task ALiBi can represent it is flat past the training length, and on the task it cannot represent it is little better than having no positional encoding at all (40% against 34% at the training length, 34% against 21% at 64 tokens, at the default seed). A recency prior is a good tool when perplexity on ordinary text is the objective and long inputs are expected; it is the wrong tool when a head has to land on an exact distant offset. The large-scale results that motivated it were perplexity results, and at scale the composition of many heads across layers recovers more than a single head can (§08).

11 The residual stream: a shared bus every sub-layer reads from and writes to

Unroll a pre-norm stack and the output is the input plus a sum of 2L writes. Nothing is ever overwritten. That one identity explains why depth is cheap to train, why the stream's norm grows, why a signal added at the bottom is still readable at the top — and why the last norm before the output head is not optional.

Three measurable consequences

The norm grows. Writes at initialisation are roughly uncorrelated with the stream and with each other, so ‖xᴸ‖² ≈ ‖x⁰‖² + ∑ ‖writes‖² and the norm grows like a power of L near ½ when the writes are of comparable size. The figure measures the root-mean-square of a token's stream after each of L random pre-norm blocks, fits the exponent (about 0.4 at the defaults — the writes are not perfectly uncorrelated), and it rises steadily; the same measurement on a post-norm stack is flat at √d, because the norm sits on the residual path and resets it. The input's share shrinks. The cosine between xᴸ and x⁰ falls with depth even though x⁰ is still exactly present in the sum — its proportion of the stream is what falls, which is the precise sense in which a door-1 positional signal (§03) "fades": nothing removed it, but the norms it must be read against have grown. The block is near-identity. The relative size of (∂Y/∂X − I)ᵀ·g for a random g is below one — about a half — at initialisation for a pre-norm block and stays there with depth, which is the constant-path statement above made numerical; a post-norm block's Jacobian has no identity term because the residual passes through the norm, and its deviation is of order one.

The stream through L blocks: its norm, its cosine with the input, and the block Jacobian's distance from the identity pre-norm grows like √L and stays near-identity; post-norm is pinned at √d and is not near-identity — the weight-scale slider is the initialisation, and scaling it up is the fastest way to see the near-identity property fail
Why the pre-norm stack ends in a norm. Read the unrolled sum once more: the last thing that happened to xᴸ was an addition, and the output head reads it directly. Without a final norm, the logits scale with ‖xᴸ‖, which grows with depth and drifts during training; the softmax's temperature would be an accident of the stack's depth. The final norm pins it. A post-norm stack already ends in N₂, so it needs nothing. This is the origin of the pattern "pre-norm blocks + one ln_f" in nearly every decoder built since 2019, and forgetting it is one of §35's bugs that does not crash. The general residual story — bottleneck blocks, pre-activation ordering, stochastic depth — is Residual / Skip Connections'; this section owns only what the transformer's stream does.

Why RMSNorm won the argument in decoders

The mean subtraction in LayerNorm removes one direction — the all-ones direction — from every row. A single causal attention layer lets position i read 0 … i. The set of positions that can influence position i is therefore {0, …, i} at any depth — the figure computes that reachability set explicitly by propagating a Boolean matrix through L layers and shows it is the lower triangle at every depth. The same computation for the encoder gives the full square after one layer; there is nothing left to reach after that, which is why encoder depth is about refining representations, not widening context. For the prefix-LM the reachable set of a suffix position is the whole prefix plus its own causal past, and of a prefix position the whole prefix — the figure draws that too. Padding is orthogonal to all three: a padded key is masked out (−∞ on its column) in every stack, and as the attention page's masking section explained, never on its row.

What the second stack buys, and what it costs

Against a decoder-only model given the source as a prefix, the encoder–decoder has three real differences. First, the source is read bidirectionally — every source position's representation sees the whole source — while a causal prefix reads it left to right; for inputs whose meaning depends on later parts (most sentences) this is a genuine representational advantage, and it is the usual explanation for why the family has stayed competitive on translation and summarisation at equal parameter count. Second, the source is encoded once and its keys and values cached, so a long input costs its encoding once and then O(n_src) per output token, the same as a KV cache; a decoder-only model pays the same after the prefill, so this is a wash. Third, the source and target live in separate streams: the decoder's residual stream carries only the target, and the source enters only through cross-attention, which means the decoder's depth is spent on generation and none of its positions is "wasted" on re-representing the input. The costs are the third sub-layer's parameters, a second set of hyper-parameters (encoder depth against decoder depth), and — arguably the one that mattered most at scale — a training objective that supervises only the target positions, against a decoder-only objective that supervises every position of every document. §34 puts the three families side by side with the counts.

20 Embedding in: the token table, the √d_model scale, and what it is for

The first thing the model does is look a row up in a table, E[token], multiply it by √d_model, and add the position vector P[i]. It is the one detail of the input that is routinely copied without being understood, and this section says what it is for.

The original recipe tied the learning rate to the width and the step count in one formula. Dropout: where, and how much

The 2017 recipe applied dropout of 0.1 in three places: to the output of every sub-layer before it is added to the residual stream (so the stream itself is never dropped), to the attention weights after the softmax (which zeros individual entries of A, so a row no longer sums to one; the survivors are rescaled by 1/(1 − p) like any dropout and nothing renormalises the row — the paper's text names the first and third placements, its reference implementation had the second as well), and to the sum of embedding and position before the first block. Encoders of the 2018 generation kept all three at 0.1. Most large decoders since 2020 use none (a few 2022 families kept 0.1): at their data scale each token is seen about once and there is little to overfit to, and the regularisation costs real capacity. The rule is therefore a function of the data-to-parameter ratio rather than of the architecture, and the training page owns the mechanism. What the architecture fixes is only the placement: on the sub-layer output, never on the residual path, and never after the final norm.

29 Decoding (1): what temperature, top-k and top-p do to one distribution

The model produces a distribution over the vocabulary at every step; decoding is the rule that turns it into a token. Greedy takes the mode, sampling draws from it, and the three knobs in every generation API are three ways of reshaping the distribution before drawing. Each has a closed form, and each does something the others cannot.

the four rules, on one logit vector zgreedy t = argmaxⱼ zⱼ deterministic; the mode, every step temperature p_T = softmax(z / T) T → 0: greedy; T = 1: the model; T → ∞: uniform changes every probability; log-odds between two tokens scale by 1/T top-k keep the k largest pⱼ, renormalise a HARD cap on the candidate set, blind to how peaked p is top-p keep the smallest set whose mass ≥ p, renormalise ADAPTIVE: one token when the model (nucleus) is sure, many when it is not the order that frameworks apply them: temperature → top-k → top-p → sample two numbers that summarise the reshaped distributionentropy H(p) = −∑ pⱼ log pⱼ (nats) and perplexity e^H = the effective number of candidates temperature moves H continuously; top-k and top-p move it by removing mass from the tail, and the figure prints H and e^H for every setting so that "T = 0.7" and "top-p = 0.9" can be compared on one scale.

Two facts that the closed forms make clear. First, temperature is not truncation: at T = 0.7 the tail is still there, only lighter, and a long generation will eventually draw from it; top-p removes it. Second, top-k is the wrong shape for a language model: the number of plausible next tokens varies by orders of magnitude between positions (one after "the United States of", hundreds after "I think"), and a fixed k is too many in the first case and too few in the second. Nucleus sampling was proposed for exactly this reason. In practice the two are combined — a top-k of 50 as a safety cap and a top-p of 0.9 to 0.95 doing the work — with a temperature slightly below one. Repetition penalties, length normalisation and the beam search of §30 are further rules on top; the KV cache that makes any of this affordable is the attention page's KV-cache section's.

The objective's efficiency: a decoder gets a loss from every position of every document, an encoder from the fraction it masked, an encoder–decoder from the target side only — a 2–7× difference in supervised positions per token processed, and one of the most-cited reasons the decoder-only stack won at scale. 35 What the architecture cannot do, and the bugs that do not crash — measured

Two lists, deliberately separate. The first is what the architecture cannot do by construction, each with the page that owns the remedy. The second is the set of implementation errors that produce a running, training, plausible-looking model that is not the model you meant — and for four of them a measurement that catches the error in one line.

What it cannot do

cost
Quadratic attention
Every layer forms an (n × n) matrix per head; the attention page's cost sections count it and its FlashAttention section shows the memory traffic can be fixed without the arithmetic changing. At the trained context it is a minority of the compute (§23); at 8× the context it is two-fifths of the total for a 7B-class model and the majority for a 125M-class one.
state
No recurrence, no compressed state
The "state" at inference is the whole KV cache, 2·L·d per token, growing without bound. A recurrent model carries a fixed dₕ; the RNN page showed what that costs in fidelity. The architectures that bring a fixed state back with a better recurrence are State Space Models.
length
A trained context
Every positional scheme degrades past the length it was trained at (§09) and one cannot run at all (§05). Extending it is a procedure, not a property — Context-Length Extension.
compute
Fixed compute per token
Every token gets exactly L blocks whether it needs them or not: the same arithmetic for "the" and for the answer to a puzzle. Conditional compute inside the block is Mixture of Experts; more compute per answer at inference is Reasoning in LLMs.
precision
Exact algorithms
A fixed-depth stack computes a fixed circuit class; tasks that need unbounded serial steps (long arithmetic, deep parity) are not in it at any width without scratch tokens. The empirical side of this is Large Language Models'.
data
A weak prior
The attention page's section on the input-computed weight matrix: the mixing matrix has no locality, no translation equivariance, no built-in structure. That is why the architecture needs the data volume it needs, and why a CNN still wins small-data vision (Vision Transformers).

The bugs that do not crash

wiring
Pre-norm stack, no final norm
The logits scale with ‖xᴸ‖, which grows with depth (§11). The figure measures the logit standard deviation against L with and without ln_f. §11.
wiring
Wiring and schedule mismatched
Post-norm at a pre-norm's peak rate stalls; pre-norm at a post-norm's rate converges slowly. Nothing throws. §12.
embedding
The √d scale forgotten (or doubled)
With a N(0, 1/d) table and no factor the position term is √(d/2)× the token term and the first layer's scores ignore the tokens (§20, correlation ≈ 1); with a N(0, 0.02²) table the factor is unnecessary and harmless. Know which table you have. §20.
head
Tied weights with a unit-variance table
Logits √d too large at initialisation; the loss starts in the hundreds instead of at ln V and the first steps are spent undoing it. §21 measures it.
mask
Padding mask applied after the softmax
Rows stop summing to one, the output is rescaled by the unmasked mass — by a different factor per row. The figure measures the row sums. The attention page's masking section; figure below.
mask
Padding mask on the query axis
Creates all-masked rows; with −∞ that is a NaN (loud); with −1e9 it is a uniform row attending to everything (silent). The figure shows both. The attention page's −∞-against-a-large-negative section.
position
RoPE applied to V
The output acquires absolute position; a common shift of every position changes the output by order one instead of round-off. The figure measures the shift residual both ways. §06.
position
RoPE pairing convention swapped
Adjacent against rotate-half: both have the offset property, both run, and a checkpoint trained under one is a different model under the other. §06.
FFN
Keys and values transposed in the memory reading
Reading the rows of W₁ as keys in the row convention. Names the wrong vectors; nothing computes differently. §16.
recipe
Weight decay on norms and biases
The gains are pulled to zero every step; the model fights its own regulariser. §27.
recipe
β₂ = 0.999 with spiky gradients
Overshoot at the spike and a starved step for the next thousand. §27 measures it.
recipe
Reporting the smoothed loss as a perplexity
A floor of 1.41 nats at ε = 0.1, V = 50k, is not a perplexity of 4.1; the unsmoothed loss is the comparable number. §25.
units
MACs reported as FLOPs
Every estimate off by exactly two. §23.

36 What to remember, and exactly where the remaining parts begin

The architecture, in the order it was built

  • The block is a response to two theorems. Attention is permutation-equivariant, so position must be injected; attention mixes tokens and does nothing to features, so a per-token non-linearity must be added (§01). A token-mixing sub-layer, a per-token sub-layer, a residual around each, a norm before each: that is all of it (§02).
  • Position enters through one of three doors — added to the input, rotated into Q and K, or added to the scores (§03). The sinusoidal table's shift is a linear map (§04, measured at 2e−13); the learned table cannot be indexed past its length (§05); RoPE makes the score depend on the offset alone (§06, 5e−15) with a spectrum of clocks the base controls (§07); ALiBi is a recency prior per head (§08) and cannot address an exact offset (§09); a causal mask alone leaks 1/(i+1) (§10).
  • Measured past the training length: RoPE degrades gently, ALiBi barely, sinusoidal fast, learned not at all — it stops (§09).
  • The residual stream is a bus. Every sub-layer adds; nothing overwrites; the norm grows like L^0.4; a pre-norm block's Jacobian is near the identity and a post-norm block's is not (§11). Post-norm gives every layer roughly the same gradient scale and a lower learning-rate ceiling; pre-norm's top-layer gradient shrinks as L^−0.35 and tolerates the larger rate (§12). RMSNorm is LayerNorm without the mean, and the mean does not appear to be load-bearing (§13).
  • The FFN is two-thirds of the block at ratio 4 and more under grouped-query attention (§14); gated units hold the count at ⅔ width and the rounding rule produces 11 008, 14 336, 28 672 (§15); the FFN is a key–value memory whose keys are the columns of W₁ (§16). Every backward pass is audited to 3e−7 or better (§17).
  • The stacks differ by a mask (§18), plus a third sub-layer for the encoder–decoder (§19). The embedding's √d is a scale-matching device whose necessity depends on the table's initialisation (§20); the head is a third of a small model and 1% of a large one, and tying it makes the untrained model predict its own input (§21).
  • Counting: six places for parameters, five closed forms (§22); 6·N·D is 3·N·D MACs plus an attention term of n/12d relative size (§23).
  • The recipe: teacher forcing through the mask gives every position's loss in one pass (§24); label smoothing sets a floor of 1.41 nats and a logit gap of 13.0 (§25); warmup then decay, with the peak scaling as (d·warmup)^−½ (§26); β₂ = 0.95 because 0.999 overshoots a spike 3× and starves the next thousand steps (§27); bf16 because gradients underflow fp16 without a loss scale (§28).
  • Decoding reshapes one distribution (§29) or searches over sequences (§30); on a solved task all rules agree. Depth against width at fixed budget barely matters within 40 < d/L < 130, and the toy's result is the depth floor, not the plateau (§31). The model trained live is 4 672 parameters and finds the copy circuit at offset −4 (§32); the logit lens reads it arriving after block 1 (§33). Three families, one budget, four numbers each (§34); six things it cannot do and thirteen bugs that do not crash (§35).
