The Original Transformer (2017)

The paper that proposed an encoder–decoder built from attention alone, with no recurrence and no convolution. The mechanics it introduced are taken apart elsewhere in these notes; this page reads the paper as a paper: the argument it made for self-attention, the exact settings it trained, the translation results it reported and what they cost, the ablations that justify each design choice, and the parsing experiment that showed the architecture was not specific to translation.

01 The setting: what the paper was replacing

By 2017 the best translation systems were recurrent encoder–decoders with attention, or convolutional sequence models. Recurrence is inherently sequential along the sequence — step t needs the hidden state from step t − 1 — so training cannot be parallelised within an example, and long sentences hit memory limits on batching. The paper's proposal: keep the encoder–decoder shape and the attention, drop the recurrence entirely.

  • The recurrent baseline. An encoder RNN reads the source, a decoder RNN writes the target, and an attention mechanism lets the decoder look back at every encoder state. Two pages here cover that lineage: Learning to Align & Translate (where attention for translation began) and RNNs & LSTMs.
  • The convolutional alternative. Convolutional sequence models compute all positions in parallel, but the number of operations needed to relate two distant positions grows with their distance — linearly for contiguous kernels, logarithmically for dilated ones.
  • The paper's claim. Self-attention relates every pair of positions in a constant number of sequential steps, so a model built only from attention trains with more parallelism and has shorter paths between any two positions. It reports a new best BLEU on two WMT'14 translation tasks at a fraction of the training cost of earlier systems (§06).

02 Why self-attention: the three-criterion argument (Table 1)

The paper compares layer types on three criteria, not one: total computation per layer, the minimum number of sequential operations (how parallel it is), and the maximum path length a signal travels between any two positions — the property it argues governs how easily long-range dependencies are learned.

layer typecomplexity per layersequential operationsmaximum path length
self-attentionO(n² · d)O(1)O(1)
recurrentO(n · d²)O(n)O(n)
convolutional (kernel k)O(k · n · d²)O(1)O(log_k(n))
self-attention, restricted (neighbourhood r)O(r · n · d)O(1)O(n / r)

n is sequence length, d the representation width. The log_k(n) path length is for dilated convolutions; with contiguous kernels a stack of O(n / k) layers is needed to connect all positions.

  • Where self-attention is cheaper than recurrence. n² · d < n · d² exactly when n < d. The paper notes this is the usual case for sentence-level translation with word-piece or byte-pair tokens: sentences are tens of tokens long, widths are hundreds.
  • Long inputs. For very long sequences the paper suggests the restricted variant — attend only to a window of r positions — at the price of raising the path length to O(n / r), and leaves it to future work. (What became of that idea is on Sparse, Linear & Flash Attention.)
  • Convolutions are more expensive than recurrence by a factor of k. Separable convolutions bring the cost down to O(k · n · d + n · d²); the paper observes that even with k = n that equals the cost of one self-attention layer plus one position-wise feed-forward layer — which is the combination it uses.
  • A side benefit claimed. Self-attention "could yield more interpretable models": individual heads appear to learn different tasks related to syntactic and semantic structure (§09).

03 The architecture in one screen

Everything below is covered in depth on its own page; what matters here is the exact form the paper used, because several of its choices (post-norm, sinusoidal positions, an encoder–decoder) were later changed by the models built on it.

the pieces, as the paper specifies themencoder: N = 6 identical layers, each [ multi-head self-attention ] → [ position-wise FFN ] decoder: N = 6 identical layers, each [ masked self-attention ] → [ encoder–decoder attention ] → [ FFN ] every sub-layer: output = LayerNorm( x + Sublayer(x) ) (post-norm, residual around each) all widths: d_model = 512 (embeddings and every sub-layer output) Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √d_k ) · V MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head₁, …, head_h) · W_O, headᵢ = Attention(Q W_Qⁱ, K W_Kⁱ, V W_Vⁱ) FFN(x) = max(0, x W₁ + b₁) · W₂ + b₂ inner width d_ff = 2048 PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 10000^(2i / d_model) ) PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 10000^(2i / d_model) ) wavelengths from 2π to 10000 · 2π
  • Three uses of attention. Encoder self-attention (every source position attends to every source position); decoder self-attention, masked so position i sees only positions ≤ i; and encoder–decoder attention, whose queries come from the decoder and keys/values from the encoder output. See Multi-Head Attention & Masking and Queries, Keys & Values.
  • Why scale by √d_k. The paper's stated reason: for large d_k the dot products grow large and push the softmax into regions with extremely small gradients. Without scaling, additive attention outperforms dot-product attention at large d_k; with it, dot-product attention keeps its speed and memory advantage. Derivation on Alignment, Scores & Scaling.
  • Heads. h = 8 heads with d_k = d_v = d_model / h = 64, so the total cost is similar to one full-width head.
  • Embeddings. The input embedding, the output embedding and the pre-softmax linear layer share one weight matrix, and the embedding is multiplied by √d_model. See Transformer Stacks & Accounting.
  • Positions. Sinusoidal encodings are added to the embeddings. The paper's reason for choosing them over learned embeddings (which scored almost the same, §07): they "may allow the model to extrapolate to sequence lengths longer than the ones encountered during training", and a fixed offset corresponds to a linear function of the encoding. Full treatment on Positional Encodings; the norm and FFN placement on Residual Stream, Norms & FFN.

05 The training setup

Data and batching

  • English→German: WMT 2014, about 4.5 million sentence pairs, byte-pair encoded with a shared source–target vocabulary of about 37 000 tokens (sub-word tokenisation is on Tokenization).
  • English→French: WMT 2014, 36 million sentences, a 32 000 word-piece vocabulary.
  • Sentence pairs were batched by approximate length; each batch held about 25 000 source tokens and 25 000 target tokens.
  • Hardware: one machine with 8 P100 GPUs.

Optimiser and the warmup schedule

as publishedAdam, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.98, ε = 10⁻⁹ lrate = d_model^(−0.5) · min( step^(−0.5), step · warmup^(−1.5) ), warmup = 4000 what that means numerically (our arithmetic)step ≤ 4000: rises linearly lrate = step · d_model^(−0.5) · 4000^(−1.5) step = 4000: peak 512^(−0.5) · 4000^(−0.5) = 0.0442 · 0.0158 ≈ 7.0 × 10⁻⁴ (base) 1024^(−0.5) · 4000^(−0.5) = 0.0313 · 0.0158 ≈ 4.9 × 10⁻⁴ (big) step > 4000: decays as 1/√step at step 100 000 the base rate is ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻⁴

The peak is lower for the wider model — the schedule bakes a width rule into the learning rate. The schedule is derived and plotted, with the ways it later changed, on Transformer Training & Decoding; this page does not repeat that plot.

Regularisation

  • Residual dropout on the output of every sub-layer before it is added to the residual and normalised, and on the sums of embeddings and positional encodings in both stacks. Base: P_drop = 0.1.
  • Label smoothing ε_ls = 0.1. In the paper's words this "hurts perplexity, as the model learns to be more unsure, but improves accuracy and BLEU score" — visible in the ablation (§07), where removing it lowers perplexity yet loses BLEU.

How the FLOP numbers were estimated

the paper's method, and a check against its own table (check is our arithmetic)training FLOPs ≈ training time × number of GPUs × sustained single-precision rate per GPU assumed rates: K80 2.8, K40 3.7, M40 6.0, P100 9.5 TFLOPS base: 12 h · 3600 · 8 · 9.5 × 10¹² ≈ 3.3 × 10¹⁸ ✓ matches the table big: 3.5 d · 86 400 · 8 · 9.5 × 10¹² ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁹ ✓ matches the table

So the cost column is a hardware-time estimate, not a count of the model's arithmetic. It is a fair like-for-like comparison only to the extent the assumed sustained rates are.

Inference settings

  • Checkpoint averaging: base models averaged the last 5 checkpoints (written every 10 minutes); big models the last 20.
  • Beam search: beam size 4, length penalty α = 0.6, maximum output length = input length + 50, terminating early when possible. These were chosen on the development set. Beam and length normalisation are explained on Machine Translation; BLEU itself on Evaluation Metrics.

08 Does it transfer? English constituency parsing

To test whether the architecture was specific to translation, the paper trained it on a task with strong structural constraints and outputs much longer than inputs: parsing a sentence into a linearised constituency tree.

  • Setup: a 4-layer Transformer with d_model = 1024; the Wall Street Journal portion of the Penn Treebank, about 40K training sentences (16K-token vocabulary), and a semi-supervised setting adding about 17M sentences (32K vocabulary). Only dropout, learning rates and beam size were tuned, on the development set; maximum output length = input length + 300, beam 21, α = 0.3.

09 What the attention heads appear to do

The paper's appendix visualises encoder self-attention in layer 5 of 6 and offers three observations — illustrations on chosen sentences, not measurements:

  • Long-distance dependencies. Many heads attend from a verb to the distant words that complete its phrase (the example completes "making … more difficult").
  • Anaphora. Two heads appear to be involved in resolving a pronoun ("its"), with very sharp attention from it to its referent.
  • Specialisation. Different heads exhibit different behaviour that seems related to sentence structure — the basis of the "more interpretable" claim in §02.

Later work found attention maps are a weak guide to what a model relies on; the caveats are on Attention Cost & Limits (beyond this paper).

11 Common misconceptions

"The paper invented attention." It removed everything else. Attention for translation predates it (Learning to Align & Translate); the contribution was a model with no recurrence or convolution, plus scaled dot-product and multi-head attention.
"Self-attention is always cheaper than recurrence." Only when n < d. Per layer it costs n² · d against n · d². Its decisive advantages are O(1) sequential operations and O(1) path length, not total FLOPs at long n.
"The original Transformer was a GPT-style decoder." It was an encoder–decoder for translation, 6 + 6 layers, post-norm, with cross-attention from decoder to encoder.
"Sinusoidal positions were chosen because they worked better." They scored the same as learned ones (25.8 vs 25.7 BLEU); they were chosen in the hope of extrapolating to longer sequences.
"The training-cost column counts the model's FLOPs." It is time × GPUs × an assumed sustained rate, an estimate of hardware spent.

12 Takeaways

  • The argument is three-part: per-layer cost (cheaper than recurrence when n < d), O(1) sequential operations, and O(1) maximum path length between positions.
  • Base: 6 + 6 layers, d_model = 512, d_ff = 2048, 8 heads, 65M parameters, 12 hours on 8 P100s. Big: 1024 / 4096 / 16 heads, 213M, 3.5 days.
  • Recipe: Adam (0.9, 0.98, 10⁻⁹), linear warmup over 4000 steps then 1/√step decay scaled by d_model^(−0.5); residual dropout 0.1; label smoothing 0.1.
  • Results: 28.4 BLEU En→De and 41.8 En→Fr on newstest2014, ahead of earlier single models and ensembles at a fraction of their estimated training cost.
  • Ablations: size and dropout matter most; one head is 0.9 BLEU worse than eight; small d_k hurts; learned and sinusoidal positions tie.
  • It transferred: 91.3 / 92.7 F1 on constituency parsing with little task-specific tuning.
