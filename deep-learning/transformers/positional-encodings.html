03 Positional encoding as an architectural choice: the three places position can enter

Hand-off 1 said position must be injected. There are three places in the block where a signal can be injected without breaking the per-row / across-row separation of §01, and every positional scheme in wide use is one of the three. Name the three first; then each scheme is a short derivation. (Vocabulary: a positional encoding is a fixed formula — sinusoids, a rotation, a bias — and a positional embedding is a learned table; the two words are used interchangeably in practice, and this page keeps the distinction only where it matters.)

The three injection points

Trace the data flow of §01 and ask where a quantity that depends on the position index i (or on the pair (i, j)) can be introduced. The rows of X are the only per-token quantity before attention; the projected Q and K are the only per-token quantities that meet each other; and the score matrix S is the only per-pair quantity in the block. Those are the three doors. (A fourth exists — adding a position-dependent term to the values, so that what is retrieved also depends on the offset — and some early relative-position schemes used it; the schemes that survived did not, and it is not opened here.)

Two things the table makes visible before any derivation. First, the parameter count is not the point — four of the six schemes have none, and the one with the most (learned absolute) is the one that cannot extrapolate at all. Second, doors 2 and 3 are both about pairs, which is why they are applied inside every attention layer, at every depth, while door 1 is applied once: information added to the input rows in door 1 has to survive every residual write above it to still be readable at layer 30, and part of what §11 measures is how much of it does.

04 The sinusoidal table, derived from one requirement, and its relative-offset property measured

The sinusoidal table gives position p a row of sines and cosines, (sin p·ωᵢ, cos p·ωᵢ) pair by pair, at wavelengths spaced geometrically from 2π to 2π·base; every row is distinct, and moving k positions is a fixed rotation of the row that does not depend on p. That second fact is the one that matters. The table is usually presented as a formula to be accepted; it is better presented as the solution to a requirement — that a fixed linear map should turn "position p" into "position p + k", for every p, so that a head can learn to look a fixed distance away without knowing where it is.

The requirement, and why it forces rotations

An attention head scores a query row against a key row with a bilinear form, score(p, p') = P[p]·(W_Q W_Kᵀ)·P[p']ᵀ when the rows carry only position. For the head to prefer "the token k places from me" (k negative for "before") it needs the score to peak at p' = p + k for every p, with the same W_Q W_Kᵀ. The cleanest way to guarantee that is to ask for a matrix M_k, depending on k alone, with P[p + k] = P[p]·M_k: then setting W_Q W_Kᵀ = M_k makes the score P[p+k]·P[p']ᵀ, which is largest when p' = p + k if the rows are most similar to themselves. What functions of p have that property — a shift in the argument is a fixed linear map on the value? Continuous solutions of f(p + k) = f(p)·M_k are matrix exponentials, f(p) = f(0)·exp(p·A). Among them, the ones that stay bounded at every position — the only usable coordinates — are the rotations, A skew-symmetric, which are sinusoid pairs; the unbounded ones (growing exponentials, and polynomial families such as f(p) = (1, p) with M_k = [[1, k], [0, 1]]) satisfy the identity too and are useless. A sine needs its cosine alongside for the map to be linear (a shift of sin alone is not a multiple of sin). So each coordinate pair is (sin ωp, cos ωp), and the shift is a 2-D rotation.

Two things the derivation makes precise

  • Relative position is available, but not for free. The table carries absolute position (every row is distinct), and relative position is encodable by a head whose W_Q W_Kᵀ is a rotation — but nothing forces the head to learn that matrix. The figure fits each rotation block of M_k from the table's own columns by least squares and recovers the closed form to round-off, which confirms the map; whether a trained head uses it is an empirical question, and §09 measures how well a trained model transfers to positions past its training length, which is the practical form of that question.
  • The kernel is shared across heads and depths. Because P is added once at the input (door 1), any head at any depth can only read position through whatever survives on the residual stream. The offset kernel is a property of the table, not of the head, so every head that wants "offset k" builds it from the same sinusoids; §11 measures how much of the added signal is still on the stream after many residual writes.
PE(p + k) = PE(p)·M_k, fitted from the table and compared with the closed form; the offset kernel; the head it builds move k and the score matrix's bright diagonal moves with it — that diagonal is a head that attends exactly k positions away, at every position, with one fixed matrix; the residual printed is the fit against the closed form
The half-truth to avoid. "Sinusoidal encodings let the model extrapolate to longer sequences because the formula is defined for any position." Defined, yes; extrapolate, no. The formula produces a vector for position 5000, but the model was trained on the geometry of positions 0–511 and a head's W_Q W_Kᵀ was fitted there; the long-wavelength pairs at position 5000 take values the model has never seen and the kernel above no longer describes what the trained head computes. §09 trains a model on length 10 and measures it at 12, 16, 24, 32, 48 and 64: sinusoidal is the scheme that degrades fastest of those that run at all.

05 Learned absolute positions: a table of n_max free vectors, and the wall at n_max + 1

Replace the sinusoids with a trainable (n_max × d) matrix and let the loss decide what position looks like. It is the least structured of the schemes, the one with the most parameters, the one that several of the best-known models used — and the only one that physically cannot be evaluated one token past its table.

the scheme, and its costP ∈ ℝ^(n_max × d) initialised like an embedding (small Gaussian), trained by the loss X⁰[i] = E[tokenᵢ] + P[i] i < n_max, or there is no row parameters n_max · d e.g. 1024 × 768 = 786 432 (0.6% of a 124M model) 2048 × 12288 = 25 165 824 (0.014% of a 175B model) gradient ∂L/∂P[i] = ∑ over every sequence in the batch of ∂L/∂X⁰[i] — a row is updated only by tokens AT that position, so late rows see fewer updates than early rows in a corpus of variable-length documents

What the table learns, and what it cannot

Nothing in the scheme says that P[i] and P[i + 1] should be similar; that has to be learned from the fact that neighbouring positions play similar roles. In practice the learned Gram matrix P·Pᵀ of a well-trained model is banded — rows close in position are close in the embedding — with additional structure the sinusoids do not have, such as a distinctive first row and a periodicity that matches the corpus's typical sentence length. The figure trains the page's tiny two-layer model on the copy task twice, once with a learned table and once with the sinusoidal one, and draws both Gram matrices. On a ten-token task there is not much for the table to learn, and that is the honest picture: the learned table is exactly as structured as the data forced it to be, no more. The sinusoidal Gram is banded before any training happens; the learned one becomes whatever separates the positions the task needed separated.

The wall is the property that matters architecturally. A learned table is indexed, not evaluated: there is no rule producing P[n_max], so a sequence of length n_max + 1 raises an index error. Every other scheme on this page at least runs past the training length (§09 measures how well). This is why context extension for models with learned tables means retraining the table, while for RoPE models it means rescaling the angles. The trade the scheme makes is flexibility for structure: given enough data the table can represent any position-dependent prior, including ones no fixed formula would guess; given little data it represents noise, which is why the usual reasoning is that a learned table earns its flexibility only on a large corpus. (The work that introduced the sinusoids compared the two on translation and found them "nearly identical", choosing the formula for its chance of extrapolating; §09 measures how far that chance goes.) The length of the table, n_max, is what a model card calls its context window — for a learned table it is a hard limit, for the other schemes a training length.

The learned table against the sinusoidal one, after the same training on the same task: Gram matrices and the wall both models are trained live on the copy task from the same seed; the learned Gram is whatever the task required, the sinusoidal Gram is banded by construction — and the bar at the right shows the one thing the learned table cannot do
Where each was used. Learned absolute tables: the original 2018–2020 decoder-only language models and the bidirectional encoders of the same period (the BERT and GPT cards say so explicitly), with n_max of 512, 1024 or 2048. Sinusoidal: the 2017 encoder–decoder, and a minority of later models. Since roughly 2021 the large decoders have moved to RoPE or, less often, ALiBi, for the reasons §09 measures: the two door-2/door-3 schemes are the ones that survive a longer context, and context has been the axis of competition.

06 Rotary position embedding: rotate the query and the key, and the score depends on the offset alone

§04 found that shifting a sinusoid is a rotation. RoPE takes the rotation out of the input table and puts it where the pair of tokens actually meet — on the query and key rows, inside every head — so that the score, and only the score, depends on the relative offset. Written on rows, it is four lines.

What RoPE changes relative to the input table, and what it does not

Compare with §04. There, relative offset was available to a head that learned a rotation for W_Q W_Kᵀ, and the signal had to survive the residual stream from the bottom of the stack. Here the offset is built into every score at every depth, the head's W_Q W_Kᵀ is free to spend itself on content, and nothing positional is added to the residual stream — position enters only where a pair of tokens is compared, and whatever position-dependent information the stream carries higher up was derived by attention (through the offset-dependent scores and the causal mask, §10) rather than injected. The cost is zero parameters and a per-head rotation of Q and K: four multiplies per pair, so about 2·dₖ per row for each of the two matrices, 4·n·dₖ per head — negligible against the 2·n·d·dₖ of the two projections that produced them. One thing to be precise about: the score depends on the offset only, so no single score carries absolute position — and yet a causal RoPE model can tell where it is. The route is the first token: under the causal mask it is visible from every position, and the offset to it is the absolute position m. On the low-frequency pairs (§07) that offset maps to a small, unwrapped angle, so a head that attends to the first token reads a monotone function of m. That is how a RoPE model answers "am I near the beginning" without any absolute signal ever being injected.

07 RoPE's spectrum: which pairs turn, which pairs barely move, and what the base does

RoPE is dₖ/2 clocks running at geometrically spaced rates. The fast ones resolve neighbouring positions; the slow ones have not completed a turn by the end of the context and act as an absolute coordinate. Which pairs are which depends on dₖ, the base and the context length — three numbers, one figure.

the spectrum, and the two regimesθᵢ = base^(−2i/dₖ) λᵢ = 2π / θᵢ = 2π · base^(2i/dₖ) i = 0 … dₖ/2 − 1 pair i completes at least one full turn inside a context of n tokens ⇔ λᵢ ≤ n ⇔ 2i/dₖ ≤ log(n/2π) / log(base) dₖ = 128, base 10 000: λ₀ = 6.28, λ₆₃ = 54 410 n = 4 096 → 46 of 64 pairs complete a turn; 18 do not (the slowest turns 0.075 of a turn) n = 32 768 → 60 of 64; the slowest turns 0.60 of a turn n = 131 072 → 64 of 64; EVERY pair has wrapped: no pair's angle is still a one-to-one function of the offset dₖ = 128, base 500 000: λ₆₃ = 2 559 000 n = 4 096 → 32 of 64 pairs complete a turn n = 131 072 → 49 of 64; 15 pairs have not wrapped and still act as coarse, unambiguous rulers

Why a model needs both regimes

A pair that turns many times over the context is a fine ruler: the score's dependence on cos((m − m′)θᵢ) distinguishes offset 3 from offset 4 sharply, and it is periodic — offset 3 and offset 3 + λᵢ look the same to that pair, which is harmless because the other pairs disambiguate. A pair whose angle never wraps inside the context (λᵢ > n) is a coarse ruler: the pair (cos, sin) of its angle is a one-to-one function of the offset, so "far" and "near" are never confused, and if it has turned less than half a turn (λᵢ > 2n) the cosine alone is monotone in the offset. And because the offset to the first token is the absolute position (§06), a head that reads such a pair through the first token gets a monotone function of m — the nearest thing to an absolute coordinate a RoPE model has. Both are needed: one for local syntax, one for "this is the start of the document". When the context is extended to a length at which every pair has wrapped, the coarse rulers are gone and the model has to be told how to rescale them; that is exactly the subject of Context-Length Extension, which owns position interpolation and its NTK-aware and YaRN refinements and is not repeated here.

The base is the knob that trades the two. Raising it stretches every wavelength except the first (λ₀ = 2π always), so more pairs stay unwrapped over a given context: the move from 10⁴ to 5·10⁵ in the newer long-context decoders amounts to a decision to keep about a quarter of the pairs (15 of 64) as coarse rulers at 128k tokens instead of none. The price is resolution in the middle of the spectrum — pairs that used to complete a few turns over 4k tokens now complete a fraction of one, and the head has fewer sharp rulers at intermediate distances. There is no free choice here; the figure shows the count on each side of the line for any triple, and the count is the design decision.

08 Biasing the scores: ALiBi's linear penalty per head, and learned relative buckets

Door 3 adds a number to S[i][j] that depends on the pair. The simplest such number is a linear penalty on distance, one slope per head and nothing learned; the most flexible is a learned scalar per distance bucket. Both leave the input rows and the projections alone, and both are added exactly where the mask is.

What a monotone bias can and cannot address

The ALiBi bias is monotone in distance, and that has a consequence §09 measures. A head whose content scores carry no positional information — which is every first-layer head, since nothing positional was added to X; deeper layers can carry whatever earlier heads derived (§10) — sees a score profile that is the content term plus a straight line falling with distance. Its softmax puts the most weight on the nearest key whose content matches, and it has no way to prefer "exactly two back" over "one back" unless content distinguishes them. "The previous token" is reachable by a trick — the head can learn to make a token's key repel its own query, so that the diagonal is suppressed and the nearest remaining key wins — but "two back" is not, within one head, because the token one back is a random symbol with no reason to be suppressed. ALiBi is therefore a recency prior, not a positional encoding in the sense of §04 or §06: it tells each head how quickly to forget, and leaves the model to derive anything sharper from the composition of heads with different slopes across layers. That is also why it extrapolates so well — nothing it uses at distance 5 000 is new to it, the penalty is simply larger — and why a model that needs precise long-range offsets does not use it.

Learned buckets are the other extreme: every offset up to ±7 has its own scalar per head, so "two back" is one parameter. The cost is that the bias is a lookup, not a formula: it cannot be extended past the last bucket except by sharing the last value, and it is added to every layer's scores, which for a long sequence means materialising an (n × n) bias per head unless the kernel fuses it. Both schemes have a property the door-1 schemes lack and RoPE shares: the score bias is the same at every depth, because it is recomputed from (i, j) in every layer rather than read off a residual stream that has been written to thirty times.

Where the bias goes in the code, and why it is free. Both biases are additive on the score matrix, exactly like the mask of the attention page's masking section, so DL.attnForward takes them through the same argument — one matrix for all heads, or an array of h matrices when the bias differs per head, which ALiBi's does. No new operation, no new gradient: the bias is a constant, the backward pass derived on the attention page is unchanged, and §17 here audits attention with RoPE and per-head ALiBi biases (1e−10) and attention with RoPE and grouped-query heads (2e−10) against central differences.

09 Which schemes extrapolate, and why — five models trained live and tested past their training length

The claims about extrapolation are usually quoted from large-scale perplexity tables. Here they are reproduced at toy scale, in the browser, on a task where the right answer is unambiguous: train a two-layer decoder on sequences of length 10 to output the previous token, then run it on sequences of 12, 16, 24, 32, 48 and 64 and measure the accuracy. Same architecture, same seed, same optimiser; only the positional scheme changes.

What the task isolates

"Output the token one position back" needs exactly one thing from the positional scheme: a head that can attend to offset −1 at every position, including positions that did not exist during training. It does not need content (the tokens are random), it does not need composition across layers, and its chance level is 1/8. That makes it a clean probe of the one property under test. Everything about the run is fixed: d = 16, h = 4, two pre-norm blocks, d_ff = 32, AdamW with the warmup-then-cosine schedule of §26, 300 steps of batch 4. The five schemes are the five of §03 minus the learned buckets (which at this width would be a trivial lookup): sinusoidal, learned absolute, RoPE, ALiBi and none.

Reading the measurement

  • Learned absolute stops. Not "degrades" — the evaluation past 10 raises an error, and the line ends. This is the property, not a limitation of the toy.
  • Sinusoidal runs and fails. It is the best or near-best in-distribution and the worst out of it, because the head's W_Q W_Kᵀ was fitted to the geometry of ten rows and the eleventh row's slow coordinates are new. The same model at 64 tokens has lost about two-thirds of its accuracy and is within a few points of the model with no positional encoding at all.
  • RoPE degrades gracefully. The offset property guarantees the form of the score at any length; what changes is that the slow pairs are evaluated at offsets — hence angles — never seen, and the head's projections were not fitted for them. (At dₖ = 4 there are two pairs, with wavelengths 6.3 and 628 positions; the first wrapped within the training length of 10 and is safe, the second is the one that meets new angles.) The curve falls, but from a high start and slowly — this is the curve that position interpolation is designed to flatten, and Context-Length Extension owns that.
  • ALiBi is flat and lower. Flat because nothing new happens at a new length; lower because the "previous token" is reachable only through the self-suppression trick of §08, which this budget learns partially. Switch the task to "two back" and ALiBi falls at the training length to the level of the model with no positional encoding (about 40% against 100% for the addressable schemes — above the 12.5% chance level only because a recency window over the last few tokens lets it guess among them): a monotone recency bias cannot address an exact offset, and no amount of training fixes what the function class cannot represent.
  • None is weak but not at chance. The causal mask alone lets a two-layer model derive a rough position (§10); it is enough to beat chance and not enough to solve the task, and it does not transfer.
The claim to be careful with. "ALiBi extrapolates better than RoPE, so it is the better positional encoding." It extrapolates better because it encodes less. The measurement above is the whole argument: on the task ALiBi can represent it is flat past the training length, and on the task it cannot represent it is little better than having no positional encoding at all (40% against 34% at the training length, 34% against 21% at 64 tokens, at the default seed). A recency prior is a good tool when perplexity on ordinary text is the objective and long inputs are expected; it is the wrong tool when a head has to land on an exact distant offset. The large-scale results that motivated it were perplexity results, and at scale the composition of many heads across layers recovers more than a single head can (§08).

10 No positional encoding at all: what a causal decoder can derive from the mask

The attention page's permutation-equivariance section showed that a causal mask breaks the permutation symmetry on its own. This section says exactly what positional information the mask leaks, measures it, and closes the positional-encoding sequence with the honest statement of what "NoPE" buys and costs.

the counting signal a causal mask providesposition i can attend to keys 0 … i : exactly i + 1 of them. take a head whose scores are all EQUAL (W_Q = 0, say): its weights are uniform, A[i][j] = 1/(i+1) for j ≤ i, and its output is the running MEAN of the values: zᵢ = (1/(i+1)) · ∑_{j≤i} vⱼ give the first token a value v₀ along a direction no other value uses (a BOS token can own one — the figure gives it a coordinate of its own): the component of zᵢ along v₀ is EXACTLY 1/(i+1) — a monotone, position-dependent number, available at layer 1 with no positional input whatsoever. (If the other values do have some component along v₀, the curve is 1/(i+1) plus noise that averages out.) A later layer can read it. That is NoPE's whole mechanism: position is INFERRED from how many keys were visible. and what an encoder cannot dobidirectional attention sees n keys at every position: the running mean is the SAME mean everywhere, and the leak is gone. NoPE is a decoder-only option.

The figure builds that head literally: a random causal attention layer with W_Q zeroed, a BOS row with a fixed value, and the projection of each output row onto the BOS value direction plotted against position. The curve is 1/(i + 1) to round-off. Then it replaces uniform attention with a random W_Q — an untrained head — and the curve is noisy but still decreasing, because the BOS key is one of i + 1 candidates and its expected weight still falls with i. Two things follow. First, the signal is weak: 1/(i+1) at i = 1000 is 0.000999 and at i = 1001 is 0.000998 — a difference of about 10⁻⁶ — so fine position at long range is not recoverable this way, which matches §09's measurement that the scheme is the weakest of the five. Second, it is real, and it is the reason a decoder with no positional encoding trains at all and has been reported to match or beat explicit schemes on some length-generalisation benchmarks; it also decays gracefully past the training length, because 1/(i+1) continues to be monotone. The trade is capacity — layers spent deriving position are layers not spent on content — and that is why nearly every production decoder uses RoPE or a relative bias rather than relying on the mask alone.

The positional sequence, closed. §03 named three doors; §04–§10 walked through every scheme in use. The summary a designer needs: door 1 (add to input) is simple and either unextendable (learned) or fragile (sinusoidal); door 2 (rotate Q, K) gives an exact offset property at every depth for zero parameters and degrades gently, and is the default of current decoders; door 3 (bias the scores) gives either a parameter-free recency prior that survives any length but cannot address an offset (ALiBi), or a learned lookup that can (buckets); and no door is a decoder-only option that works because the mask counts. Everything past the training length — interpolation, NTK scaling, YaRN — is Context-Length Extension's.
