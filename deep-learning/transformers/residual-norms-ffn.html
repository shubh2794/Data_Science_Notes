11 The residual stream: a shared bus every sub-layer reads from and writes to

Unroll a pre-norm stack and the output is the input plus a sum of 2L writes. Nothing is ever overwritten. That one identity explains why depth is cheap to train, why the stream's norm grows, why a signal added at the bottom is still readable at the top — and why the last norm before the output head is not optional.

the unrolled stream — every block ADDS, none replacesx⁰ = √d·E[token] + P (the embedding, §20) x¹ = x⁰ + a₁(x⁰) + f₁(x⁰ + a₁(x⁰)) x² = x¹ + a₂(x¹) + f₂(·) … xᴸ = x⁰ + ∑ₗ [ aₗ(·) + fₗ(·) ] 2L writes, each a function of the stream BELOW it every sub-layer READS the whole stream through its norm, N(xˡ), and WRITES a vector back onto it. What sub-layer 2 wrote is still present, unchanged, in the sum that sub-layer 37 reads — addition commutes, so the writes are superposed, not chained — though it is read through a norm that also sees every write since. the Jacobian, and why depth is trainable∂xˡ⁺¹/∂xˡ = I + Jₐ + J_f·(I + Jₐ) Jₐ = ∂aₗ/∂xˡ, J_f = ∂fₗ/∂(xˡ + aₗ) — the identity is ALWAYS there = I + Jₗ with Jₗ everything except the identity ∂xᴸ/∂x⁰ = ∏ₗ ( I + Jₗ ) = I + ∑ₗ Jₗ + ∑ₗ<ₘ JₘJₗ + … with ‖Jₗ‖ small at initialisation the product is dominated by I and the first-order terms — a sum over paths of every length, in which the direct path is a constant. The RNN page's recurrent product ∏ W had no such constant, and that is the whole difference between "depth 96" being trainable and not.

Three measurable consequences

The norm grows. Writes at initialisation are roughly uncorrelated with the stream and with each other, so ‖xᴸ‖² ≈ ‖x⁰‖² + ∑ ‖writes‖² and the norm grows like a power of L near ½ when the writes are of comparable size. The figure measures the root-mean-square of a token's stream after each of L random pre-norm blocks, fits the exponent (about 0.4 at the defaults — the writes are not perfectly uncorrelated), and it rises steadily; the same measurement on a post-norm stack is flat at √d, because the norm sits on the residual path and resets it. The input's share shrinks. The cosine between xᴸ and x⁰ falls with depth even though x⁰ is still exactly present in the sum — its proportion of the stream is what falls, which is the precise sense in which a door-1 positional signal (§03) "fades": nothing removed it, but the norms it must be read against have grown. The block is near-identity. The relative size of (∂Y/∂X − I)ᵀ·g for a random g is below one — about a half — at initialisation for a pre-norm block and stays there with depth, which is the constant-path statement above made numerical; a post-norm block's Jacobian has no identity term because the residual passes through the norm, and its deviation is of order one.

The stream through L blocks: its norm, its cosine with the input, and the block Jacobian's distance from the identity pre-norm grows like √L and stays near-identity; post-norm is pinned at √d and is not near-identity — the weight-scale slider is the initialisation, and scaling it up is the fastest way to see the near-identity property fail
Why the pre-norm stack ends in a norm. Read the unrolled sum once more: the last thing that happened to xᴸ was an addition, and the output head reads it directly. Without a final norm, the logits scale with ‖xᴸ‖, which grows with depth and drifts during training; the softmax's temperature would be an accident of the stack's depth. The final norm pins it. A post-norm stack already ends in N₂, so it needs nothing. This is the origin of the pattern "pre-norm blocks + one ln_f" in nearly every decoder built since 2019, and forgetting it is one of §35's bugs that does not crash. The general residual story — bottleneck blocks, pre-activation ordering, stochastic depth — is Residual / Skip Connections'; this section owns only what the transformer's stream does.

12 Pre-norm against post-norm: the gradient scale through the stack, and what it does to the learning rate

§02 showed the two wirings compute legitimate functions and §11 showed their streams behave differently. The practical difference is what the optimiser sees at step zero. Measure the gradient of every layer's weights at initialisation for both wirings, and the reason one of them needs a gentler start is in the plot.

where the scale comes from — one block, the FFN's second matrix, in both wirings (H = the FFN's hidden activations, (n × d_ff))POST-NORM U = X₁ + FFN(X₁), Y = N₂(U) ∂L/∂W₂ = Hᵀ · ∂L/∂U, ∂L/∂U = ∂L/∂Y · ∂N₂/∂U the norm's Jacobian is ≈ (1/σ)·(I − …) with σ the row std of U, which is O(1) at every depth at init — so every layer's gradient has ROUGHLY the same scale, set by d rather than by depth (measured: the top/bottom ratio stays near 0.7 as L grows) PRE-NORM Y = X₁ + FFN(N₂(X₁)), and the loss reads N_f(xᴸ) at the top of the stack ∂L/∂W₂ = Hᵀ · ∂L/∂Y, ∂L/∂Y = ∂L/∂xᴸ (top block) with ∂L/∂xᴸ = (∂L/∂out)·∂N_f/∂xᴸ ∝ 1/‖xᴸ‖ and ‖xᴸ‖ grows like √L (§11): the TOP layer's gradient shrinks like 1/√L, the bottom layers' less so (their writes are read through many near-identity Jacobians and the same 1/‖xᴸ‖) these are the leading-order scalings the analyses of the two wirings derive; the figure measures them rather than assuming them.

What the measurement shows, and how far the toy goes

The left panel is that computation done exactly, with the audited backward pass of §17, for a random stack: the parameter-gradient norm of each block's W₂, bottom to top. Post-norm is roughly flat — the top-to-bottom ratio does not fall as the stack deepens. Pre-norm decreases towards the top and the top layer's gradient falls with L as a power between −⅓ and −½ (−0.35 fitted at the defaults, against the −½ the leading-order argument predicts), which is the ‖xᴸ‖ growth of §11 seen from the other side. Neither profile is "better" in isolation; what matters is that under post-norm a large learning rate applied at step zero moves every layer by a large amount at once, through norms whose Jacobians are not near-identity, and the stack can leave the regime where the initialisation's scales hold before the optimiser's statistics have settled. That is the mechanism the analyses of the two wirings propose for a widely reported finding: a post-norm stack trains reliably only with warmup and a smaller peak rate, while a pre-norm stack tolerates a larger rate with little or no warmup. It is a proposed mechanism consistent with the measurement here, not a theorem. Two honest caveats. Post-norm is not simply worse: when it does train, it has repeatedly been reported to reach a slightly lower final loss than pre-norm at the same size, which is why work on very deep post-norm stacks (with down-weighted residual branches) continues. And the 2017 recipe's warmup (§26) was designed for post-norm; the pre-norm recipes of §27 keep a short warmup anyway, because Adam's early statistics (§27) are a second, independent reason for one.

The right panel reproduces the part of that finding this page's scale can reach: the tiny two-layer copy model of §32, trained live in both wirings at two peak learning rates. At the smaller rate both wirings learn; at the larger one, pre-norm learns faster and post-norm stalls. What the toy does not reproduce — and this is stated rather than fudged — is a clean dependence on warmup: at two layers, 150 steps and d = 16, adding a 30-step warmup changes the post-norm curve only marginally, because the gradient scales that make warmup necessary at d = 4096 and L = 96 are not large here. The learning-rate ceiling is the toy-scale shadow of the same effect, and it is the one this figure measures.

Gradient norm of every block's W₂ at initialisation, pre- against post-norm; and the two wirings trained live at two learning rates raise the depth: post-norm's profile stays flat, pre-norm's top layer keeps shrinking; the right panel is four short live trainings and its claim is only about the peak rate, not warmup
The mismatch that does not crash. A pre-norm model trained with a post-norm recipe — long warmup, small peak rate — converges, slowly, and wastes the tolerance the wiring bought; a post-norm model trained with a pre-norm recipe — short warmup, large peak rate — diverges in the first few hundred steps or, worse, does not diverge and settles into a bad basin with a loss that looks plausible. Neither raises an error. The wiring and the schedule are one decision, and §26 sets the schedule for the wiring this page defaults to.

13 LayerNorm against RMSNorm: what the mean subtraction buys, what it costs, and when the two coincide

Both normalise a single row of the stream to a fixed scale; one also removes its mean. The training page owns LayerNorm as a layer. This section owns the choice between the two as a transformer design decision, with the formulas side by side, the parameter count, and a measurement of exactly how far apart they are on real rows.

the two, on one ROW x of d features — every quantity is per token, never per batchLayerNorm μ = (1/d)∑ⱼ xⱼ σ² = (1/d)∑ⱼ (xⱼ − μ)² y = γ ⊙ (x − μ)/√(σ² + ε) + β 2d parameters, two passes over x RMSNorm r = √( (1/d)∑ⱼ xⱼ² + ε ) y = γ ⊙ x / r d parameters, one pass over x identity: if μ = 0 then σ² = (1/d)∑xⱼ² = r² − ε ⇒ LayerNorm(x) = RMSNorm(x) at β = 0. in general ‖LN(x) − RMS(x)‖ grows with |μ|/σ — the figure measures it. backward (audited, §17): ∂L/∂x = (1/σ)·[ g − mean(g) − x̂·mean(g ⊙ x̂) ] with g = γ ⊙ ∂L/∂y RMS: ∂L/∂x = g/r − x·⟨g, x⟩/(d·r³)

Why RMSNorm won the argument in decoders

The mean subtraction in LayerNorm removes one direction — the all-ones direction — from every row. The claim that this centring is what makes normalisation work has not held up in practice: in the published comparisons, models trained with RMSNorm match LayerNorm models, so the centring does not appear to be load-bearing. What the centring costs is real: a second reduction over d per row per norm, in a layer that is evaluated four times per block per training step (N₁ and N₂ forward, and their backward passes) and is memory-bound rather than compute-bound, so its cost is the pass over the row, not the arithmetic. Dropping β as well halves the parameters, which at d = 8192 and 80 blocks is 1.3 million numbers — negligible against 70 billion, but not nothing when they are also optimiser state. The result is that the 2017 wiring's LayerNorm survives in encoders and in the older decoders, and most large decoders since 2022 use RMSNorm without a bias (a few 2022–23 families kept LayerNorm). The direction of travel in the table is uniform: post-norm to pre-norm, LayerNorm to RMSNorm, biases dropped. Each step removed something that was not load-bearing and kept the property that was — a normalised input to every sub-layer and an untouched residual path. The parallel-block row is the same principle taken one step further: if the norm's job is only to give each sub-layer a clean read of the stream, one read can serve both.

Two placement rules that are not stylistic. (1) In pre-norm wiring the norm is applied to the input of a sub-layer and never to the residual path, so the stream keeps its scale; §11 and §12 are consequences. (2) Whatever norm is used inside the blocks, the same one is used as the final norm before the output head, with its own γ: the unembedding of §21 was designed for rows of that scale, and a tied embedding matrix in particular assumes it.

14 The position-wise feedforward network: shapes, the width ratio, and two-thirds of the parameters

Hand-off 2 asked for a per-token non-linearity. The answer is the smallest thing that qualifies — one hidden layer, applied to every row with the same weights — made wide. How wide is the single number that decides most of the parameter count of the model, and this section counts it.

Why four, and why it does not matter much

The ratio d_ff/d = 4 is the 2017 choice and it was not derived. Published sweeps since then report a broad, flat optimum: for a fixed parameter budget, moving the ratio anywhere between about 2 and 8 while adjusting depth or width to compensate changes the loss by amounts that are small compared with the effect of the budget itself. What the ratio does decide sharply is where the parameters sit: at ratio 4 the FFN is two-thirds of a block, at ratio 8 it is four-fifths, at ratio 2 it is half. Since the FFN's cost per token is one MAC per parameter and attention's projections are the same, the ratio moves parameters between the token-mixing and the per-token sub-layer at constant cost per token, and the flat optimum says the model does not care much which. The gated variants of §15 change the arithmetic of the ratio but not this conclusion; the sparse variants — several FFNs per block with a router choosing among them — are Mixture of Experts, and they exist precisely because the FFN is where the parameters are.

15 ReLU, GELU, and the gated units: three matrices, the ⅔ width correction, and the rounding rule real models use

The 2017 FFN used ReLU; the encoders of 2018 moved to GELU; the decoders since 2022 use a gated hidden layer, SwiGLU, that multiplies two projections of the input together. The gated form has three matrices instead of two, so its width is cut to two-thirds to hold the parameter count — a rule with a rounding convention that produces the odd-looking d_ff values in published configurations.

What the gate buys

An ungated hidden unit is a fixed direction w₁ in input space, thresholded: it fires when x·w₁ is large. A gated unit is the product of two projections, act(x·w_g)·(x·w_u): the first decides whether the unit is on, the second decides what it passes, and the product is a bilinear function of x — quadratic in the input where the ungated unit was linear-then-thresholded. That is more expressive per unit, which is the usual explanation for why the ⅔-width gated FFN matches or beats the full-width ungated one at the same parameter count in the published controlled comparisons, by a small but consistent margin. The cost is a third matrix multiply per token (at ⅔ width, so no more MACs) and an element-wise product, and a hidden unit whose output is no longer bounded below the way a GELU unit's is: silu(z) itself has a minimum of −0.278, but its product with x·w_u can take either sign and any size. The choice of act inside the gate matters less than the gate: SwiGLU and GeGLU are within noise of each other, and both are ahead of the ungated forms.

16 The FFN as a key–value memory: keys are the columns of W₁, values are the rows of W₂

Write the FFN as a sum over hidden units and it is a soft memory with d_ff slots: each slot has a key it compares the token against and a value it writes if the comparison fires. That is the same shape as attention's dictionary lookup in the attention page's query/key/value section — but with keys and values that are parameters, fixed at training time, rather than other tokens.

What the slots hold, on a model small enough to read

On the page's trained two-layer copy model the FFN of each block has 32 slots and the vocabulary has 10 symbols, which is small enough to read every slot. The figure computes, for every token type at a chosen position, the match score of every slot — the (V × d_ff) activation matrix — and, for every slot, what its value row does to the output distribution when pushed through the unembedding: the (d_ff × V) "value logits". A slot whose key fires on symbol 3 and whose value raises the logit of symbol 3 is a slot that has learned "when I see a 3, say 3", which is what a copy task rewards in the layer whose job is to produce the answer. Not every slot is legible — several fire on everything and write something small, which is the unhelpful but harmless case — and the point of the figure is not that FFNs are interpretable in general but that the key–value reading is literally what the arithmetic computes, and that the two matrices play the two roles.

17 The backward pass of every new primitive, audited against central differences

Every figure on this page that trains anything uses the analytic backward passes added to dl-viz.js for this part: the two norms, the ungated and gated FFN, attention with RoPE and per-head biases, the pre-norm and post-norm block, and the whole tiny model. What the audit does and does not establish. It establishes that each backward pass computes the gradient of the forward pass as implemented, to the precision central differences allow. It does not establish that the forward pass is the intended function — that RoPE pairs the right coordinates, that the mask is on the right axis, that the FFN's keys are the columns — because a wrong-but-consistent forward pass has a perfectly correct gradient. Those are the bugs of §35, and they are caught by the property tests of §04, §06 and §35, not by this figure. Both kinds of check are needed and they are independent.
