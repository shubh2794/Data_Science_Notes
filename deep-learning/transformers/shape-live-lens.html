31 Depth, width and what scales: the aspect ratio at a fixed parameter budget

With the counting of §22 the block share of a model is 12·L·d², so a budget N fixes a curve in the (L, d) plane, not a point. Where on the curve to sit — deep and narrow or shallow and wide — is the last architectural decision on this page. It is one of the few for which the large-scale answer is "it barely matters", and the reasons it barely matters are worth having.

What to look at once it has trained. The loss curve falls from the chance level ln 8 = 2.08 to near zero on copy and reverse within the default budget, to about a tenth on sort, and to well below chance but not to zero on induction, which needs the most steps of the four. The attention maps, averaged over held-out examples, show the circuit: on copy, a head whose weight sits on a fixed diagonal four positions back — the offset kernel of §04 learned from data, in the first block at the default seed; on reverse, an anti-diagonal; on induction, the textbook circuit is a first-block head on the previous token and a second-block head on the token after the previous occurrence of the current one — and at this budget the model does not find it cleanly (at 300 steps every head peaks at offset −1 and the held-out accuracy is about 74%; at 600 steps the first-block heads move to fixed offsets and accuracy reaches about 89%). Not every seed finds the clean version of anything — heads are defined up to permutation, as the attention page's multi-head section found — and the readout reports the loss rather than a story.

33 The logit lens: reading the residual stream layer by layer through the output head

§21 introduced the idea: the head is a linear map after a norm, so it can be applied to the stream at any depth. On the trained model of §32 that gives, for every position, a prediction after the embedding, after block 1 and after block 2 — and the point at which the answer appears on the stream is the point at which the circuit that computes it has finished.

the lens, and what it can and cannot claimpˡᵢ = softmax( LN_f(xˡᵢ) · Eᵀ ) l = 0 (after the embedding), 1, 2 (= the model's actual output) the reading is exact at l = L and a DIAGNOSTIC below it: the head was trained on x², and block 2 may write in directions the head cannot read, or rotate what block 1 wrote. it is informative because the blocks are near-identity (§11): x¹ is mostly still in x². two numbers per layer: the probability of the correct token, and the KL from the final output — how far the stream's "current guess" is from where it will end up.

On the copy task the pattern is the one the circuit predicts. After the embedding, the lens reads the current token with high confidence at every position — that is §21's identity leak, and it is exactly what a tied head applied to an embedding row gives. After block 1 the answer positions have moved: the correct next token's probability rises and the current token's falls, because a first-block head has already fetched the token four back. After block 2 the answer is essentially one-hot. On induction the textbook expectation is that the answer arrives only after block 2, where a two-layer circuit closes; what the page's model actually shows at 300 steps is a partial answer already after block 1 (the correct token's mean probability around 0.4–0.9 depending on the example) and little or no further gain from block 2 — the tiny model has found a shallower, position-assisted route through the short prefixes it is trained on, and the lens reports that rather than the textbook. On examples whose prefix repeats a symbol the answer is readable after the embedding alone: that is the identity leak doing the work. The figure draws the three readings for every position as heat strips and plots the correct-token probability and the KL to the final output against depth.

The lens as a decoding rule

The same read-out is used at inference by one of the decoding rules of §48. Contrasting layers ("DoLa") computes the lens distribution at an earlier layer and at the last one and prefers tokens whose probability grew between them — on the reasoning that the later layers add content the earlier ones did not have. It is this section's diagnostic turned into an objective; §48 works one step of it by hand.

Four things the table decides, none of them by taste. The objective's efficiency: a decoder gets a loss from every position of every document, an encoder from the fraction it masked, an encoder–decoder from the target side only — a 2–7× difference in supervised positions per token processed, and one of the most-cited reasons the decoder-only stack won at scale. The input's representation: only the two stacks with a bidirectional component see the whole input at every position, which is why encoders have tended to win on understanding tasks at equal size and why the encoder–decoder has stayed competitive on translation. The cost of long inputs: for a decoder the input and output share one quadratic term over n_src + n_tgt; the encoder–decoder pays n_src² once and then a linear cross term, which is cheaper when the output is long and the input is fixed. Generation: an encoder has no native way to produce text. The decision guide built on these four is Encoder vs Decoder Models; the cards are BERT (encoder), GPT (decoder), and the T5/BART families for the third column.
