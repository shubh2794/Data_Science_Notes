18 Amortized analysis, and how it differs from the average case

Amortized analysis averages the cost of an operation over a sequence of operations, in the worst case, with no probability anywhere. It exists because multiplying "worst case per operation" by "number of operations" often gives an answer that no execution can actually achieve — the expensive operations are expensive because of work the cheap ones did not have to do, so they cannot all be expensive at once.

the definitionif every sequence of n operations costs at most T(n) in total, then the amortized cost per operation is T(n)/n the guarantee this gives∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ (actual cost of operation i) ≤ n · (amortized cost) for EVERY sequence what it does NOT giveany bound at all on one individual operation

The motivating over-count

Take a stack supporting PUSH, POP and MULTIPOP(S, k), which pops min(k, s) items. A single MULTIPOP can cost Θ(n), so the naive bound for n operations is n operations × O(n) each = O(n²). That bound is correct and unachievable. Every item popped must first have been pushed, and there are at most n pushes in the sequence, so the total number of pops — including those inside MULTIPOP — is at most n. The whole sequence costs O(n), and the amortized cost per operation is O(1).

  • Amortized is a worst-case statement. It is not "usually cheap" — it is "provably cheap in total, no matter what an adversary asks for". That is why it is a legitimate guarantee and average case, in an adversarial setting, is not.
  • It is still a statement about a sequence. "This append is O(1) amortized" does not mean this append will be fast. It means that if you do n of them, the total is O(n).
  • Where that distinction bites. In a real-time system, a garbage collector, an audio callback, or any latency-sensitive path, the tail is what matters, and an O(1)-amortized structure with an O(n) worst-case operation will produce a visible stall exactly when it resizes. The engineering responses are to pre-size the structure, to use an incremental variant that spreads the copy over many operations, or to accept the tail and measure it.
  • The bookkeeping is fictional. Credits and potentials exist in the proof, not in the code. Nothing in a dynamic array stores a "credit" field; the accounting is a device for reasoning about a program that is unaware of it.
One sentence to keep. Average case answers "what usually happens?" and needs a distribution. Amortized answers "what does a whole run cost?" and needs none. Confusing them turns a guarantee into a hope.

19 The three methods: aggregate, accounting, potential

Three techniques, increasing in power and in setup cost, that all prove the same kind of statement. The example that shows off all three at once is a k-bit binary counter incremented n times from zero, where the cost of an increment is the number of bits it flips.

the procedure being analysedINCREMENT(A) 1 i = 0 2 while i < A.length and A[i] == 1 3 A[i] = 0 // clear a run of trailing 1s — each is a flip 4 i = i + 1 5 if i < A.length 6 A[i] = 1 // set the first 0 — one more flip the naive boundone INCREMENT flips up to k bits ⇒ n INCREMENTs cost O(n·k) — correct, and not tight

Method 1 — aggregate analysis: bound the total, then divide

  • The observation. Not every bit flips on every increment. Bit A[0] flips every time; A[1] every second time; A[2] every fourth. In general A[i] flips floor(n/2ⁱ) times.
  • The sum. A geometric series, bounded by its infinite version: total flips < n·∑ᵢ₌₀^∞ 1/2ⁱ = 2n.
  • The conclusion. n increments cost O(n), so the amortized cost is O(n)/n = O(1) — and the constant is 2, independent of k.
  • Its limitation. Aggregate analysis assigns the same amortized cost to every operation. When a structure has several operation types with genuinely different costs, that is too blunt.
the aggregate sumtotal flips = ∑ᵢ₌₀^(k−1) floor(n/2ⁱ) < n·∑ᵢ₌₀^∞ (1/2)ⁱ = 2n amortized cost = 2n/n = 2 = O(1)

  • Aggregate. Total = 15 flips for 8 increments, so 15/8 = 1.875 < 2. The formula agrees exactly: ∑ᵢ₌₀³ floor(8/2ⁱ) = 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 15 ✓ — and you can read the four terms straight off the column, since bit 0 flipped on all 8 increments, bit 1 on 4 of them, bit 2 on 2 and bit 3 on 1.
  • Accounting. Every increment in the trace sets exactly one bit (the final if of the procedure), so each is charged 2: charges = 2·8 = 16. Of the 15 actual flips, 8 were sets and 7 were clears, and each clear was paid by the credit parked on that bit when it was set. Charges 16 ≥ 15 ✓, with 8 − 7 = 1 credit left over — sitting on the single 1 standing in 1000 at the end. Leftover credit = the final number of 1s, which is the accounting method's version of the next bullet.
  • Potential. ĉᵢ = 2 on every row without exception, which is what the cancellation (tᵢ + 1) + (1 − tᵢ) = 2 promised. And the telescoping identity checks out: ∑ĉᵢ = 16, ∑cᵢ = 15, Φ(D₈) − Φ(D₀) = 1 − 0 = 1, and 16 = 15 + 1 ✓.
  • The expensive operation is exactly the one the potential has been paying for. Row 8 costs 4 — the worst in the trace — and it is preceded by three rows that each banked +1 of potential. That is the whole mechanism, on eight lines.

Method 2 — the accounting method: charge more, bank the surplus

  • The device. Assign each operation an amortized charge that may differ from its actual cost. Overcharge stores credit on specific objects in the structure; undercharge spends credit already there.
  • The obligation. The credit balance must never go negative — equivalently ∑ĉᵢ ≥ ∑cᵢ for every prefix of every sequence. Violate that and the "amortized" total stops being an upper bound on the real total.
  • For the counter: charge 2 to set a bit from 0 to 1. One unit pays for the flip; one is left on that bit as credit. Clearing a bit back to 0 is then charged 0, paid for by the credit sitting on it. Since INCREMENT sets at most one bit, its amortized cost is at most 2. The number of 1s is never negative, so neither is the credit. Amortized O(1).
  • For the stack: charge PUSH 2, POP 0, MULTIPOP 0. Each pushed item carries a credit that pays for its own eventual pop, however that pop happens. Different operations, different amortized costs — the thing aggregate analysis cannot express.

Method 3 — the potential method: credit attached to the structure, not to objects

the definitionΦ maps each state Dᵢ of the structure to a real number ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φ(Dᵢ) − Φ(Dᵢ₋₁) — amortized = actual + change in potential summing, and telescoping∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ ĉᵢ = ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ cᵢ + Φ(Dₙ) − Φ(D₀) so the amortized total bounds the actual total wheneverΦ(Dₙ) ≥ Φ(D₀); in practice one shows Φ(D₀) = 0 and Φ(Dᵢ) ≥ 0 for all i
  • The intuition. Φ is stored energy. An operation that leaves the structure in a more "loaded" state pays extra now and raises the potential; an expensive operation is paid for by the potential it releases.
  • For the counter, take Φ = bᵢ, the number of 1s. If the i-th increment clears tᵢ bits, its actual cost is at most tᵢ + 1, and the number of 1s changes by at most 1 − tᵢ. So ĉᵢ ≤ (tᵢ + 1) + (1 − tᵢ) = 2. The tᵢ cancels — which is the whole trick, and the reason the bound is independent of how long the run of trailing 1s was.
  • It handles a non-zero starting state, which the others do not. Rearranging the telescoped sum gives ∑cᵢ = ∑ĉᵢ − Φ(Dₙ) + Φ(D₀) ≤ 2n − bₙ + b₀. So a counter starting at an arbitrary value with b₀ ones still costs O(n) for n increments as long as b₀ = O(n) — a strictly stronger theorem than the aggregate argument can reach.
  • Choosing Φ is the creative step. Different potentials give different (valid) amortized costs; the right one measures exactly the "debt" the structure is carrying toward its next expensive operation. §20 constructs one from that principle rather than guessing.
A real binary counter — every bar is a measured flip count the green line is the running average; the theory says it stays under 2

The bars are the measured cost of each increment and they are wildly uneven: exactly half of them flip a single bit, a quarter flip two, and the occasional one flips six or seven. The running average is not uneven at all — it settles almost immediately and never crosses the dashed line at 2. The readout cross-checks the measurement against the aggregate argument by evaluating ∑ᵢ₌₀^(k−1) floor(n/2ⁱ) independently; the two agree exactly at every setting of both sliders.

Now widen the counter with the k slider, and read the result precisely, because the obvious summary of it is wrong. At n = 64 the worst single operation is 6 flips when k = 6 and 7 flips for every k ≥ 7 — it does not keep growing. It cannot: counting from zero, the longest run of trailing 1s the counter ever holds within n increments is floor(log₂ n) — the run of j ones sitting in 2ʲ − 1, cleared by the increment at step 2ʲ — so the worst operation saturates at floor(log₂ n) + 1 flips and any bits above that are never touched. At n = 64 that is 6 + 1 = 7 ✓. Push n to 256 and the saturation point moves with it — 6, 7, 8, then 9 flips for every k ≥ 9, since floor(log₂ 256) + 1 = 9. The honest statement of what the two sliders show is this: the worst single operation costs exactly min(k, floor(log₂ n) + 1) flips, while the running average stays under 2 no matter what either slider says. The second half of that sentence is the content of "the amortized cost is independent of k"; the first half is why the naive O(n·k) bound was loose in the first place.

Which method to reach for. Aggregate when one operation type dominates and the total is easy to bound. Accounting when different operations should carry different charges and there is an obvious object to park credit on. Potential when the structure's state is what determines the coming cost, when you need a non-empty starting state, or when the other two get tangled — it is the most mechanical of the three once Φ is chosen.

20 The dynamic array, worked three ways

The case study that justifies the whole chapter, because it is under every list, vector and ArrayList you have ever used. A table stores items in a contiguous block; when it fills, a bigger block is allocated and everything is copied. A single insert can cost Θ(n). A sequence of n inserts costs Θ(n) in total.

TABLE-INSERT, with cost counted in elementary insertionsTABLE-INSERT(T, x) 1 if T.size == 0: allocate T.table with 1 slot; T.size = 1 2 if T.num == T.size: 3 allocate new-table with 2 · T.size slots 4 copy all T.num items across // this is the expensive part 5 free T.table; T.table = new-table; T.size = 2 · T.size 6 insert x; T.num = T.num + 1 the cost of the i-th insertcᵢ = i if i − 1 is an exact power of 2 (an expansion happened) cᵢ = 1 otherwise

Aggregate: the geometric series again

split the total into the cheap part and the expansions∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ cᵢ ≤ n + ∑ⱼ₌₀^(floor(log₂ n)) 2ʲ < n + 2n = 3n soamortized cost ≤ 3n/n = 3 = O(1)

Every insert pays 1 for placing its own item — that is the n. The copies happen only at sizes 1, 2, 4, …, and those sum to less than 2n because a geometric series is dominated by its last term. The deep point: the sum of all previous doublings is smaller than the current one. Everything about amortized array growth is that sentence.

Accounting: why the charge is exactly 3

Charge each insert 3 credits. One pays to place the item now. One is banked on the item itself, to pay for moving it at the next expansion. One is banked on an item in the older half of the table, which already spent its own moving credit in the previous expansion. When the table of size m fills, it holds m/2 items that arrived before the last expansion and m/2 that arrived after; the later m/2 arrivals have deposited exactly m/2 credits on themselves and m/2 on the older items, so every one of the m items has a credit to pay for its own copy. The bank is never overdrawn, and 3 is exactly enough.

Potential: designing Φ instead of guessing it

Build the function from its requirements rather than pulling it out of the air. It must be 0 right after an expansion (nothing owed — the table is half full and has just been paid for) and must have grown to num by the time the table is full (enough stored energy to pay for copying every item). Right after an expansion num = size/2; right before the next one num = size. A linear function meeting both is immediate:

the potentialΦ(T) = 2·T.num − T.size check the two design constraintsjust after an expansion, num = size/2 ⇒ Φ = 0 ✓ just before one, num = size ⇒ Φ = num ✓ the table is always at least half full ⇒ Φ ≥ 0 ✓ no expansion: cᵢ = 1, sizeᵢ = sizeᵢ₋₁ĉᵢ = 1 + (2·numᵢ − sizeᵢ) − (2·(numᵢ−1) − sizeᵢ) = 1 + 2 = 3 expansion: cᵢ = numᵢ, sizeᵢ = 2·sizeᵢ₋₁ = 2·(numᵢ − 1)ĉᵢ = numᵢ + (2·numᵢ − 2(numᵢ−1)) − (2(numᵢ−1) − (numᵢ−1)) = numᵢ + 2 − (numᵢ − 1) = 3

Both branches give exactly 3 — the expensive case and the cheap case have the same amortized cost, which is what a well-chosen potential buys you. The numᵢ terms cancel, exactly as the tᵢ terms did for the counter.

Per-append cost, with the running amortized mean every bar and every total below is counted during the run

The picture is the whole idea: a flat floor of cost-1 appends, punctuated by spikes that double in height and halve in frequency, and a running mean that stays bounded while the spikes grow without bound. Read the running mean carefully — it is a sawtooth, not a flat line. At the default (g = 2, n = 64) it jumps to 2.91 at append 33, the moment the 32-item copy is charged, then decays to 1.98 by append 64 as 31 cost-1 appends dilute it. It never reaches 3, and it does not settle at 2 either; it cycles between roughly 2 and just under 3, once per doubling, forever.

That also corrects the natural misreading of the readout. At n = 64 the measured amortized cost is 127/64 = 1.98, and the tempting conclusion is that the 3 of the accounting argument is simply generous. It is not: n = 64 is the most flattering place to stop, because the table has just filled exactly and no capacity is being paid for twice. Nudge the slider one step to n = 72 and the measured cost is 2.76; stop at n = 65 — one append after the expansion that copies 64 items — and it is 2.95. The bound of 3 is very nearly tight, and where you stop counting decides which impression you get. That is worth internalising as a general hazard of amortized measurement: a partial sequence is not a sequence, and an amortized figure quoted at a lucky stopping point is an advertisement, not a bound.

Now move the growth factor. Drop it to 1.1 and the spikes become frequent and small while the amortized cost climbs to about 12; raise it to 4 and it falls to about 1.33, but the table now wastes up to three quarters of its space immediately after every resize. That is the real trade-off — time ≈ g/(g−1) per append against wasted space ≈ 1 − 1/g — and the conventional choice of 2 is a point on that curve, not a law: 1.5 is common in implementations that want the allocator to be able to reuse the previously freed blocks, and at least one widely used dynamic array grows by only about 1.125 plus a small constant, trading ~9 elementary insertions per append for very low memory slack. The readout prints the g/(g−1) prediction beside the measurement. The two agree only in the limit, and the gap you see is the sawtooth again, not an error in either. At g = 1.5, n = 64 the measurement reads 3.97 against a prediction of 3 — because with that factor the capacities run 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 13, 19, 28, 42, 63, 94 and append number 64 is exactly the expansion out of capacity 63, so the run ends by paying for 63 copies it has had no appends to amortise against. Move to n = 128 and the same setting reads 3.22. One genuinely different effect is also visible at small sizes: a rule stated as a factor cannot grow a 1-slot table at all (floor(1 × 1.5) = 1), so the figure — like every real implementation — falls back on max(size + 1, floor(size·g)), which means the first few expansions add a single slot and the geometric argument has not started yet. Real containers handle this with a minimum initial capacity instead.

The same run, seen as potential: cᵢ, Φᵢ, and ĉᵢ = cᵢ + Φᵢ − Φᵢ₋₁ the amortized-cost line is flat at 3 — measured, not assumed

The potential is a sawtooth: it climbs by 2 per append, reaching num when the table is exactly full, and the expansion spends almost all of it. Precisely: at the instant the table of size m has just been reallocated to 2m and the m old items copied, Φ = 2m − 2m = 0 — which is the design constraint it was built to satisfy — and then the new item is placed in the same operation, so the value you see plotted immediately after every expansion is Φ = 2, not 0. (Rows i = 3, 5, 9, 17, 33 in the table all read Φ = 2.) The actual cost is spiky. Their sum is a flat line at 3, apart from the very first insert — which costs 2, because allocating the initial one-slot table is not an expansion and there is nothing yet to copy. The table shows the first ten operations with every column measured during the run, so the two algebraic branches derived above can be checked row by row: the rows with ΔΦ = +2 are the cheap branch, where cᵢ = 1; the rows where ΔΦ is smaller — zero, or sharply negative once the table is large — are the expansions, whose big cᵢ is paid for exactly by the potential it releases. ĉᵢ is 3 on both. The readout also verifies the inequality the whole method rests on, ∑ĉᵢ ≥ ∑cᵢ, and reports the gap as Φₙ − Φ₀.

Deletion, and the trap of shrinking at half

  • The obvious policy is wrong. Double when full, halve when the table drops below half full. Fill a table to exactly n/2, then run insert, delete, delete, insert, insert, …: each insert triggers an expansion and each pair of deletes triggers a contraction, both costing Θ(n), Θ(n) times. Total Θ(n²), amortized Θ(n) — the guarantee is destroyed.
  • The diagnosis. After an expansion the table sits exactly at the contraction threshold, so there is no slack: nothing has been "paid in" before the next resize is demanded. The structure has no hysteresis.
  • The fix. Double when full, but halve only when the table falls below one quarter full. After any resize the load factor is 1/2, so Θ(n) operations must occur before either threshold can be reached again. The load factor stays in [1/4, 1], wasted space is bounded by a constant fraction, and both insert and delete are O(1) amortized.
  • The potential generalises accordingly. Take Φ = 2·num − size when the load factor is at least 1/2, and Φ = size/2 − num when it is below — zero at load factor 1/2 in both branches, rising to num at load factor 1 and at load factor 1/4. The design principle is unchanged: potential measures distance from the comfortable state.
Where this shows up in practice. Every growable array; the hash table's rehash on load factor (see Data Structures); the two-stack queue, where each element is moved between stacks at most once; the disjoint-set forest with path compression; and the reserve/with_capacity call you make when the amortized guarantee is not good enough because the tail is what you are optimising.
