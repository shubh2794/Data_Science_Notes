Dynamic Programming

Dynamic programming solves a problem by solving each of its overlapping subproblems exactly once, storing the answers, and combining them through a recurrence evaluated in a well-founded order. It is the paradigm that takes over when a recursion is correct but its call tree is exponentially larger than the set of distinct questions it asks — and it turns that recursion into a table whose size is the running time. This page states the two properties a problem must have (optimal substructure, with the shortest-path / longest-path contrast that shows how it fails; and overlapping subproblems), builds the method end to end on rod cutting, compares memoization and tabulation honestly with measurements, and then walks the classical recurrences — Fibonacci and its 1-D relatives, coin change in both its forms, 0/1 and unbounded knapsack, weighted interval scheduling, longest common subsequence, edit distance and its linear-space refinement, longest increasing subsequence in Θ(n²) and O(n log n), matrix-chain multiplication, optimal search trees, interval DP, DP on trees, DAGs and subsets, digit DP — with every table filled by the real routine under a counter, every answer cross-checked against brute force in the figure and in a load-time audit, and the hand-offs to Bellman-Ford, value iteration and Viterbi stated as what they are: the same recursion on a different state space.

01 What dynamic programming is

Dynamic programming is a method for solving a problem whose answer is determined by the answers to smaller instances of the same problem — the subproblems — when those subproblems are shared: it solves each distinct subproblem once, records the answer, and evaluates the recurrence over the subproblems in an order that guarantees every answer is available before it is needed. Three words in that sentence carry the definition. Recurrence: the answer to an instance is a fixed function — a max, a min, a sum, a count — of the answers to smaller instances, plus a cost attributable to the choice that links them. Overlapping: the recursion, unrolled, asks the same questions again and again, so there are far fewer distinct subproblems than there are calls. Once: each distinct subproblem is computed a single time and reused thereafter, which converts a call tree of exponential size into a table whose size is the number of distinct subproblems.

Where it sits among the paradigms

Dynamic programming is recursion plus one observation. Divide and conquer also solves a problem by combining the answers to subproblems, but its subproblems are disjoint — mergesort's two halves share nothing, and its call tree, though it has Θ(n) nodes, has no repeated question in it. When the subproblems repeat, divide and conquer does exponential work re-answering them; Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §11 diagnoses the disease ("the call tree is far larger than the set of distinct subproblems") and names the cure. Everything after that diagnosis is this page: how to choose the state so the recurrence is correct, how to order the evaluation, what memoization and tabulation each cost, how to recover the solution and not just its value, and the catalogue of state spaces — prefixes, prefix pairs, intervals, capacities, subtrees, subsets, digits — that cover most problems anyone meets. Greedy algorithms are the other neighbour: they share optimal substructure and add the greedy-choice property, which lets them commit to one choice without comparing subproblems; that page owns the boundary and this page assumes it. Where a greedy rule has a counterexample but a cut-and-paste argument still goes through, the answer is a table.

What "programming" means, and what the method is not

The word is the 1950s sense of planning — a tabular method for multistage decisions — and has nothing to do with writing code. The technique is not a data structure, not a specific algorithm, and not "recursion with a cache" in general: a cache on a recursion whose subproblems never repeat (mergesort) is pure overhead, and a cache on a recursion whose answer depends on state its arguments do not capture returns wrong answers rather than slow ones. Nor is it a guarantee of polynomial time: the running time is the number of states times the work per state, and the number of states is whatever the problem forces it to be — Θ(n) for rod cutting (Θ(n²) time), Θ(nW) for knapsack (exponential in the bit length of W, §12), Θ(n·2ⁿ) for the travelling salesman (Θ(n²·2ⁿ) time, §24). A dynamic program is a promise that the exponential redundancy of naive recursion has been removed; the residual state space is the problem's own.

29 Recognising DP against greedy, divide and conquer, and backtracking

A problem is a dynamic-programming problem when three things hold at once: an optimal solution is built from optimal solutions to subproblems (optimal substructure, checked by cut and paste), the natural recursion asks the same subproblem many times (overlap), and the set of distinct subproblems is small enough to tabulate (a polynomial state space, or an exponential one that is still far smaller than the space of solutions). Each of the three failing routes the problem to a different paradigm: no optimal substructure and there is no correct recurrence at all; overlap absent and the recursion is divide and conquer, where a memo would never hit; a state space too large and the honest tools are backtracking, branch and bound, or a hand-off to NP-hardness. And one further property, the greedy-choice property, lets a problem that has everything DP needs skip the table entirely. This section is the decision procedure, the four paradigms side by side on the questions that separate them, and the quick "state-space smell test" that predicts the cost of a recurrence before it is written.

Step 1 in practice: the cut-and-paste one-liner, and the two ways it fails

The test for optimal substructure is a sentence: "take an optimal solution, look at its first (or last) choice, and argue that what remains is optimal for the residual instance — else swap in something better and contradict optimality". For every problem in §04–§25 that sentence is one line, and the page wrote it each time: the remainder of a rod, the load without item i, the alignment without its last column, the subchain on either side of a split, the subtree under a root. It fails in two recognisable ways. The shared resource failure (§02, §21): the two parts of the solution draw on something in common — vertices, a moving boundary — so improving one part changes the other's instance; the signature is that "swap in a better sub-solution" produces something that is not a legal solution. The hidden history failure (§07): the residual instance depends on how the state was reached, not only on the state; the signature is that two paths to the same state deserve different answers. Both have the same cure, enlarging the state, and the cure has a cost — one bit for the ring robber, the visited set for the salesman — which is what step 4 then measures.

Step 2 in practice: draw the graph before writing the loop

Overlap is the property that separates this page from its predecessor, and the diagnosis is quantitative: count distinct states and count calls. Fibonacci makes 2·F(n + 1) − 1 calls for n + 1 states; mergesort makes 2n − 1 calls for 2n − 1 distinct sub-arrays. The first is a table; the second is a tree, and giving it a memo adds a hash lookup per call and saves nothing. The intermediate case is worth naming: a divide and conquer whose combine step shares work across the split — closest pair's strip, which both halves' points feed — still has no repeated subproblem, is still divide and conquer, and its cost comes from the master theorem, not from a state count. The rule of thumb is the depth of the recursion: a subproblem half the size gives log n depth and a tree that is polynomial without help; a subproblem one smaller gives depth n and a tree that is exponential unless the states are merged (§03).

Step 3 in practice: when the table collapses

Every greedy algorithm is a dynamic program whose max over choices has been replaced by a rule, and the licence to do that is the greedy-choice property: an exchange argument showing that some optimal solution contains the locally chosen element, so the other branches of the max never need to be evaluated. Weighted interval scheduling (§13) shows the collapse and its failure in one recurrence: OPT(j) = max(OPT(j − 1), vⱼ + OPT(p(j))) must compare both branches in general, but with unit weights the earliest-finishing interval is always safe and the comparison can be skipped — the greedy page's §04 proof. Coin change (§10) collapses on canonical systems and not on {1, 3, 4}; knapsack collapses in its fractional form and not in its 0/1 form (160 against 220). The practical procedure is the greedy page's: propose the rule, then hunt for a three-element counterexample; if one exists, the table is the algorithm, and if a proof exists, the table was never needed. What one should not do is run the greedy rule on random instances and infer correctness from agreement — that page's fewest-conflicts rule passed 300 random tests and is wrong.

Step 4 in practice: the state-space smell test

The test is to list the quantities the recurrence's right-hand side needs to know about the subproblem, and nothing more (§07's "too large" failure is what happens when the list is padded). If every entry is an integer bounded by a polynomial in the input size, the table is polynomial and the problem is a DP problem in the strong sense. If one entry is a number from the input — a capacity, a target sum — the table is pseudo-polynomial, fine in practice for small numbers and exponential in the input's bit length. If one entry is a subset, the table is 2ⁿ and the question is whether n ≤ 20. And if the honest state is a sequence or a path, the recurrence is the brute force in disguise, and the choice is between backtracking with pruning, branch and bound with a relaxation bound, an approximation scheme (the FPTAS sketch of §12 is a DP plus rounding), and a heuristic with no guarantee. That last hand-off is not a failure of the method: knapsack, subset sum and the travelling salesman are all NP-hard, and their dynamic programs are the best exact algorithms known for them — the smell test says only what the exponent will be.

Three quick diagnoses

  • "Longest path in a general graph." Optimal substructure fails on four vertices (§02); the state needed is the visited set; the smell test says n·2ⁿ; the answer is Held–Karp for small n and NP-hardness otherwise. On a DAG the same question is a one-pass DP (§23). The graph's acyclicity is the whole difference.
  • "Split a sequence into K contiguous groups at minimum cost." Substructure holds (the last group is independent of the rest); overlap is heavy (the same prefix, many K); the state is (groups, prefix), K·n cells with n choices each — O(Kn²), and O(Kn log n) under the divide-and-conquer optimisation's hypothesis (§27). A greedy "close the group when it exceeds a threshold" has a counterexample in three elements.
  • "Sort n numbers." Substructure holds; overlap does not — the two halves of mergesort are disjoint, and the sub-arrays of quicksort's recursion never repeat; the smell test gives Θ(n) distinct sub-arrays in a balanced tree. Divide and conquer, Θ(n log n), and a memo would only slow it down.
The one-paragraph version. Cut and paste says whether a recurrence is correct; the subproblem graph says whether a table is useful; an exchange argument says whether the table can be skipped; the smell test says whether it can be afforded. Answer the four in that order and the paradigm is decided — and most of the difficulty of dynamic programming in practice is the first step, choosing a state for which the first answer is yes.

30 Pitfalls

Every pitfall below was exhibited by a routine on this page and caught by a brute-force comparison in a figure — the wrong answers are measured numbers, not hypotheticals. Most of them are of one kind: the recurrence was right and the evaluation was wrong (an order, a base, a cell), so the program computed a plausible number for a different question. The cure in every case is the same habit the figures practise — run the table beside an exhaustive enumeration on an instance small enough to enumerate, and do not trust the table until they agree.

  • The capacity loop's direction decides which knapsack is being solved (§12). The same three lines return 46 swept downward and 48 swept upward on the four worked items at W = 10; the upward pass reads f[w − wᵢ] after it has been rewritten in the same pass, and the extra item that enters is a second copy. Neither number is wrong — each is the exact answer to its own problem, confirmed by its own brute force (16 subsets, 28 multisets) — which is why the bug survives testing: it is a change of problem, not an arithmetic error.
  • The coin loops' nesting decides what is counted (§10). Coins outer counts multisets, amount outer counts sequences; on {1, 2, 5} at A = 5 that is 4 against 9, at A = 11 it is 11 against 218. Both nests perform the same 10 updates from the same base, and the min recurrence is indifferent to the order — so the bug is invisible in the fewest-coins version and silent in the counting ones. Test on an amount two coins both fit.
  • A state that forgets what the future depends on returns wrong answers, silently (§02, §07). "Best over the prefix" for the house robber gives 16 where the optimum is 19; the linear robber on a ring gives illegal 4 and 10 on 2, 3, 2 and 5, 1, 1, 5; "longest simple path ending at v" has no recurrence at all. The signature is two paths to the same state deserving different answers; the cure is to carry the missing quantity (the last element, one bit, the visited set) and to accept the cost of carrying it.
  • "Burst first" is not "burst last" (§21). Choosing the first balloon to burst leaves two sides that share a moving boundary, and the memoized version of that recurrence returns 66 on [3, 1, 5, 8] where the optimum, found by choosing the last balloon and confirmed over all 24 orders, is 167. The same trap catches every "remove an element and its neighbours touch" problem; the fix is to condition on the operation whose context is fixed for the whole history of the range.
  • The tails array is not the longest increasing subsequence (§18). On [2, 6, 3, 4, 1] the final array is [1, 3, 4], which is not a subsequence of the input; its length is the answer and its contents are the smallest known endpoints of subsequences of each length, drawn from different subsequences. Reconstruction needs the predecessor links recorded at placement time; reading the array as the answer is wrong the moment a small element arrives late. The worked instance, where the array happens to be a valid LIS, is the misleading case.
  • ⌈log₂ k⌉ is not ⌊log₂ k⌋ + 1 (§12). The number of bundles binary splitting makes from k copies is ⌊log₂ k⌋ + 1 = ⌈log₂(k + 1)⌉: one bundle for k = 1, three for k = 4, four for k = 8. The tidier ⌈log₂ k⌉ gives zero bundles for k = 1 and one too few at every power of two; the routine was checked against both formulas for k = 1..20. Off-by-one in a logarithm is an off-by-one in an item count.
  • Pseudo-polynomial is not polynomial (§10, §12). Θ(nW) counts the cells honestly and is exponential in the number of bits of W; the same for Θ(m·A) in coin change and Θ(nT) in subset sum. The bound is fine when the numbers are small, is the reason the FPTAS works (round the values, then it is polynomial), and is no contradiction of NP-hardness. Saying "knapsack is in P because the DP is O(nW)" is the error; the input size is n log W, not nW.
  • Memoization keeps the recursion's depth (§03, §06). The memoized rod-cutting routine is n + 1 frames deep before its first return, Fibonacci n, LCS m + n; a table of a few thousand entries can exhaust a runtime's depth counter (Python's default limit is 1 000), a few hundred thousand a raw 8 MiB stack (Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §06), while the bottom-up loop uses none. The §06 figure prints the depth beside the counts. Convert to a loop, or run the memo with an explicit stack, before n is large.
  • Rolling the table deletes the walk-back (§26). The one-row knapsack and the two-row LCS return the right value and cannot reconstruct: the cells the walk-back reads are gone. Decide before writing the loop whether the solution is wanted (§07, item 6); if it is, keep the choice bits, checkpoint, or use Hirschberg — and note that on a 7 × 6 table checkpointing every third row keeps 8 rows alive, which saves nothing.
  • Hirschberg's "twice the table" is a slogan, not a bound (§16). The exact bound is n·∑ₖ⌈m/2ᵏ⌉, which equals 2mn only up to the ceilings; on kitten → sitting the measured 80 cells sit under both (84), but over 3 000 random pairs of lengths up to 14 the checking script found 241 that exceeded the bare 2mn and none that exceeded the true bound. Quote the sum, or say "about twice".
  • Ties make the reconstructed solution a convention (§04, §14, §20, §24). Rod cutting at n = 7 has five optimal cuttings and the table returns 1 + 6; the LCS table yields BCBA preferring up and BDAB preferring left, and a third LCS exists; two roots (k₂ and k₄) give the same optimal search-tree cost 2.75; three of four closing candidates tie at 26 in Held–Karp. The value is unique, the solution is not, and a test that compares reconstructed solutions across implementations will fail on every tie. Compare values, or fix the tie rule in the specification.
  • The base case is where the off-by-one lives (§08, §10, §20). W[0] = 1 for the staircase (setting it to 0 loses the single 2-step and makes W[2] = 1); E[0] = a[0] for Kadane (a base of 0 returns 0 on −3, −1, −2 instead of −1); C[0] = 1 for counting and N[0] = 0 for minimising; e[i][i − 1] = qᵢ₋₁ for the empty search tree, not 0; and Fibonacci at n = 1 is a base case whose subproblem graph has no edges, which the §05 figure shows. The empty solution's value — 0, 1, ∞, the first element, a dummy probability — is a decision, and the wrong one is a plausible number.
  • The answer is not always in the last cell (§07, §08, §18). Kadane's optimum 6 is E[6], not E[8] = 5; the LIS is max over i of dp[i], first attained at i = 7; the unbounded knapsack's best load may not use all the capacity. When the objective may end anywhere, the answer is a maximum over states, and reading the final state answers the question "best that ends at n".
  • A DP optimisation is a theorem with a hypothesis (§20, §27). Knuth's window is licensed by the quadrangle inequality on w(i, j), which the search-tree cost satisfies and the matrix-chain cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ does not; the narrowed loop on the wrong problem returns the best of the candidates it happened to try. The §20 figure verifies monotonicity on the finished table rather than assuming it; do the same before deleting a loop.
  • Beam search is not an approximate DP (§28). A width-1 beam on the figure's trellis returns a path of probability 1.54·10⁻⁴ where Viterbi finds 4.59·10⁻⁴, and across all 64 observation strings of length 6 on that model width 1 fails on 33 and width 2 on 23. Viterbi keeps a value per state and is exact; the beam ranks hypotheses and discards, and its answer is a lower bound with no guarantee. Reporting a beam result as "the most likely sequence" is a false claim.
  • Boundary conventions change p(j) and the table (§13). Under half-open intervals [3, 5) and [5, 9) are compatible and p(6) = 2; under closed intervals they are not and p(6) = 1. The binary search that computes p must use the same inequality as the compatibility test, and a silent change of ≤ to < is a different problem with a different optimum.
  • Closed forms in floating point stop being exact (§08). round(φⁿ/√5) agrees with the Fibonacci loop for every n ≤ 70 and first fails at n = 71, returning one more than 308 061 521 170 129. A DP over integers is exact; a closed form in doubles is exact until it is not, and the boundary is not where intuition puts it.
  • "Increasing S" is a valid subset order; "increasing j, then S" is not (§24). The Held–Karp state (S, j) reads (S ∖ {j}, i), and S ∖ {j} < S numerically, so a single loop over masks is a valid order; but a loop with the end city outside the subset loop would read entries not yet written. The order of a bitmask DP is by subset, and the other index is inside.

Where this goes next in the series. The two algorithms this page named and did not build — Bellman-Ford with its negative-cycle test and Floyd-Warshall with its all-pairs table, together with Dijkstra and Johnson's reweighting — are the subject of Shortest Paths, MST & Flow, and the hand-off of §12 and §24 — why knapsack, subset sum and the travelling salesman have pseudo-polynomial or exponential dynamic programs and nothing better, the FPTAS that a DP plus rounding yields, and the taxonomy of NP-hardness — is the subject of NP-Completeness & Approximation. Upstream, the recursion whose redundancy this page removed is Recursion, Divide & Conquer; the paradigm that skips the table when an exchange argument allows it is Greedy Algorithms; the topological order that every DAG DP walks, and the DFS whose post-order memoization discovers, are Graphs; and the cost language, including what "pseudo-polynomial" means for the size of an input, is Algorithm Analysis.
Where it goes sideways, out of this domain. The Bellman equation of §28 is the foundation of Reinforcement Learning — value iteration and policy iteration are its planning algorithms when the model is known, and temporal-difference learning is the same backup sampled from experience when it is not. Viterbi and the forward algorithm are the decoding and evaluation problems of Hidden Markov Models & CRFs, where the trellis carries tags over words; CTC, the forward recursion on a blank-padded trellis, is the training objective of the end-to-end recognisers in Speech Processing; and the edit-distance and LCS tables of §14–§16, with a substitution matrix and gap penalties in place of unit costs, are global (Needleman–Wunsch) and, with the max-with-0 base named in §15, local (Smith–Waterman) sequence alignment in bioinformatics — Hirschberg's split is how genome-length alignments fit in memory. The beam search that §28 set against Viterbi is the decoder of every neural sequence model, and the greedy page's §25 is where its heuristic status is discussed.
