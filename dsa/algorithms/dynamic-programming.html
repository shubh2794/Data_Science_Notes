Dynamic Programming

Dynamic programming solves a problem by solving each of its overlapping subproblems exactly once, storing the answers, and combining them through a recurrence evaluated in a well-founded order. It is the paradigm that takes over when a recursion is correct but its call tree is exponentially larger than the set of distinct questions it asks — and it turns that recursion into a table whose size is the running time. This page states the two properties a problem must have (optimal substructure, with the shortest-path / longest-path contrast that shows how it fails; and overlapping subproblems), builds the method end to end on rod cutting, compares memoization and tabulation honestly with measurements, and then walks the classical recurrences — Fibonacci and its 1-D relatives, coin change in both its forms, 0/1 and unbounded knapsack, weighted interval scheduling, longest common subsequence, edit distance and its linear-space refinement, longest increasing subsequence in Θ(n²) and O(n log n), matrix-chain multiplication, optimal search trees, interval DP, DP on trees, DAGs and subsets, digit DP — with every table filled by the real routine under a counter, every answer cross-checked against brute force in the figure and in a load-time audit, and the hand-offs to Bellman-Ford, value iteration and Viterbi stated as what they are: the same recursion on a different state space.

01 What dynamic programming is

Dynamic programming is a method for solving a problem whose answer is determined by the answers to smaller instances of the same problem — the subproblems — when those subproblems are shared: it solves each distinct subproblem once, records the answer, and evaluates the recurrence over the subproblems in an order that guarantees every answer is available before it is needed. Three words in that sentence carry the definition. Recurrence: the answer to an instance is a fixed function — a max, a min, a sum, a count — of the answers to smaller instances, plus a cost attributable to the choice that links them. Overlapping: the recursion, unrolled, asks the same questions again and again, so there are far fewer distinct subproblems than there are calls. Once: each distinct subproblem is computed a single time and reused thereafter, which converts a call tree of exponential size into a table whose size is the number of distinct subproblems.

Where it sits among the paradigms

Dynamic programming is recursion plus one observation. Divide and conquer also solves a problem by combining the answers to subproblems, but its subproblems are disjoint — mergesort's two halves share nothing, and its call tree, though it has Θ(n) nodes, has no repeated question in it. When the subproblems repeat, divide and conquer does exponential work re-answering them; Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §11 diagnoses the disease ("the call tree is far larger than the set of distinct subproblems") and names the cure. Everything after that diagnosis is this page: how to choose the state so the recurrence is correct, how to order the evaluation, what memoization and tabulation each cost, how to recover the solution and not just its value, and the catalogue of state spaces — prefixes, prefix pairs, intervals, capacities, subtrees, subsets, digits — that cover most problems anyone meets. Greedy algorithms are the other neighbour: they share optimal substructure and add the greedy-choice property, which lets them commit to one choice without comparing subproblems; that page owns the boundary and this page assumes it. Where a greedy rule has a counterexample but a cut-and-paste argument still goes through, the answer is a table.

What "programming" means, and what the method is not

The word is the 1950s sense of planning — a tabular method for multistage decisions — and has nothing to do with writing code. The technique is not a data structure, not a specific algorithm, and not "recursion with a cache" in general: a cache on a recursion whose subproblems never repeat (mergesort) is pure overhead, and a cache on a recursion whose answer depends on state its arguments do not capture returns wrong answers rather than slow ones. Nor is it a guarantee of polynomial time: the running time is the number of states times the work per state, and the number of states is whatever the problem forces it to be — Θ(n) for rod cutting (Θ(n²) time), Θ(nW) for knapsack (exponential in the bit length of W, §11), Θ(n·2ⁿ) for the travelling salesman (Θ(n²·2ⁿ) time, §22). A dynamic program is a promise that the exponential redundancy of naive recursion has been removed; the residual state space is the problem's own.

02 Optimal substructure: the cut-and-paste argument, and the case where it fails

A problem has optimal substructure when an optimal solution to any instance contains, as its parts, optimal solutions to the subproblems those parts define. It is the property that makes a recurrence correct: if the best answer to the whole is built from the best answers to the pieces, then "take the best over all first choices of (cost of the choice) + (best answer to what remains)" really does compute the optimum. Without it the recurrence computes something, and that something is wrong. Its original statement, from the multistage-decision setting in which the method was invented, is Bellman's principle of optimality: an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial state and first decision, the remaining decisions form an optimal policy for the state that results. That is the same claim with "policy" for solution and "state" for subproblem, and the Bellman equation of §26 is the principle written out as a recurrence.

the property, stated for a maximisation with one subproblem per choicelet S* be an optimal solution of instance I, and let c be the first choice S* makes, leaving residual instance I − c. CLAIM: the part of S* that solves I − c is an OPTIMAL solution of I − c. PROOF (cut and paste): if some solution T of I − c were better, then c ∪ T would be a solution of I with value cost(c) + value(T) > cost(c) + value(S* restricted to I − c) = value(S*) — contradicting S*'s optimality. ∎ HENCE: OPT(I) = max over choices c of [ cost(c) + OPT(I − c) ] ← the recurrence is licensed by the property the same property as the principle of optimalityPRINCIPLE OF OPTIMALITY whatever the initial state and the first decision, the remaining decisions must form an optimal policy for the state the first decision produces — "first decision" is the choice c above, "the state that results" is the residual instance I − c, and "optimal policy for it" is the claim just proved the four-step discovery pattern, used for every recurrence on this page1. a solution consists of making a CHOICE (the first cut, the split point, whether to take item i, the root of the subtree) 2. suppose the choice that leads to an optimum is HANDED to you; do not yet ask how to find it 3. characterise the SUBPROBLEMS the choice leaves — and keep the space of subproblems as small as possible, then widen it only when forced 4. show the subproblem solutions inside the optimum are themselves optimal, by cut and paste then the recurrence tries EVERY choice (step 2's gift is replaced by a max/min), and the number of choices × the number of subproblems is the cost

Two things the property must be checked for, not assumed

Optimal substructure varies along two axes that decide the cost. How many subproblems an optimal solution uses: rod cutting (§04), in its "first piece then the remainder" form, uses one — the remainder; matrix-chain multiplication (§17) uses two — the chains on either side of the split. How many choices there are for the subproblem(s): n for the first piece of a rod, j − i for the split point of a chain. The running time is, informally, (number of distinct subproblems) × (number of choices per subproblem): Θ(n)·O(n) = O(n²) for rod cutting, Θ(n²)·O(n) = O(n³) for matrix chains. The subproblem space must also be closed: if solving A₁…Aⱼ could require solving Aₖ₊₁…Aⱼ, then "prefixes" is not a closed space and the state must be widened to "intervals" Aᵢ…Aⱼ. A space that is too small produces a recurrence that references states it never defined; a space that is too large produces a correct algorithm that is slower than necessary. §07 gives the checklist.

The classic failure: shortest simple paths have it, longest simple paths do not

The property is not automatic, and the standard demonstration is a pair of problems that look identical. Unweighted shortest path: the fewest edges from u to v in a directed graph. If p is a shortest u → v path and w is any vertex on it, then the u → w part of p is a shortest u → w path — cut and paste: a shorter u → w path spliced onto the w → v part would give a shorter u → v path. (If the spliced walk repeated a vertex, excising the loop between the two visits would give a still shorter u → v walk, and a walk shorter than p contains a path shorter than p — the same contradiction. For longest simple paths this is exactly the step that fails.) So shortest paths have optimal substructure, and the DP over "shortest path to each vertex" (§21) and the all-pairs recurrence of Floyd-Warshall both rest on it. Unweighted longest simple path: the most edges from u to v without repeating a vertex. The word "simple" is forced — without it a cycle could be traversed forever — and it is exactly what breaks the property.

the counterexample — a directed 4-cycle with one chordvertices q, r, s, t; edges q→r, r→t, q→s, s→t, t→r, r→q (the cycle q→s→t→r→q, with chords q→r and r→t) a longest simple path from q to t is q → r → t (2 edges; q → s → t also has 2) is its prefix q → r a longest simple path from q to r? NO: q → s → t → r has 3 edges is its suffix r → t a longest simple path from r to t? NO: r → q → s → t has 3 edges and gluing those two "optimal" subpaths gives q → s → t → r → q → s → t — not even a simple path, so not a legal solution why: the subproblems are not independentthe u→w subproblem CONSUMES vertices (s and t) that the w→v subproblem then cannot use. Solving one changes the other's instance. shortest-path subproblems share no resource (a shared vertex would shorten the path); longest-simple-path subproblems compete for vertices. "independent" (no shared resources) and "overlapping" (the same subproblem reached along different routes) are DIFFERENT notions, and DP needs both.

The consequence is not a slower algorithm but no algorithm: longest simple path is NP-hard, and no polynomial dynamic program for it is known. The lesson generalises. Whenever the pieces of a solution compete for a shared resource — vertices in a path, a budget that both halves draw on, a constraint that one subproblem's choice imposes on another — the naive state does not capture enough, and either the state must be enlarged to carry the shared resource (the travelling-salesman state of §22 carries the entire set of visited cities, which is why it is exponential) or the problem is not a DP problem at all. The greedy page's §02 makes the same point from the other side: optimal substructure is necessary for greedy and for DP alike; the two paradigms differ in whether the choice can be made before the subproblems are solved.

The test to apply. Write the recurrence, then try to break it: find an instance where the optimum's parts are not optimal for their subproblems. For shortest paths, knapsack, LCS, matrix chains and every problem in §08–§23 the cut-and-paste argument goes through in one line. For longest simple paths it fails on four vertices. If you cannot write the one line, do not trust the table.

03 Overlapping subproblems: the call tree has 2ⁿ nodes, the subproblem graph has n + 1

A problem has overlapping subproblems when its natural recursion reaches the same subproblem along more than one path. Optimal substructure makes the recurrence correct; overlap is what makes remembering answers pay. The gap between the two counts — the size of the call tree and the number of distinct subproblems — is the entire speed-up of dynamic programming, and it is worth seeing measured before it is seen proved.

When memoization applies, and the disease it does not cure

A memo helps exactly when the recursion revisits states, and is safe exactly when a state's answer depends only on its arguments — referential transparency, or for optimisation problems, optimal substructure. Both are needed. Mergesort has the second property and not the first: its subproblems are distinct halves, a memo would never hit, and the Θ(n log n) bound is already optimal. A recursion over mutable global state has the first and not the second: caching it returns stale answers. Overlap is also the property that distinguishes the two paradigms' recursion shapes. In divide and conquer a subproblem is much smaller — half the size — so the tree is shallow (log n levels) and has polynomially many nodes even without a memo. In a typical dynamic program a subproblem is only slightly smaller — n − 1, or one fewer item, or one shorter prefix — so the tree has polynomial depth and exponentially many nodes, nearly all of them repeats. That depth is the cost memoization does not remove: the memoized rod-cutting routine is still n + 1 frames deep — CUT(n) down to CUT(0) — before the first value returns, and Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §05–§06 explains why a Θ(n)-deep recursion is a real limit. Tabulation (§06) removes it; the memo does not.

04 Rod cutting: from 2ⁿ to Θ(n²), with reconstruction

Rod cutting asks for the most revenue obtainable from a rod of length n when a piece of length i sells for pᵢ and the rod may be cut, at no cost, into any number of integer-length pieces. Its recurrence — the best value of a rod of length j is the best over the length i of the first piece of pᵢ plus the best value of the remaining j − i — is the one §03 unrolled into a tree of 2ⁿ calls, and the point of this section is to run that same recurrence over its n + 1 distinct subproblems in increasing order, so that every answer it needs has already been written down. The result is a table r[0..n] filled in Θ(n²) time, a companion table s[1..n] that records which first piece won each maximum, and from s an actual optimal cutting recovered in O(n) further steps. Why the recurrence is correct: cut and paste, one line

An optimal cutting of a rod of length j has some first piece, of length i for some 1 ≤ i ≤ j (a rod of positive length is sold either whole, i = j, or with a first cut somewhere). The rest of that cutting is a cutting of a rod of length j − i, and it must be an optimal one: if a better cutting of j − i existed, replacing the rest with it — the paste — would give a cutting of j worth more than the optimum, a contradiction. So the optimum is pᵢ + r[j − i] for the right i, and since the recurrence takes the maximum over every i it cannot miss the right one. This is exactly §02's argument, and it goes through because the two parts of a cutting — the first piece and the remainder — share nothing: the remainder can be cut however it likes without affecting the first piece. Overlap, the other property, is what §03 measured: the recursion asks for r[j − i] along many paths, and the table answers each question once.

Hand-worked: n = 4, then n = 7

The table is built by evaluating the recurrence's candidates for each j in turn. With r[0] = 0: r[1] = p₁ + r[0] = 1, s[1] = 1. r[2] = max(p₁ + r[1], p₂ + r[0]) = max(2, 5) = 5, s[2] = 2. r[3] = max(1 + 5, 5 + 1, 8 + 0) = 8, s[3] = 3. For j = 4 the four candidates are i = 1: 1 + r[3] = 9, i = 2: 5 + r[2] = 10, i = 3: 8 + r[1] = 9, i = 4: 9 + r[0] = 9, so r[4] = 10 and s[4] = 2; reconstruction outputs s[4] = 2, then s[2] = 2, and the optimal cutting is 2 + 2 for revenue 10. That is the unique optimum: the brute-force enumeration of all 2³ = 8 cuttings of a length-4 rod gives 4 → 9, 1+3 → 9, 2+2 → 10, 3+1 → 9, 1+1+2 → 7, 1+2+1 → 7, 2+1+1 → 7, 1+1+1+1 → 4, and only one cutting attains 10. Ten candidates were examined in total (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 4·5/2), against eight cuttings enumerated by brute force — at this size the two costs are close, which is why the comparison is worth making at n = 10, where they are 55 and 512.

Continuing to j = 7 needs r[5] and r[6] first: r[5] = max(1 + 10, 5 + 8, 8 + 5, 9 + 1, 10 + 0) = 13 with s[5] = 2; r[6] = max(1 + 13, 5 + 10, 8 + 8, 9 + 5, 10 + 1, 17 + 0) = 17 with s[6] = 6 (the whole rod, since p₆ = 17 beats every split). Then the seven candidates for r[7] are i = 1: 1 + 17 = 18, i = 2: 5 + 13 = 18, i = 3: 8 + 10 = 18, i = 4: 9 + 8 = 17, i = 5: 10 + 5 = 15, i = 6: 17 + 1 = 18, i = 7: 17 + 0 = 17. The maximum 18 is attained four times, at i = 1, 2, 3 and 6. The routine keeps the first candidate that strictly beats the running best, so s[7] = 1 and reconstruction produces 1 then s[6] = 6: the cutting 1 + 6. The brute force over all 2⁶ = 64 cuttings confirms 18 and finds five cuttings that attain it — 1+6, 6+1, 2+2+3, 2+3+2, 3+2+2 (the last three are one multiset in three orders) — so the table's answer is an optimum, not the optimum; a different tie-break in s would recover a different one, and all are equally good. The "one cut, two halves" formulation, and why it costs the same

The recurrence above is the first-piece form: one choice (the first piece) and one subproblem (the remainder). The more symmetric form treats a cutting as either no cut at all, worth pⱼ, or one cut at position i that leaves two rods, both solved optimally: r[j] = max(pⱼ, max over i = 1..j−1 of r[i] + r[j − i]). It is equally correct — cut and paste on either half — and, at first sight, no more expensive: for each j there are j candidates (the whole rod plus j − 1 cut positions), so the total is again n(n + 1)/2 = 55 at n = 10. The split is symmetric, though, so i and j − i name the same cutting and only i ≤ ⌊j/2⌋ need be examined, which the routine measures at 35 examinations for n = 10 — the sum of 1 + ⌊j/2⌋ over j = 1..10. The saving is a constant factor; both are Θ(n²), and the first-piece form is preferred because its reconstruction is a loop rather than a recursion and because it is the shape that generalises to knapsack (§10) and coin change (§09), where "the first item" is the natural choice. What the split form does show is that the number of subproblems per choice — one or two — does not change the state space: it is still r[0..n], and the running time is still states times choices.

What to take from rod cutting. The table is the recursion of §03 with the repeated questions removed: the same recurrence, the same n + 1 states, evaluated in an order (j increasing) that guarantees every reference r[j − i] is already answered. Time is states × choices, Θ(n²), and the exponent comes from the choice loop, not the state space. Recording the argmax in s[] alongside the max in r[] costs nothing extra and turns the value into a solution; without s the solution can still be recovered by re-testing the candidates at each step, at the same O(n) per step, but the argmax table is the habit to form. §05 draws the structure this section walked as a graph, and §06 measures what the same table costs when the recursion is kept and a memo added.

05 The subproblem graph and the evaluation order

The subproblem graph of a dynamic program is the directed graph with one vertex per distinct subproblem and an edge x → y whenever the recurrence for x consults the answer to y. It is the call tree of §03 with every repeated node merged into one — the tree "collapsed" onto the set of distinct questions — and it answers three things at once: how much the program costs (the sum over vertices of one plus the out-degree, i.e. Θ(V + E) when each edge is O(1) work), in what order the table may be filled (any order that puts every vertex after all the vertices it points to — a topological order of the reversed graph), and which subproblems a memoized recursion will actually touch (those reachable from the goal). Drawing the graph is the fastest way to check a recurrence: if it has a cycle the recurrence is not well-founded and no order exists; if it has far fewer vertices than the call tree has nodes, a table pays.

How the graph is read

For rod cutting the vertices are 0, 1, …, n and vertex j has an edge to every j − i for i = 1..j: j edges out of vertex j, n(n + 1)/2 in all — the same count as the candidate examinations of §04, which is no coincidence, since each candidate is one consultation of a smaller entry. For Fibonacci vertex j ≥ 2 has exactly two out-edges, to j − 1 and j − 2, and vertices 0 and 1 (the base cases) have none; the routine that builds the graph from the recurrence measures 0 edges at n = 0 and at n = 1, 2 at n = 2, 4 at n = 3 — that is 2(n − 1) for n ≥ 1, and the memoized call count 2n − 1 quoted in §03 is one more than the edge count, because the first call to the goal is not made along any edge. For the longest common subsequence (§13) the vertices are the prefix pairs (i, j), 0 ≤ i ≤ m, 0 ≤ j ≤ n; a vertex with x[i] = y[j] has one out-edge, to (i − 1, j − 1), and one with x[i] ≠ y[j] has two, to (i − 1, j) and (i, j − 1); the border vertices with i = 0 or j = 0 are base cases with none. So |E| lies between mn and 2mn and the bound Θ(mn) holds either way. In every case the running time of the bottom-up program is the size of the graph, provided the work done per edge is constant — which it is for max, min, sum and count recurrences, and is not for recurrences whose combine step is itself a loop, where the per-edge cost multiplies in.

Tabulation is a reverse topological sort

A bottom-up program fills the table in an order in which every entry is written before any entry that reads it. In the language of the graph: y is processed before x for every edge x → y. That is a topological order of the graph with its edges reversed (equivalently, the reverse of a topological order of G), and it exists precisely when G is acyclic — Graphs, §20 proves both directions and gives Kahn's algorithm, which is what the figure runs: it repeatedly removes a vertex with no remaining out-edges (a subproblem all of whose dependencies are already done). For rod cutting and Fibonacci that yields 0, 1, 2, …, n, the loop of §04; for a two-index state it yields row-major, column-major and anti-diagonal orders alike, and the choice among them is what §15 and §24 exploit when only one row needs to be kept. For a DAG-shaped problem the topological order is not merely an order but the algorithm (§21). The acyclicity requirement is worth stating in the other direction, too: a recurrence in which a state can reach itself — "the shortest path to v is the min over predecessors u of the shortest path to u plus w(u, v)" on a graph with cycles — has a cyclic subproblem graph and no evaluation order, which is why Bellman-Ford adds an edge-count index to the state (§21) and why such a recurrence, memoized, recurses forever.

Memoization is depth-first search from the goal

The memoized recursion does not compute an order in advance; it discovers one. A call on x descends into each consulted y in turn, returns when all of them have returned, and stores the answer — which is exactly a depth-first traversal of G starting at the goal vertex, with the memo playing the part of the visited set (Graphs, §15). The answers are fixed in DFS post-order, and post-order is by construction an order in which every vertex follows everything it points to, so the memo never reads an entry that is not yet filled — the correctness of top-down DP is the correctness of DFS finishing order, one theorem serving two pages. The count is the same too: the DFS follows every out-edge of every vertex it enters, once, so the number of calls is one plus the number of edges from reached vertices; on rod cutting at n = 10 that is 1 + 55 = 56, of which 11 are first visits (computations) and 45 are arrivals at an already-visited vertex (memo hits). And it only enters what the goal reaches. In rod cutting, and in Fibonacci for n ≥ 2, that is everything — vertex n reaches every smaller vertex (at n = 1 the Fibonacci goal is itself a base case and vertex 0 is never asked for, which the figure shows) — so memoization and tabulation compute the same set. In the LCS instance above the goal (4, 5) reaches 15 of the 30 vertices, because a match edge jumps diagonally and leaves the cells it skips over unvisited; the memo computes half the table and the bottom-up program computes all of it. That gap, harmless here, is the entire case for memoization in §06.

The graph is the cost model. When a recurrence is written, count its vertices (the distinct states) and its edges (the consultations per state, summed); the product "states × choices" of §01 is this sum in disguise, and the sum is the honest version when the number of choices varies by state — j choices at rod-cutting vertex j, giving n(n + 1)/2 rather than n². 06 Memoization versus tabulation, measured

Memoization keeps the recursive formulation and adds a table that each call checks before computing and writes after; tabulation discards the recursion and fills the same table with a loop in an order chosen in advance. Both evaluate every subproblem they touch exactly once, both are correct for the same reason (§05: DFS post-order and reverse topological order are both valid orders), and on a problem where every subproblem is needed both cost Θ(|V| + |E|). The differences are real but second-order to that shared bound: the top-down version computes only the subproblems the goal reaches, which on a sparse state space can be a tiny fraction of the table; the bottom-up version pays no call overhead, uses no stack, and — because the order is explicit — can discard entries once nothing later will read them. Each is the right choice for some problems, and the honest comparison is a set of measurements rather than a slogan.

Same asymptotics — and the constant that separates them

When the goal reaches every state, the two evaluators do the same multiset of work: one combine per edge, one write per vertex. The measurements above show it — 55 table updates against 56 memoized calls on rod cutting at n = 10, and the difference of one is the initial call. What differs is the cost of a unit of that work. A memoized step is a function call (a stack frame pushed and popped), a lookup in the memo (a hash or a map probe, or an array index if the state is an integer), and the branch; a tabulated step is an array index and an arithmetic operation inside a loop the compiler or engine can keep in registers. The ratio is implementation-dependent and not something this page will put a number on, but it is the reason a bottom-up program is the usual production form when both are available: it is the same algorithm with the cheapest possible inner loop. It is not a reason to prefer tabulation on asymptotic grounds — there are none — and a claim that "tabulation is O(n²) and memoization is worse" is a confusion between the constant and the exponent.

What only memoization gives: reachability

The bottom-up loop must decide in advance which states to fill, and the safe decision is all of them — the whole rectangle for a two-index state, all of 0..n for a one-index state. The top-down evaluation fills only what the goal reaches, and there are recurrences for which that set is exponentially or polynomially smaller than the rectangle. The recurrence in the formula block is the cleanest example: it consults ⌊n/2⌋, ⌊n/3⌋ and ⌊n/4⌋, so the states reachable from n are the numbers of the form ⌊n / (2ᵃ·3ᵇ)⌋ (nested floors compose, and 4 = 2²), of which there are at most (log₂n + 1)(log₃n + 1) = O(log² n) — the script that traced this page counted 108 distinct states at n = 10⁶ and 242 at n = 10⁹, against the 10⁶ + 1 cells a table over 0..n would fill (which the bottom-up routine does, and it agrees on the value 2 566 393) and the 10⁹ + 1 it would need. The same effect, less dramatic, appeared in §05's LCS instance, where the diagonal match edges let the goal reach 15 of 30 cells, and it appears in interval and subset problems whenever a constraint prunes the reachable region. In every such case memoization is not a convenience but the algorithm, and the recursion depth it incurs — here about log₂ n frames, so 30 at n = 10⁹ — is harmless. There are hybrids: a bottom-up program preceded by a reachability pass that marks the needed states, or a top-down program with an explicit stack (Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §10) to remove the depth cost; they are worth knowing but rarely needed.

What only tabulation gives: no stack, and a known order

The memoized rod-cutting routine, asked for CUT(n), calls CUT(n − 1) before anything else, which calls CUT(n − 2), and so on: it is n + 1 frames deep before the first value returns, and the memoized Fibonacci is n deep. A recursion whose depth is linear in the input runs into the limits described in Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §06 — a fixed call-stack budget that a table of a few thousand entries can exhaust under a runtime with a depth counter (Python's default limit is 1 000), or a few hundred thousand against a raw 8 MiB stack — and that a tabulated loop never touches. The other gift is space. A bottom-up program knows its order, so it knows when an entry is dead: Fibonacci's loop needs only the last two values; the knapsack table needs only the previous row (§11); LCS needs two rows for the value and, with Hirschberg's split, still recovers the alignment (§15); §24 gives the general rule. A memoized program cannot easily discard, because it does not know which entries a future call will ask for — it keeps the whole reachable set alive until the top-level call returns. Tabulation also composes with the machine: a loop over a contiguous array is predictable to the cache and to a vectorising compiler, and a GPU or SIMD alignment kernel is a tabulation too — but over anti-diagonals, not rows, because the row-major inner loop reads the cell to its left (§14, §24); the point is that an explicit order can be chosen for the machine, not that this particular loop is the one it runs.

07 Designing the state: the checklist

The state of a dynamic program is the tuple of parameters that identifies a subproblem, and it is correctly chosen when the subproblem's answer is a function of those parameters alone — nothing about how the subproblem was reached may affect what it is worth. Every other decision follows from that one: the recurrence enumerates the choices available in a state and names the states they lead to; the base cases are the states with no choices; the order is a topological order of the resulting graph (§05); the answer is read from a specific state or a maximum over several; the cost is the number of states times the choices per state. Almost every wrong dynamic program is wrong at the first step — the state forgets something the recurrence needs, or remembers something it does not — and this section is the checklist for getting it right, with the two failure modes shown on problems where the obvious state is the wrong one.

Too small, first example: "the best of the prefix" is not enough

Take the longest increasing subsequence (§16). The natural first state is "L[i] = the length of the longest increasing subsequence within the first i elements", and it does not work: to decide whether element i + 1 extends the best subsequence one needs to know what that subsequence ends with, and L[i] does not say. Two prefixes with the same L[i] can deserve different answers at i + 1, so L is not a function of the state. The fix is to make the state remember what the future depends on — "L[i] = the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending exactly at i" — after which the recurrence is a pure function (1 + max L[j] over j < i with a[j] < a[i]) and the answer is max over i of L[i], checklist item 5. Kadane's algorithm (§08) is the same repair on maximum subarray: "best sum ending here" rather than "best sum so far", which the Recursion, Divide & Conquer page's §17 calls strengthening the subproblem. The general rule: if the recurrence for state x needs to know something about the solution to y beyond its value, that something must be part of y's state.

Too small, second example: one forgotten bit

The house-robber problem of §08 asks for the maximum sum of a subset of v[0..n−1] with no two chosen elements adjacent. A one-number state — "the best total over the prefix, extended by v[i] whenever the previous house was not taken" — carries the running total but not whether house i − 1 was taken, and it commits greedily; on the instance 2, 7, 9, 3, 1, 8, 4 shared with the figure that rule returns 16 where the optimum is 19 (2 + 9 + 8). The recurrence best[i] = max(best[i − 1], best[i − 2] + v[i]) repairs it by looking back two, which is the same as carrying one bit: define take[i] (house i is taken) and skip[i] (it is not), with take[i] = skip[i − 1] + v[i] and skip[i] = max(take[i − 1], skip[i − 1]); the state is (i, bit), twice as many states, each a pure function. The bit becomes unavoidable in the ring version, where house 0 and house n − 1 are also neighbours: the linear recurrence, which does not know whether house 0 was taken when it reaches house n − 1, returns 4 on 2, 3, 2 and 10 on 5, 1, 1, 5, both illegal, where the optima are 3 and 6. The state must carry "was house 0 taken?" — or, equivalently, the problem is solved twice, once with house 0 forbidden and once with house n − 1 forbidden, and the better taken; the routine that does so agrees with brute force over every legal subset on each of those instances.

Too small, third example: the visited set

§02 showed that "the longest simple path ending at v" has no valid recurrence: two paths reaching v with the same length may have consumed different vertices, and the future — which vertices can still be used — depends on which. The state that repairs it is (v, S) with S the set of vertices used, and it is a correct dynamic program with n · 2ⁿ states (§22 uses exactly this state for the travelling salesman). Here the checklist's diagnosis is right and the cure is exponential, which is the third possible outcome: the state that makes the recurrence pure may be too large to be useful, and the honest conclusion is that the problem is hard, not that DP was misapplied.

Too large: remembering the order of things that only matter as a set

The opposite error is to include in the state something the future does not depend on. In the travelling-salesman recurrence the future depends on which cities have been visited and which one the tour is currently at — not on the order they were visited in — so the state (S, v) with 2ⁿ · n entries is right and a state carrying the visited sequence, with n!-many entries, is a correct program that is merely the brute force with extra bookkeeping. In 0/1 knapsack (§10) the future depends on the remaining capacity and the set of items still undecided — after deciding items 1..i in order, that set is always {i + 1, …, n}, so the state is (i, remaining capacity), Θ(nW) entries, and not (set of items chosen), 2ⁿ entries. The checklist's sanity line catches both: count the distinct questions the recursion can ask, and if the state space is bigger than that count, two states are the same question in different clothes. The test that decides what belongs in the state is always the same one: does the best completion from here depend on it? If yes, it stays; if no, it goes; and if the answer is "yes, but only through its effect on some smaller summary" — the total weight so far, not the items; the last element, not the whole subsequence — then the summary is the state.

The order and the answer's location, two smaller traps

  • Order. The recurrence only tells you which states are consulted; the loop nest must visit them first. For a one-index state that is increasing i; for intervals it is increasing length, not increasing i (§17); for a two-index state where (i, j) reads (i − 1, ·) and (i, j − 1) either nest works; for the 1-D knapsack it is decreasing capacity (§11) and getting it backwards changes the problem. §09's loop-order bug is the same trap in coin change: two loop orders, two different counting problems.
  • Answer. When the objective may end anywhere — a subsequence, a subarray, a path in a DAG to any sink — the answer is a maximum over states, not the last state; forgetting this returns the answer to a different question ("the best that ends at n").
  • Base cases. The empty state's value is 0 for sums and lengths, 1 for counts ("one way to make amount 0", "one way to climb zero stairs"), +∞ for minimisations where the empty state is unreachable, and −∞ or the first element for maximisations that forbid the empty solution (Kadane's non-empty subarray). The wrong base is the most common off-by-one in the whole subject.
  • Reconstruction. Decide before writing the loop whether the solution or only its value is wanted; the argmax table is free to record and awkward to retrofit, and after space rolling (§24) it is no longer free.
The one question. Everything in this section is a form of: what does the best completion from here depend on? Whatever it depends on is the state; whatever it does not is not. If the dependence is on a history that cannot be summarised in a few integers, the problem may be beyond polynomial DP (§02, §22); if it can, the recurrence is a pure function and the rest of the checklist is mechanical. The next two sections apply it to the one-dimensional states — where the checklist looks trivial and the base-case and loop-order traps are still live.

08 One-dimensional DP: Fibonacci, stairs, house robber, maximum subarray

A one-dimensional dynamic program has a state that is a single index — a prefix length — and a recurrence that reads a bounded number of earlier entries, so its table is a strip of n + 1 cells filled left to right in Θ(n) time, and usually in Θ(1) space once the strip is rolled. The four problems here share that shape and differ in exactly the places §07's checklist says they can: the base case (Fibonacci and the staircase have the same recurrence and different bases), the combine (a sum for counts, a max for values), the extra quantity the state must carry (Kadane's "best ending here"), and where the answer is read (the last cell, or the maximum over all cells). They are also the problems on which a wrong base or a wrong answer-location is most often written, which is why each is checked against brute force in the figure.

Fibonacci and the staircase: one recurrence, two bases

The Fibonacci recurrence is the smallest dynamic program there is, and §03 already used it to show the exponential-versus-linear gap: the naive recursion makes 2·F(n + 1) − 1 calls, the memo 2n − 1, the loop n − 1 cell updates. The correctness argument is trivial because the recurrence is the definition. The staircase problem is where the argument has content: the number of ways to reach step i is the number of ways to reach step i − 1 and then take a 1-step, plus the number of ways to reach step i − 2 and then take a 2-step, and these two families are disjoint (they differ in the last move) and exhaustive (the last move is one or the other) — so the count is a sum with no double counting. That disjoint-and-exhaustive check is the counting analogue of cut-and-paste and is what makes a counting recurrence right; it fails, and the count is inflated, whenever two cases can describe the same object, which is exactly what goes wrong in §09 when the coin-change loops are nested the wrong way round. The base is the other place to be careful. W[0] = 1 is not a convention: there is one way to climb zero stairs (the empty sequence of moves), and setting W[0] = 0 would make W[2] = 1 instead of 2, losing the single 2-step. With the correct base the table is Fibonacci shifted by one, W[n] = F[n + 1], and the enumeration in the figure — every sequence of 1s and 2s summing to n, generated explicitly — returns the same 8 at n = 5 and 89 at n = 10. Generalisations keep the shape: steps of 1, 2 or 3 give a three-term recurrence (the "tribonacci" numbers), steps from an arbitrary set S give W[i] = ∑ over s ∈ S of W[i − s], which is coin change's composition count (§09), and a forbidden step is a state with W = 0 that the recurrence routes around.

House robber: a max in place of a sum

Replacing the sum by a max and adding a value per element gives the house-robber recurrence B[i] = max(B[i − 1], B[i − 2] + v[i]). The cut-and-paste step is the standard one: an optimal selection from houses 0..i either omits house i, in which case it is an optimal selection from 0..i − 1 (otherwise swap in a better one), or includes it, in which case it cannot include i − 1 and its remainder is an optimal selection from 0..i − 2. §07 showed why the look-back of two is necessary — the one-number state that does not know whether i − 1 was taken returns 16 on the instance above against the optimum 19 — and why the ring variant needs a further bit. Reconstruction follows the checklist: walk back from i = n − 1; if B[i] = B[i − 1] house i was skipped, otherwise it was taken and the walk jumps to i − 2. On the instance this yields houses 5, 2, 0 in that order, values 8 + 9 + 2 = 19. The brute force in the figure enumerates all 2⁷ = 128 subsets, rejects the 94 with an adjacent pair, and takes the best of the 34 that remain — 34 being F(9), since the number of adjacency-free subsets of a path of n houses satisfies the staircase recurrence itself. The problem is also the simplest instance of DP on a tree (§20): a path is a tree, and (take, skip) per node is the state that generalises.

Maximum subarray: Kadane's algorithm, and where the answer lives

The maximum-subarray problem has a Θ(n log n) divide-and-conquer solution and a Θ(n) scan; the Recursion, Divide & Conquer page's §16–§17 builds both and explains the scan as a strengthened induction. Read as a dynamic program it is the clearest one-dimensional case of §07's first failure mode. The state "best subarray within a[0..i]" has no recurrence, because the best subarray within a[0..i + 1] may start anywhere. The state "best subarray ending at i" does: such a subarray is either just a[i] or a subarray ending at i − 1 extended by a[i], and the best of the latter is the best subarray ending at i − 1 plus a[i] — disjoint cases, cut and paste on the prefix. The recurrence is then E[i] = max(a[i], E[i − 1] + a[i]), with the familiar reading "if the running sum has gone negative, drop it". Two details are the whole difficulty. The base is E[0] = a[0], not 0: the subarray must be non-empty, so on the all-negative input −3, −1, −2 the answer is −1 and a base of 0 would return the wrong 0. And the answer is max over i of E[i], not E[n − 1]: on the shared instance E[n − 1] = 5 and the optimum 6 was attained at i = 6. The figure carries a second strip, the running maximum, so the moment the answer is fixed is visible; the brute force sums every one of the n(n + 1)/2 = 45 subarrays and agrees. Reconstruction of the subarray itself needs its endpoints: the right end is the argmax of E, and the left end is found by walking back while E[i − 1] > 0, or by recording, at each i, whether the run was restarted.

A fourth: minimum-cost stairs, and the counting cousin

The same strip handles costs: with a fee cost[i] to step off stair i, starting from stair 0 or 1 and finishing past the last stair, the cheapest way to leave stair i is D[i] = cost[i] + min(D[i − 1], D[i − 2]) with D[0] = cost[0], D[1] = cost[1], and the answer is min(D[n − 1], D[n − 2]) — again a max-over-states-style answer, because the final move may come from either of the last two stairs. On cost = 10, 15, 20 the table is 10, 15, 30 and the answer is 15 (start on stair 1, pay 15, step off); on 1, 100, 1, 1, 1, 100, 1, 1, 100, 1 it is 1, 100, 2, 3, 3, 103, 4, 5, 104, 6 and the answer is 6, which the exhaustive walk over every path of 1- and 2-steps from both starts confirms. The counting cousin is decode-ways — the number of ways to read a digit string as letters 1 → A … 26 → Z: D[i] = D[i − 1]·[s[i] ≠ 0] + D[i − 2]·[10 ≤ s[i − 1..i] ≤ 26], with D[0] = 1; on "226" it counts 3 (2·2·6, 22·6, 2·26), on "11106" it counts 2, and on "100" it counts 0, all matching an explicit enumeration. Each is the staircase with guards on which steps are allowed, and each is wrong the moment a guard is forgotten, which is why they are checked and not merely stated.

Aside: the closed form and the matrix power

Fibonacci alone among these has structure beyond the strip. Because the recurrence is linear, one step is a matrix: (F[i + 1], F[i]) = M · (F[i], F[i − 1]) with M = [[1, 1], [1, 0]], so F[n] is the top-right entry of Mⁿ, and repeated squaring computes Mⁿ in O(log n) matrix products — the routine measures 6 products for n = 10 and 16 for n = 1000, against 999 additions for the loop. The same trick works for any fixed-order linear recurrence (the k-step staircase, counting walks in a graph) and is how large-n counts are computed modulo a prime. There is also the closed form F[n] = round(φⁿ / √5) with φ = (1 + √5)/2; it is exact in exact arithmetic, but in double precision the rounding is right only while φⁿ / √5 is represented to within a half — the script that traced this page found it agrees with the loop for every n ≤ 70 and first disagrees at n = 71, where F = 308 061 521 170 129 and the rounded power returns one more. The point of the aside is negative: the closed form is a curiosity, the matrix power is a genuine algorithm, and neither is available for house robber or Kadane, whose max is not linear. For them the strip is the algorithm.

09 Coin change: combinations, compositions, fewest coins — and the loop-order bug

Coin change is three different problems on the same input — denominations c₁ < c₂ < … < cₘ in unlimited supply and a target amount A — and the three have three different loop nests over the same one-dimensional table. (i) The number of combinations: multisets of coins summing to A, where 2 + 2 + 1 and 1 + 2 + 2 are the same. (ii) The number of compositions, or ordered ways: sequences of coins summing to A, where those two are different. (iii) The fewest coins that sum to A, the optimisation problem that Greedy Algorithms, §09 solves by largest-coin-first on canonical systems and that the table solves on every system. The two counting problems are distinguished by nothing but the nesting of two loops, and swapping the loops silently converts one into the other — the wrong answer is a plausible integer, not an error — which is why this section works the counts by hand and the figure re-derives them from explicit enumeration.

Why the outer loop decides what is counted

Both counting recurrences are sums over cases, and a sum counts correctly when the cases are disjoint and exhaustive (§08's staircase argument). In (ii) the cases are "the last coin is k": every non-empty sequence has one last coin, so each sequence lands in exactly one case, and P[a] = ∑ P[a − k] counts sequences. The same sum applied to multisets fails, because a multiset has no "last" coin — {2, 2, 1} is reached both as "{2, 2} plus a 1" and as "{2, 1} plus a 2" — and it is counted once per distinct coin it contains. Nest (i) removes the ambiguity by imposing a canonical order: after the outer loop has finished coin k, C[a] counts multisets drawn from {c₁, …, cₖ} only, and the update C[a] += C[a − k] splits those into "no coin k" (the old C[a], computed with coins c₁..cₖ₋₁) and "at least one coin k" (remove one and what remains is a multiset from {c₁..cₖ} summing to a − k, which is C[a − k] after its update in the same pass). Those two cases are disjoint and exhaustive for multisets, so the count is right. Written out in two dimensions the recurrence is C[k][a] = C[k − 1][a] + C[k][a − cₖ], and nest (i) is that table with the k dimension rolled away — legitimately, because when C[a] is updated the entry C[a] still holds the previous row's value and C[a − k] already holds the current row's, which is exactly what the recurrence reads. This is the unbounded knapsack's rolling array (§11), and the loop-direction argument there is this one.

The bug, and how it presents

Write the combinations program with the loops the other way — amount outer, coins inner — and it computes P, not C: on {1, 2, 5} at A = 5 it prints 9 where 4 was wanted, at A = 11 it prints 218 where 11 was wanted. Nothing fails: the base case, the update rule and the number of updates are identical, and the output is a positive integer that grows with A like the correct one. The reverse mistake — coins outer when ordered sequences were wanted — under-counts by the same mechanism. The figure fills the strip under either nest with the update it is making named at every step, and the readout checks each count against a routine that enumerates the objects themselves: every multiset (as a non-increasing sequence of coins) and every sequence. On {1, 3, 4} at 6 the two counts are 4 and 9; on {2, 5} at 7 they are 1 (the multiset {5, 2}) and 2 (5 + 2 and 2 + 5); on {2, 5} at 3 both are 0. A useful habit: state in the docstring which of the two objects is being counted, and test on an instance where the counts differ — any instance with at least two coins that both fit will do.

Fewest coins: where greedy is wrong and why the order is free

The optimisation version replaces the sum by a min and the base 1 by 0, with ∞ marking unreachable amounts (needed whenever there is no 1-coin: {2, 5} cannot make 3, and the table says so rather than inventing an answer). Its correctness is cut and paste: an optimal set of coins for a contains some coin k, and the rest is an optimal set for a − k, otherwise swap in a better one; the min over k then cannot miss it. Unlike the counts, the min is indifferent to the loop order: examining the candidate "{2, 2, 1}" through the door marked 2 and again through the door marked 1 does not change the minimum, so the coins-outer and amount-outer nests return the same N. The table also settles the greedy question. On {1, 3, 4} at 6 largest-coin-first takes 4, then 1, then 1 — three coins — while the table finds N[6] = 2 via from[6] = 3, from[3] = 3: two 3s. That system is non-canonical in the sense of the greedy page, and on it the greedy answer is wrong for 24 of the amounts from 1 to 100 (that page's figure counts them); the DP is right on all of them, at Θ(m·A) cost instead of greedy's Θ(m). On {1, 2, 5} at 11 both give 3 (5 + 5 + 1), and on {1, 5, 10, 25} they agree everywhere, because that system is canonical — a fact the DP can be used to check, by comparing against greedy for every amount up to cₘ₋₁ + cₘ, which is where the smallest counterexample must lie if there is one. The from[] table is the argmax of §07's checklist and gives the coins themselves in O(N[A]) steps.

Cost, and the word "pseudo-polynomial"

All three programs run in Θ(m·A) time (the nests visit the pairs (k, a) with cₖ ≤ a ≤ A, at most m·A of them) and Θ(A) space. That looks polynomial and is not, in the sense that matters for complexity theory: the input is m numbers and the amount A, which is written in log₂A bits, so a running time linear in A is exponential in the size of the input. The same is true of knapsack's Θ(nW) (§11), where the point is discussed at length; for coin change with the small amounts of practice it is a curiosity, and for the counting problems the answers themselves grow so fast (218 compositions of 11, and exponentially thereafter) that the cost of the table is not the concern. What is the concern is which object is being counted, and that is decided by one line of loop nesting.

10 0/1 knapsack: the (item, capacity) table

Given n items, the i-th of weight wᵢ and value vᵢ (positive integers), and a capacity W, choose a subset of the items of total weight at most W whose total value is largest; each item is taken whole or not at all. The state is the pair (how many items have been considered, how much capacity remains), the recurrence is the two-way choice "skip item i, or take it and lose wᵢ of capacity", and the table has (n + 1)(W + 1) entries, each filled in O(1): Θ(nW) time, with a reconstruction that walks the table back from its last cell. The problem is the canonical instance of a recurrence that greedy cannot shortcut — Greedy Algorithms, §11 shows the density rule taking 160 where the optimum is 220 on a four-item instance this page reuses — and the canonical instance of a running time that is polynomial in the value of an input number rather than in its length (§11).

Why the state needs two coordinates

The first attempt at a state — "the best value at capacity w", one coordinate — is the right state for the unbounded problem, where an item may be taken any number of times: removing one copy of item i from an optimal load of capacity w leaves an optimal load of capacity w − wᵢ, and the recurrence K(w) = max over i with wᵢ ≤ w of K(w − wᵢ) + vᵢ is licensed by cut and paste. In the 0/1 problem that subproblem is useless: knowing that K(w − wᵢ) is large does not tell whether the load that achieves it already contains item i, and if it does, adding i again is illegal. The state must carry enough information to prevent the double take, and the cheapest such information is which items have been considered: restrict the subproblem to items 1..i, so that item i is decided exactly once, at the transition from row i − 1 to row i. This is the principle of §07 in its purest form — widen the state only as far as the recurrence forces, and no further. Carrying the set of items already taken would also work and would cost 2ⁿ states; carrying a prefix index costs n + 1.

11 Knapsack variants: the rolling array and its loop direction, unbounded, bounded, and why Θ(nW) is pseudo-polynomial

Row i of the knapsack table reads only row i − 1, so the value of the optimum needs a single array f[0..W] that is overwritten once per item — provided the capacity loop runs downward, from W to wᵢ. The bounded variant by binary splitting

If item i is available kᵢ times, the naive reduction lists it kᵢ times and runs the 0/1 algorithm on ∑kᵢ items, Θ(W·∑kᵢ). Binary splitting replaces the kᵢ copies with bundles of 1, 2, 4, …, 2ᵗ⁻¹ copies and one remainder bundle of kᵢ − (2ᵗ − 1) copies, where t is the largest integer with 2ᵗ − 1 ≤ kᵢ. Every count from 0 to kᵢ is the sum of some sub-collection of those bundles — the powers of two represent 0..2ᵗ − 1 and the remainder lifts the range to kᵢ — and no sub-collection exceeds kᵢ, so the 0/1 problem on the bundles is exactly the bounded problem on the items. The number of bundles is ⌊log₂ kᵢ⌋ + 1, which equals ⌈log₂(kᵢ + 1)⌉: k = 1 → 1 bundle, k = 2 → 2 (1, 1), k = 3 → 2 (1, 2), k = 4 → 3 (1, 2, 1), k = 7 → 3, k = 8 → 4 (1, 2, 4, 1); the routine behind the figure was checked against both formulas for every k from 1 to 20. (The tidier-looking ⌈log₂ k⌉ is wrong at k = 1 and at every power of two.) Total cost Θ(W·∑ᵢ log kᵢ). On the worked items with multiplicities (1, 3, 2, 5) the split produces 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 8 bundles — 1×1; 2×1, 2×2; 3×1, 3×1; 4×1, 4×2, 4×2 — and the 0/1 routine on them at W = 10 fills 8 × 11 = 88 cells and returns 48 (1×1 and 4×2: item 1 once, item 4 twice), which brute force over all bounded multisets confirms. Pseudo-polynomial: the right n

Θ(nW) is an honest count of the cells, and it is not a polynomial bound. The input to knapsack is n pairs of numbers and one capacity, written in binary: about n · log₂(max wᵢ, max vᵢ) + log₂ W bits. W itself occupies log₂ W bits, so a table with W + 1 columns has 2^(log₂ W) columns — exponential in the length of the number that determines it. An algorithm whose running time is polynomial in the numeric values in the input but not in their bit lengths is pseudo-polynomial, and Algorithm Analysis, §23 puts the numbers on it: n = 100 and W = 10⁹ is a 375-byte input and a 10¹¹-cell table. Two consequences frame the variants above. The DP is a fine algorithm whenever the weights are small integers or can be made so — the ÷5 scaling of §10 is that trick in miniature, and it is why the capacity loop's width, not the item count, decides whether knapsack is easy in practice. And the DP does not contradict the problem's NP-hardness: knapsack, subset sum and partition are NP-hard when the numbers are written in binary, and they are the standard examples of problems whose hardness disappears when the numbers are written in unary (the weakly NP-hard problems); a problem that stays hard in unary — bin packing, 3-partition — is strongly NP-hard and has no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P = NP.

The FPTAS sketch, as a hand-off

Because the table's width is set by the values if the DP is run "over value" instead of over weight — g[i][v] = the least weight achieving value exactly v from items 1..i, answer = the largest v with g[n][v] ≤ W, cost Θ(n·∑vᵢ) = O(n²·vₘₐₓ) — the values can be coarsened to make that width small. Fix ε > 0, let K = ε·vₘₐₓ/n, and replace each value by ⌊vᵢ/K⌋: the scaled values are at most n/ε, the value-DP runs in O(n³/ε), and the set it returns, valued at the original prices, is within a factor 1 − ε of the optimum, because rounding loses at most K per item and at most nK = ε·vₘₐₓ ≤ ε·OPT in total (OPT ≥ vₘₐₓ when every item fits alone). That is a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme — polynomial in n and in 1/ε — and the greedy page's 1/2-approximation (§11) is its crude ancestor. The proof of the guarantee and the taxonomy of approximation schemes belong to the NP-completeness and approximation part of this series (see the series map); this page's contribution is the observation that a pseudo-polynomial DP plus rounding is the standard recipe for an FPTAS.

12 Weighted interval scheduling and p(j)

Given n intervals [sⱼ, fⱼ), each with a weight vⱼ > 0, choose a set of pairwise non-overlapping intervals of maximum total weight. Sort by finish time, define p(j) as the largest index i < j whose interval ends no later than interval j starts, and the recurrence is a two-way choice: OPT(j) = max(OPT(j − 1), vⱼ + OPT(p(j))). The state is a single index — a prefix of the finish-sorted list — and the whole algorithm is a sort, n binary searches and one linear pass: O(n log n). It is the weighted cousin of the activity-selection problem whose greedy solution Greedy Algorithms, §04 proves; that page's §06 shows the greedy rule fails as soon as weights differ, and this section is the algorithm that replaces it — the DP that the greedy scan is a collapse of.

Why finish order, and why p(j) makes the prefix state closed

The state is a prefix, and a prefix works only if "take j" leaves a subproblem that is again a prefix. In finish order it does. If interval j is taken, every other interval in the same compatible set must end at or before sⱼ (any interval with a larger index ends at or after fⱼ > sⱼ and so cannot precede j, and cannot follow j either, because we are looking at the prefix 1..j); the intervals ending at or before sⱼ are exactly 1..p(j), since finish times are sorted and p(j) is the last one with fᵢ ≤ sⱼ. So the residual instance is the prefix 1..p(j), and the state space is closed. Sorting by start time would break this: the intervals compatible with j would not form a prefix, and the state would have to carry more. This is the discipline of §07 again — the sort is not a preprocessing convenience, it is what makes a one-integer state sufficient.

Computing p(j): n binary searches on the finish times

Because the finish times are sorted, "the largest i < j with fᵢ ≤ sⱼ" is a boundary search on a sorted array: find the first index whose finish time exceeds sⱼ among 1..j − 1, and subtract one. A two-pointer sweep over the intervals sorted by start time computes all p(j) in O(n) after the two sorts, but the binary search is the version whose count the figure measures: on the eight-interval instance below the routine makes 16 probes in total, against the bound ∑ⱼ ⌈log₂ j⌉ = 0 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 17. The recursion OPT(j) with a memo is the natural top-down form and is worth writing once to see the overlap: OPT(j) calls OPT(j − 1) and OPT(p(j)), and without a memo the call tree is the Fibonacci-shaped tree of §03 in disguise, exponential whenever p(j) is close to j. 13 Longest common subsequence

A subsequence of a string is the string with zero or more characters deleted (the rest keep their order); the longest common subsequence (LCS) of X = x₁…xₘ and Y = y₁…yₙ is a longest string that is a subsequence of both. The state is a prefix pair (i, j) — the LCS length of x₁…xᵢ and y₁…yⱼ — and the recurrence has three cases: if the last characters match, extend the LCS of both prefixes shortened by one; otherwise drop the last character of one string or the other and take the better. The table has (m + 1)(n + 1) entries, mn of them computed, in Θ(mn) time; the LCS itself is read back by a traceback of O(m + n) steps, and it is in general not unique. LCS is the archetype of the two-string state space that edit distance (§14), Hirschberg (§15) and sequence alignment all share.

Traceback without the arrows, and the length in linear space

14 Edit distance and its DAG

The edit distance (Levenshtein distance) between strings a = a₁…aₘ and b = b₁…bₙ is the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn a into b. The state is again a prefix pair, D[i][j] = the cost of turning a₁…aᵢ into b₁…bⱼ; the recurrence is a three-way minimum — delete aᵢ, insert bⱼ, or line aᵢ up with bⱼ (free if they match, one substitution if not) — and the table costs Θ(mn). Weighted operations

Prices change the answer and the alignment, not the algorithm. With cₛᵤ𝒷 = 3 and insertions and deletions at 1, a substitution is dearer than a deletion followed by an insertion, so the recurrence never chooses it: kitten → sitting becomes 5 — insert s, delete k, keep itt, insert i, delete e, keep n, insert g, in the column order the routine's walk-back produces — and the naive recursion, re-run with the same prices, also returns 5. With insertions and deletions at 2 and substitutions at 1 the unit-cost alignment survives and the cost is 1 + 1 + 2 = 4. The general rule is the triangle inequality on the prices: whenever cₛᵤ𝒷 ≥ cᵢₙₛ + c𝒹ₑₗ substitution is dominated and the distance collapses to the insert/delete distance c𝒹ₑₗ·(m − LCS) + cᵢₙₛ·(n − LCS) of §13 — which is c·(m + n − 2·LCS) when the two gap costs are equal (the worked pair gives 7 + 6 − 2·4 = 5 for the LCS strings, and 6 + 7 − 2·4 = 5 here, since LCS(kitten, sitting) = ittn has length 4). Costs may also depend on the characters — a substitution matrix in sequence alignment, keyboard distance in spelling correction — and the recurrence is unchanged as long as every cost is non-negative. (Negative costs still give a well-defined minimum over the DAG below, but the result is no longer a distance — a path could profit from inserting a character and deleting it again — which is why sequence alignment in biology is phrased as a score to maximise, with a substitution matrix and gap penalties; affine gap penalties then need two extra states per cell — a gap open in either string, three values per cell in the standard formulation — an extension rather than a new idea.)

The named variants: Wagner–Fischer, Needleman–Wunsch, Smith–Waterman, Damerau–Levenshtein

The table above carries several names, each attached to a different reading of the costs. Wagner–Fischer is the edit-distance table itself: the three-way minimum over prefix pairs with per-operation costs, exactly as the formula block at the top of the section states it. Needleman–Wunsch is the same recurrence turned into global alignment: a maximisation with a substitution matrix s(aᵢ, bⱼ) in place of [aᵢ ≠ bⱼ]·cₛᵤ𝒷 and a gap penalty −g in place of the insert and delete costs, the whole of both strings aligned end to end, and the answer at (m, n) as before. Smith–Waterman is local alignment — the best-scoring pair of substrings, one from each string — and it changes two things only: every cell takes a max with 0, so that an alignment may start afresh anywhere, and the answer is the largest entry anywhere in the table rather than H[m][n], with the alignment traced back from that cell until a 0 is reached. That second change is the same trap as Kadane's maximum subarray in §08: the recurrence computes "best alignment ending here", and the answer is a maximum over all states, not the value at the last one. Damerau–Levenshtein adds a fourth operation, the transposition of two adjacent characters, as one more candidate in the minimum — D[i−2][j−2] + 1 when aᵢ₋₁ = bⱼ and aᵢ = bⱼ₋₁ — so that ca → ac costs 1 (one transposition) instead of 2 (two substitutions, or a deletion and an insertion). That is the restricted form, in which a transposed pair is not edited again; the unrestricted distance, where it may be, needs a table indexed also by the last row and column at which each character occurred, and is the form that is a metric.

Seeing the table as a graph explains several things at once. The DP's Θ(mn) is the O(V + E) of DAG shortest paths with V = (m + 1)(n + 1) and E < 3V. Any topological order works — anti-diagonals i + j = const are one, which is the order that lets the table be filled in parallel wavefronts. Dijkstra's algorithm would also solve it, the weights being non-negative, and with a heap that is the basis of the "only explore cells with D ≤ d" trick: if the distance is small, only the cells within a band of width about d around the diagonal can lie on a shortest path, and the table can be restricted to O(d·min(m, n)) cells. The DAG view also shows why the alignment is not unique — distinct shortest paths — and why the walk-back is O(m + n): every edge advances i or j or both. §21 takes the general case: any DP whose recurrence reads "min over incoming edges" is a shortest path on the subproblem DAG, and the choice of algorithm is the choice of how to traverse it.

15 Hirschberg: the alignment in linear space

Hirschberg's algorithm recovers an optimal alignment — not just its cost — in O(m + n) space and still Θ(mn) time, by finding the column where an optimal path crosses the middle row of the edit-distance table using one forward and one backward linear-space pass, and then recursing on the two sub-rectangles that split creates. The two-row trick of §13–§14 computes the value in linear space but destroys the rows a walk-back would need; Hirschberg keeps the value's economy and replaces the walk-back by divide and conquer. Its cost is a geometric sum: the top level touches mn cells, the next level about half, and the total stays below roughly 2mn — on the worked pair 80 cells against a full table of 42. Everything here is stated for edit distance; the same construction works for LCS and for any prefix-pair DP whose recurrence looks only at adjacent cells.

What the argument needs, and where it generalises

Three properties made the split legitimate: the DP is a shortest path on a grid DAG whose every edge goes down, right or diagonally, so every path crosses every row; the recurrence at a cell reads only the previous row and the current one, so a forward pass in two rows and a backward pass on the reversed inputs are both available; and the objective is additive along the path, so prefix cost plus suffix cost is path cost. LCS has all three with max in place of min (and F[j] + R[j] maximised), as does global sequence alignment with a substitution matrix and linear gap penalties; affine gap penalties need the split to record whether the path enters row mid in a gap state, which is a bookkeeping extension rather than a new idea. What it does not extend to are DPs whose recurrence reaches arbitrarily far back — weighted interval scheduling's OPT(p(j)), the interval DPs of §17–§19 — because there is no "middle row" that every solution path must cross with local information on either side. §24 collects the general question of reconstruction after rolling and the cheaper trick (store every k-th row, recompute the gaps) that suffices when memory is merely tight rather than absent.

16 Longest increasing subsequence, two ways

The longest increasing subsequence (LIS) of a sequence a₁…aₙ is a longest subsequence — indices i₁ < i₂ < … < iₖ, not necessarily adjacent — whose values are strictly increasing. Two algorithms are standard and worth knowing together. The Θ(n²) dynamic program uses the state "the LIS that ends at position i" and the recurrence dp[i] = 1 + max{dp[j] : j < i, aⱼ < aᵢ}; the O(n log n) method keeps, for each length, the smallest value that can end an increasing subsequence of that length — the tails array — and places each element by binary search. Both are worked on [10, 9, 2, 5, 3, 7, 101, 18], whose LIS has length 4; the two return different LISs of that length, and the tails array, despite appearances, is not in general one of them.

Why tails is not the LIS, and why reconstruction needs the links

On this instance the final tails array [2, 3, 7, 18] is itself an increasing subsequence of the input — a coincidence that misleads. Run the same routine on [2, 6, 3, 4, 1]: the trace is [2] → [2, 6] → [2, 3] → [2, 3, 4] → [1, 3, 4], and the final array [1, 3, 4] is not a subsequence of the input, because the 1 that sits in front arrived after the 3 and the 4. The LIS has length 3 ([2, 3, 4]), which is what the length of tails reports correctly; the contents of tails are the smallest-known endpoints for each length, drawn from different subsequences, and they lose the order in which they were placed. That is why tails[k] ← aᵢ alone cannot reconstruct anything: it overwrites the evidence. The fix is the pred link recorded at placement time — the index holding tails[k − 1] at that moment is a genuine predecessor of aᵢ in a length-(k + 1) subsequence, and those links are never overwritten. With them, reconstruction is a walk of L steps, and the whole method is O(n log n) time and Θ(n) space for the links. The picture behind the name "patience sorting" is a row of piles: each card goes on the leftmost pile whose top card is at least as large (or starts a new pile at the right), the tops of the piles are the tails array, and the number of piles is the LIS length — with the same caveat that the tops are not a subsequence.

The reduction to LCS, and the variants

When the values are distinct, an increasing subsequence of a is exactly a common subsequence of a and sorted(a) — any common subsequence appears in sorted order, hence increasing; any increasing subsequence is a subsequence of the sorted list — so LIS(a) = LCS(a, sorted(a)), a Θ(n²) algorithm by §13 that the figure runs as a third check (64 cells on the worked sequence; it returns 4). With duplicates the reduction computes the longest non-decreasing subsequence instead — on [3, 3, 3] it answers 3 where the strict LIS is 1 — and the repair is to sort and remove duplicates before taking the LCS. The same distinction governs the binary search in method 2: a lower-bound search (first tail ≥ aᵢ) gives the strictly increasing version, an upper-bound search (first tail > aᵢ) the non-decreasing one, and using the wrong boundary is the commonest bug in an otherwise correct implementation — Sorting & Searching, §27 is the reference for the two searches. Longest decreasing is LIS on the negated values. The Θ(n²) method is the one to generalise — to weighted versions (maximise the sum of the chosen values rather than their count), to two-dimensional dominance (box stacking, envelope nesting: sort by one coordinate, LIS on the other), to "longest chain" problems — because its state is explicit; the tails method is the one to use when only an increasing sequence of scalars is wanted and n is large.

17 Matrix-chain multiplication and the Catalan count

Given a chain of matrices A₁A₂…Aₙ in which Aᵢ is pᵢ₋₁ × pᵢ, the matrix-chain problem asks for the full parenthesization that multiplies them in the fewest scalar multiplications — the product itself is the same whatever the order, because matrix multiplication is associative, but its cost is not. The state is an interval of the chain, the choice is where to make the outermost split, and the answer is a table indexed by (i, j) filled in order of increasing interval length. It is the first problem on this page whose state is a pair of endpoints rather than a prefix or a capacity, and the pattern it establishes — interval, split point, two subintervals, O(n³) — recurs in §18 and §19.

Why parenthesization matters

Multiplying a p × q matrix by a q × r matrix with the standard algorithm performs p·q·r scalar multiplications (each of the p·r output entries is an inner product of length q). Take three matrices with dimensions 10 × 100, 100 × 5 and 5 × 50. Multiplying the first two first costs 10·100·5 = 5 000 and yields a 10 × 5 matrix, which times the third costs 10·5·50 = 2 500 — 7 500 in all. Multiplying the last two first costs 100·5·50 = 25 000, yields a 100 × 50 matrix, and the final product costs 10·100·50 = 50 000 — 75 000, ten times more, for the same answer. The dimension 100 is the culprit: the good order kills it early (it appears in only one product), the bad order carries it into both. With six matrices the spread between orders is wider still, and the number of orders is no longer two.

Optimal substructure, by cut and paste

Take an optimal parenthesization of Aᵢ…Aⱼ. Its outermost multiplication joins two pieces, Aᵢ…Aₖ and Aₖ₊₁…Aⱼ, for some k. The claim is that the parenthesization it uses inside Aᵢ…Aₖ must be an optimal one for that subchain: if a cheaper way of computing Aᵢ…Aₖ existed, cutting the sub-parenthesization out and pasting the cheaper one in would leave the outermost product's cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ unchanged (the dimensions of the pieces do not depend on how they were computed) and lower the total — contradicting optimality. The same holds for the right piece. So the optimum for (i, j) is, for its own split k, exactly m[i][k] + m[k+1][j] + pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ; and since the recurrence takes the minimum over every k, including that one, it cannot be larger than the optimum, and since every value it considers is the cost of some legal parenthesization, it cannot be smaller. That is the whole correctness proof, and its two halves — "the optimum is among the candidates" and "every candidate is achievable" — are the shape of every such argument on this page. Note what made the cut-and-paste legal: the two subchains share nothing (no matrix is in both) and the joining cost depends only on their dimensions, which is the independence that longest simple paths lacked in §02.

Counting the inner iterations exactly

For chain length l there are n − l + 1 intervals, and each tries l − 1 split points. Summing (n − l + 1)(l − 1) over l = 2 … n and substituting t = l − 1 gives ∑ₜ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ t(n − t) = n·∑t − ∑t² = n·(n−1)n/2 − (n−1)n(2n−1)/6 = (n³ − n)/6. What the pattern generalises to

The three ingredients — an interval as the state, a split point as the choice, and a combining cost that depends only on the interval's endpoints and the split — define interval DP, and §18–§19 are its next two instances. Optimal binary search trees replace "split between Aₖ and Aₖ₊₁" with "make kᵣ the root" and the joining cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ with the interval's probability mass; palindromic-subsequence problems shrink the interval from both ends and need no split at all, which is what drops them from O(n³) to O(n²). Two facts about this particular problem are worth carrying. The O(n³) bound is not the last word: an O(n log n) algorithm exists for matrix-chain ordering, based on a very different (polygon-triangulation) view of the same Catalan structure, but it is intricate and the cubic table is what everyone uses. And the table answers the question "in what order" for any associative operation with an order-dependent cost — chained joins in a query planner are the same problem with row-count estimates in place of dimensions, and a query optimiser's join-order table is, structurally, this m[i][j].

18 Optimal binary search trees

Given sorted keys k₁ < k₂ < … < kₙ, the probability pᵢ that a search asks for kᵢ, and the probability qᵢ that a search asks for a value strictly between kᵢ and kᵢ₊₁ (with q₀ below k₁ and qₙ above kₙ), an optimal binary search tree is a BST on those keys that minimises the expected number of nodes examined per search. A balanced tree minimises the worst search; when the access distribution is skewed, the tree that minimises the expected search puts hot keys near the root even at the cost of height, and finding it is an interval DP whose state is a run of consecutive keys and whose choice is which key to make the root. The search-tree invariant itself is Trees & BSTs, §14; here the shape is the unknown.

The cost being minimised

the model: n keys, n + 1 "dummy" leaves for the misses∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ pᵢ + ∑ᵢ₌₀ⁿ qᵢ = 1 a search ends at key kᵢ with probability pᵢ, or in the gap dᵢ (between kᵢ and kᵢ₊₁) with probability qᵢ in a tree T, a search that ends at key kᵢ examines depth(kᵢ) + 1 nodes; one that ends in gap dᵢ examines depth(dᵢ) + 1 (the dummy leaf counted as a node) E[search cost in T] = ∑ᵢ (depth(kᵢ) + 1)·pᵢ + ∑ᵢ (depth(dᵢ) + 1)·qᵢ = 1 + ∑ᵢ depth(kᵢ)·pᵢ + ∑ᵢ depth(dᵢ)·qᵢ the number of candidate shapes is the Catalan number C(n): 42 for n = 5, 16 796 for n = 10, ≈ 6.6·10⁹ for n = 20 — enumeration is out

Two features of the model matter for the recurrence. First, the dummies make every search terminate at some leaf of the extended tree, so the cost of a subtree is a clean function of the keys and gaps it contains. Second, when a subtree rooted at some key is hung one level deeper in a larger tree, every node in it gains one unit of depth, so the subtree's expected cost rises by exactly the total probability mass inside it — call that w. That single observation is what makes the cost decompose over the root's two subtrees, and it is the only algebra the derivation needs.

Optimal substructure

Let T be an optimal tree on kᵢ…kⱼ with root kᵣ. Its left subtree is a BST on kᵢ…kᵣ₋₁ with dummies dᵢ₋₁…dᵣ₋₁, and the claim is that it is an optimal one. The cut-and-paste step: the contribution of the left subtree to T's cost is its own expected cost plus w[i][r−1] (every node in it is one level deeper than in isolation); so a cheaper BST on the same keys, spliced in under the same root, would lower T's cost by exactly the difference — the root's contribution and the right subtree's are untouched, because the BST property is preserved (everything in the left subtree is still smaller than kᵣ) and no depth outside the left subtree changes. That contradiction proves the left subtree optimal; the right is symmetric. Unlike matrix chains, an empty subtree is a real possibility (when r = i or r = j), and the base case e[i][i−1] = qᵢ₋₁ is what makes the recurrence uniform over those edges — the empty "tree" is the one dummy leaf, examined once. The i ≤ n + 1 range of the row index exists solely so that e[n+1][n] = qₙ is defined.

What the problem is and is not

This is the static version: the distribution is known in advance and the tree is built once. Its dynamic cousins — splay trees, which adapt to the access sequence without knowing it, and the self-balancing trees of Balanced & Multiway Trees — solve a different problem, and the celebrated (still open) dynamic-optimality conjecture asks whether a splay tree is within a constant factor of the best sequence of trees. Two cheaper alternatives to the table are worth knowing when n is large. The greedy rule — put the most probable key at the root and recurse on both sides — is the obvious heuristic and has no guarantee: on the instance above it roots the tree at k₅, the hottest key, then at k₁, and builds a chain whose cost is 3.05 (or 3.10 with the other tie-break) against the optimal 2.75 — and instances exist where it is far worse. The weight-balanced rule — choose the root so that the probability mass on the two sides is as equal as possible — is the good heuristic: it runs in O(n log n) (or O(n) with care), and its expected cost is bounded by a small constant plus a constant multiple of the entropy of the access distribution, which is within a constant factor of the optimum because the optimum is itself at least a constant multiple of that entropy. Both are heuristics; the table is the algorithm, and the Knuth refinement makes it O(n²), which for a few thousand keys is instantaneous.

19 Interval DP: palindromes, and the "last operation" trick

Interval dynamic programming is the family of recurrences whose state is a contiguous range [i, j] of a sequence, whose base cases are the ranges of length 0 or 1, and whose evaluation order is increasing length, so that every subrange a state depends on is filled before it. Matrix chains (§17) and optimal search trees (§18) are its two classical members; this section treats the family as such — what the state looks like, when the transition needs a split point and when it does not, and the one modelling trick that turns an apparently non-decomposable problem (bursting balloons) into an interval recurrence: choose the last operation, not the first.

Longest palindromic subsequence: an endpoint recurrence

A subsequence keeps some of the characters in order and drops the rest; a palindrome reads the same backwards. The longest palindromic subsequence of s is the longest subsequence that is a palindrome, and the recurrence follows from one question about the two ends of a range: if s[i] = s[j], some longest palindromic subsequence of s[i..j] uses both ends — cut and paste: take a longest one, P; if P uses neither end, wrapping the pair s[i]…s[j] around it gives a longer palindrome, a contradiction; if P uses exactly one, say s[i], then P's last character is some s[j′] with j′ < j and s[j′] = s[i] = s[j], and swapping index j′ for j keeps a palindrome of the same length that uses both. Strip the two ends off that P and what remains is a palindromic subsequence of s[i+1..j−1], and it must be a longest one (else paste a longer one back in). If s[i] ≠ s[j], no palindrome uses both as its two endpoints, so a longest one lies inside s[i+1..j] or inside s[i..j−1].

Substring is a different problem

The longest palindromic substring requires contiguity, and the same word shows the difference: bbabcbcab has a palindromic subsequence of length 7 but its longest palindromic substrings (bab, bcb, cbc) have length 3. The substring problem has its own O(n²) interval recurrence — P[i][j] = (s[i] = s[j]) and P[i+1][j−1], a boolean table — but it is more naturally solved by expanding outward from each of the 2n − 1 centres (each character, and each gap between characters), which is also O(n²) worst case, O(1) extra space, and fast in practice; an O(n) algorithm (Manacher's) exists and belongs with string algorithms. The subsequence problem has no such centre structure, because dropped characters break contiguity, and its natural home is the interval table. Confusing the two is the standard error: "palindrome" names the property, "substring" versus "subsequence" names the problem.

Burst balloons: choose the last, not the first

Balloons with values v₀ … vₙ₋₁ stand in a row. Bursting balloon k earns vₗ · vₖ · vᵣ, where l and r are its current left and right neighbours (an absent neighbour counts as 1), and then its neighbours become adjacent. The question is the maximum total for bursting all of them, in some order. The tempting recurrence — "burst k first, then solve the left and right parts independently" — is wrong, and it is worth seeing exactly why. After k is gone, the balloons k − 1 and k + 1 are neighbours: the coin earned by bursting k − 1 later depends on what is still standing on the right side, and vice versa. The two "subproblems" share a boundary that changes as each is solved, so their optimal values do not add — the same failure of independence as longest simple paths in §02, in miniature. The trick is general. Whenever an operation changes the neighbourhood structure — merging adjacent piles, removing an element so its neighbours touch, inserting a separator, cutting a stick at a point — the sub-ranges after the first operation interact, but the sub-ranges before the last one do not, because the last operation's context (the two boundary elements) is fixed for the entire history of the range. Matrix chains are the same idea already in disguise: the outermost multiplication is the last one performed, and the two sides are computed independently before it. Optimal BSTs choose the root, which is the first comparison of every search — but there the subproblems are independent anyway, because a key's subtree does not change when the rest of the tree is rearranged. The question to ask is not "first or last" by rote but "which choice leaves subproblems whose instances do not depend on each other's solutions".

When it is O(n²) and when it is O(n³)

The cost is set by the transition's shape, not by the state's. Endpoint recurrences — palindromic subsequence, palindromic substring, "can this range be reduced to empty by removing matching ends", interval scheduling variants that only look at the ends — read O(1) neighbours per state and cost Θ(n²). Split recurrences — matrix chains, optimal BSTs, burst balloons, merging adjacent stones with a cost equal to their total weight, triangulating a polygon at minimum total edge length (the Catalan structure again) — try O(n) splits per state and cost Θ(n³); the exact count of the inner loop is a cubic polynomial with leading coefficient 1/6, as §17 derived. A split recurrence drops to O(n²) only under an extra hypothesis such as the root monotonicity of §18 (Knuth) or the related quadrangle-inequality conditions of §25, and the hypothesis must be checked per problem: it holds for optimal BSTs and for some merging costs, and fails for matrix chains. Space is Θ(n²) for the table, but an endpoint recurrence reads only the previous two lengths and rolls to Θ(n); a split recurrence reads every shorter length and does not.

20 DP on trees: the subtree is the state

Dynamic programming on a rooted tree uses the subtree hanging from each node as the subproblem: a node's answer is computed from its children's answers, in post-order, so that every subtree is solved exactly once and the root's answer is the answer for the whole tree. The structure that makes it work is that removing a node disconnects its children's subtrees from each other and from everything above — the subproblems are independent — and the reason the same problems are hard on general graphs is that removing a vertex from a graph does not. The maximum-weight independent set is the canonical example, because it is NP-hard on graphs and linear on trees, and the (take, skip) pair it needs is the pattern most tree DPs follow.

Maximum-weight independent set

An independent set is a set of vertices no two of which are adjacent; its weight is the sum of the vertex weights. On a tree rooted at r, consider any node u and an optimal independent set of the subtree Tᵤ. Either u is in it — then none of its children are, and what remains inside each grandchild's subtree must be an optimal independent set of that subtree (cut and paste: the grandchildren's subtrees are pairwise disconnected and not adjacent to u, so improving one improves the whole) — or u is not in it, and each child's subtree contributes an optimal independent set of its own. Writing that as two quantities per node avoids the grandchildren entirely.

Why the tree is easy and the graph is not

Everything above rests on one fact: in a tree, the subproblem "the best set inside Tᵤ given whether u is used" is completely described by the pair (u, taken?) — 2n states — because no vertex outside Tᵤ is adjacent to any vertex inside it except through u. In a general graph, the vertices already decided constrain the undecided ones along arbitrary edges, and the smallest state that makes the recurrence correct is the set of vertices excluded so far — the same enlargement that §07 describes for problems where the natural state is too small, and that §22 pays in full with a 2ⁿ bitmask. That is not a weakness of the method; maximum independent set is NP-hard on general graphs, so no polynomial state space is expected. The tree DP is also the base case of the DP on bounded treewidth graphs: a tree decomposition organises a graph into overlapping bags of at most k + 1 vertices arranged as a tree, and the (take, skip) pair becomes a table of 2^{k+1} entries per bag, giving O(2ᵏ·n) — linear in n for every fixed k, and the tree is the case k = 1. A tree DP can also run on the DFS tree of a graph when the problem tolerates back edges (lowest common ancestors, bridges, articulation points), but that is graph theory rather than DP and lives with Graphs, §15.

21 DP on DAGs, and the hand-off to Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall

On a directed acyclic graph, shortest paths, longest paths and path counts from a source are all computed by one pass over the vertices in topological order, relaxing each edge exactly once: Θ(V + E), with no priority queue and no restriction on edge weights. The reason is that a DAG's topological order is a dynamic-programming evaluation order — every vertex's predecessors come before it — and the converse is the deeper fact: every dynamic program on this page is a shortest- or longest-path computation on the DAG of its subproblems. Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall are what the same recursion becomes when the graph is allowed to have cycles and the state must be enlarged to keep the recursion well-founded.

Why it is correct, and why the DAG is essential

Shortest paths have optimal substructure (§02): a shortest s → v path's last edge is some (u, v), and the part before it is a shortest s → u path, else splice a shorter one in. So d[v] = min over in-edges of d[u] + w(u, v) — provided every d[u] is final when d[v] is computed, which the topological order guarantees since u precedes v. Longest paths need the same argument plus one thing more: the cut-and-paste step splices a longer s → u path onto the edge (u, v), and in a graph with cycles the spliced path might revisit a vertex and stop being simple — that was the failure of §02. In a DAG no path revisits anything, "simple" is automatic, and the argument goes through. Path counting is the same recurrence with ∑ in place of min: every s → v path ends in exactly one in-edge, and the paths ending in (u, v) are in bijection with s → u paths. Acyclicity is essential there too — in a graph with a cycle there are infinitely many walks.

Every DP table is a DAG, and every DAG problem is a DP

Turn the perspective round. The subproblem graph of §05 — a vertex per state, an edge from each state to the states its recurrence reads — is a DAG by construction, and the recurrence is a shortest- or longest-path computation on it. Rod cutting: vertices 0 … n, an edge j → j − i of weight pᵢ for every piece length i; the best cutting is the longest path from n to 0. Edit distance: the (m + 1) × (n + 1) grid with right, down and diagonal edges weighted 1, 1 and [xᵢ ≠ yⱼ]; the distance is the shortest path from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, and an alignment is a path. 0/1 knapsack: vertices (i, c), an edge to (i + 1, c) of weight 0 (skip item i) and, when it fits, to (i + 1, c − wᵢ) of weight vᵢ (take it); the answer is the longest path from (0, W) to any (n, ·). Longest increasing subsequence: an edge i → j whenever i < j and aᵢ < aⱼ, unit weights, longest path. In every case the table's fill order is a topological order of that DAG, the "relaxation" is the recurrence's inner step, and the running time is (vertices + edges) of the subproblem DAG — which is the "(states) × (choices)" of §01 said in graph language. The reason to know this is practical: when a DP is unclear, drawing its DAG often makes the state and the order obvious, and when a graph problem lives on a DAG (scheduling with precedence, critical paths, counting lattice paths), a one-pass DP is the whole algorithm.

The hand-off: cycles, and two ways to enlarge the state

22 DP over subsets: Held–Karp for the travelling salesman, and bitmask states

When the natural state must record which elements have been used — not how many — the state is a subset, there are 2ⁿ of them, and a dynamic program over subsets is exponential but still far better than the factorial enumeration it replaces. The travelling-salesman problem is the canonical case: the shortest tour that visits every one of n cities once and returns home. Brute force tries (n − 1)! orders; the Held–Karp recurrence tries Θ(n²·2ⁿ) (subset, last city, previous city) triples, and the gap between those two counts — a factor of about 2.9·10⁸ at n = 20, or 5.8·10⁹ once each tour is charged its n additions, which is the n! column of the table below — is the whole point of the section. The subset is stored as an integer bitmask, which makes "the state" a table index like any other.

Why the state has to be a set

A partial tour that starts at city 1, has visited some cities, and now stands at city j needs two things to be extended optimally: where it is (j, because the next distance depends on it) and which cities it has already visited (so none is repeated). The count of visited cities is not enough — two partial tours with the same count and the same endpoint may have visited different sets, and their best completions differ. So the smallest state that makes the recurrence correct is (S, j) with S the set visited so far and j ∈ S the current city. This is the enlargement §02 predicted for problems whose pieces compete for a shared resource (the cities), and the reason §20's independent-set DP is linear on trees and exponential on graphs. The cut-and-paste argument is then standard: if the optimal path through S ending at j arrived from i, its prefix is an optimal path through S ∖ {j} ending at i — a better prefix, followed by the same edge (i, j), would still visit exactly S and would be shorter.

23 Digit DP: counting the numbers up to N with a property of their digits

Digit dynamic programming counts (or optimises over) the integers in [0, N] whose decimal digits satisfy some property, by walking the digits of N from the most significant one and keeping as state the position, a single bit that records whether the number built so far is still equal to N's prefix (and therefore still constrained by N's next digit), and whatever summary of the digits placed so far the property needs. The count is exponential in the number of digits — there are N + 1 candidates — but the state space is tiny: (number of digits) × 2 × (size of the property summary), and the answer is read off a memo over it. It is the last state space in this page's catalogue and the one that looks least like the others, until the tight bit is recognised as just another state variable that makes the recursion well-founded.

The problem and the state

Take the property "the digits sum to k" and N = 253, k = 7. A number in [0, 253] is a string of three digits d₀d₁d₂ (leading zeros allowed, so 7 is 007) that is lexicographically at most 253. Building the string left to right, the constraint ≤ 253 bites only while the prefix built so far equals the prefix of N: if the first digit is 0 or 1, every completion is below 253 and the remaining digits are free (0 … 9); if it is 2, the next digit is limited to 0 … 5, and only if it is exactly 5 does the last digit stay limited, to 0 … 3. So the state needs the position, the tight flag ("prefix still equal to N's"), and — for this property — the digit sum so far. Nothing else about the prefix matters: two prefixes at the same position, with the same tightness and the same sum, have identical sets of valid completions.

Other properties, other summaries

The summary is whatever the property needs to be checked one digit at a time; the position and tight bit never change. "No two adjacent digits equal" needs the previous digit (10 values) and — because leading zeros are not real digits — a started flag, so that 007 is not rejected for its two zeros; the state is (pos, tight, started, prev), at most L × 2 × 2 × 10. On N = 253 that property holds for 212 of the 254 integers (the 42 exceptions are 11, 22, …, 99, 100, 110–119, 122, 133, …, 200, 211, 220–229, 233, 244), computed from 16 memo states and confirmed by brute force. "Divisible by m" needs the remainder so far (m values, updated as (10·r + d) mod m). "Contains the digit 4" needs one bit. "Number of digits equal to 1" (the classic "count the 1s written from 1 to N") needs the count so far, or better, is computed as a plain sum of per-position counts. "Digits non-decreasing" needs the previous digit. The general recipe is: identify the smallest summary of a prefix that determines which completions are valid, and the memo is over (position, tight, summary). When the summary is Θ(L)-sized — the sum, the count — the state space is O(L²); when it is a bitmask of the digits used, it is O(L·2¹⁰); in every case it is polynomial in L = log₁₀ N, hence polylogarithmic in N.

Where it fits

Digit DP is a prefix DP in disguise. The "string" is the decimal expansion of N, the state is a prefix of it plus a summary, and the tight bit is exactly the device that turns the inequality x ≤ N — a global constraint on the whole number — into a local one that each position can check on its own. The same device appears wherever a global bound must be enforced digit by digit or character by character: counting strings lexicographically below a given string with a pattern constraint, counting binary numbers with a bit property (the base is 2 and the tight bit works unchanged), and the "minimum number ≥ N with a property" questions, which reverse the sum into a min and add a reconstruction. What makes it worth its own section is the habit it teaches: when a problem's natural search space is the integers up to N, and N is far too large to enumerate, the right question is "what does a position-by-position walk need to remember" — and the answer is almost always a state of size polynomial in log N.

24 Space optimisation, and reconstruction after rolling

A table entry may be discarded the moment no later entry will read it, so the memory a bottom-up dynamic program needs is not the size of its table but the size of the window of entries still to be read — one row when the recurrence looks back one row, two rows when it also reads the current row, k rows for a look-back of depth k, and a constant when the state is one-dimensional. Every table on this page was drawn in full because the figures step through it, but the value alone never needed it: Fibonacci keeps two numbers, the knapsack keeps one row of W + 1, LCS and edit distance keep two rows of the shorter string. The price of rolling is the walk-back. Reconstruction reads cells that a rolled program has already overwritten, and this section is about that trade — what the window rule gives, what it takes, and the three ways to get the solution back without paying for the whole table: Hirschberg's split, checkpointing, and storing the choices instead of the values.

Why the window is what it is

The rule follows from §05: the working set is the set of states written but still to be read, and a row-major order over a two-index recurrence that reads only the previous row leaves exactly one row pending at any moment. LCS reads three neighbours, two of them in the previous row and one in the current, so the current row must exist alongside the previous one — two rows — and by making the shorter string index the columns the two rows are as short as possible: 2·(min(m, n) + 1) words, which the §13 figure measures as 14 on the worked pair against 8 × 7 = 56 table entries. The knapsack recurrence reads only row i − 1, and the two reads it makes — dp[i − 1][w] and dp[i − 1][w − wᵢ] — are both at columns ≤ w, so a single row overwritten from the right never destroys a value before it is read; that is the downward loop of §11, and the same row swept upward is a different program (the unbounded knapsack), not a faster one. For a one-dimensional state the window is the look-back depth: two scalars for a recurrence in i − 1 and i − 2. And for the interval family the window is decided by the transition's shape rather than the state's: the palindromic-subsequence recurrence reads L[i + 1][j − 1], L[i + 1][j] and L[i][j − 1], all of length l − 1 or l − 2, so filling by length keeps two diagonals — 2n words against the n(n + 1)/2 = 45 cells of the n = 9 figure — whereas matrix chains read m[i][k] + m[k + 1][j] for every split k, which touches every shorter length, and nothing can be discarded until the top entry is done.

What is lost: the walk-back

Every reconstruction on this page walks the finished table backwards — s[j] for rod cutting, the keep test dp[i][w] ≠ dp[i − 1][w] for knapsack, the three-way case test at (i, j) for LCS and edit distance, root[i][j] for search trees — and each step reads a cell chosen by the previous step, anywhere in the table. A rolled program has overwritten all but the last row, so the first step of the walk-back succeeds and the second reads garbage. The value is intact; the solution is gone. This is not a small loss: for alignment, diff, parsing and scheduling the solution is the output and the value is a by-product, and §07's checklist item 6 ("decide before writing the loop whether the solution is wanted") exists because rolling and reconstruction pull in opposite directions. There are three ways to have both, in increasing order of generality and decreasing order of elegance.

Way one: Hirschberg — recompute the split, recurse

§15 is the full treatment. The forward pass in two rows gives the middle row's scores; the same pass on the reversed strings gives the scores from the middle row to the end; their sum's minimum locates a column through which some optimal path crosses the middle row; recurse on the two sub-rectangles. Space O(m + n), time under n·∑ₖ⌈m/2ᵏ⌉ ≈ 2mn, measured on kitten → sitting at 80 cells against the plain table's 42. Its hypothesis is the one stated there — a grid DAG in which every path crosses every row, a recurrence local to adjacent rows, an additive objective — which LCS, edit distance and linear-gap alignment satisfy and interval DPs and weighted interval scheduling do not. When it applies it is the answer: linear space, a constant-factor time penalty, and an exact solution.

Way three: store the choice, not the value

A walk-back does not need the numbers; it needs, at each cell, which case won. That is a small integer — one of two cases for knapsack and weighted intervals, one of three for LCS and edit distance, one of n for a split table — and it fits in one or two bits per cell where the value needed a word. A bitset over mn cells at one bit each is what production diff and alignment tools keep when they keep anything.

When the full table is cheap anyway

Rolling is an engineering decision, and on most of this page it would be the wrong one. A table of a few thousand entries is nothing; a table of 10⁶ entries is a few megabytes; the trouble starts at 10⁸–10⁹ entries, which for a prefix-pair DP means strings of tens of thousands of characters and for knapsack means n·W in the hundreds of millions. Below that, keep the table: the walk-back is free, the code is the recurrence and nothing else, and every cell can be inspected when the answer looks wrong, which is the debugging tool §28 will recommend. Two further remarks. Memoized programs do not roll at all — the memo holds every reached state until the top call returns (§06), and when memory is the constraint that is a reason to convert to a loop. And rolling interacts with parallelism in the opposite direction from what one might expect: the anti-diagonal order that lets a prefix-pair table be filled in wavefronts (§14) needs three diagonals alive rather than two rows, so the narrowest order and the most parallel order are different topological orders of the same graph, and a program picks one.

The rule and its price. Alive = written but not yet read; the window is the look-back depth of the recurrence in the chosen order; rolling costs nothing in time and everything in reconstruction. Get the solution back by Hirschberg's split when the recurrence is local and the objective additive (linear space, twice the time), by checkpointing when it is not (√m rows, twice the time, any row-by-row DP), or by keeping the choices as bits when a factor of sixteen in bytes is enough. And before doing any of it, ask whether the table fits — usually it does.

25 The named optimisations and their hypotheses

A dynamic-programming optimisation is a theorem of the form "if the cost function has property P, then the inner minimisation over choices can be answered in less than linear time", and each of the named ones trades one factor of n for one hypothesis on the costs. None of them changes the state space or the recurrence; they change how the min (or max, or ∑) over candidates is evaluated, by exploiting an order among the candidates that the hypothesis guarantees. That is also why every one of them is dangerous: applied to a recurrence whose costs lack the property, the narrowed search silently returns a suboptimal table, with no error and often a plausible number. This section states six of them — the monotone deque, Knuth's optimisation, the divide-and-conquer optimisation, the convex hull trick with its Li Chao generalisation, the sum-over-subsets transform, and bitset acceleration — each with its exact hypothesis, its cost before and after, and a two-line reason it works. Two are measured on this page: the monotone deque in the figure below, and Knuth's window in §18's figure, where it examined 28 candidate roots instead of 35 and returned the same 2.75. 1. The sliding window and the monotone deque — measured

the recurrence shapedp[i] = c(i) + min over j ∈ [i − k, i) of dp[j] (reach position i by a jump of at most k from some j, paying c(i) on landing; dp[0] = c(0)) naive: examine every j in the window → ∑ᵢ min(i, k) ≈ nk candidate examinations = Θ(nk) HYPOTHESISthe candidate set is a WINDOW whose two ends only move forward as i grows, and the quantity minimised is a function of j alone (dp[j], or dp[j] + g(j)) — nothing in the candidate depends on i the deque, and why it is O(n)keep a deque of indices from the window with dp INCREASING from front to back. At step i: expire the front if it has left the window (index < i − k); the front is now the window minimum → dp[i] = c(i) + dp[front] pop from the back every j with dp[j] ≥ dp[i], then push i (such a j is older than i AND no better: it leaves the window before i does and is never the minimum again — DOMINATED) each index is pushed once and popped or expired at most once ⇒ ≤ 2n deque operations in all, whatever k is ⇒ O(n), amortised O(1) per step measured at k = 3 on the figure's 12 costs: naive 30 examinations = ∑ min(i, 3); deque 16 comparisons, 12 pushes, 5 pops + 4 expiries; both return 18; brute force over all 504 jump sequences: 18

The dominance argument is the whole proof. An index j at the back with dp[j] ≥ dp[i] can never again be the minimum of any future window, because every future window that contains j also contains the newer, no-worse i; so j may be forgotten, and after the pops the deque is increasing in dp from front to back, which is exactly the invariant that makes the front the minimum. Expiry handles the other end: the front is the oldest survivor, so it is the only index that can fall out of the window at step i, and one comparison per step keeps the window honest. The same structure answers "minimum of every window of width k over an array" in O(n), and it is what turns the bounded knapsack of §11 into Θ(nW) without the logarithm: for item i with multiplicity kᵢ, the recurrence over capacity w reads f[w − t·wᵢ] + t·vᵢ for t = 0..kᵢ, which, after grouping capacities by their residue modulo wᵢ and subtracting the linear term, is a sliding-window maximum over each residue class. The deque itself is the Linked Stacks & Queues page's deque used as an algorithm rather than a container.

2. Knuth's optimisation — measured in §18

the recurrence shapedp[i][j] = w(i, j) + min over r = i … j of ( dp[i][r − 1] + dp[r + 1][j] ) (an interval recurrence whose split cost depends only on the interval, not on r) naive: Θ(n²) intervals × O(n) roots = O(n³) HYPOTHESIS (on w alone)quadrangle inequality w(a, c) + w(b, d) ≤ w(a, d) + w(b, c) for all a ≤ b ≤ c ≤ d monotonicity w(b, c) ≤ w(a, d) whenever [b, c] ⊆ [a, d] then dp inherits the quadrangle inequality, and the smallest optimal root satisfies opt(i, j − 1) ≤ opt(i, j) ≤ opt(i + 1, j) the narrowed loop and its costr ranges over [opt(i, j − 1), opt(i + 1, j)] instead of [i, j]; for a fixed length l the ranges telescope: ∑ᵢ (opt(i + 1, j) − opt(i, j − 1) + 1) ≤ n + (n − l + 1) ≤ 2n ⇒ O(n) per length, O(n²) in all. §18 measured 28 candidates against 35 for the plain loop, and verified the monotonicity on all 10 pairs of the finished table

Why the hypothesis is needed is visible in the one interval problem on this page where it fails: matrix chains. There the split cost pᵢ₋₁·pₖ·pⱼ depends on the split point k, so it is not a w(i, j) at all, and the roots are not monotone — a narrowed loop would examine the wrong k range and return a table whose top entry is merely the best among the splits it tried. For the optimal search tree w(i, j) is a sum of probabilities over the interval, which satisfies the quadrangle inequality with equality and is monotone on inclusion, so the theorem applies. The same test — is the split cost a function of the interval only, and does it satisfy the inequality? — settles the other classical instances: merging adjacent piles with cost equal to their total weight (yes), and most cases where the cost is a sum over the merged range; the general result is Yao's quadrangle-inequality theorem — at bottom a monotonicity lemma about the argmin of a function with the quadrangle property, the same lemma that licenses the divide-and-conquer optimisation below — and the practical rule is that the hypothesis must be checked on the cost function, not assumed because the recurrence has the right shape.

3. The divide-and-conquer optimisation — hand-worked, not measured

the recurrence shapedp[i][j] = min over k < j of ( dp[i − 1][k] + C(k, j) ) (split the prefix of length j into i groups; the last group is (k, j]; i = 1 … K) naive: K rows × n cells × O(n) candidates = O(K·n²) HYPOTHESISthe argmin is MONOTONE along a row: opt(i, j) ≤ opt(i, j + 1) (guaranteed when C satisfies the quadrangle inequality, as a cost that is a sum over the group, or a "square of the sum", does) the algorithm, one row at a timeSOLVE(row i, cells [lo, hi], candidates [klo, khi]): j = ⌊(lo + hi)/2⌋; scan k ∈ [klo, khi] for the best; then SOLVE(i, [lo, j − 1], [klo, opt(j)]) and SOLVE(i, [j + 1, hi], [opt(j), khi]) — each recursion level scans O(n + number of cells) candidates in total ⇒ O(n log n) per row, O(K·n log n) overall

The two-line reason: computing the middle cell's argmin by a full scan tells you that every cell to its left has its argmin at or before that point and every cell to its right at or after it, so the two halves search disjoint-ish candidate ranges whose total length is about n per level of recursion, and there are log n levels. It is the optimisation of choice for "partition into K contiguous groups" problems — segmenting a sequence into K pieces at minimum total cost, placing K facilities — where Knuth's interval form does not apply because the state is (groups, prefix) rather than (interval). Its hypothesis is weaker than Knuth's (monotone argmin only), and it fails, silently, on costs that reward long groups more than proportionally in a non-convex way.

4. The convex hull trick, and the Li Chao tree — stated, not measured

the recurrence shapedp[i] = min over j < i of ( mⱼ · xᵢ + bⱼ ) where the slope mⱼ and intercept bⱼ depend on j (typically bⱼ = dp[j] + something), and xᵢ on i — every earlier state contributes a LINE in the query variable x, and dp[i] is the lower envelope of those lines evaluated at xᵢ. naive: O(n²) HYPOTHESIS, three strengths(a) slopes mⱼ arrive in monotone order AND queries xᵢ are monotone → keep the lower envelope in a deque; a line that is never below the envelope (cross-product test on three consecutive lines) is discarded; queries walk a pointer forward: amortised O(1) per state, O(n) in all (b) slopes monotone, queries arbitrary → the same envelope, binary search per query: O(log n) per state (c) neither monotone → a LI CHAO TREE over the query range: a segment tree in which each node keeps the line that wins at its midpoint; insert by descending and swapping the loser down; query by descending and taking the min: O(log C) per insert and per query over a coordinate range of size C (O(log n) after compressing the n query points)

The two-line reason for (a): with slopes sorted, the lower envelope of the lines is a convex chain whose pieces appear in slope order, so a new line either extends the chain at the end or makes some trailing pieces redundant — a stack discipline — and monotone queries sweep the chain once, exactly as the deque of optimisation 1 sweeps a window. For (c): at each node of a segment tree exactly one line can be the best at the node's midpoint, and any other line beats it on at most one side of the midpoint, so it is pushed into one child; a query descends one root-to-leaf path and one of the lines it meets is the minimum at that point. The trick appears whenever a cost has the form "a rate times a distance plus a state-dependent constant" — a batch that starts at time xᵢ and pays mⱼ per unit, a segment whose cost is a product of an aggregate of each side — and it is how O(n²) recurrences of that form become O(n) or O(n log n). The hypothesis to check before reaching for it is that the candidate really is linear in a query variable; a quadratic term in xᵢ that also multiplies j's data breaks the envelope structure.

6. Bitset acceleration — stated, not measured

The two-line reason: the recurrence for subset sum is "s is reachable after item i if it was reachable before or if s − aᵢ was", which is a word-parallel OR of a shifted row, and the loop-direction rule of §11 is satisfied automatically because the shift reads the old row. This is a constant-factor optimisation (a factor of w), not an asymptotic one, but the factor is 64 on a 64-bit machine: a subset sum with n = 1 000 and T = 10⁶ is 10⁹ cell updates as a loop and about 1.6·10⁷ word operations as a bitset, and it is the version to use when the table is boolean and wide. The hypothesis is the boolean-ness: the moment the cells hold values that must be maximised, the shift-and-OR is gone and the ordinary row update returns.

26 The Bellman equation, Viterbi, CTC — and why beam search is not DP

The Bellman optimality equation, V*(s) = max over actions a of [ r(s, a) + γ·∑ over s′ of P(s′ | s, a)·V*(s′) ], is a dynamic-programming recurrence over a state space that may contain cycles, and value iteration is its tabulation — a sweep of Bellman backups repeated until the table stops changing, with convergence guaranteed not by acyclicity but by the discount γ < 1, which makes each sweep a contraction. Viterbi decoding is the same recursion on a trellis of hidden states against time, with a product in place of the sum and a maximum over predecessors; the forward algorithm is the sum-product version on the same trellis; and CTC is the forward or Viterbi recursion on a trellis extended with blank states. All four are exact, because each keeps one value per state and the optimal-substructure argument goes through; beam search, which keeps the k best partial hypotheses and discards the rest, is not dynamic programming and has no such guarantee — which the figures below demonstrate with a width-1 beam that returns the wrong path on the default instance.

Why it is dynamic programming, and what replaces the evaluation order

Every ingredient of §01's template is present: a state, a recurrence over choices, a base case, a table and a reconstruction (the greedy policy). What is missing is a well-founded order, because the subproblem graph has cycles — from a cell of the gridworld an action can lead back to the same cell, and V(s) then depends on itself. §21 handled cycles in shortest paths by indexing the state with an edge count, so that distₖ(v) reads only distₖ₋₁; value iteration is exactly that move — Vₖ reads only Vₖ₋₁ — with one difference. Bellman-Ford's index is bounded by V − 1 because a shortest path is simple; an infinite-horizon return has no such bound, and the iteration would run forever were it not for the discount. The contraction is what the acyclicity was for: it guarantees that the sequence Vₖ settles, geometrically, at the unique fixed point, so the "table" is infinite in k but effectively finite, and it gives the stopping rule that turns the infinite recursion into a finite loop. The hand-off is the whole of model-based reinforcement learning: Reinforcement Learning, §03 derives the equation from the definition of return, and its §04 builds policy iteration and value iteration as the planning algorithms; the model-free methods that follow there are sampled approximations of the same backup. Here the point is narrower: it is §21's DP with the DAG condition replaced by a contraction, and the figure measures the contraction rather than assuming it.

Why beam search is not dynamic programming

Beam search over the same trellis keeps, at each time step, the k partial state sequences of highest probability so far, extends each by every state, and keeps the best k of the results; at k = 1 it is greedy decoding. It looks like Viterbi with a smaller table, and the difference is where the values live. Viterbi keeps one value per state — δₜ(j) for every j, S values per column — and the optimal-substructure argument guarantees that the best full path passes through some (t, j) and that its prefix is the best path to that (t, j), so nothing is lost by keeping only the best path to each state. Beam search keeps k hypotheses, ranked by their own probability, and a prefix that ranks below the k-th at time t is gone, even if it is the prefix of the eventual optimum. On the figure's default observations the width-1 beam stays in S₁ for all six steps and returns a path of probability 1.543·10⁻⁴, while Viterbi finds S₁ S₂ S₂ S₂ S₃ S₃ at 4.593·10⁻⁴, three times as likely; the width-2 beam happens to recover it on this instance. Over all 64 observation strings of length 6 on this model, the checking script found width 1 wrong on 33 and width 2 wrong on 23 — and the load-time audit of §29 counts the same failure on random models. Beam search earns its place where DP cannot be run: when the "state" a hypothesis carries is its whole prefix — a neural decoder's hidden state depends on every token before it — no two hypotheses share a state, the trellis is a tree with Vᵀ leaves, and pruning to k is the only option. That is the greedy page's territory (Greedy Algorithms, §25), and the honest description of beam search is a k-wide greedy heuristic with no optimality guarantee, not an approximate DP. The test is the one this section keeps returning to: if the values are indexed by state and every state is kept, it is dynamic programming and it is exact; if hypotheses are ranked and discarded, it is search, and its answer is a lower bound.

One recursion, four costumes. Value iteration: max over actions of an expectation, on a cyclic state graph, converging by contraction. Viterbi: max over predecessors of a product, on a trellis, exact in one pass. Forward: the same with a sum, giving a total instead of a best. CTC: forward or Viterbi on a trellis whose states are the positions of a blank-padded label. In each the table has one entry per state, the entry is computed from its predecessors' entries, and the answer is exact because of §02's cut-and-paste argument — which is precisely what beam search gives up when it ranks hypotheses instead of indexing states.

27 Recognising DP against greedy, divide and conquer, and backtracking

A problem is a dynamic-programming problem when three things hold at once: an optimal solution is built from optimal solutions to subproblems (optimal substructure, checked by cut and paste), the natural recursion asks the same subproblem many times (overlap), and the set of distinct subproblems is small enough to tabulate (a polynomial state space, or an exponential one that is still far smaller than the space of solutions). Each of the three failing routes the problem to a different paradigm: no optimal substructure and there is no correct recurrence at all; overlap absent and the recursion is divide and conquer, where a memo would never hit; a state space too large and the honest tools are backtracking, branch and bound, or a hand-off to NP-hardness. And one further property, the greedy-choice property, lets a problem that has everything DP needs skip the table entirely. This section is the decision procedure, the four paradigms side by side on the questions that separate them, and the quick "state-space smell test" that predicts the cost of a recurrence before it is written.

Step 1 in practice: the cut-and-paste one-liner, and the two ways it fails

The test for optimal substructure is a sentence: "take an optimal solution, look at its first (or last) choice, and argue that what remains is optimal for the residual instance — else swap in something better and contradict optimality". For every problem in §04–§23 that sentence is one line, and the page wrote it each time: the remainder of a rod, the load without item i, the alignment without its last column, the subchain on either side of a split, the subtree under a root. It fails in two recognisable ways. The shared resource failure (§02, §19): the two parts of the solution draw on something in common — vertices, a moving boundary — so improving one part changes the other's instance; the signature is that "swap in a better sub-solution" produces something that is not a legal solution. The hidden history failure (§07): the residual instance depends on how the state was reached, not only on the state; the signature is that two paths to the same state deserve different answers. Both have the same cure, enlarging the state, and the cure has a cost — one bit for the ring robber, the visited set for the salesman — which is what step 4 then measures.

Step 2 in practice: draw the graph before writing the loop

Overlap is the property that separates this page from its predecessor, and the diagnosis is quantitative: count distinct states and count calls. Fibonacci makes 2·F(n + 1) − 1 calls for n + 1 states; mergesort makes 2n − 1 calls for 2n − 1 distinct sub-arrays. The first is a table; the second is a tree, and giving it a memo adds a hash lookup per call and saves nothing. The intermediate case is worth naming: a divide and conquer whose combine step shares work across the split — closest pair's strip, which both halves' points feed — still has no repeated subproblem, is still divide and conquer, and its cost comes from the master theorem, not from a state count. The rule of thumb is the depth of the recursion: a subproblem half the size gives log n depth and a tree that is polynomial without help; a subproblem one smaller gives depth n and a tree that is exponential unless the states are merged (§03).

Step 3 in practice: when the table collapses

Every greedy algorithm is a dynamic program whose max over choices has been replaced by a rule, and the licence to do that is the greedy-choice property: an exchange argument showing that some optimal solution contains the locally chosen element, so the other branches of the max never need to be evaluated. Weighted interval scheduling (§12) shows the collapse and its failure in one recurrence: OPT(j) = max(OPT(j − 1), vⱼ + OPT(p(j))) must compare both branches in general, but with unit weights the earliest-finishing interval is always safe and the comparison can be skipped — the greedy page's §04 proof. Coin change (§09) collapses on canonical systems and not on {1, 3, 4}; knapsack collapses in its fractional form and not in its 0/1 form (160 against 220). The practical procedure is the greedy page's: propose the rule, then hunt for a three-element counterexample; if one exists, the table is the algorithm, and if a proof exists, the table was never needed. What one should not do is run the greedy rule on random instances and infer correctness from agreement — that page's fewest-conflicts rule passed 300 random tests and is wrong.

The test is to list the quantities the recurrence's right-hand side needs to know about the subproblem, and nothing more (§07's "too large" failure is what happens when the list is padded). If every entry is an integer bounded by a polynomial in the input size, the table is polynomial and the problem is a DP problem in the strong sense. If one entry is a number from the input — a capacity, a target sum — the table is pseudo-polynomial, fine in practice for small numbers and exponential in the input's bit length. If one entry is a subset, the table is 2ⁿ and the question is whether n ≤ 20. And if the honest state is a sequence or a path, the recurrence is the brute force in disguise, and the choice is between backtracking with pruning, branch and bound with a relaxation bound, an approximation scheme (the FPTAS sketch of §11 is a DP plus rounding), and a heuristic with no guarantee. That last hand-off is not a failure of the method: knapsack, subset sum and the travelling salesman are all NP-hard, and their dynamic programs are the best exact algorithms known for them — the smell test says only what the exponent will be.

Three quick diagnoses

  • "Longest path in a general graph." Optimal substructure fails on four vertices (§02); the state needed is the visited set; the smell test says n·2ⁿ; the answer is Held–Karp for small n and NP-hardness otherwise. On a DAG the same question is a one-pass DP (§21). The graph's acyclicity is the whole difference.
  • "Split a sequence into K contiguous groups at minimum cost." Substructure holds (the last group is independent of the rest); overlap is heavy (the same prefix, many K); the state is (groups, prefix), K·n cells with n choices each — O(Kn²), and O(Kn log n) under the divide-and-conquer optimisation's hypothesis (§25). A greedy "close the group when it exceeds a threshold" has a counterexample in three elements.
  • "Sort n numbers." Substructure holds; overlap does not — the two halves of mergesort are disjoint, and the sub-arrays of quicksort's recursion never repeat; the smell test gives Θ(n) distinct sub-arrays in a balanced tree. Divide and conquer, Θ(n log n), and a memo would only slow it down.
The one-paragraph version. Cut and paste says whether a recurrence is correct; the subproblem graph says whether a table is useful; an exchange argument says whether the table can be skipped; the smell test says whether it can be afforded. Answer the four in that order and the paradigm is decided — and most of the difficulty of dynamic programming in practice is the first step, choosing a state for which the first answer is yes.

28 Pitfalls

Every pitfall below was exhibited by a routine on this page and caught by a brute-force comparison in a figure — the wrong answers are measured numbers, not hypotheticals. Most of them are of one kind: the recurrence was right and the evaluation was wrong (an order, a base, a cell), so the program computed a plausible number for a different question. The cure in every case is the same habit the figures practise — run the table beside an exhaustive enumeration on an instance small enough to enumerate, and do not trust the table until they agree.

  • The capacity loop's direction decides which knapsack is being solved (§11). The same three lines return 46 swept downward and 48 swept upward on the four worked items at W = 10; the upward pass reads f[w − wᵢ] after it has been rewritten in the same pass, and the extra item that enters is a second copy. Neither number is wrong — each is the exact answer to its own problem, confirmed by its own brute force (16 subsets, 28 multisets) — which is why the bug survives testing: it is a change of problem, not an arithmetic error.
  • The coin loops' nesting decides what is counted (§09). Coins outer counts multisets, amount outer counts sequences; on {1, 2, 5} at A = 5 that is 4 against 9, at A = 11 it is 11 against 218. Both nests perform the same 10 updates from the same base, and the min recurrence is indifferent to the order — so the bug is invisible in the fewest-coins version and silent in the counting ones. Test on an amount two coins both fit.
  • A state that forgets what the future depends on returns wrong answers, silently (§02, §07). "Best over the prefix" for the house robber gives 16 where the optimum is 19; the linear robber on a ring gives illegal 4 and 10 on 2, 3, 2 and 5, 1, 1, 5; "longest simple path ending at v" has no recurrence at all. The signature is two paths to the same state deserving different answers; the cure is to carry the missing quantity (the last element, one bit, the visited set) and to accept the cost of carrying it.
  • "Burst first" is not "burst last" (§19). Choosing the first balloon to burst leaves two sides that share a moving boundary, and the memoized version of that recurrence returns 66 on [3, 1, 5, 8] where the optimum, found by choosing the last balloon and confirmed over all 24 orders, is 167. The same trap catches every "remove an element and its neighbours touch" problem; the fix is to condition on the operation whose context is fixed for the whole history of the range.
  • The tails array is not the longest increasing subsequence (§16). On [2, 6, 3, 4, 1] the final array is [1, 3, 4], which is not a subsequence of the input; its length is the answer and its contents are the smallest known endpoints of subsequences of each length, drawn from different subsequences. Reconstruction needs the predecessor links recorded at placement time; reading the array as the answer is wrong the moment a small element arrives late. The worked instance, where the array happens to be a valid LIS, is the misleading case.
  • ⌈log₂ k⌉ is not ⌊log₂ k⌋ + 1 (§11). The number of bundles binary splitting makes from k copies is ⌊log₂ k⌋ + 1 = ⌈log₂(k + 1)⌉: one bundle for k = 1, three for k = 4, four for k = 8. The tidier ⌈log₂ k⌉ gives zero bundles for k = 1 and one too few at every power of two; the routine was checked against both formulas for k = 1..20. Off-by-one in a logarithm is an off-by-one in an item count.
  • Pseudo-polynomial is not polynomial (§09, §11). Θ(nW) counts the cells honestly and is exponential in the number of bits of W; the same for Θ(m·A) in coin change and Θ(nT) in subset sum. The bound is fine when the numbers are small, is the reason the FPTAS works (round the values, then it is polynomial), and is no contradiction of NP-hardness. Saying "knapsack is in P because the DP is O(nW)" is the error; the input size is n log W, not nW.
  • Memoization keeps the recursion's depth (§03, §06). The memoized rod-cutting routine is n + 1 frames deep before its first return, Fibonacci n, LCS m + n; a table of a few thousand entries can exhaust a runtime's depth counter (Python's default limit is 1 000), a few hundred thousand a raw 8 MiB stack (Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §06), while the bottom-up loop uses none. The §06 figure prints the depth beside the counts. Convert to a loop, or run the memo with an explicit stack, before n is large.
  • Rolling the table deletes the walk-back (§24). The one-row knapsack and the two-row LCS return the right value and cannot reconstruct: the cells the walk-back reads are gone. Decide before writing the loop whether the solution is wanted (§07, item 6); if it is, keep the choice bits, checkpoint, or use Hirschberg — and note that on a 7 × 6 table checkpointing every third row keeps 8 rows alive, which saves nothing.
  • Hirschberg's "twice the table" is a slogan, not a bound (§15). The exact bound is n·∑ₖ⌈m/2ᵏ⌉, which equals 2mn only up to the ceilings; on kitten → sitting the measured 80 cells sit under both (84), but over 3 000 random pairs of lengths up to 14 the checking script found 241 that exceeded the bare 2mn and none that exceeded the true bound. Quote the sum, or say "about twice".
  • Ties make the reconstructed solution a convention (§04, §13, §18, §22). Rod cutting at n = 7 has five optimal cuttings and the table returns 1 + 6; the LCS table yields BCBA preferring up and BDAB preferring left, and a third LCS exists; two roots (k₂ and k₄) give the same optimal search-tree cost 2.75; three of four closing candidates tie at 26 in Held–Karp. The value is unique, the solution is not, and a test that compares reconstructed solutions across implementations will fail on every tie. Compare values, or fix the tie rule in the specification.
  • The base case is where the off-by-one lives (§08, §09, §18). W[0] = 1 for the staircase (setting it to 0 loses the single 2-step and makes W[2] = 1); E[0] = a[0] for Kadane (a base of 0 returns 0 on −3, −1, −2 instead of −1); C[0] = 1 for counting and N[0] = 0 for minimising; e[i][i − 1] = qᵢ₋₁ for the empty search tree, not 0; and Fibonacci at n = 1 is a base case whose subproblem graph has no edges, which the §05 figure shows. The empty solution's value — 0, 1, ∞, the first element, a dummy probability — is a decision, and the wrong one is a plausible number.
  • The answer is not always in the last cell (§07, §08, §16). Kadane's optimum 6 is E[6], not E[8] = 5; the LIS is max over i of dp[i], first attained at i = 7; the unbounded knapsack's best load may not use all the capacity. When the objective may end anywhere, the answer is a maximum over states, and reading the final state answers the question "best that ends at n".
  • A DP optimisation is a theorem with a hypothesis (§18, §25). Knuth's window is licensed by the quadrangle inequality on w(i, j), which the search-tree cost satisfies and the matrix-chain cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ does not; the narrowed loop on the wrong problem returns the best of the candidates it happened to try. The §18 figure verifies monotonicity on the finished table rather than assuming it; do the same before deleting a loop.
  • Beam search is not an approximate DP (§26). A width-1 beam on the figure's trellis returns a path of probability 1.54·10⁻⁴ where Viterbi finds 4.59·10⁻⁴, and across all 64 observation strings of length 6 on that model width 1 fails on 33 and width 2 on 23. Viterbi keeps a value per state and is exact; the beam ranks hypotheses and discards, and its answer is a lower bound with no guarantee. Reporting a beam result as "the most likely sequence" is a false claim.
  • Boundary conventions change p(j) and the table (§12). Under half-open intervals [3, 5) and [5, 9) are compatible and p(6) = 2; under closed intervals they are not and p(6) = 1. The binary search that computes p must use the same inequality as the compatibility test, and a silent change of ≤ to < is a different problem with a different optimum.
  • Closed forms in floating point stop being exact (§08). round(φⁿ/√5) agrees with the Fibonacci loop for every n ≤ 70 and first fails at n = 71, returning one more than 308 061 521 170 129. A DP over integers is exact; a closed form in doubles is exact until it is not, and the boundary is not where intuition puts it.
  • "Increasing S" is a valid subset order; "increasing j, then S" is not (§22). The Held–Karp state (S, j) reads (S ∖ {j}, i), and S ∖ {j} < S numerically, so a single loop over masks is a valid order; but a loop with the end city outside the subset loop would read entries not yet written. The order of a bitmask DP is by subset, and the other index is inside.

Where this goes next in the series. The two algorithms this page named and did not build — Bellman-Ford with its negative-cycle test and Floyd-Warshall with its all-pairs table, together with Dijkstra and Johnson's reweighting — are the subject of the shortest-paths part, and the hand-off of §11 and §22 — why knapsack, subset sum and the travelling salesman have pseudo-polynomial or exponential dynamic programs and nothing better, the FPTAS that a DP plus rounding yields, and the taxonomy of NP-hardness — is the subject of the NP-completeness and approximation part; both are reached through the series map. Upstream, the recursion whose redundancy this page removed is Recursion, Divide & Conquer; the paradigm that skips the table when an exchange argument allows it is Greedy Algorithms; the topological order that every DAG DP walks, and the DFS whose post-order memoization discovers, are Graphs; and the cost language, including what "pseudo-polynomial" means for the size of an input, is Algorithm Analysis.
Where it goes sideways, out of this domain. The Bellman equation of §26 is the foundation of Reinforcement Learning — value iteration and policy iteration are its planning algorithms when the model is known, and temporal-difference learning is the same backup sampled from experience when it is not. Viterbi and the forward algorithm are the decoding and evaluation problems of Hidden Markov Models & CRFs, where the trellis carries tags over words; CTC, the forward recursion on a blank-padded trellis, is the training objective of the end-to-end recognisers in Speech Processing; and the edit-distance and LCS tables of §13–§15, with a substitution matrix and gap penalties in place of unit costs, are global (Needleman–Wunsch) and, with the max-with-0 base named in §14, local (Smith–Waterman) sequence alignment in bioinformatics — Hirschberg's split is how genome-length alignments fit in memory. The beam search that §26 set against Viterbi is the decoder of every neural sequence model, and the greedy page's §25 is where its heuristic status is discussed.
