02 Optimal substructure: the cut-and-paste argument, and the case where it fails

A problem has optimal substructure when an optimal solution to any instance contains, as its parts, optimal solutions to the subproblems those parts define. It is the property that makes a recurrence correct: if the best answer to the whole is built from the best answers to the pieces, then "take the best over all first choices of (cost of the choice) + (best answer to what remains)" really does compute the optimum. Without it the recurrence computes something, and that something is wrong. Its original statement, from the multistage-decision setting in which the method was invented, is Bellman's principle of optimality: an optimal policy has the property that, whatever the initial state and first decision, the remaining decisions form an optimal policy for the state that results. That is the same claim with "policy" for solution and "state" for subproblem, and the Bellman equation of §28 is the principle written out as a recurrence.

the property, stated for a maximisation with one subproblem per choicelet S* be an optimal solution of instance I, and let c be the first choice S* makes, leaving residual instance I − c. CLAIM: the part of S* that solves I − c is an OPTIMAL solution of I − c. PROOF (cut and paste): if some solution T of I − c were better, then c ∪ T would be a solution of I with value cost(c) + value(T) > cost(c) + value(S* restricted to I − c) = value(S*) — contradicting S*'s optimality. ∎ HENCE: OPT(I) = max over choices c of [ cost(c) + OPT(I − c) ] ← the recurrence is licensed by the property the same property as the principle of optimalityPRINCIPLE OF OPTIMALITY whatever the initial state and the first decision, the remaining decisions must form an optimal policy for the state the first decision produces — "first decision" is the choice c above, "the state that results" is the residual instance I − c, and "optimal policy for it" is the claim just proved the four-step discovery pattern, used for every recurrence on this page1. a solution consists of making a CHOICE (the first cut, the split point, whether to take item i, the root of the subtree) 2. suppose the choice that leads to an optimum is HANDED to you; do not yet ask how to find it 3. characterise the SUBPROBLEMS the choice leaves — and keep the space of subproblems as small as possible, then widen it only when forced 4. show the subproblem solutions inside the optimum are themselves optimal, by cut and paste then the recurrence tries EVERY choice (step 2's gift is replaced by a max/min), and the number of choices × the number of subproblems is the cost

Two things the property must be checked for, not assumed

Optimal substructure varies along two axes that decide the cost. How many subproblems an optimal solution uses: rod cutting (§04), in its "first piece then the remainder" form, uses one — the remainder; matrix-chain multiplication (§19) uses two — the chains on either side of the split. How many choices there are for the subproblem(s): n for the first piece of a rod, j − i for the split point of a chain. The running time is, informally, (number of distinct subproblems) × (number of choices per subproblem): Θ(n)·O(n) = O(n²) for rod cutting, Θ(n²)·O(n) = O(n³) for matrix chains. The subproblem space must also be closed: if solving A₁…Aⱼ could require solving Aₖ₊₁…Aⱼ, then "prefixes" is not a closed space and the state must be widened to "intervals" Aᵢ…Aⱼ. A space that is too small produces a recurrence that references states it never defined; a space that is too large produces a correct algorithm that is slower than necessary. §07 gives the checklist.

The classic failure: shortest simple paths have it, longest simple paths do not

The property is not automatic, and the standard demonstration is a pair of problems that look identical. Unweighted shortest path: the fewest edges from u to v in a directed graph. If p is a shortest u → v path and w is any vertex on it, then the u → w part of p is a shortest u → w path — cut and paste: a shorter u → w path spliced onto the w → v part would give a shorter u → v path. (If the spliced walk repeated a vertex, excising the loop between the two visits would give a still shorter u → v walk, and a walk shorter than p contains a path shorter than p — the same contradiction. For longest simple paths this is exactly the step that fails.) So shortest paths have optimal substructure, and the DP over "shortest path to each vertex" (§23) and the all-pairs recurrence of Floyd-Warshall both rest on it. Unweighted longest simple path: the most edges from u to v without repeating a vertex. The word "simple" is forced — without it a cycle could be traversed forever — and it is exactly what breaks the property.

the counterexample — a directed 4-cycle with one chordvertices q, r, s, t; edges q→r, r→t, q→s, s→t, t→r, r→q (the cycle q→s→t→r→q, with chords q→r and r→t) a longest simple path from q to t is q → r → t (2 edges; q → s → t also has 2) is its prefix q → r a longest simple path from q to r? NO: q → s → t → r has 3 edges is its suffix r → t a longest simple path from r to t? NO: r → q → s → t has 3 edges and gluing those two "optimal" subpaths gives q → s → t → r → q → s → t — not even a simple path, so not a legal solution why: the subproblems are not independentthe u→w subproblem CONSUMES vertices (s and t) that the w→v subproblem then cannot use. Solving one changes the other's instance. shortest-path subproblems share no resource (a shared vertex would shorten the path); longest-simple-path subproblems compete for vertices. "independent" (no shared resources) and "overlapping" (the same subproblem reached along different routes) are DIFFERENT notions, and DP needs both.

The consequence is not a slower algorithm but no algorithm: longest simple path is NP-hard, and no polynomial dynamic program for it is known. The lesson generalises. Whenever the pieces of a solution compete for a shared resource — vertices in a path, a budget that both halves draw on, a constraint that one subproblem's choice imposes on another — the naive state does not capture enough, and either the state must be enlarged to carry the shared resource (the travelling-salesman state of §24 carries the entire set of visited cities, which is why it is exponential) or the problem is not a DP problem at all. The greedy page's §02 makes the same point from the other side: optimal substructure is necessary for greedy and for DP alike; the two paradigms differ in whether the choice can be made before the subproblems are solved.

The test to apply. Write the recurrence, then try to break it: find an instance where the optimum's parts are not optimal for their subproblems. For shortest paths, knapsack, LCS, matrix chains and every problem in §08–§25 the cut-and-paste argument goes through in one line. For longest simple paths it fails on four vertices. If you cannot write the one line, do not trust the table.

03 Overlapping subproblems: the call tree has 2ⁿ nodes, the subproblem graph has n + 1

A problem has overlapping subproblems when its natural recursion reaches the same subproblem along more than one path. Optimal substructure makes the recurrence correct; overlap is what makes remembering answers pay. The gap between the two counts — the size of the call tree and the number of distinct subproblems — is the entire speed-up of dynamic programming, and it is worth seeing measured before it is seen proved.

When memoization applies, and the disease it does not cure

A memo helps exactly when the recursion revisits states, and is safe exactly when a state's answer depends only on its arguments — referential transparency, or for optimisation problems, optimal substructure. Both are needed. Mergesort has the second property and not the first: its subproblems are distinct halves, a memo would never hit, and the Θ(n log n) bound is already optimal. A recursion over mutable global state has the first and not the second: caching it returns stale answers. Overlap is also the property that distinguishes the two paradigms' recursion shapes. In divide and conquer a subproblem is much smaller — half the size — so the tree is shallow (log n levels) and has polynomially many nodes even without a memo. In a typical dynamic program a subproblem is only slightly smaller — n − 1, or one fewer item, or one shorter prefix — so the tree has polynomial depth and exponentially many nodes, nearly all of them repeats. That depth is the cost memoization does not remove: the memoized rod-cutting routine is still n + 1 frames deep — CUT(n) down to CUT(0) — before the first value returns, and Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §06–§06 explains why a Θ(n)-deep recursion is a real limit. Tabulation (§06) removes it; the memo does not.

04 Rod cutting: from 2ⁿ to Θ(n²), with reconstruction

Rod cutting asks for the most revenue obtainable from a rod of length n when a piece of length i sells for pᵢ and the rod may be cut, at no cost, into any number of integer-length pieces. Its recurrence — the best value of a rod of length j is the best over the length i of the first piece of pᵢ plus the best value of the remaining j − i — is the one §03 unrolled into a tree of 2ⁿ calls, and the point of this section is to run that same recurrence over its n + 1 distinct subproblems in increasing order, so that every answer it needs has already been written down. The result is a table r[0..n] filled in Θ(n²) time, a companion table s[1..n] that records which first piece won each maximum, and from s an actual optimal cutting recovered in O(n) further steps. The price table used throughout this page is the one in the formula block; every figure reads it from the same shared array.

Why the recurrence is correct: cut and paste, one line

An optimal cutting of a rod of length j has some first piece, of length i for some 1 ≤ i ≤ j (a rod of positive length is sold either whole, i = j, or with a first cut somewhere). The rest of that cutting is a cutting of a rod of length j − i, and it must be an optimal one: if a better cutting of j − i existed, replacing the rest with it — the paste — would give a cutting of j worth more than the optimum, a contradiction. So the optimum is pᵢ + r[j − i] for the right i, and since the recurrence takes the maximum over every i it cannot miss the right one. This is exactly §02's argument, and it goes through because the two parts of a cutting — the first piece and the remainder — share nothing: the remainder can be cut however it likes without affecting the first piece. Overlap, the other property, is what §03 measured: the recursion asks for r[j − i] along many paths, and the table answers each question once.

Hand-worked: n = 4, then n = 7

The table is built by evaluating the recurrence's candidates for each j in turn. With r[0] = 0: r[1] = p₁ + r[0] = 1, s[1] = 1. r[2] = max(p₁ + r[1], p₂ + r[0]) = max(2, 5) = 5, s[2] = 2. r[3] = max(1 + 5, 5 + 1, 8 + 0) = 8, s[3] = 3. For j = 4 the four candidates are i = 1: 1 + r[3] = 9, i = 2: 5 + r[2] = 10, i = 3: 8 + r[1] = 9, i = 4: 9 + r[0] = 9, so r[4] = 10 and s[4] = 2; reconstruction outputs s[4] = 2, then s[2] = 2, and the optimal cutting is 2 + 2 for revenue 10. That is the unique optimum: the brute-force enumeration of all 2³ = 8 cuttings of a length-4 rod gives 4 → 9, 1+3 → 9, 2+2 → 10, 3+1 → 9, 1+1+2 → 7, 1+2+1 → 7, 2+1+1 → 7, 1+1+1+1 → 4, and only one cutting attains 10. Ten candidates were examined in total (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 4·5/2), against eight cuttings enumerated by brute force — at this size the two costs are close, which is why the comparison is worth making at n = 10, where they are 55 and 512.

Continuing to j = 7 needs r[5] and r[6] first: r[5] = max(1 + 10, 5 + 8, 8 + 5, 9 + 1, 10 + 0) = 13 with s[5] = 2; r[6] = max(1 + 13, 5 + 10, 8 + 8, 9 + 5, 10 + 1, 17 + 0) = 17 with s[6] = 6 (the whole rod, since p₆ = 17 beats every split). Then the seven candidates for r[7] are i = 1: 1 + 17 = 18, i = 2: 5 + 13 = 18, i = 3: 8 + 10 = 18, i = 4: 9 + 8 = 17, i = 5: 10 + 5 = 15, i = 6: 17 + 1 = 18, i = 7: 17 + 0 = 17. The maximum 18 is attained four times, at i = 1, 2, 3 and 6. The routine keeps the first candidate that strictly beats the running best, so s[7] = 1 and reconstruction produces 1 then s[6] = 6: the cutting 1 + 6. The brute force over all 2⁶ = 64 cuttings confirms 18 and finds five cuttings that attain it — 1+6, 6+1, 2+2+3, 2+3+2, 3+2+2 (the last three are one multiset in three orders) — so the table's answer is an optimum, not the optimum; a different tie-break in s would recover a different one, and all are equally good. Ties are the norm in DP, not the exception, and the reconstruction rule should be stated when the solution and not just its value matters.

The "one cut, two halves" formulation, and why it costs the same

The recurrence above is the first-piece form: one choice (the first piece) and one subproblem (the remainder). The more symmetric form treats a cutting as either no cut at all, worth pⱼ, or one cut at position i that leaves two rods, both solved optimally: r[j] = max(pⱼ, max over i = 1..j−1 of r[i] + r[j − i]). It is equally correct — cut and paste on either half — and, at first sight, no more expensive: for each j there are j candidates (the whole rod plus j − 1 cut positions), so the total is again n(n + 1)/2 = 55 at n = 10. The split is symmetric, though, so i and j − i name the same cutting and only i ≤ ⌊j/2⌋ need be examined, which the routine measures at 35 examinations for n = 10 — the sum of 1 + ⌊j/2⌋ over j = 1..10. The saving is a constant factor; both are Θ(n²), and the first-piece form is preferred because its reconstruction is a loop rather than a recursion and because it is the shape that generalises to knapsack (§11) and coin change (§10), where "the first item" is the natural choice. What the split form does show is that the number of subproblems per choice — one or two — does not change the state space: it is still r[0..n], and the running time is still states times choices.

What to take from rod cutting. The table is the recursion of §03 with the repeated questions removed: the same recurrence, the same n + 1 states, evaluated in an order (j increasing) that guarantees every reference r[j − i] is already answered. Time is states × choices, Θ(n²), and the exponent comes from the choice loop, not the state space. Recording the argmax in s[] alongside the max in r[] costs nothing extra and turns the value into a solution; without s the solution can still be recovered by re-testing the candidates at each step, at the same O(n) per step, but the argmax table is the habit to form. §05 draws the structure this section walked as a graph, and §06 measures what the same table costs when the recursion is kept and a memo added.

05 The subproblem graph and the evaluation order

The subproblem graph of a dynamic program is the directed graph with one vertex per distinct subproblem and an edge x → y whenever the recurrence for x consults the answer to y. It is the call tree of §03 with every repeated node merged into one — the tree "collapsed" onto the set of distinct questions — and it answers three things at once: how much the program costs (the sum over vertices of one plus the out-degree, i.e. Θ(V + E) when each edge is O(1) work), in what order the table may be filled (any order that puts every vertex after all the vertices it points to — a topological order of the reversed graph), and which subproblems a memoized recursion will actually touch (those reachable from the goal). Drawing the graph is the fastest way to check a recurrence: if it has a cycle the recurrence is not well-founded and no order exists; if it has far fewer vertices than the call tree has nodes, a table pays.

How the graph is read

For rod cutting the vertices are 0, 1, …, n and vertex j has an edge to every j − i for i = 1..j: j edges out of vertex j, n(n + 1)/2 in all — the same count as the candidate examinations of §04, which is no coincidence, since each candidate is one consultation of a smaller entry. For Fibonacci vertex j ≥ 2 has exactly two out-edges, to j − 1 and j − 2, and vertices 0 and 1 (the base cases) have none; the routine that builds the graph from the recurrence measures 0 edges at n = 0 and at n = 1, 2 at n = 2, 4 at n = 3 — that is 2(n − 1) for n ≥ 1, and the memoized call count 2n − 1 quoted in §03 is one more than the edge count, because the first call to the goal is not made along any edge. For the longest common subsequence (§14) the vertices are the prefix pairs (i, j), 0 ≤ i ≤ m, 0 ≤ j ≤ n; a vertex with x[i] = y[j] has one out-edge, to (i − 1, j − 1), and one with x[i] ≠ y[j] has two, to (i − 1, j) and (i, j − 1); the border vertices with i = 0 or j = 0 are base cases with none. So |E| lies between mn and 2mn and the bound Θ(mn) holds either way. In every case the running time of the bottom-up program is the size of the graph, provided the work done per edge is constant — which it is for max, min, sum and count recurrences, and is not for recurrences whose combine step is itself a loop, where the per-edge cost multiplies in.

Tabulation is a reverse topological sort

A bottom-up program fills the table in an order in which every entry is written before any entry that reads it. In the language of the graph: y is processed before x for every edge x → y. That is a topological order of the graph with its edges reversed (equivalently, the reverse of a topological order of G), and it exists precisely when G is acyclic — Graphs, §20 proves both directions and gives Kahn's algorithm, which is what the figure runs: it repeatedly removes a vertex with no remaining out-edges (a subproblem all of whose dependencies are already done). For rod cutting and Fibonacci that yields 0, 1, 2, …, n, the loop of §04; for a two-index state it yields row-major, column-major and anti-diagonal orders alike, and the choice among them is what §16 and §26 exploit when only one row needs to be kept. For a DAG-shaped problem the topological order is not merely an order but the algorithm (§23). The acyclicity requirement is worth stating in the other direction, too: a recurrence in which a state can reach itself — "the shortest path to v is the min over predecessors u of the shortest path to u plus w(u, v)" on a graph with cycles — has a cyclic subproblem graph and no evaluation order, which is why Bellman-Ford adds an edge-count index to the state (§23) and why such a recurrence, memoized, recurses forever.

Memoization is depth-first search from the goal

The memoized recursion does not compute an order in advance; it discovers one. A call on x descends into each consulted y in turn, returns when all of them have returned, and stores the answer — which is exactly a depth-first traversal of G starting at the goal vertex, with the memo playing the part of the visited set (Graphs, §15). The answers are fixed in DFS post-order, and post-order is by construction an order in which every vertex follows everything it points to, so the memo never reads an entry that is not yet filled — the correctness of top-down DP is the correctness of DFS finishing order, one theorem serving two pages. The count is the same too: the DFS follows every out-edge of every vertex it enters, once, so the number of calls is one plus the number of edges from reached vertices; on rod cutting at n = 10 that is 1 + 55 = 56, of which 11 are first visits (computations) and 45 are arrivals at an already-visited vertex (memo hits). And it only enters what the goal reaches. In rod cutting, and in Fibonacci for n ≥ 2, that is everything — vertex n reaches every smaller vertex (at n = 1 the Fibonacci goal is itself a base case and vertex 0 is never asked for, which the figure shows) — so memoization and tabulation compute the same set. In the LCS instance above the goal (4, 5) reaches 15 of the 30 vertices, because a match edge jumps diagonally and leaves the cells it skips over unvisited; the memo computes half the table and the bottom-up program computes all of it. That gap, harmless here, is the entire case for memoization in §06.

The graph is the cost model. When a recurrence is written, count its vertices (the distinct states) and its edges (the consultations per state, summed); the product "states × choices" of §01 is this sum in disguise, and the sum is the honest version when the number of choices varies by state — j choices at rod-cutting vertex j, giving n(n + 1)/2 rather than n². Then decide which order to walk it in: the topological order if every vertex is needed and a loop is wanted (§06's case for tabulation), the DFS from the goal if many vertices are not (§06's case for memoization). Either way the graph must be acyclic, and if it is not, the state is missing an index.

06 Memoization versus tabulation, measured

Memoization keeps the recursive formulation and adds a table that each call checks before computing and writes after; tabulation discards the recursion and fills the same table with a loop in an order chosen in advance. Both evaluate every subproblem they touch exactly once, both are correct for the same reason (§05: DFS post-order and reverse topological order are both valid orders), and on a problem where every subproblem is needed both cost Θ(|V| + |E|). The differences are real but second-order to that shared bound: the top-down version computes only the subproblems the goal reaches, which on a sparse state space can be a tiny fraction of the table; the bottom-up version pays no call overhead, uses no stack, and — because the order is explicit — can discard entries once nothing later will read them. Each is the right choice for some problems, and the honest comparison is a set of measurements rather than a slogan.

Same asymptotics — and the constant that separates them

When the goal reaches every state, the two evaluators do the same multiset of work: one combine per edge, one write per vertex. The measurements above show it — 55 table updates against 56 memoized calls on rod cutting at n = 10, and the difference of one is the initial call. What differs is the cost of a unit of that work. A memoized step is a function call (a stack frame pushed and popped), a lookup in the memo (a hash or a map probe, or an array index if the state is an integer), and the branch; a tabulated step is an array index and an arithmetic operation inside a loop the compiler or engine can keep in registers. The ratio is implementation-dependent and not something this page will put a number on, but it is the reason a bottom-up program is the usual production form when both are available: it is the same algorithm with the cheapest possible inner loop. It is not a reason to prefer tabulation on asymptotic grounds — there are none — and a claim that "tabulation is O(n²) and memoization is worse" is a confusion between the constant and the exponent.

What only memoization gives: reachability

The bottom-up loop must decide in advance which states to fill, and the safe decision is all of them — the whole rectangle for a two-index state, all of 0..n for a one-index state. The top-down evaluation fills only what the goal reaches, and there are recurrences for which that set is exponentially or polynomially smaller than the rectangle. The recurrence in the formula block is the cleanest example: it consults ⌊n/2⌋, ⌊n/3⌋ and ⌊n/4⌋, so the states reachable from n are the numbers of the form ⌊n / (2ᵃ·3ᵇ)⌋ (nested floors compose, and 4 = 2²), of which there are at most (log₂n + 1)(log₃n + 1) = O(log² n) — the script that traced this page counted 108 distinct states at n = 10⁶ and 242 at n = 10⁹, against the 10⁶ + 1 cells a table over 0..n would fill (which the bottom-up routine does, and it agrees on the value 2 566 393) and the 10⁹ + 1 it would need. The same effect, less dramatic, appeared in §05's LCS instance, where the diagonal match edges let the goal reach 15 of 30 cells, and it appears in interval and subset problems whenever a constraint prunes the reachable region. In every such case memoization is not a convenience but the algorithm, and the recursion depth it incurs — here about log₂ n frames, so 30 at n = 10⁹ — is harmless. There are hybrids: a bottom-up program preceded by a reachability pass that marks the needed states, or a top-down program with an explicit stack (Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §10) to remove the depth cost; they are worth knowing but rarely needed.

What only tabulation gives: no stack, and a known order

The memoized rod-cutting routine, asked for CUT(n), calls CUT(n − 1) before anything else, which calls CUT(n − 2), and so on: it is n + 1 frames deep before the first value returns, and the memoized Fibonacci is n deep. A recursion whose depth is linear in the input runs into the limits described in Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §06 — a fixed call-stack budget that a table of a few thousand entries can exhaust under a runtime with a depth counter (Python's default limit is 1 000), or a few hundred thousand against a raw 8 MiB stack — and that a tabulated loop never touches. The other gift is space. A bottom-up program knows its order, so it knows when an entry is dead: Fibonacci's loop needs only the last two values; the knapsack table needs only the previous row (§12); LCS needs two rows for the value and, with Hirschberg's split, still recovers the alignment (§16); §26 gives the general rule. A memoized program cannot easily discard, because it does not know which entries a future call will ask for — it keeps the whole reachable set alive until the top-level call returns. Tabulation also composes with the machine: a loop over a contiguous array is predictable to the cache and to a vectorising compiler, and a GPU or SIMD alignment kernel is a tabulation too — but over anti-diagonals, not rows, because the row-major inner loop reads the cell to its left (§15, §26); the point is that an explicit order can be chosen for the machine, not that this particular loop is the one it runs.

The rule of thumb, with its justification. Write the recursion first — it is the specification — and memoize it to get a correct program; that program is Θ(|V| + |E|) on the reached subgraph and is the final answer when the reached subgraph is small. Convert to a loop when every state is needed anyway, when n is large enough that a Θ(n)-deep recursion is a liability, or when memory must be rolled; the conversion is mechanical (the order is a topological sort of §05's graph) and changes nothing about correctness. Neither form is "more dynamic programming" than the other.

07 Designing the state: the checklist

The state of a dynamic program is the tuple of parameters that identifies a subproblem, and it is correctly chosen when the subproblem's answer is a function of those parameters alone — nothing about how the subproblem was reached may affect what it is worth. Every other decision follows from that one: the recurrence enumerates the choices available in a state and names the states they lead to; the base cases are the states with no choices; the order is a topological order of the resulting graph (§05); the answer is read from a specific state or a maximum over several; the cost is the number of states times the choices per state. Almost every wrong dynamic program is wrong at the first step — the state forgets something the recurrence needs, or remembers something it does not — and this section is the checklist for getting it right, with the two failure modes shown on problems where the obvious state is the wrong one.

Too small, first example: "the best of the prefix" is not enough

Take the longest increasing subsequence (§18). The natural first state is "L[i] = the length of the longest increasing subsequence within the first i elements", and it does not work: to decide whether element i + 1 extends the best subsequence one needs to know what that subsequence ends with, and L[i] does not say. Two prefixes with the same L[i] can deserve different answers at i + 1, so L is not a function of the state. The fix is to make the state remember what the future depends on — "L[i] = the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending exactly at i" — after which the recurrence is a pure function (1 + max L[j] over j < i with a[j] < a[i]) and the answer is max over i of L[i], checklist item 5. Kadane's algorithm (§08) is the same repair on maximum subarray: "best sum ending here" rather than "best sum so far", which the Recursion, Divide & Conquer page's §17 calls strengthening the subproblem. The general rule: if the recurrence for state x needs to know something about the solution to y beyond its value, that something must be part of y's state.

Too small, second example: one forgotten bit

The house-robber problem of §08 asks for the maximum sum of a subset of v[0..n−1] with no two chosen elements adjacent. A one-number state — "the best total over the prefix, extended by v[i] whenever the previous house was not taken" — carries the running total but not whether house i − 1 was taken, and it commits greedily; on the instance 2, 7, 9, 3, 1, 8, 4 shared with the figure that rule returns 16 where the optimum is 19 (2 + 9 + 8). The recurrence best[i] = max(best[i − 1], best[i − 2] + v[i]) repairs it by looking back two, which is the same as carrying one bit: define take[i] (house i is taken) and skip[i] (it is not), with take[i] = skip[i − 1] + v[i] and skip[i] = max(take[i − 1], skip[i − 1]); the state is (i, bit), twice as many states, each a pure function. The bit becomes unavoidable in the ring version, where house 0 and house n − 1 are also neighbours: the linear recurrence, which does not know whether house 0 was taken when it reaches house n − 1, returns 4 on 2, 3, 2 and 10 on 5, 1, 1, 5, both illegal, where the optima are 3 and 6. The state must carry "was house 0 taken?" — or, equivalently, the problem is solved twice, once with house 0 forbidden and once with house n − 1 forbidden, and the better taken; the routine that does so agrees with brute force over every legal subset on each of those instances.

Too small, third example: the visited set

§02 showed that "the longest simple path ending at v" has no valid recurrence: two paths reaching v with the same length may have consumed different vertices, and the future — which vertices can still be used — depends on which. The state that repairs it is (v, S) with S the set of vertices used, and it is a correct dynamic program with n · 2ⁿ states (§24 uses exactly this state for the travelling salesman). Here the checklist's diagnosis is right and the cure is exponential, which is the third possible outcome: the state that makes the recurrence pure may be too large to be useful, and the honest conclusion is that the problem is hard, not that DP was misapplied.

Too large: remembering the order of things that only matter as a set

The opposite error is to include in the state something the future does not depend on. In the travelling-salesman recurrence the future depends on which cities have been visited and which one the tour is currently at — not on the order they were visited in — so the state (S, v) with 2ⁿ · n entries is right and a state carrying the visited sequence, with n!-many entries, is a correct program that is merely the brute force with extra bookkeeping. In 0/1 knapsack (§11) the future depends on the remaining capacity and the set of items still undecided — after deciding items 1..i in order, that set is always {i + 1, …, n}, so the state is (i, remaining capacity), Θ(nW) entries, and not (set of items chosen), 2ⁿ entries. The checklist's sanity line catches both: count the distinct questions the recursion can ask, and if the state space is bigger than that count, two states are the same question in different clothes. The test that decides what belongs in the state is always the same one: does the best completion from here depend on it? If yes, it stays; if no, it goes; and if the answer is "yes, but only through its effect on some smaller summary" — the total weight so far, not the items; the last element, not the whole subsequence — then the summary is the state.

The order and the answer's location, two smaller traps

  • Order. The recurrence only tells you which states are consulted; the loop nest must visit them first. For a one-index state that is increasing i; for intervals it is increasing length, not increasing i (§19); for a two-index state where (i, j) reads (i − 1, ·) and (i, j − 1) either nest works; for the 1-D knapsack it is decreasing capacity (§12) and getting it backwards changes the problem. §10's loop-order bug is the same trap in coin change: two loop orders, two different counting problems.
  • Answer. When the objective may end anywhere — a subsequence, a subarray, a path in a DAG to any sink — the answer is a maximum over states, not the last state; forgetting this returns the answer to a different question ("the best that ends at n").
  • Base cases. The empty state's value is 0 for sums and lengths, 1 for counts ("one way to make amount 0", "one way to climb zero stairs"), +∞ for minimisations where the empty state is unreachable, and −∞ or the first element for maximisations that forbid the empty solution (Kadane's non-empty subarray). The wrong base is the most common off-by-one in the whole subject.
  • Reconstruction. Decide before writing the loop whether the solution or only its value is wanted; the argmax table is free to record and awkward to retrofit, and after space rolling (§26) it is no longer free.
The one question. Everything in this section is a form of: what does the best completion from here depend on? Whatever it depends on is the state; whatever it does not is not. If the dependence is on a history that cannot be summarised in a few integers, the problem may be beyond polynomial DP (§02, §24); if it can, the recurrence is a pure function and the rest of the checklist is mechanical. The next two sections apply it to the one-dimensional states — where the checklist looks trivial and the base-case and loop-order traps are still live.
