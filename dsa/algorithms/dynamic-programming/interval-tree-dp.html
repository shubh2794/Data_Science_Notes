19 Matrix-chain multiplication and the Catalan count

Given a chain of matrices A₁A₂…Aₙ in which Aᵢ is pᵢ₋₁ × pᵢ, the matrix-chain problem asks for the full parenthesization that multiplies them in the fewest scalar multiplications — the product itself is the same whatever the order, because matrix multiplication is associative, but its cost is not. The state is an interval of the chain, the choice is where to make the outermost split, and the answer is a table indexed by (i, j) filled in order of increasing interval length. It is the first problem on this page whose state is a pair of endpoints rather than a prefix or a capacity, and the pattern it establishes — interval, split point, two subintervals, O(n³) — recurs in §20 and §21.

Why parenthesization matters

Multiplying a p × q matrix by a q × r matrix with the standard algorithm performs p·q·r scalar multiplications (each of the p·r output entries is an inner product of length q). Take three matrices with dimensions 10 × 100, 100 × 5 and 5 × 50. Multiplying the first two first costs 10·100·5 = 5 000 and yields a 10 × 5 matrix, which times the third costs 10·5·50 = 2 500 — 7 500 in all. Multiplying the last two first costs 100·5·50 = 25 000, yields a 100 × 50 matrix, and the final product costs 10·100·50 = 50 000 — 75 000, ten times more, for the same answer. The dimension 100 is the culprit: the good order kills it early (it appears in only one product), the bad order carries it into both. With six matrices the spread between orders is wider still, and the number of orders is no longer two.

How many parenthesizations there are

P(n) = number of full parenthesizations of a chain of n matricesP(1) = 1; P(n) = ∑ₖ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ P(k)·P(n − k) for n ≥ 2 (outermost split after Aₖ; the two sides are parenthesized independently) P(n) = C(n − 1), the (n − 1)-th CATALAN number: C(k) = (1/(k + 1))·(2k choose k) = (2k)! / ((k + 1)!·k!) C(0..10) = 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, 1430, 4862, 16796 so P(3) = 2, P(4) = 5, P(6) = 42, P(11) = 16 796 growth: C(k) ~ 4ᵏ / (k^{3/2}·√π) — hence P(n) = Ω(4ⁿ / n^{3/2}): exponential, and brute force over all orders is hopeless past n ≈ 20 (C(19) ≈ 1.8·10⁹ orders; C(14) = 2 674 440 at n = 15 is still a fraction of a second) (at k = 15 the ratio C(k) / (4ᵏ/k^{3/2}) is 0.5245 and creeping toward 1/√π ≈ 0.5642 — the script that checked this page computed it)

The recurrence for P(n) is the recurrence of the problem itself with "sum over splits" in place of "min over splits", which is why the same Catalan numbers count binary trees on n leaves, well-formed bracket strings of n − 1 pairs, and the search-tree shapes of §20. Their 4ⁿ growth says that enumerating orders is not an option; the dynamic program below examines every split point of every interval, which is polynomial, and the readout of figure 15 confirms on the six-matrix instance that it agrees with an honest enumeration of all 42 orders.

Optimal substructure, by cut and paste

Take an optimal parenthesization of Aᵢ…Aⱼ. Its outermost multiplication joins two pieces, Aᵢ…Aₖ and Aₖ₊₁…Aⱼ, for some k. The claim is that the parenthesization it uses inside Aᵢ…Aₖ must be an optimal one for that subchain: if a cheaper way of computing Aᵢ…Aₖ existed, cutting the sub-parenthesization out and pasting the cheaper one in would leave the outermost product's cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ unchanged (the dimensions of the pieces do not depend on how they were computed) and lower the total — contradicting optimality. The same holds for the right piece. So the optimum for (i, j) is, for its own split k, exactly m[i][k] + m[k+1][j] + pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ; and since the recurrence takes the minimum over every k, including that one, it cannot be larger than the optimum, and since every value it considers is the cost of some legal parenthesization, it cannot be smaller. That is the whole correctness proof, and its two halves — "the optimum is among the candidates" and "every candidate is achievable" — are the shape of every such argument on this page. Note what made the cut-and-paste legal: the two subchains share nothing (no matrix is in both) and the joining cost depends only on their dimensions, which is the independence that longest simple paths lacked in §02.

Counting the inner iterations exactly

For chain length l there are n − l + 1 intervals, and each tries l − 1 split points. Summing (n − l + 1)(l − 1) over l = 2 … n and substituting t = l − 1 gives ∑ₜ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ t(n − t) = n·∑t − ∑t² = n·(n−1)n/2 − (n−1)n(2n−1)/6 = (n³ − n)/6. For n = 6 the five lengths contribute 5·1 + 4·2 + 3·3 + 2·4 + 1·5 = 5 + 8 + 9 + 8 + 5 = 35 = (216 − 6)/6; the counter in the figure reports exactly that, and the checking script found the formula to hold for n = 2 … 8 (1, 4, 10, 20, 35, 56, 84). Compared with the n³ that "three nested loops" suggests, the constant is 1/6 — the same (n choose 3)-flavoured count that appears whenever an interval DP tries every split, and it is why a chain of a few hundred matrices is trivial.

What the pattern generalises to

The three ingredients — an interval as the state, a split point as the choice, and a combining cost that depends only on the interval's endpoints and the split — define interval DP, and §20–§21 are its next two instances. Optimal binary search trees replace "split between Aₖ and Aₖ₊₁" with "make kᵣ the root" and the joining cost pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ with the interval's probability mass; palindromic-subsequence problems shrink the interval from both ends and need no split at all, which is what drops them from O(n³) to O(n²). Two facts about this particular problem are worth carrying. The O(n³) bound is not the last word: an O(n log n) algorithm exists for matrix-chain ordering, based on a very different (polygon-triangulation) view of the same Catalan structure, but it is intricate and the cubic table is what everyone uses. And the table answers the question "in what order" for any associative operation with an order-dependent cost — chained joins in a query planner are the same problem with row-count estimates in place of dimensions, and a query optimiser's join-order table is, structurally, this m[i][j].

20 Optimal binary search trees

Given sorted keys k₁ < k₂ < … < kₙ, the probability pᵢ that a search asks for kᵢ, and the probability qᵢ that a search asks for a value strictly between kᵢ and kᵢ₊₁ (with q₀ below k₁ and qₙ above kₙ), an optimal binary search tree is a BST on those keys that minimises the expected number of nodes examined per search. A balanced tree minimises the worst search; when the access distribution is skewed, the tree that minimises the expected search puts hot keys near the root even at the cost of height, and finding it is an interval DP whose state is a run of consecutive keys and whose choice is which key to make the root. The search-tree invariant itself is Trees & BSTs, §14; here the shape is the unknown.

The cost being minimised

the model: n keys, n + 1 "dummy" leaves for the misses∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ pᵢ + ∑ᵢ₌₀ⁿ qᵢ = 1 a search ends at key kᵢ with probability pᵢ, or in the gap dᵢ (between kᵢ and kᵢ₊₁) with probability qᵢ in a tree T, a search that ends at key kᵢ examines depth(kᵢ) + 1 nodes; one that ends in gap dᵢ examines depth(dᵢ) + 1 (the dummy leaf counted as a node) E[search cost in T] = ∑ᵢ (depth(kᵢ) + 1)·pᵢ + ∑ᵢ (depth(dᵢ) + 1)·qᵢ = 1 + ∑ᵢ depth(kᵢ)·pᵢ + ∑ᵢ depth(dᵢ)·qᵢ the number of candidate shapes is the Catalan number C(n): 42 for n = 5, 16 796 for n = 10, ≈ 6.6·10⁹ for n = 20 — enumeration is out

Two features of the model matter for the recurrence. First, the dummies make every search terminate at some leaf of the extended tree, so the cost of a subtree is a clean function of the keys and gaps it contains. Second, when a subtree rooted at some key is hung one level deeper in a larger tree, every node in it gains one unit of depth, so the subtree's expected cost rises by exactly the total probability mass inside it — call that w. That single observation is what makes the cost decompose over the root's two subtrees, and it is the only algebra the derivation needs.

Optimal substructure

Let T be an optimal tree on kᵢ…kⱼ with root kᵣ. Its left subtree is a BST on kᵢ…kᵣ₋₁ with dummies dᵢ₋₁…dᵣ₋₁, and the claim is that it is an optimal one. The cut-and-paste step: the contribution of the left subtree to T's cost is its own expected cost plus w[i][r−1] (every node in it is one level deeper than in isolation); so a cheaper BST on the same keys, spliced in under the same root, would lower T's cost by exactly the difference — the root's contribution and the right subtree's are untouched, because the BST property is preserved (everything in the left subtree is still smaller than kᵣ) and no depth outside the left subtree changes. That contradiction proves the left subtree optimal; the right is symmetric. Unlike matrix chains, an empty subtree is a real possibility (when r = i or r = j), and the base case e[i][i−1] = qᵢ₋₁ is what makes the recurrence uniform over those edges — the empty "tree" is the one dummy leaf, examined once. The i ≤ n + 1 range of the row index exists solely so that e[n+1][n] = qₙ is defined.

Knuth's refinement: the roots are monotone

the hypothesis, and the O(n²) it buysCLAIM (root monotonicity) root[i][j−1] ≤ root[i][j] ≤ root[i+1][j] for all i < j (taking the smallest optimal root when there are ties) — adding a key on the right does not move the optimal root left, and dropping one on the left does not move it left either REFINED LOOP r ranges over [root[i][j−1], root[i+1][j]] instead of [i, j] WORK PER LENGTH ∑ᵢ (root[i+1][j] − root[i][j−1] + 1) telescopes to ≤ (n − l + 1) + root[n−l+2][n] − root[1][l−1] ≤ 2n ⇒ O(n) per length, O(n²) in all ON THE INSTANCE windows for l = 5: [2..4] (3 roots, not 5); l = 4: [2..2] and [2..5]; total 28 candidates examined against 35 for the full loop, same e[1][5] = 2.75 monotonicity checked on all 10 pairs (i, j), i < j: holds

The claim is a theorem for this problem — it follows from the fact that w[i][j] satisfies the quadrangle inequality, w[a][c] + w[b][d] ≤ w[a][d] + w[b][c] for a ≤ b ≤ c ≤ d (here with equality, since w is a sum over an interval), and a general result — Yao's quadrangle-inequality theorem — says that a recurrence of the form e[i][j] = w[i][j] + min(e[i][r−1] + e[r+1][j]) with such a w (monotone on inclusion and satisfying the inequality) has monotone roots — but it should be treated as an optimisation with a hypothesis: it is valid when the cost function satisfies the inequality, and applying the narrowed loop to an interval recurrence whose w does not (matrix chains, for one, where pᵢ₋₁pₖpⱼ depends on the split) can silently produce a suboptimal table. §27 places it beside the other named optimisations — monotone queue, convex hull trick, divide-and-conquer optimisation — each of which trades a hypothesis about the cost for a factor of n. The figure has a switch for the narrowed loop, counts the candidates it examines, and checks the monotonicity on the finished table, so that the hypothesis is verified on the instance rather than trusted.

What the problem is and is not

This is the static version: the distribution is known in advance and the tree is built once. Its dynamic cousins — splay trees, which adapt to the access sequence without knowing it, and the self-balancing trees of Balanced & Multiway Trees — solve a different problem, and the celebrated (still open) dynamic-optimality conjecture asks whether a splay tree is within a constant factor of the best sequence of trees. Two cheaper alternatives to the table are worth knowing when n is large. The greedy rule — put the most probable key at the root and recurse on both sides — is the obvious heuristic and has no guarantee: on the instance above it roots the tree at k₅, the hottest key, then at k₁, and builds a chain whose cost is 3.05 (or 3.10 with the other tie-break) against the optimal 2.75 — and instances exist where it is far worse. The weight-balanced rule — choose the root so that the probability mass on the two sides is as equal as possible — is the good heuristic: it runs in O(n log n) (or O(n) with care), and its expected cost is bounded by a small constant plus a constant multiple of the entropy of the access distribution, which is within a constant factor of the optimum because the optimum is itself at least a constant multiple of that entropy. Both are heuristics; the table is the algorithm, and the Knuth refinement makes it O(n²), which for a few thousand keys is instantaneous.

21 Interval DP: palindromes, and the "last operation" trick

Interval dynamic programming is the family of recurrences whose state is a contiguous range [i, j] of a sequence, whose base cases are the ranges of length 0 or 1, and whose evaluation order is increasing length, so that every subrange a state depends on is filled before it. Matrix chains (§19) and optimal search trees (§20) are its two classical members; this section treats the family as such — what the state looks like, when the transition needs a split point and when it does not, and the one modelling trick that turns an apparently non-decomposable problem (bursting balloons) into an interval recurrence: choose the last operation, not the first.

the family, in the templateSTATE f[i][j] = the answer for the subsequence xᵢ … xⱼ, i ≤ j (Θ(n²) states) TRANSITION one of two shapes: (a) ENDPOINT f[i][j] from f[i+1][j], f[i][j−1], f[i+1][j−1] — shrink from either end O(1) per state ⇒ O(n²) total (b) SPLIT f[i][j] = best over k in [i, j) of f[i][k] ⊕ f[k+1][j] ⊕ cost(i, k, j) — try every split O(n) per state ⇒ O(n³) total BASE f[i][i] (and f[i][i−1] when empty ranges are meaningful) ORDER for l = 1 … n: for i = 0 … n − l: j = i + l − 1 — every range on the right of a transition is shorter than [i, j] ANSWER f[0][n−1] (the whole sequence)

Longest palindromic subsequence: an endpoint recurrence

A subsequence keeps some of the characters in order and drops the rest; a palindrome reads the same backwards. The longest palindromic subsequence of s is the longest subsequence that is a palindrome, and the recurrence follows from one question about the two ends of a range: if s[i] = s[j], some longest palindromic subsequence of s[i..j] uses both ends — cut and paste: take a longest one, P; if P uses neither end, wrapping the pair s[i]…s[j] around it gives a longer palindrome, a contradiction; if P uses exactly one, say s[i], then P's last character is some s[j′] with j′ < j and s[j′] = s[i] = s[j], and swapping index j′ for j keeps a palindrome of the same length that uses both. Strip the two ends off that P and what remains is a palindromic subsequence of s[i+1..j−1], and it must be a longest one (else paste a longer one back in). If s[i] ≠ s[j], no palindrome uses both as its two endpoints, so a longest one lies inside s[i+1..j] or inside s[i..j−1].

Substring is a different problem

The longest palindromic substring requires contiguity, and the same word shows the difference: bbabcbcab has a palindromic subsequence of length 7 but its longest palindromic substrings (bab, bcb, cbc) have length 3. The substring problem has its own O(n²) interval recurrence — P[i][j] = (s[i] = s[j]) and P[i+1][j−1], a boolean table — but it is more naturally solved by expanding outward from each of the 2n − 1 centres (each character, and each gap between characters), which is also O(n²) worst case, O(1) extra space, and fast in practice; an O(n) algorithm (Manacher's) exists and belongs with string algorithms. The subsequence problem has no such centre structure, because dropped characters break contiguity, and its natural home is the interval table. Confusing the two is the standard error: "palindrome" names the property, "substring" versus "subsequence" names the problem.

Burst balloons: choose the last, not the first

Balloons with values v₀ … vₙ₋₁ stand in a row. Bursting balloon k earns vₗ · vₖ · vᵣ, where l and r are its current left and right neighbours (an absent neighbour counts as 1), and then its neighbours become adjacent. The question is the maximum total for bursting all of them, in some order. The tempting recurrence — "burst k first, then solve the left and right parts independently" — is wrong, and it is worth seeing exactly why. After k is gone, the balloons k − 1 and k + 1 are neighbours: the coin earned by bursting k − 1 later depends on what is still standing on the right side, and vice versa. The two "subproblems" share a boundary that changes as each is solved, so their optimal values do not add — the same failure of independence as longest simple paths in §02, in miniature. On [3, 1, 5, 8] a memoized version of the "first" recurrence (with each side scored as if its outer neighbour were 1) returns 66; the true optimum is 167.

the fix: pad with 1s, and let k be the LAST balloon burst in the open range (i, j)a = [1, v₀, …, vₙ₋₁, 1] (indices 0 … n + 1; the two 1s are never burst) B[i][j] = the maximum coins from bursting every balloon strictly between i and j, while a[i] and a[j] stay standing B[i][j] = 0 if j ≤ i + 1 (nothing between them) B[i][j] = max over k = i+1 … j−1 of [ B[i][k] + B[k][j] + a[i]·a[k]·a[j] ] — if k is burst LAST among (i, j), then at that moment its neighbours are exactly a[i] and a[j]; before it, everything in (i, k) is burst with a[i] and a[k] standing at its edges, and everything in (k, j) with a[k] and a[j] standing: the two sides never see each other. The trick is general. Whenever an operation changes the neighbourhood structure — merging adjacent piles, removing an element so its neighbours touch, inserting a separator, cutting a stick at a point — the sub-ranges after the first operation interact, but the sub-ranges before the last one do not, because the last operation's context (the two boundary elements) is fixed for the entire history of the range. Matrix chains are the same idea already in disguise: the outermost multiplication is the last one performed, and the two sides are computed independently before it. Optimal BSTs choose the root, which is the first comparison of every search — but there the subproblems are independent anyway, because a key's subtree does not change when the rest of the tree is rearranged. The question to ask is not "first or last" by rote but "which choice leaves subproblems whose instances do not depend on each other's solutions".

When it is O(n²) and when it is O(n³)

The cost is set by the transition's shape, not by the state's. Endpoint recurrences — palindromic subsequence, palindromic substring, "can this range be reduced to empty by removing matching ends", interval scheduling variants that only look at the ends — read O(1) neighbours per state and cost Θ(n²). Split recurrences — matrix chains, optimal BSTs, burst balloons, merging adjacent stones with a cost equal to their total weight, triangulating a polygon at minimum total edge length (the Catalan structure again) — try O(n) splits per state and cost Θ(n³); the exact count of the inner loop is a cubic polynomial with leading coefficient 1/6, as §19 derived. A split recurrence drops to O(n²) only under an extra hypothesis such as the root monotonicity of §20 (Knuth) or the related quadrangle-inequality conditions of §27, and the hypothesis must be checked per problem: it holds for optimal BSTs and for some merging costs, and fails for matrix chains. Space is Θ(n²) for the table, but an endpoint recurrence reads only the previous two lengths and rolls to Θ(n); a split recurrence reads every shorter length and does not.

22 DP on trees: the subtree is the state

Dynamic programming on a rooted tree uses the subtree hanging from each node as the subproblem: a node's answer is computed from its children's answers, in post-order, so that every subtree is solved exactly once and the root's answer is the answer for the whole tree. The structure that makes it work is that removing a node disconnects its children's subtrees from each other and from everything above — the subproblems are independent — and the reason the same problems are hard on general graphs is that removing a vertex from a graph does not. The maximum-weight independent set is the canonical example, because it is NP-hard on graphs and linear on trees, and the (take, skip) pair it needs is the pattern most tree DPs follow.

Maximum-weight independent set

An independent set is a set of vertices no two of which are adjacent; its weight is the sum of the vertex weights. On a tree rooted at r, consider any node u and an optimal independent set of the subtree Tᵤ. Either u is in it — then none of its children are, and what remains inside each grandchild's subtree must be an optimal independent set of that subtree (cut and paste: the grandchildren's subtrees are pairwise disconnected and not adjacent to u, so improving one improves the whole) — or u is not in it, and each child's subtree contributes an optimal independent set of its own. Writing that as two quantities per node avoids the grandchildren entirely.

state · recurrence · base · order · costSTATE take[u] = max weight of an independent set of Tᵤ that CONTAINS u; skip[u] = max weight of one that does NOT contain u RECURRENCE take[u] = w(u) + ∑ over children v of skip[v] (u is in; no child may be) skip[u] = ∑ over children v of max(take[v], skip[v]) (u is out; each child is free) BASE a leaf: take = w(u), skip = 0 ORDER post-order (children before parents) — a DFS from the root computes it in one pass ANSWER max(take[r], skip[r]); reconstruct top-down: at u, if u may be taken and take[u] ≥ skip[u] then take it and forbid its children, else skip it and recurse freely COST n states, each combining its own children: ∑ deg(u) = n − 1 child visits ⇒ Θ(n) time, Θ(n) space — the counter reports exactly n node visits the single-quantity form: I(u) = max( w(u) + ∑ over grandchildren g of I(g), ∑ over children v of I(v) ) with I(u) = max(take[u], skip[u]) — equivalent, but a node is then read by its parent AND its grandparent; the (take, skip) pair keeps every edge used once

Two details in the trace are the ones that go wrong in practice. First, the reconstruction's "forbidden" flag is not "skip[u] was larger": node 1 has take[1] = 15 > skip[1] = 14 and is skipped anyway, because its parent was taken — the decision at a node depends on the parent's decision, which is why the walk is top-down and carries one bit. Second, the tie rule: with take[u] ≥ skip[u] a node is taken on ties, which yields an optimal set; the strict version yields another when ties exist, and neither is "the" answer. The re-rooting pattern is the tree analogue of the "second pass" in many DPs: one post-order pass computes each node's answer for its own subtree, and one pre-order pass computes what the node sees above it from its parent's totals, so that every node has the answer as if it were the root, for the price of two traversals. It applies to any quantity that decomposes as (contribution from the subtree) + (contribution from the rest) and can be updated in O(1) when the root moves across one edge — sums of distances, counts of paths through a node, the farthest node from each node — and it is the standard way to answer "for every vertex" questions on trees without paying Θ(n²).

Why the tree is easy and the graph is not

Everything above rests on one fact: in a tree, the subproblem "the best set inside Tᵤ given whether u is used" is completely described by the pair (u, taken?) — 2n states — because no vertex outside Tᵤ is adjacent to any vertex inside it except through u. In a general graph, the vertices already decided constrain the undecided ones along arbitrary edges, and the smallest state that makes the recurrence correct is the set of vertices excluded so far — the same enlargement that §07 describes for problems where the natural state is too small, and that §24 pays in full with a 2ⁿ bitmask. That is not a weakness of the method; maximum independent set is NP-hard on general graphs, so no polynomial state space is expected. The tree DP is also the base case of the DP on bounded treewidth graphs: a tree decomposition organises a graph into overlapping bags of at most k + 1 vertices arranged as a tree, and the (take, skip) pair becomes a table of 2^{k+1} entries per bag, giving O(2ᵏ·n) — linear in n for every fixed k, and the tree is the case k = 1. A tree DP can also run on the DFS tree of a graph when the problem tolerates back edges (lowest common ancestors, bridges, articulation points), but that is graph theory rather than DP and lives with Graphs, §15.
