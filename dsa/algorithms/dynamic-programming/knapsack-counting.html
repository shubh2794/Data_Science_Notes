08 One-dimensional DP: Fibonacci, stairs, house robber, maximum subarray

A one-dimensional dynamic program has a state that is a single index — a prefix length — and a recurrence that reads a bounded number of earlier entries, so its table is a strip of n + 1 cells filled left to right in Θ(n) time, and usually in Θ(1) space once the strip is rolled. The four problems here share that shape and differ in exactly the places §07's checklist says they can: the base case (Fibonacci and the staircase have the same recurrence and different bases), the combine (a sum for counts, a max for values), the extra quantity the state must carry (Kadane's "best ending here"), and where the answer is read (the last cell, or the maximum over all cells). They are also the problems on which a wrong base or a wrong answer-location is most often written, which is why each is checked against brute force in the figure.

Fibonacci and the staircase: one recurrence, two bases

The Fibonacci recurrence is the smallest dynamic program there is, and §03 already used it to show the exponential-versus-linear gap: the naive recursion makes 2·F(n + 1) − 1 calls, the memo 2n − 1, the loop n − 1 cell updates. The correctness argument is trivial because the recurrence is the definition. The staircase problem is where the argument has content: the number of ways to reach step i is the number of ways to reach step i − 1 and then take a 1-step, plus the number of ways to reach step i − 2 and then take a 2-step, and these two families are disjoint (they differ in the last move) and exhaustive (the last move is one or the other) — so the count is a sum with no double counting. That disjoint-and-exhaustive check is the counting analogue of cut-and-paste and is what makes a counting recurrence right; it fails, and the count is inflated, whenever two cases can describe the same object, which is exactly what goes wrong in §10 when the coin-change loops are nested the wrong way round. The base is the other place to be careful. W[0] = 1 is not a convention: there is one way to climb zero stairs (the empty sequence of moves), and setting W[0] = 0 would make W[2] = 1 instead of 2, losing the single 2-step. With the correct base the table is Fibonacci shifted by one, W[n] = F[n + 1], and the enumeration in the figure — every sequence of 1s and 2s summing to n, generated explicitly — returns the same 8 at n = 5 and 89 at n = 10. Generalisations keep the shape: steps of 1, 2 or 3 give a three-term recurrence (the "tribonacci" numbers), steps from an arbitrary set S give W[i] = ∑ over s ∈ S of W[i − s], which is coin change's composition count (§10), and a forbidden step is a state with W = 0 that the recurrence routes around.

House robber: a max in place of a sum

Replacing the sum by a max and adding a value per element gives the house-robber recurrence B[i] = max(B[i − 1], B[i − 2] + v[i]). The cut-and-paste step is the standard one: an optimal selection from houses 0..i either omits house i, in which case it is an optimal selection from 0..i − 1 (otherwise swap in a better one), or includes it, in which case it cannot include i − 1 and its remainder is an optimal selection from 0..i − 2. §07 showed why the look-back of two is necessary — the one-number state that does not know whether i − 1 was taken returns 16 on the instance above against the optimum 19 — and why the ring variant needs a further bit. Reconstruction follows the checklist: walk back from i = n − 1; if B[i] = B[i − 1] house i was skipped, otherwise it was taken and the walk jumps to i − 2. On the instance this yields houses 5, 2, 0 in that order, values 8 + 9 + 2 = 19. The brute force in the figure enumerates all 2⁷ = 128 subsets, rejects the 94 with an adjacent pair, and takes the best of the 34 that remain — 34 being F(9), since the number of adjacency-free subsets of a path of n houses satisfies the staircase recurrence itself. The problem is also the simplest instance of DP on a tree (§22): a path is a tree, and (take, skip) per node is the state that generalises.

Maximum subarray: Kadane's algorithm, and where the answer lives

The maximum-subarray problem has a Θ(n log n) divide-and-conquer solution and a Θ(n) scan; the Recursion, Divide & Conquer page's §16–§17 builds both and explains the scan as a strengthened induction. Read as a dynamic program it is the clearest one-dimensional case of §07's first failure mode. The state "best subarray within a[0..i]" has no recurrence, because the best subarray within a[0..i + 1] may start anywhere. The state "best subarray ending at i" does: such a subarray is either just a[i] or a subarray ending at i − 1 extended by a[i], and the best of the latter is the best subarray ending at i − 1 plus a[i] — disjoint cases, cut and paste on the prefix. The recurrence is then E[i] = max(a[i], E[i − 1] + a[i]), with the familiar reading "if the running sum has gone negative, drop it". Two details are the whole difficulty. The base is E[0] = a[0], not 0: the subarray must be non-empty, so on the all-negative input −3, −1, −2 the answer is −1 and a base of 0 would return the wrong 0. And the answer is max over i of E[i], not E[n − 1]: on the shared instance E[n − 1] = 5 and the optimum 6 was attained at i = 6. The figure carries a second strip, the running maximum, so the moment the answer is fixed is visible; the brute force sums every one of the n(n + 1)/2 = 45 subarrays and agrees. Reconstruction of the subarray itself needs its endpoints: the right end is the argmax of E, and the left end is found by walking back while E[i − 1] > 0, or by recording, at each i, whether the run was restarted.

A fourth: minimum-cost stairs, and the counting cousin

The same strip handles costs: with a fee cost[i] to step off stair i, starting from stair 0 or 1 and finishing past the last stair, the cheapest way to leave stair i is D[i] = cost[i] + min(D[i − 1], D[i − 2]) with D[0] = cost[0], D[1] = cost[1], and the answer is min(D[n − 1], D[n − 2]) — again a max-over-states-style answer, because the final move may come from either of the last two stairs. On cost = 10, 15, 20 the table is 10, 15, 30 and the answer is 15 (start on stair 1, pay 15, step off); on 1, 100, 1, 1, 1, 100, 1, 1, 100, 1 it is 1, 100, 2, 3, 3, 103, 4, 5, 104, 6 and the answer is 6, which the exhaustive walk over every path of 1- and 2-steps from both starts confirms. The counting cousin is decode-ways — the number of ways to read a digit string as letters 1 → A … 26 → Z: D[i] = D[i − 1]·[s[i] ≠ 0] + D[i − 2]·[10 ≤ s[i − 1..i] ≤ 26], with D[0] = 1; on "226" it counts 3 (2·2·6, 22·6, 2·26), on "11106" it counts 2, and on "100" it counts 0, all matching an explicit enumeration. Each is the staircase with guards on which steps are allowed, and each is wrong the moment a guard is forgotten, which is why they are checked and not merely stated.

Aside: the closed form and the matrix power

Fibonacci alone among these has structure beyond the strip. Because the recurrence is linear, one step is a matrix: (F[i + 1], F[i]) = M · (F[i], F[i − 1]) with M = [[1, 1], [1, 0]], so F[n] is the top-right entry of Mⁿ, and repeated squaring computes Mⁿ in O(log n) matrix products — the routine measures 6 products for n = 10 and 16 for n = 1000, against 999 additions for the loop. The same trick works for any fixed-order linear recurrence (the k-step staircase, counting walks in a graph) and is how large-n counts are computed modulo a prime. There is also the closed form F[n] = round(φⁿ / √5) with φ = (1 + √5)/2; it is exact in exact arithmetic, but in double precision the rounding is right only while φⁿ / √5 is represented to within a half — the script that traced this page found it agrees with the loop for every n ≤ 70 and first disagrees at n = 71, where F = 308 061 521 170 129 and the rounded power returns one more. The point of the aside is negative: the closed form is a curiosity, the matrix power is a genuine algorithm, and neither is available for house robber or Kadane, whose max is not linear. For them the strip is the algorithm.

What the four have in common, and what separates them. Each is a prefix state with a two-term look-back, filled in Θ(n) and rollable to Θ(1) space. They differ in the combine (sum for counts, max for values), the base (0, 1 for Fibonacci; 1, 1 for stairs; 0, 0 for the robber; a[0] for Kadane), the extra quantity the state carries (Kadane's "ending here"), and where the answer is read (the last cell, or the maximum over all cells). Get those four details right and the strip is correct; get one wrong and it is off by exactly the kind of case — the empty solution, the all-negative input, the answer that ends early — that brute force on a small instance exposes at once.

09 Binomial coefficients: Pascal's triangle as a table, and a row rolled right to left

The binomial coefficient C(n, k) — the number of k-element subsets of an n-element set — satisfies C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k), and tabulating that recurrence row by row is Pascal's triangle. It is the smallest two-parameter dynamic program on the page, and the first whose table can be rolled into a single row — provided the row is updated in the right direction, the same condition §12 meets again for knapsack.

the template instantiatedSTATE (i, j), 0 ≤ j ≤ min(i, k) — C[i][j] = the number of j-subsets of {1, …, i} RECURRENCE C[i][j] = C[i − 1][j − 1] + C[i − 1][j] (subsets that CONTAIN element i, plus those that do not) BASE C[i][0] = 1 (the empty subset); C[i][j] = 0 for j > i ORDER row by row, i = 1 … n; ANSWER C[n][k]; COST Θ(nk) additions, Θ(nk) space as a triangle the triangle, rows 0 – 61 1 1 1 2 1 1 3 3 1 1 4 6 4 1 1 5 10 10 5 1 1 6 15 20 15 6 1 ← row i sums to 2ⁱ: every subset is counted once, by its size one row of k + 1 entries, updated RIGHT TO LEFTrow = [1, 0, 0, …, 0] for i = 1 … n: for j = min(i, k) down to 1: row[j] = row[j] + row[j − 1] ← row[j − 1] is still the PREVIOUS row's value additions = ∑ᵢ min(i, k) = nk − k(k − 1)/2 measured: C(10, 4) = 210 in 34 additions; Θ(k) space

The correctness argument is the staircase's from §08, in two dimensions: a j-subset of {1, …, i} either contains i — and is then a (j − 1)-subset of {1, …, i − 1} with i added — or does not, and is then a j-subset of {1, …, i − 1}; the two families are disjoint and exhaustive, so the count is a sum with no double counting. The rolled row is where the care goes. Updating left to right, row[j] += row[j − 1] would read an entry that has already been advanced to row i, and the "subsets containing i" term would silently count subsets containing i twice — the 0/1-versus-unbounded distinction of knapsack (§12) and of coin change (§10), met here first. The same table counts monotone lattice paths — the number of right/down paths across an a × b grid is C(a + b, a), because a path is a choice of which a of its a + b steps go right — and the Catalan numbers of §19 are one subtraction away: Catalan(k) = C(2k, k) − C(2k, k + 1) = C(2k, k)/(k + 1).

Overflow, and why the multiplicative formula needs care

The values grow like 2ⁿ/√n, so width is the real constraint. With k replaced by min(k, n − k), every entry the additive table touches is at most the answer, so the table overflows only when the answer itself does: in IEEE doubles every entry is exact up to n = 56, and C(57, 28) = 15 033 633 249 770 520 > 2⁵³ is the first to be rounded; in signed 64-bit integers the first central coefficient that does not fit is C(67, 33) = 14 226 520 737 620 288 370. The closed form C(n, k) = n! / (k!(n − k)!) overflows long before that — 21! already exceeds 64 bits. The multiplicative loop avoids factorials:

the multiplicative formula, and its trapr = 1; for i = 1 … k: r = r · (n − k + i) / i ← exact in integers: after step i, r = C(n − k + i, i) BUT the product r · (n − k + i) is i · C(n − k + i, i) — up to k times the final answer — before the division brings it back C(62, 28) = 349 615 716 557 887 465 fits in signed 64 bits; the last intermediate 28 · C(62, 28) = 9 789 240 063 620 849 020 does NOT dividing first (r · ((n − k + i)/i)) is not an integer step, and in floating point it rounds the fixescancel first: g = gcd(r, i); r = (r/g) · ((n − k + i)/(i/g)) ← i/g divides n − k + i, so both factors are integers or use a double-width intermediate; or the additive row, O(nk) but never wider than the answer; or, for C(n, k) mod p with p prime, precomputed factorials and modular inverses — O(1) per query after O(n) setup

Each version has a regime: the multiplicative loop is O(k) and right for one exact coefficient when the intermediate width is checked; the additive row is right when a whole row or column of coefficients is wanted, or when only addition is available (big-integer or modular arithmetic without cheap division); the factorial table is right for many queries modulo a prime.

10 Coin change: combinations, compositions, fewest coins — and the loop-order bug

Coin change is three different problems on the same input — denominations c₁ < c₂ < … < cₘ in unlimited supply and a target amount A — and the three have three different loop nests over the same one-dimensional table. (i) The number of combinations: multisets of coins summing to A, where 2 + 2 + 1 and 1 + 2 + 2 are the same. (ii) The number of compositions, or ordered ways: sequences of coins summing to A, where those two are different. (iii) The fewest coins that sum to A, the optimisation problem that Greedy Algorithms, §09 solves by largest-coin-first on canonical systems and that the table solves on every system. The two counting problems are distinguished by nothing but the nesting of two loops, and swapping the loops silently converts one into the other — the wrong answer is a plausible integer, not an error — which is why this section works the counts by hand and the figure re-derives them from explicit enumeration.

Why the outer loop decides what is counted

Both counting recurrences are sums over cases, and a sum counts correctly when the cases are disjoint and exhaustive (§08's staircase argument). In (ii) the cases are "the last coin is k": every non-empty sequence has one last coin, so each sequence lands in exactly one case, and P[a] = ∑ P[a − k] counts sequences. The same sum applied to multisets fails, because a multiset has no "last" coin — {2, 2, 1} is reached both as "{2, 2} plus a 1" and as "{2, 1} plus a 2" — and it is counted once per distinct coin it contains. Nest (i) removes the ambiguity by imposing a canonical order: after the outer loop has finished coin k, C[a] counts multisets drawn from {c₁, …, cₖ} only, and the update C[a] += C[a − k] splits those into "no coin k" (the old C[a], computed with coins c₁..cₖ₋₁) and "at least one coin k" (remove one and what remains is a multiset from {c₁..cₖ} summing to a − k, which is C[a − k] after its update in the same pass). Those two cases are disjoint and exhaustive for multisets, so the count is right. Written out in two dimensions the recurrence is C[k][a] = C[k − 1][a] + C[k][a − cₖ], and nest (i) is that table with the k dimension rolled away — legitimately, because when C[a] is updated the entry C[a] still holds the previous row's value and C[a − k] already holds the current row's, which is exactly what the recurrence reads. This is the unbounded knapsack's rolling array (§12), and the loop-direction argument there is this one.

The bug, and how it presents

Write the combinations program with the loops the other way — amount outer, coins inner — and it computes P, not C: on {1, 2, 5} at A = 5 it prints 9 where 4 was wanted, at A = 11 it prints 218 where 11 was wanted. Nothing fails: the base case, the update rule and the number of updates are identical, and the output is a positive integer that grows with A like the correct one. The reverse mistake — coins outer when ordered sequences were wanted — under-counts by the same mechanism. The figure fills the strip under either nest with the update it is making named at every step, and the readout checks each count against a routine that enumerates the objects themselves: every multiset (as a non-increasing sequence of coins) and every sequence. On {1, 3, 4} at 6 the two counts are 4 and 9; on {2, 5} at 7 they are 1 (the multiset {5, 2}) and 2 (5 + 2 and 2 + 5); on {2, 5} at 3 both are 0. A useful habit: state in the docstring which of the two objects is being counted, and test on an instance where the counts differ — any instance with at least two coins that both fit will do.

Fewest coins: where greedy is wrong and why the order is free

The optimisation version replaces the sum by a min and the base 1 by 0, with ∞ marking unreachable amounts (needed whenever there is no 1-coin: {2, 5} cannot make 3, and the table says so rather than inventing an answer). Its correctness is cut and paste: an optimal set of coins for a contains some coin k, and the rest is an optimal set for a − k, otherwise swap in a better one; the min over k then cannot miss it. Unlike the counts, the min is indifferent to the loop order: examining the candidate "{2, 2, 1}" through the door marked 2 and again through the door marked 1 does not change the minimum, so the coins-outer and amount-outer nests return the same N. The table also settles the greedy question. On {1, 3, 4} at 6 largest-coin-first takes 4, then 1, then 1 — three coins — while the table finds N[6] = 2 via from[6] = 3, from[3] = 3: two 3s. That system is non-canonical in the sense of the greedy page, and on it the greedy answer is wrong for 24 of the amounts from 1 to 100 (that page's figure counts them); the DP is right on all of them, at Θ(m·A) cost instead of greedy's Θ(m). On {1, 2, 5} at 11 both give 3 (5 + 5 + 1), and on {1, 5, 10, 25} they agree everywhere, because that system is canonical — a fact the DP can be used to check, by comparing against greedy for every amount up to cₘ₋₁ + cₘ, which is where the smallest counterexample must lie if there is one. The from[] table is the argmax of §07's checklist and gives the coins themselves in O(N[A]) steps.

Cost, and the word "pseudo-polynomial"

All three programs run in Θ(m·A) time (the nests visit the pairs (k, a) with cₖ ≤ a ≤ A, at most m·A of them) and Θ(A) space. That looks polynomial and is not, in the sense that matters for complexity theory: the input is m numbers and the amount A, which is written in log₂A bits, so a running time linear in A is exponential in the size of the input. The same is true of knapsack's Θ(nW) (§12), where the point is discussed at length; for coin change with the small amounts of practice it is a curiosity, and for the counting problems the answers themselves grow so fast (218 compositions of 11, and exponentially thereafter) that the cost of the table is not the concern. What is the concern is which object is being counted, and that is decided by one line of loop nesting.

Compositions: amount outer, so each sequence is counted by its last coin. Fewest coins: a min, indifferent to the nesting, and correct on every coin system where greedy is correct only on canonical ones. The cost is Θ(m·A) for all three, pseudo-polynomial in the strict sense, and irrelevant next to the question of which loop is outside. §11 makes the state two-dimensional — items and capacity — and §12 rolls it back to one, where the same loop-direction question returns with a different answer.

11 0/1 knapsack: the (item, capacity) table

Given n items, the i-th of weight wᵢ and value vᵢ (positive integers), and a capacity W, choose a subset of the items of total weight at most W whose total value is largest; each item is taken whole or not at all. The state is the pair (how many items have been considered, how much capacity remains), the recurrence is the two-way choice "skip item i, or take it and lose wᵢ of capacity", and the table has (n + 1)(W + 1) entries, each filled in O(1): Θ(nW) time, with a reconstruction that walks the table back from its last cell. The problem is the canonical instance of a recurrence that greedy cannot shortcut — Greedy Algorithms, §11 shows the density rule taking 160 where the optimum is 220 on a four-item instance this page reuses — and the canonical instance of a running time that is polynomial in the value of an input number rather than in its length (§12).

the template instantiatedSTATE (i, w) for 0 ≤ i ≤ n, 0 ≤ w ≤ W — the best value obtainable from items 1..i with capacity w; call it dp[i][w] RECURRENCE dp[i][w] = dp[i−1][w] if wᵢ > w (item i cannot fit: skip it) dp[i][w] = max( dp[i−1][w], dp[i−1][w − wᵢ] + vᵢ ) if wᵢ ≤ w (skip it, or take it) BASE dp[0][w] = 0 for every w (no items: nothing to take); dp[i][0] = 0 is then implied (no capacity: every item is skipped) ORDER row by row, i = 1..n; within a row any order of w (row i reads only row i − 1) ANSWER dp[n][W]; the chosen set by walking back from (n, W): at (i, w), if dp[i][w] ≠ dp[i−1][w] then item i was taken, move to (i−1, w − wᵢ); else to (i−1, w) COST (n + 1)(W + 1) states × O(1) each = Θ(nW) time; Θ(nW) space for the full table (needed for the walk-back); O(W) if only the value is wanted (§12)

Why the state needs two coordinates

The first attempt at a state — "the best value at capacity w", one coordinate — is the right state for the unbounded problem, where an item may be taken any number of times: removing one copy of item i from an optimal load of capacity w leaves an optimal load of capacity w − wᵢ, and the recurrence K(w) = max over i with wᵢ ≤ w of K(w − wᵢ) + vᵢ is licensed by cut and paste. In the 0/1 problem that subproblem is useless: knowing that K(w − wᵢ) is large does not tell whether the load that achieves it already contains item i, and if it does, adding i again is illegal. The state must carry enough information to prevent the double take, and the cheapest such information is which items have been considered: restrict the subproblem to items 1..i, so that item i is decided exactly once, at the transition from row i − 1 to row i. This is the principle of §07 in its purest form — widen the state only as far as the recurrence forces, and no further. Carrying the set of items already taken would also work and would cost 2ⁿ states; carrying a prefix index costs n + 1.

Correctness: cut and paste on the two cases

The greedy page's instance, and why scaling it is legitimate

The four items of Greedy Algorithms, §10–§11 — A (10, 60), B (20, 100), C (30, 120), D (25, 90) at W = 50 — fill a table of 4 × 51 = 204 cells and return 220 for {B, C}, agreeing with that page's brute force over 16 subsets and exposing the density greedy's 160 as a 27% shortfall. That scaling is a special case of a general remark: the table's width is W + 1, so the running time depends on the granularity of the weights, not on the number of items alone — the theme of §12.

Cost, and what dominates

Filling is Θ(nW) — n rows of W + 1 cells, each cell one comparison and at most one addition — and no cell can be skipped when the full table is wanted, because the walk-back may visit any of them. Reconstruction is Θ(n): one step per row, since every step moves up exactly one row whether or not the item is taken. Space is Θ(nW) for the table as drawn, and that is the cost reconstruction pays; §12 shows the value alone needs one row of W + 1 numbers, and §26 shows how to recover the chosen set anyway. The comparison with brute force is the point of the paradigm: 2ⁿ subsets against nW cells, so the DP wins whenever W is small compared with 2ⁿ/n, and loses — the pseudo-polynomial catch — when W is astronomically large and n small. 12 Knapsack variants: the rolling array and its loop direction, unbounded, bounded, and why Θ(nW) is pseudo-polynomial

Row i of the knapsack table reads only row i − 1, so the value of the optimum needs a single array f[0..W] that is overwritten once per item — provided the capacity loop runs downward, from W to wᵢ. Run it upward and the same three lines compute a different, equally well-defined quantity: the unbounded knapsack, in which every item may be taken any number of times. That is not a bug in the arithmetic but a change of problem, and the two loop directions are the cleanest example on this page of an evaluation order that decides what is computed rather than merely how fast. The section also covers the bounded variant (each item available kᵢ times) through binary splitting, subset sum and partition as knapsack with the value column deleted, and the sense in which Θ(nW) — genuinely the cost of the table — is exponential in the size of the input.

Both are Θ(nW) time and Θ(W) space. the invariant that the direction protectsduring the pass for item i, f[w] holds dp[i][w] for the w already updated in this pass and dp[i−1][w] for the rest. DOWNWARD: f[w − wᵢ] is read BEFORE it is updated in this pass, so it is dp[i−1][w − wᵢ] — a load from items 1..i−1 — and item i enters at most once. → dp[i][w] = max(dp[i−1][w], dp[i−1][w − wᵢ] + vᵢ) (the 0/1 recurrence) UPWARD: f[w − wᵢ] has ALREADY been updated in this pass, so it is dp[i][w − wᵢ] — a load that may contain item i — and item i can be stacked. → g[i][w] = max(g[i−1][w], g[i][w − wᵢ] + vᵢ) (the unbounded recurrence)

Anyone who has seen the same recurrence give two different answers and reached for the debugger has met this: the code is the recurrence, the loop is the evaluation order, and the evaluation order determines which table is being filled, because the in-place array conflates two rows that the 2-D table kept apart. On the worked instance the routine's walk-back returns {4, 4, 1}; the arrays above and the one-coordinate K agree at every w, as they must, and the figure prints the multiset. Two remarks. The unbounded problem has a natural DAG: vertices 0..W, an edge w − wᵢ → w of weight vᵢ for every item, and K(W) is the longest path into W — a longest path that does have optimal substructure, because the DAG has no cycles for a path to reuse (§02's failure needed a cycle). And the unbounded problem is where the greedy density rule comes closest to working: if the highest-density item's weight divides W the greedy load is optimal, but in general it is not — the same items with W = 7 would let density pick item 1 (weight 6) and then nothing, value 30, while K(7) = 32 takes item 2 and two of item 4 (3 + 2 + 2 = 7, 14 + 9 + 9 = 32) — and it is the upward array above that shows f[7] = 32.

The bounded variant by binary splitting

If item i is available kᵢ times, the naive reduction lists it kᵢ times and runs the 0/1 algorithm on ∑kᵢ items, Θ(W·∑kᵢ). Binary splitting replaces the kᵢ copies with bundles of 1, 2, 4, …, 2ᵗ⁻¹ copies and one remainder bundle of kᵢ − (2ᵗ − 1) copies, where t is the largest integer with 2ᵗ − 1 ≤ kᵢ. Every count from 0 to kᵢ is the sum of some sub-collection of those bundles — the powers of two represent 0..2ᵗ − 1 and the remainder lifts the range to kᵢ — and no sub-collection exceeds kᵢ, so the 0/1 problem on the bundles is exactly the bounded problem on the items. The number of bundles is ⌊log₂ kᵢ⌋ + 1, which equals ⌈log₂(kᵢ + 1)⌉: k = 1 → 1 bundle, k = 2 → 2 (1, 1), k = 3 → 2 (1, 2), k = 4 → 3 (1, 2, 1), k = 7 → 3, k = 8 → 4 (1, 2, 4, 1); the routine behind the figure was checked against both formulas for every k from 1 to 20. (The tidier-looking ⌈log₂ k⌉ is wrong at k = 1 and at every power of two.) Total cost Θ(W·∑ᵢ log kᵢ). On the worked items with multiplicities (1, 3, 2, 5) the split produces 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 8 bundles — 1×1; 2×1, 2×2; 3×1, 3×1; 4×1, 4×2, 4×2 — and the 0/1 routine on them at W = 10 fills 8 × 11 = 88 cells and returns 48 (1×1 and 4×2: item 1 once, item 4 twice), which brute force over all bounded multisets confirms. A monotone-queue formulation (§27) does the bounded problem in Θ(nW) with no logarithm at all; binary splitting is the version to reach for first because it reuses the 0/1 code unchanged.

Pseudo-polynomial: the right n

Θ(nW) is an honest count of the cells, and it is not a polynomial bound. The input to knapsack is n pairs of numbers and one capacity, written in binary: about n · log₂(max wᵢ, max vᵢ) + log₂ W bits. W itself occupies log₂ W bits, so a table with W + 1 columns has 2^(log₂ W) columns — exponential in the length of the number that determines it. An algorithm whose running time is polynomial in the numeric values in the input but not in their bit lengths is pseudo-polynomial, and Algorithm Analysis, §23 puts the numbers on it: n = 100 and W = 10⁹ is a 375-byte input and a 10¹¹-cell table. Two consequences frame the variants above. The DP is a fine algorithm whenever the weights are small integers or can be made so — the ÷5 scaling of §11 is that trick in miniature, and it is why the capacity loop's width, not the item count, decides whether knapsack is easy in practice. And the DP does not contradict the problem's NP-hardness: knapsack, subset sum and partition are NP-hard when the numbers are written in binary, and they are the standard examples of problems whose hardness disappears when the numbers are written in unary (the weakly NP-hard problems); a problem that stays hard in unary — bin packing, 3-partition — is strongly NP-hard and has no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P = NP.

The FPTAS sketch, as a hand-off

Because the table's width is set by the values if the DP is run "over value" instead of over weight — g[i][v] = the least weight achieving value exactly v from items 1..i, answer = the largest v with g[n][v] ≤ W, cost Θ(n·∑vᵢ) = O(n²·vₘₐₓ) — the values can be coarsened to make that width small. Fix ε > 0, let K = ε·vₘₐₓ/n, and replace each value by ⌊vᵢ/K⌋: the scaled values are at most n/ε, the value-DP runs in O(n³/ε), and the set it returns, valued at the original prices, is within a factor 1 − ε of the optimum, because rounding loses at most K per item and at most nK = ε·vₘₐₓ ≤ ε·OPT in total (OPT ≥ vₘₐₓ when every item fits alone). That is a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme — polynomial in n and in 1/ε — and the greedy page's 1/2-approximation (§11) is its crude ancestor. The proof of the guarantee and the taxonomy of approximation schemes belong to NP-Completeness & Approximation, §25; this page's contribution is the observation that a pseudo-polynomial DP plus rounding is the standard recipe for an FPTAS.

13 Weighted interval scheduling and p(j)

Given n intervals [sⱼ, fⱼ), each with a weight vⱼ > 0, choose a set of pairwise non-overlapping intervals of maximum total weight. Sort by finish time, define p(j) as the largest index i < j whose interval ends no later than interval j starts, and the recurrence is a two-way choice: OPT(j) = max(OPT(j − 1), vⱼ + OPT(p(j))). The state is a single index — a prefix of the finish-sorted list — and the whole algorithm is a sort, n binary searches and one linear pass: O(n log n). It is the weighted cousin of the activity-selection problem whose greedy solution Greedy Algorithms, §04 proves; that page's §06 shows the greedy rule fails as soon as weights differ, and this section is the algorithm that replaces it — the DP that the greedy scan is a collapse of.

the template instantiated (intervals renumbered so that f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ … ≤ fₙ; two intervals are compatible when fᵢ ≤ sⱼ or fⱼ ≤ sᵢ)p(j) the largest i < j with fᵢ ≤ sⱼ, or 0 if there is none — the last interval, in finish order, that can precede j in a compatible set STATE OPT(j) for 0 ≤ j ≤ n — the maximum weight of a compatible subset of intervals 1..j RECURRENCE OPT(j) = max( OPT(j − 1), vⱼ + OPT(p(j)) ) skip j, or take j and then only intervals 1..p(j) remain available BASE OPT(0) = 0 ORDER j = 1..n (OPT(j) reads OPT(j − 1) and OPT(p(j)), both smaller) ANSWER OPT(n); reconstruct from j = n: if vⱼ + OPT(p(j)) > OPT(j − 1) take j and jump to p(j), else step to j − 1 COST sort Θ(n log n) + n binary searches for p, each ≤ ⌈log₂ n⌉ probes + n cells × O(1) + reconstruction O(n) = O(n log n) time, Θ(n) space

Why finish order, and why p(j) makes the prefix state closed

The state is a prefix, and a prefix works only if "take j" leaves a subproblem that is again a prefix. In finish order it does. If interval j is taken, every other interval in the same compatible set must end at or before sⱼ (any interval with a larger index ends at or after fⱼ > sⱼ and so cannot precede j, and cannot follow j either, because we are looking at the prefix 1..j); the intervals ending at or before sⱼ are exactly 1..p(j), since finish times are sorted and p(j) is the last one with fᵢ ≤ sⱼ. So the residual instance is the prefix 1..p(j), and the state space is closed. Sorting by start time would break this: the intervals compatible with j would not form a prefix, and the state would have to carry more. This is the discipline of §07 again — the sort is not a preprocessing convenience, it is what makes a one-integer state sufficient.

correctness — cut and paste on the two caseslet S* be an optimal compatible subset of 1..j. (a) j ∉ S*: S* ⊆ 1..j−1, so value(S*) ≤ OPT(j − 1); conversely any subset of 1..j−1 is a subset of 1..j, so OPT(j) ≥ OPT(j − 1). (b) j ∈ S*: every other member ends ≤ sⱼ, so S* − {j} ⊆ 1..p(j) and is compatible; if a heavier compatible subset T of 1..p(j) existed, T ∪ {j} would be compatible (everything in T ends ≤ f_p(j) ≤ sⱼ) and heavier than S* — contradiction. So value(S* − {j}) = OPT(p(j)) and value(S*) = vⱼ + OPT(p(j)). OPT(j) is the larger of the two, and both are achievable. ∎

Computing p(j): n binary searches on the finish times

Because the finish times are sorted, "the largest i < j with fᵢ ≤ sⱼ" is a boundary search on a sorted array: find the first index whose finish time exceeds sⱼ among 1..j − 1, and subtract one. That is the upper-bound variant of binary search — Sorting & Searching, §28 gives the half-open loop and §29 the boundary variants that return an insertion point rather than a match — and it costs at most ⌈log₂ j⌉ probes for interval j, O(n log n) in all. A two-pointer sweep over the intervals sorted by start time computes all p(j) in O(n) after the two sorts, but the binary search is the version whose count the figure measures: on the eight-interval instance below the routine makes 16 probes in total, against the bound ∑ⱼ ⌈log₂ j⌉ = 0 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 17. A naive scan backwards from j − 1 would also be correct and O(n²) in the worst case; on this instance it happens to be cheap, and the lesson is only that p is a separate O(n log n) phase, not part of the Θ(n) table fill.

The two versions, and what the memo looks like

The recursion OPT(j) with a memo is the natural top-down form and is worth writing once to see the overlap: OPT(j) calls OPT(j − 1) and OPT(p(j)), and without a memo the call tree is the Fibonacci-shaped tree of §03 in disguise, exponential whenever p(j) is close to j. With the memo each OPT(j) is computed once and the tree collapses to n computations. The bottom-up version is a single loop and, since OPT(j) is a max of two earlier entries, is the form that keeps every entry for reconstruction; a rolling version does not exist here because p(j) may point arbitrarily far back, so the full Θ(n) array is the working set — small enough that no one minds. With all weights equal to 1 the recurrence is max(OPT(j − 1), 1 + OPT(p(j))), and the greedy-choice property of the unweighted problem (the exchange in the greedy page's §04) says that some optimum always contains the earliest-finishing available interval — so the comparison can be replaced by a rule when the scan runs from the front; the DP is the general case and the greedy the special case in which the comparison can be skipped.
