Computational Geometry

Computational geometry is the design and analysis of algorithms whose inputs are points, segments, polygons and boxes rather than numbers and graphs. Its algorithms are built from the paradigms of the earlier parts of this series — sorting, divide and conquer, balanced search trees, priority queues, randomisation — but it adds two things of its own. The first is a primitive: nearly every algorithm on this page asks one question over and over, "does this point lie to the left of, to the right of, or on that directed line?", and answers it with a two-by-two determinant rather than with an angle or a slope. The second is a discipline: geometric algorithms branch on the exact sign of a computed quantity, so an arithmetic error that would be harmless in a numerical program flips a branch and produces an answer that is not merely inaccurate but combinatorially impossible. This page builds the orientation test and the arithmetic it needs, derives segment intersection from it, develops the sweep-line paradigm with its event queue and balanced-tree status, works through four convex-hull algorithms and the lower bound that limits them all, treats closest pair as a packing problem, covers the polygon primitives, and ends with the kd-tree and range tree for orthogonal range searching — every cost measured by running the routine under a counter, every answer checked against brute force.

01 Why geometry needs its own methods

A geometric algorithm is a combinatorial algorithm whose every decision is the sign of a polynomial in the input coordinates. Graham scan decides whether to pop a stack by the sign of a 2×2 determinant; a sweep line decides the order of two segments by the sign of a difference of interpolated heights; a point-in-polygon test decides "inside" by the parity of a count of such signs. The combinatorial skeleton — a stack, a heap, a balanced tree, a recursion — is the familiar material of the earlier parts. What is new is that correctness now depends on those signs being right, and the naive way to compute them, in floating point, is not always right.

02 Exactness, degeneracy and floating-point robustness

A geometric algorithm is only as correct as the signs it computes, and floating-point arithmetic computes signs incorrectly exactly where it matters — near zero, on the nearly-degenerate inputs that real data is full of. The failure is not an inaccuracy of a few units in the last place; it is a combinatorial contradiction. A float orientation test can report that c is left of ab, a is left of bc, and yet b is right of ca — a configuration that no three points in the plane can have — and an algorithm that trusts those three answers builds a structure that cannot exist.

What goes wrong downstream

  • Inconsistent answers. The orientation predicate has algebraic identities — orient(a, b, c) = orient(b, c, a) = −orient(a, c, b) — that a float evaluation does not honour, because each call rounds differently. An algorithm whose correctness proof uses those identities (every hull algorithm does) is no longer covered by its proof.
  • Impossible outputs. Incremental hull algorithms fed inconsistent orientations have been observed to produce polygons with reflex vertices, to drop genuine hull vertices, and to fail to terminate. A sweep line that orders segments inconsistently can lose a segment from its status tree entirely.
  • Epsilon tests do not fix it. Replacing d > 0 by d > ε makes the predicate answer "collinear" for nearly-collinear triples, but "within ε" is not transitive: a ≈ b and b ≈ c do not give a ≈ c. The failures move; they do not disappear.

The two principled responses

Exact geometric computation makes every predicate return the true sign of the true polynomial on the given input coordinates. The algorithm is then correct by its textbook proof, on all inputs, and the only question is cost — which §05 answers: with integer or fixed-point inputs the bit growth is bounded and small, and a floating-point filter makes the exact path rare. This is the dominant approach in production geometry libraries. Symbolic perturbation addresses the other problem, degeneracy: pretend every point is moved by an infinitesimal, index-dependent amount, so that no determinant is ever zero, and resolve each exact zero by a fixed tie-breaking rule derived from that perturbation. Combined with exact arithmetic it lets an implementation assume general position without the input satisfying it. The alternative — enumerating every degenerate case by hand — is what §07, §17 and §20 do for the small algorithms, and it is manageable there; for complex algorithms it is where bugs live.

The rule to carry forward. Keep the input coordinates exact (integers, or doubles taken as exact rationals), make every decision an exact predicate on those inputs, and never feed a computed coordinate — an intersection point, a rotated point — back into a predicate unless its representation is exact too. Constructions (new points) can be approximate for display; predicates (branches) cannot.

03 Points, vectors and the cross product

Everything on this page is built from differences of points and one scalar product of two such differences — the 2-D cross product — whose sign says which way you turn and whose magnitude is an area. A point is a location; a vector is a displacement. Subtracting two points gives a vector; adding a vector to a point gives a point; nothing else about coordinates is needed for the algorithms here.

The geometric meaning is the one from Vectors & Vector Spaces: in three dimensions u × v is a vector normal to both, with length equal to the area they span and direction given by the right-hand rule. Embed the plane as z = 0 and the 3-D cross product of two planar vectors points straight up or straight down; its single non-zero component is the 2-D cross product, and its sign is "up" (counter-clockwise) or "down" (clockwise). That is the entire content of the orientation test, and it is why "turn left" and "positive area" are the same statement.

Two remarks save trouble later. First, the cross product involves no division and no transcendental function: it is two multiplications and a subtraction on coordinate differences, so for integer inputs it is an integer, computable exactly. Second, the dot product answers a different question — "is the angle acute?" — and the algorithms on this page need it only for tie-breaking (which of two collinear points is farther, §12) and for projections (§15). The cross product carries the combinatorics.

04 The orientation test, and why it replaces angles and slopes

orient(a, b, c) is the sign of (b − a) × (c − a): positive if a → b → c turns left (counter-clockwise), negative if it turns right, zero if the three points are collinear. It is the single predicate from which segment intersection, every convex-hull algorithm, the sweep-line order, point-in-polygon and convexity tests are built. It is a degree-2 polynomial in the coordinates, it needs no division, and it has an exact integer answer on integer input.

Why not angles, and why not slopes

The obvious way to ask "does the path turn left at b?" is to compute the direction angles of a → b and b → c with atan2 and compare them. That costs two transcendental evaluations, each rounded; it needs a wrap-around case at ±π; and it answers the question only approximately, so the collinear case — the one that matters for degeneracies — is exactly the one it cannot decide. The next obvious way is slopes: compare (b.y − a.y)/(b.x − a.x) with (c.y − a.y)/(c.x − a.x). That divides, so it fails on vertical lines (division by zero) and rounds on everything else; and the direction of the inequality flips when a denominator is negative, which is a second source of bugs. Multiply the slope comparison out — clear the denominators — and you get the cross product. The orientation test is simply the slope comparison with the divisions removed, and removing them is what makes it total (no special cases for vertical lines), sign-correct (no flipped inequality), and exact.

What it gives you directly

  • Side of a line — the test itself. Two points are on the same side of line ab iff their orientations have the same sign.
  • Turn direction at a vertex of a polyline — used to decide convexity (§19) and to maintain every hull stack (§12–§13).
  • Angular order around a point — p comes before q counter-clockwise around a iff orient(a, p, q) > 0, valid whenever all points lie in a half-plane through a; this is Graham's polar sort without a single angle (§12).
  • Distance to a line, up to a constant — |orient(a, b, c)| / |b − a| is the distance from c to line ab; for comparing distances to the same line the denominator cancels, which is how quickhull picks its farthest point (§15).
  • Area — twice the signed area of a triangle, and by summing, of any polygon (§19).

05 Exact integer arithmetic and the bit width it needs

A predicate that is a polynomial of degree d in b-bit integer coordinates has an exact value of about d·b bits, so exactness is a matter of choosing an integer type that wide — and for the orientation test on 32-bit coordinates, 64 bits is not enough. The count is worth doing carefully once, because the off-by-a-few-bits error is the usual reason a "robust" implementation overflows.

Why doubles are exact below 2²⁵ and not above

A double represents every integer of magnitude up to 2⁵³ exactly, and an operation whose exact result is such an integer is performed without rounding. With coordinates below 2²⁵, each difference is below 2²⁶, each product below 2⁵², and the final difference below 2⁵³: every intermediate result is an exactly representable integer, so the float computation is the exact computation. That is why the orientation explorer above can use ordinary JavaScript numbers and be exact, and it is a practical design rule: snap input coordinates to a 25-bit integer grid and the orientation test is exact in plain doubles. Above that, some product exceeds 2⁵³, its low bits are rounded away, and §02's failures return.

Filtered predicates: exactness at float speed

Arbitrary-precision arithmetic is an order of magnitude or more slower than a float multiply, and paying it on every call would be wasteful, because on typical input the float answer is almost always right — only near-degenerate triples are at risk. A floating-point filter exploits this. Compute the determinant in floating point and, alongside it, a rigorous upper bound on its rounding error. For the orientation test with differences taken from one point, the bound is (3 + 16ε)·ε·(|t₁| + |t₂|), where t₁, t₂ are the two products and ε = 2⁻⁵³. If the computed value exceeds the bound in magnitude, its sign is certainly correct; return it. Otherwise fall back to an exact evaluation. The figure in §02 counts both paths: at one-ulp spacing every query is near-degenerate and falls back, while at 256 ulps only the truly collinear diagonal does. Adaptive schemes refine this into several stages of increasing precision, so that the cost paid is proportional to how close to degenerate the input actually is.

The design choice in practice. Two strategies dominate. Integer coordinates with a wide enough type: snap input to a grid, compute predicates in int64 for coordinates up to about 30 bits (2b + 4 ≤ 64 gives b ≤ 30) or in 128-bit integers beyond — simple and fast, the norm in GIS and VLSI. Doubles as exact rationals with filtered predicates: accept arbitrary double input, evaluate predicates by float-plus-filter with an exact fallback — the norm in general-purpose geometry libraries. Both give the same guarantee: every branch is taken on the true sign.

06 Bresenham's line algorithm: rasterising a segment in integers

To draw the segment from pixel p₀ to pixel p₁ on a raster, light one pixel per step along the longer axis — the one nearest the true line — and decide each step with the sign of an integer error term that is the line's implicit equation evaluated at a midpoint, updated by additions only. It is the same discipline as §05 applied to output rather than to predicates: the decision is a sign test on an integer polynomial, so it is exact, and incremental evaluation removes even the multiplications.

The midpoint test is exactly "which of the two candidate pixels is nearer the line, measured along the minor axis": the true line crosses the column x + 1 at height y*, and F(M) > 0 iff y* > y + ½. So every lit pixel is within half a pixel of the line along the minor axis, which is the definition of the best one-pixel-per-column approximation; the figure checks it in integers on every drag. The value of D is never an approximation: it is 2·F at a point with half-integer coordinates, an integer computed by adding integers. A floating-point version — step y by the slope dy/dx, or compute t = i/A and round the interpolated point — accumulates rounding in y and, worse, decides the half-way cases by rounding error: on 50 000 random segments with coordinates in [−100, 100), the integer loop matched a correctly rounded reference on every one, while the interpolated float version with the same tie rule lit a different pixel on 298, every one of them at an exact tie.

Where the idea goes

The incremental-implicit-function trick generalises directly. A circle has the implicit function x² + y² − r², whose change between neighbouring pixels is linear in x and y, so the midpoint circle algorithm draws an octant with additions only and mirrors it eight ways; conics follow the same pattern with second differences. Triangle rasterisation on a GPU evaluates three edge functions — the orientation tests of the triangle's edges against each pixel centre — and lights the pixels where all three are non-negative, with a fixed tie-breaking rule for pixels exactly on an edge so that triangles sharing an edge never both claim, or both miss, a pixel: the degeneracy discipline of §02, carried to hardware. And the same error term, stepping one grid cell at a time along a ray, is how a ray walks a uniform grid or voxel volume, visiting the cells it crosses in order — with the difference that it must visit every cell the segment touches, not one per column, so it steps whichever axis boundary is nearer rather than the major axis each time.

07 Segment intersection: two straddle tests and the collinear cases

Two closed segments ab and cd intersect iff each one's endpoints lie on opposite sides of the other's supporting line — or, in the degenerate case, an endpoint of one lies on the other. Four orientation tests decide it, with no division and no computed intersection point; the point itself is needed only if you want to report where, and it is a construction, not a predicate.

Why each clause is needed

One straddle is not enough: in "straddles one way only", c and d are on opposite sides of line ab, but segment cd crosses that line beyond the end of ab — the second straddle test, of a and b against line cd, is what rules it out. The strict inequalities make the general case exclude every configuration with a zero, and then each zero is examined on its own: a zero means an endpoint is on the other segment's line, and the bounding-box check decides whether it is on the segment. The "collinear, disjoint" preset has all four signs zero and fails every box check; "collinear, overlapping" has all four zero and passes two. "Parallel" has two non-zero signs of the same sign — no straddle — and no zero, so it is disjoint without reaching the special cases. "Shared endpoint" is caught by a zero with a passing box check. Remove any special case and one of those presets gives the wrong answer; remove the box check and "collinear, disjoint" reports a hit.

The test is also the smallest example of the robustness point of §02. Implemented by computing the intersection point of the two lines and then checking whether it lies within both segments, it divides by den (zero for parallel segments, tiny for nearly parallel ones), rounds the point, and then applies inequality tests to a rounded point — three opportunities to be wrong. The orientation form asks four exact questions about the inputs and never builds the point.

08 The sweep-line paradigm: event queue, status structure, invariant

A sweep line converts a static 2-D problem into a dynamic 1-D one: move a vertical line across the plane from left to right, maintain the 1-D cross-section of the input along it, and do work only at the finitely many x-coordinates where that cross-section changes combinatorially. It is the geometric counterpart of processing a sorted sequence, and it has exactly two data structures.

the two structuresEVENT QUEUE the x-coordinates where the cross-section changes, in increasing order. A priority queue (heap or balanced tree) when new events are discovered during the sweep; a sorted array when all events are known up front. STATUS the objects currently cut by the sweep line, in their order ALONG the line (bottom to top). A balanced search tree: insert, delete, find predecessor/successor, swap two neighbours, each O(log n). the sweep invariant — what makes the method correctwhen the sweep line is at x: (1) every answer lying strictly to the left of x has been produced (2) the status holds exactly the objects crossing the line, correctly ordered (3) the queue holds every future event that the current status can reveal The status changes only at events, so (2) needs repair only at events; (3) is maintained by checking only objects that have JUST become adjacent in the status. the cost templateT = (number of events) × (cost of processing one event) = E · O(log n)

The key design question is always the same: what must be true at the sweep line for the next answer to be detectable locally? For segment intersection the answer is adjacency: two segments that intersect are adjacent in the status immediately before the sweep reaches their intersection point (§09). For closest pair, it is a band of width δ behind the line. For the union of rectangles, it is a count per elementary interval. In each case the status is keyed so that the relevant neighbours are its tree neighbours, and the invariant says only neighbours need checking.

A comparator that changes as the line moves

The status for segments is a balanced search tree keyed by "height where the segment crosses the sweep line". That key is not a fixed number: it is a function of the sweep position. What makes a tree usable anyway is that the relative order of two segments changes only at their intersection point — which is itself an event. Between events, every comparison the tree has ever made remains true, so the tree's shape stays valid; at an intersection event the two segments involved are adjacent, and the algorithm swaps them explicitly. The tree from Balanced & Multiway Trees works unmodified; only its comparator is evaluated at the current x. Implementations that forget this subtlety — comparing at a stale x, or at exactly the intersection x where the two keys are equal — are the classic source of sweep-line bugs, and the robust fix is to compare at the event point with a tie-break by slope, as the figure's code does.

09 Does any pair intersect? O(n log n) by sweeping

Given n segments, deciding whether any two intersect takes O(n log n) time by a sweep that checks only pairs that become adjacent in the status — at most O(n) of them — against n(n−1)/2 for brute force. The algorithm stops at the first intersection it detects, which is why it never needs to process intersection events at all.

Why checking only neighbours is enough

Suppose some pair intersects, and let p be the leftmost intersection point (break ties by lowest y), where segments s and t cross. Consider the sweep line just to the left of p. No intersection lies strictly to the left of p, so on the interval between the later of the two left endpoints and p nothing changes the relative order of the segments near s and t; and as the sweep approaches p, the vertical gap between s and t shrinks to zero. Any segment lying between them in the status would have to pass through that shrinking gap and hence through p — excluded by general position (no three segments through a point) — or cross s or t to the left of p — excluded by leftmost-ness. So s and t are adjacent in the status just before p. They became adjacent at some event — the insertion of one of them, or the deletion of a segment between them — and that event tests exactly the newly adjacent pair. Hence the intersection is detected no later than the event that made them neighbours, which occurs at or before p. (The algorithm may report a different intersection first; it only promises to report that one exists.)

This argument is also the reason the method does not need to process intersection events: it stops at the first detection, and before the first intersection point the status order never changes except by insertions and deletions. The figure in §10 marks the event at which this decision procedure would already have stopped.

The lower bound matches. Deciding whether any two of n segments intersect needs Ω(n log n) in the algebraic decision-tree model, by reduction from element uniqueness (given numbers x₁, …, xₙ, build n short segments at those positions that overlap iff two numbers are equal). So the sweep is optimal for the decision problem, and the extra cost of §10 is paid purely for output.

Why the swap-and-test step is enough

The correctness argument is §09's applied repeatedly. Every intersecting pair is adjacent in the status just before its crossing point (general position), became adjacent at some earlier event, and was tested at that event — so its crossing was scheduled before the sweep reached it. The queue therefore contains every crossing that the sweep has not yet passed, and each one is reported when extracted. Step through the figure and watch a hollow circle appear the moment two segments become neighbours in the right-hand panel, and turn green when the sweep reaches it; the brute-force comparison in the readout confirms that the set of reported pairs is complete. Note also that a crossing can be scheduled, the pair separated by an insertion between them, and later re-discovered when they become adjacent again; the implementation keeps a set of scheduled pairs so each crossing enters the queue once.

What "output-sensitive" buys, and what it costs

An input of n segments can have anywhere from 0 to n(n−1)/2 intersections, so any algorithm that reports them all needs Ω(k) time just to write the answer, and the brute-force Θ(n²) is optimal in the worst case. Stating the bound as O((n + k) log n) is a claim about typical inputs: road networks, polygon boundaries, and circuit layouts have k close to linear in n, and there the sweep is O(n log n) against brute force's n². The price is a log factor per reported intersection, which is why the sweep loses to brute force when nearly all pairs cross, and why more intricate algorithms with the additive O(n log n + k) bound exist. Hull algorithms (§14) and range queries (§21–§22) are stated the same way; output sensitivity is the standard vocabulary of geometric complexity.

Degeneracies the general-position version ignores

  • Several segments through one point. They are contiguous in the status just before the point; process them in one event by reversing that contiguous block, and report all pairs.
  • Vertical segments, equal x-coordinates. Order events lexicographically by (x, y) — equivalent to rotating the plane by an infinitesimal angle — so a vertical segment's lower end comes first.
  • Endpoint on another segment, overlapping collinear segments. Handled by the special cases of §07 in the pair test and by consistent tie-breaking in the status comparator.
  • Computed crossing points. The event coordinates are rational constructions (§05); comparing them exactly needs degree-5 predicates or exact rationals. Snap-rounding to a grid is the standard practical alternative.

11 Convex hulls: definition, output, and why they matter

The convex hull of a finite point set S is the smallest convex set containing it — equivalently, the intersection of all half-planes containing S — and it is a convex polygon whose vertices are points of S. Computing it means listing those vertices in boundary order; with h the number of hull vertices, the output has size h, anywhere from 3 (or fewer, for degenerate input) to n.

Every algorithm in §12–§15 produces a list that passes this test, and the figure prints the result.

Hulls are the first computation in a surprising range of tasks: the diameter of a point set (the farthest pair is a pair of hull vertices, found in O(h) by rotating calipers); collision detection between convex shapes, where a hull is the standard bounding volume tighter than a box; linear separability of two classes (their hulls are disjoint), which is the geometric content of the hard-margin support-vector machine; and the lower envelope of lines, which is a hull in the dual plane and is how the "convex hull trick" speeds up certain dynamic programs. The hull is also the 2-D face of linear programming: the optimum of a linear objective over a polytope is attained at a vertex.

12 Graham scan and the polar-sort invariant

Sort the points by angle around a point known to be on the hull, then walk them in that order with a stack, popping the top whenever the last two stack entries and the next point fail to make a left turn. Why the invariant holds

Before pᵢ arrives, the stack is the hull of the points so far, and pᵢ is the most counter-clockwise point yet seen around a, so it is certainly a vertex of the new hull — it is extreme in the direction perpendicular to the ray a → pᵢ. The old hull vertices that remain are exactly those that still turn left on the way to pᵢ: if orient(sₖ₋₁, sₖ, pᵢ) ≤ 0, then sₖ lies inside or on triangle a sₖ₋₁ pᵢ and is not extreme any more, so popping it is right; and because the stack's own turns are all left, once one triple turns left every triple below it does too, so the while-loop can stop at the first left turn. Push pᵢ, and the invariant holds for i. At the end the stack is the hull of all points.

Two details make the proof go through. The sort is valid with orientation alone because every point lies in the closed upper half-plane of a (that is what choosing the lowest point buys): within a half-plane, "counter-clockwise from" is a total order. And the tie-break "nearer first" for points collinear with a puts the farther one later, so the scan pops the nearer one when the farther arrives (orientation 0 ⇒ pop). 16 The Ω(n log n) lower bound, by reduction from sorting

Any algorithm that outputs the convex hull in boundary order can be used to sort, with only linear extra work — so it cannot be faster than sorting. This is the geometric counterpart of the reductions in NP-Completeness: there, a reduction transfers hardness from a problem believed hard; here it transfers a proved lower bound.

the reduction: SORTING ≤ HULL in O(n) extra timeinput: distinct reals x₁, …, xₙ map: pᵢ = (xᵢ, xᵢ²) — every point lies on the parabola y = x² fact: the parabola is strictly convex, so EVERY pᵢ is a hull vertex (h = n) and the counter-clockwise boundary order is the order of increasing x recover: find the leftmost hull vertex (O(n)), read the cycle from there ⇒ x's in sorted order ⇒ T_hull(n) + O(n) ≥ T_sort(n) what the bound says, preciselySORTING needs Ω(n log n) comparisons (decision-tree counting, n! leaves), and Ω(n log n) operations in the algebraic decision-tree model (arithmetic and sign tests on the inputs, not floor or hashing). The map is algebraic (squaring), so the bound transfers to hulls in that same model: any algorithm that REPORTS THE HULL IN ORDER needs Ω(n log n) in the worst case. Stronger: merely identifying WHICH points are hull vertices (unordered) is also Ω(n log n), and the output-sensitive bound is Ω(n log h).

The argument, from Sorting & Searching, is that a comparison sort must distinguish n! input orders and a binary decision tree with that many leaves has depth at least log₂ n! = Θ(n log n). The parabola map feeds exactly those inputs to the hull algorithm, and the hull's boundary order encodes the permutation. So Graham scan and the monotone chain are optimal in the worst case, and no clever geometric idea can beat them on points in convex position — which is what the "convex position" distribution in the hull figure is.

  • This does not contradict Jarvis's O(nh). On the parabola input h = n, and O(n·n) is well above n log n. Output-sensitive bounds escape the worst-case lower bound only on inputs whose hull is small.
  • It is a statement about a model. For integer coordinates in a bounded range, sorting can be done faster than n log n with radix sort, and the monotone chain then runs in the time of that sort plus O(n). The lower bound constrains algorithms that only compare and compute polynomials.
  • The same trick proves other bounds. Element uniqueness reduces to closest pair (a zero distance), so closest pair is Ω(n log n) in the algebraic model too — which is why §18's linear-time randomized algorithm must, and does, use the floor function.

18 Closest pair as geometry: the packing argument and the randomized grid

Both fast closest-pair algorithms rest on one geometric fact: points that are pairwise at least δ apart cannot be crowded — a δ × δ square holds at most four of them. The divide-and-conquer algorithm uses it to bound the work in the strip; the randomized grid uses it to bound the work in a cell, and with hashing reaches expected linear time.

The divide-and-conquer algorithm, its presort-and-merge structure and its recurrence T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n log n) are derived in Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §20. The question here is the geometric one that algorithm leans on: why can only a constant number of strip points be close to a given one?

Why the grid does not contradict the lower bound

Closest pair is Ω(n log n) in the algebraic decision-tree model (§16), yet the grid runs in expected O(n). There is no contradiction: computing a cell index ⌊x/δ⌋ is the floor function, and looking a cell up in a hash table is indirect addressing, neither of which is an algebraic operation. With floor and hashing available — as on every real computer — the lower bound's model does not apply. That is the same escape route radix sort takes from the comparison lower bound for sorting, and it is the characteristic trade of randomized geometric algorithms: simpler code, better expected bounds, and a model that is closer to the machine. A third approach, a sweep line keeping the points of the last δ in a balanced tree ordered by y, gives a deterministic O(n log n) without recursion.

In d dimensions the packing constant is 2^O(d), since a cube of side δ split into cubes of side δ/√d needs (√d)^d pieces, and a grid neighbourhood has 3^d cells. Both algorithms remain linear or n log n in n, but the constant explodes with dimension — the first appearance of the curse of dimensionality that makes exact nearest-neighbour search impractical for high-dimensional embeddings (§23).

20 Point in polygon: ray casting against winding number

Cast a ray from the query point and count how many boundary edges it crosses: odd means inside (the even–odd rule). Or add up how many times the boundary winds around the point: non-zero means inside (the non-zero rule). For a simple polygon the two agree; for a self-intersecting one they differ exactly on regions of even, non-zero winding number. Both take Θ(n) time, and for a convex polygon a binary search reduces it to O(log n).

The degenerate cases, and why the half-open rule settles them

A ray through a vertex is the classic failure of a naive crossing count: the two edges meeting there both "cross" the ray, so the vertex is counted twice when the boundary passes through it and zero or two times when it merely touches it — and the parity comes out wrong in one of the two cases. The half-open rule counts an edge only if exactly one of its endpoints is at or below the ray's height: at a vertex where the boundary passes through, one of the two edges qualifies; at a vertex where the boundary touches and turns back, both or neither do. A horizontal edge never qualifies. The figure's polygons all have vertices at integer heights and the query point snaps to integers, so rays through vertices happen constantly as you drag, and the parity check in the readout stays green. Points exactly on the boundary are a separate case that neither rule defines; the readout reports them, and an application must decide by convention whether they are in or out.

Even–odd or non-zero?

For simple polygons — the usual case in GIS and CAD — the rules agree, and ray casting is the one usually written because it only counts. The winding number is the more informative quantity: it is signed, it is additive over boundaries (holes are just boundaries with the opposite orientation), and it generalises to curves and to the question "how many times does this path go around that point". Vector graphics formats expose both as fill rules, and the pentagram shows the difference: under even–odd its centre is a hole; under non-zero it is filled.

21 kd-trees: build, orthogonal range query, and the O(√n + k) bound

A kd-tree recursively splits the point set at the median, alternating between x and y; each node owns a rectangle of the plane, and a range query visits only nodes whose rectangle meets the query rectangle. Build is O(n log n), space is O(n), and an axis-parallel rectangle query reports its k points in O(√n + k) — sublinear, but not logarithmic.

kd-trees are nevertheless the everyday choice for low-dimensional point data: linear space, simple construction, good constants, and they support nearest-neighbour queries by the same descend-and-prune scheme (visit the side containing the query first, and prune any region farther than the best distance so far), which is expected O(log n) on well-distributed data in fixed low dimension. In d dimensions the range-query bound degrades to O(n^(1−1/d) + k), and nearest-neighbour pruning degrades toward a linear scan once d is more than about ten to twenty — which is why high-dimensional search uses approximate methods (§23).

22 Range trees: O(log² n + k), fractional cascading, and grids

A 2-D range tree is a balanced tree on x in which every node also stores all the points of its subtree sorted by y. A query decomposes its x-interval into O(log n) canonical subtrees and does one binary search on y in each, for O(log² n + k) time — exponentially better than the kd-tree's √n — at the price of O(n log n) space.

The thin-strip query that is the kd-tree's worst case is no harder for the range tree than any other, because it splits on x and y at different levels of the structure rather than alternately.

Grids: the structure to try first

A uniform grid of about √(n/2) × √(n/2) cells, each holding a list of its points, answers a range query by visiting the cells the rectangle overlaps. Its build is O(n), its space is O(n + cells), and on roughly uniform data a query costs O(cells overlapped + k) — which for a query of fixed shape is O(1 + k), better than either tree. Its failure mode is clustered data: if most points fall in a few cells, a query examines all of them regardless of how few it reports, and no bound in terms of n alone survives. 23 Where it is used

Wherever a program reasons about shape, position or proximity, one of the primitives on this page is on its inner loop. The list below is organised by field, with the section that supplies each piece.

  • Graphics and rendering. Triangle rasterisation tests each pixel centre against three edge functions — three orientation tests (§04). Clipping a polygon to the screen is repeated segment–line intersection (§07). Polygon fill uses the even–odd or non-zero rule (§20), and scan-line rendering is a sweep line (§08) whose status is the list of edges crossing the current row.
  • Geographic information systems. Map overlay — intersecting two layers of polygons, such as parcels and flood zones — is segment-intersection reporting (§10) on inputs where k is near-linear, which is exactly the regime the output-sensitive bound was designed for. "Which region contains this GPS fix" is point-in-polygon (§20); "what is within this bounding box" is a range query (§21–§22), answered by R-trees and kd-trees in spatial databases. Robustness (§02, §05) is a first-class concern: GIS systems snap coordinates to integer grids precisely so that predicates are exact.
  • Robotics and motion planning. Expanding obstacles by the shape of the robot is a Minkowski sum, computed for convex shapes by merging their hull edges in angular order (§11–§13); the free space is then a polygon with holes, and a shortest path for a point robot runs along the visibility graph of its vertices, built by an angular sweep (§08). Sampling-based planners call collision checks and nearest-neighbour queries (§21) millions of times.
  • Collision detection. Physics engines use a broad phase — a sweep over interval endpoints on one axis, or a grid (§22), to find pairs of objects whose bounding boxes overlap — followed by a narrow phase on convex hulls (§11) of the few surviving pairs, where the separating-axis test is a sequence of orientation and projection tests.
  • Nearest-neighbour search. kd-trees (§21) are the exact method in low dimension, and the closest-pair packing argument (§18) is the reason exact search degrades with dimension. At the scale of learned embeddings — hundreds of dimensions, millions of vectors — exact search gives way to approximate graph and quantisation indexes; Vector Databases covers that step, and k-Nearest Neighbors the classifier that motivates it.
  • Machine learning more broadly. Linear separability is disjointness of convex hulls (§11), and the support-vector machine's margin is the distance between them; decision trees partition feature space with axis-parallel splits exactly as a kd-tree does (§21).
