18 Closest pair as geometry: the packing argument and the randomized grid

Both fast closest-pair algorithms rest on one geometric fact: points that are pairwise at least δ apart cannot be crowded — a δ × δ square holds at most four of them. The divide-and-conquer algorithm uses it to bound the work in the strip; the randomized grid uses it to bound the work in a cell, and with hashing reaches expected linear time.

The divide-and-conquer algorithm, its presort-and-merge structure and its recurrence T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n log n) are derived in Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §20. The question here is the geometric one that algorithm leans on: why can only a constant number of strip points be close to a given one?

the packing lemmaif points in a closed δ × δ square are pairwise ≥ δ apart, there are at most 4 of them. proof 1 (pigeonhole) split the square into four (δ/2) × (δ/2) quarters; each has diameter δ/√2 < δ, so each contains ≤ 1 point; 4 corners of the square attain the bound. proof 2 (area) disks of radius δ/2 around the points are disjoint and lie inside the square grown by δ/2, of area (2δ)² − (4 − π)(δ/2)² ≈ 3.785·δ²; each disk has area π·δ²/4 ≈ 0.785·δ² ⇒ at most ⌊4.82⌋ = 4 points. applied to the strip (divide and conquer)a partner q of strip point p with |pq| < δ lies in the 2δ × δ box above p (walking up in y); the box is two δ × δ squares, one in L and one in R; each side's points are pairwise ≥ δ apart (that is what the recursive calls established) ⇒ ≤ 4 per side ⇒ ≤ 8 points including p ⇒ at most 7 forward partners — a CONSTANT, so the strip scan is O(strip size). applied to a grid (randomized incremental)grid of cell side δ = current closest distance; each cell holds ≤ 4 points (packing lemma) a point closer than δ to p lies in p's 3 × 3 block of cells ⇒ ≤ 36 distance evaluations per insertion insert points in RANDOM order; if the new point p makes δ smaller, rebuild the grid on the i points so far: O(i) P[point i changes δ] ≤ 2/i (backwards analysis: δ changes only if p is one of the ≤ 2 points of the closest pair among the first i, each equally likely to be last) E[total] = Σᵢ ( O(1) + O(i)·2/i ) = O(n) with O(1) expected-time hashing of cells

What the measurements say

The lemma gives worst-case bounds — 7 partners per strip point, 4 points per cell, 36 per neighbourhood — and the figure measures how close random input comes to them: the maxima it prints are well inside, as expected, since the worst cases require points arranged exactly at the corners of squares. The more interesting column is the grid's work per point. Divide and conquer's distance evaluations grow like n log n; the grid's insertions-plus-distances per point stay roughly flat as n grows, because the expected number of rebuilds is only about 2·ln n and each rebuild's cost is paid for by the 2/i probability that triggers it. Drag n from 20 to 600 and watch both columns.

Why the grid does not contradict the lower bound

Closest pair is Ω(n log n) in the algebraic decision-tree model (§16), yet the grid runs in expected O(n). There is no contradiction: computing a cell index ⌊x/δ⌋ is the floor function, and looking a cell up in a hash table is indirect addressing, neither of which is an algebraic operation. With floor and hashing available — as on every real computer — the lower bound's model does not apply. That is the same escape route radix sort takes from the comparison lower bound for sorting, and it is the characteristic trade of randomized geometric algorithms: simpler code, better expected bounds, and a model that is closer to the machine. A third approach, a sweep line keeping the points of the last δ in a balanced tree ordered by y, gives a deterministic O(n log n) without recursion.

In d dimensions the packing constant is 2^O(d), since a cube of side δ split into cubes of side δ/√d needs (√d)^d pieces, and a grid neighbourhood has 3^d cells. Both algorithms remain linear or n log n in n, but the constant explodes with dimension — the first appearance of the curse of dimensionality that makes exact nearest-neighbour search impractical for high-dimensional embeddings (§23).

19 Polygons: shoelace area, orientation and convexity

A polygon is a cyclic list of vertices v₀, …, vₙ₋₁; its signed area is half the sum of the cross products of consecutive vertices, positive when the list runs counter-clockwise. One linear pass gives the area, the orientation of the boundary, and — with the turn signs — whether the polygon is convex.

the shoelace formula2·A = Σᵢ (xᵢ·yᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ₊₁·yᵢ) = Σᵢ vᵢ × vᵢ₊₁ (indices mod n) A > 0 ⟺ the boundary runs counter-clockwise A < 0 ⟺ clockwise why: fan-triangulate from the origin o; vᵢ × vᵢ₊₁ = 2 × signed area of triangle (o, vᵢ, vᵢ₊₁); triangles outside the polygon are counted once positively and once negatively and cancel. exact: integer vertices ⇒ 2A is an integer (so A is a multiple of ½) — and Pick's theorem gives A = I + B/2 − 1 for lattice polygons (I interior, B boundary lattice points) convexity, correctlyLOCALLY CONVEX every turn orient(vᵢ, vᵢ₊₁, vᵢ₊₂) has the same sign (zeros allowed) CONVEX locally convex AND the boundary winds around once test: the sign of Δx changes at most twice going around (it is x-monotone in two chains) the trap a pentagram has five left turns and is not convex — it winds around TWICE

For a self-intersecting boundary the shoelace sum is still well-defined, but it is not the area of the region: each part of the plane is counted with multiplicity equal to its winding number (§20). For the pentagram, the central pentagon has winding number 2 and is counted twice, the five points once. The polygon figure below prints the shoelace value for each shape; compare the convex octagon, the comb, the comb traversed clockwise (the same area, negated), and the pentagram.

The convexity trap is worth a figure of its own, and the one below includes it. The naive test — "all turns have the same sign" — accepts the pentagram, because walking its boundary you always turn the same way; you simply turn through 720° instead of 360°. The fix is to check that the boundary goes around only once, which in exact arithmetic is simplest to state as "the direction of travel in x reverses at most twice" (a convex polygon has one leftmost and one rightmost vertex, so its boundary is two x-monotone chains). The zero-turn case, a vertex in the middle of an edge, is convex by this definition; whether to allow it is the same strict-versus-inclusive decision as §17.

20 Point in polygon: ray casting against winding number

Cast a ray from the query point and count how many boundary edges it crosses: odd means inside (the even–odd rule). Or add up how many times the boundary winds around the point: non-zero means inside (the non-zero rule). For a simple polygon the two agree; for a self-intersecting one they differ exactly on regions of even, non-zero winding number. Both take Θ(n) time, and for a convex polygon a binary search reduces it to O(log n).

ray casting (even–odd), with the half-open rulecrossings ← 0 for each edge (a, b): if (a.y ≤ q.y) ≠ (b.y ≤ q.y): — the edge straddles the horizontal line through q, counting its LOWER endpoint and not its upper one if q is left of the edge, taken upward: crossings += 1 — one orientation test, no division inside ⟺ crossings is odd winding number (non-zero)w ← 0 for each edge (a, b): if a.y ≤ q.y < b.y and orient(a, b, q) > 0: w += 1 — upward edge, q on its left if b.y ≤ q.y < a.y and orient(a, b, q) < 0: w −= 1 — downward edge, q on its right inside ⟺ w ≠ 0 and always crossings ≡ w (mod 2) convex polygon, counter-clockwise, in O(log n)the diagonals from v₀ cut it into a fan of triangles (wedges); binary-search the wedge containing q by orient(v₀, vₘ, q) — O(log n) tests; then one test against its outer edge

The degenerate cases, and why the half-open rule settles them

A ray through a vertex is the classic failure of a naive crossing count: the two edges meeting there both "cross" the ray, so the vertex is counted twice when the boundary passes through it and zero or two times when it merely touches it — and the parity comes out wrong in one of the two cases. The half-open rule counts an edge only if exactly one of its endpoints is at or below the ray's height: at a vertex where the boundary passes through, one of the two edges qualifies; at a vertex where the boundary touches and turns back, both or neither do. A horizontal edge never qualifies. The figure's polygons all have vertices at integer heights and the query point snaps to integers, so rays through vertices happen constantly as you drag, and the parity check in the readout stays green. Points exactly on the boundary are a separate case that neither rule defines; the readout reports them, and an application must decide by convention whether they are in or out.

Even–odd or non-zero?

For simple polygons — the usual case in GIS and CAD — the rules agree, and ray casting is the one usually written because it only counts. The winding number is the more informative quantity: it is signed, it is additive over boundaries (holes are just boundaries with the opposite orientation), and it generalises to curves and to the question "how many times does this path go around that point". Vector graphics formats expose both as fill rules, and the pentagram shows the difference: under even–odd its centre is a hole; under non-zero it is filled.

21 kd-trees: build, orthogonal range query, and the O(√n + k) bound

A kd-tree recursively splits the point set at the median, alternating between x and y; each node owns a rectangle of the plane, and a range query visits only nodes whose rectangle meets the query rectangle. Build is O(n log n), space is O(n), and an axis-parallel rectangle query reports its k points in O(√n + k) — sublinear, but not logarithmic.

buildBUILD(P, depth): if P empty: return null axis ← x if depth even, else y m ← median of P on axis; node stores m and its region left ← BUILD(points below m on axis), right ← BUILD(points above) time: O(n log n) with linear-time median selection or with P presorted on both axes; O(n log² n) if each level sorts, as the figure's code does space: O(n) height: ⌈log₂(n + 1)⌉ query with rectangle RQUERY(v): if region(v) ∩ R = ∅: return — pruned if v's point ∈ R: report it if region(v) ⊆ R: report the whole subtree — O(size) QUERY(left), QUERY(right) cost = (nodes whose region INTERSECTS the boundary of R) + O(k) why √n: how many regions can a vertical line cross?two levels down, a node's region is split into 4 grandchildren by one vertical and two horizontal lines; a vertical line meets at most 2 of the 4. So Q(n) = 2 + 2·Q(n/4) ⇒ Q(n) = O(√n). R's boundary is 4 axis-parallel segments ⇒ O(4·√n) = O(√n) boundary regions ⇒ query O(√n + k). In d dimensions: O(n^(1 − 1/d) + k) — useless as d grows.

The right-hand chart is the recurrence made visible. A zero-width vertical line reports nothing (k = 0), so every node it visits is pure search overhead, and the measured counts climb along the 2√n reference, with slope ½ on the log–log axes — not along a logarithm. That is the cost of a structure that splits on one axis at a time: a line parallel to the y-axis gets no help from the y-splits, which are half of all levels. In the left-hand panel the "thin vertical strip" query shows the same thing on the current points: the amber visited region is a column of cells cutting through every level of the tree.

kd-trees are nevertheless the everyday choice for low-dimensional point data: linear space, simple construction, good constants, and they support nearest-neighbour queries by the same descend-and-prune scheme (visit the side containing the query first, and prune any region farther than the best distance so far), which is expected O(log n) on well-distributed data in fixed low dimension. In d dimensions the range-query bound degrades to O(n^(1−1/d) + k), and nearest-neighbour pruning degrades toward a linear scan once d is more than about ten to twenty — which is why high-dimensional search uses approximate methods (§23).

22 Range trees: O(log² n + k), fractional cascading, and grids

A 2-D range tree is a balanced tree on x in which every node also stores all the points of its subtree sorted by y. A query decomposes its x-interval into O(log n) canonical subtrees and does one binary search on y in each, for O(log² n + k) time — exponentially better than the kd-tree's √n — at the price of O(n log n) space.

structure and costprimary tree balanced BST on x, points at the leaves associated at every node v: the points of v's subtree, sorted by y (an array, or a 1-D tree) space each point appears once per level ⇒ O(n log n) build O(n log n): presort by y, build bottom-up by MERGING the children's y-lists query [x₁,x₂]×[y₁,y₂]: find the O(log n) CANONICAL nodes whose subtrees exactly cover the leaves with x ∈ [x₁, x₂] (the right children hanging off the path to x₁, the left children hanging off the path to x₂) in each, binary-search y₁ in its y-list and scan up to y₂ time: O(log n) nodes × O(log n) search + O(k) = O(log² n + k) fractional cascading: removing one logthe O(log n) binary searches are all for the SAME key y₁, in lists that are subsets of one another (a child's y-list ⊆ its parent's). Store, with each entry of a parent's list, a pointer to the first entry ≥ it in each child's list. Search y₁ ONCE at the split node — O(log n) — then follow pointers down in O(1) per level. Query: O(log n + k). Space unchanged: O(n log n). d dimensions: O(logᵈ⁻¹ n + k) query with cascading, O(n logᵈ⁻¹ n) space.

The table under the kd-tree figure measures the range tree on the same points and query: the number of tree nodes it touches stays around a few multiples of log₂ n whatever the query's shape, the canonical nodes number O(log n), and the binary-search steps are the log² n term; the "stored y-entries" column is the n log n space. The thin-strip query that is the kd-tree's worst case is no harder for the range tree than any other, because it splits on x and y at different levels of the structure rather than alternately.

Grids: the structure to try first

A uniform grid of about √(n/2) × √(n/2) cells, each holding a list of its points, answers a range query by visiting the cells the rectangle overlaps. Its build is O(n), its space is O(n + cells), and on roughly uniform data a query costs O(cells overlapped + k) — which for a query of fixed shape is O(1 + k), better than either tree. Its failure mode is clustered data: if most points fall in a few cells, a query examines all of them regardless of how few it reports, and no bound in terms of n alone survives.
