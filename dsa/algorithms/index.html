Algorithm Design & Analysis

One question, asked seven ways: what is the cheapest procedure that provably solves this, and how do I know? Two halves that only work together — a small set of design paradigms (divide and conquer, greedy, dynamic programming) that generate candidate algorithms, and an analysis discipline that prices them and proves they are correct. This is the overview: the map, the thread that connects the parts, and a one-screen reference. The notes live on seven sub-pages, ordered so each part only assumes the earlier ones.

map Seven parts, one method

Analysis comes first because every later claim is stated in its language. Recursion and divide-and-conquer supply the recurrence machinery; sorting is where that machinery gets its first hard lower bound; greedy and dynamic programming are the two answers to "the obvious recursion is exponential"; the graph algorithms are those paradigms applied to the structure that models everything; and the last part draws the line where this whole approach stops working.

why The thread that runs through it

Nearly every algorithm in this series is the same move: find structure in the problem that lets you avoid looking at most of the search space, then prove that what you skipped could not have contained the answer. The paradigms differ only in what structure they exploit and how the proof goes.

a problem→structure to exploit→a paradigm→a recurrence or invariant→a cost bound→a correctness proof
  • Each paradigm exploits a different structure. Divide and conquer exploits subproblems that are independent. Dynamic programming exploits subproblems that overlap — the whole point is that they are not independent. Greedy exploits a much stronger property: that one locally optimal choice is provably part of some global optimum, so you never need to compare alternatives at all. Knowing which structure your problem has is the design decision; the code follows.
  • Correctness is a proof obligation, not a test suite. Each paradigm has its own proof shape: a loop invariant for iteration, induction on subproblem size for divide and conquer, an exchange argument for greedy, and optimal substructure for DP. An algorithm you cannot prove is an algorithm you cannot trust on the input you have not tried.
  • A lower bound is a different kind of result from an upper bound. "Mergesort runs in O(n log n)" is a statement about one algorithm; "no comparison sort can beat Ω(n log n)" is a statement about every possible algorithm in a model, proved by counting the leaves of a decision tree. Lower bounds are what tell you to stop optimising — or to change the model, which is exactly how counting sort gets to linear time.
  • The recurrence is where the cost actually comes from. Most design work ends with T(n) = a·T(n/b) + f(n), and the interesting question is which of the three terms dominates: the leaves, the root, or every level equally. That single comparison explains why mergesort is n log n, why binary search is log n, and why Karatsuba beats schoolbook multiplication.
  • Data structure and algorithm are chosen together. Dijkstra is O(V²) with a linear scan and O((V+E) log V) with a binary heap — the algorithm did not change, the priority queue did. Most "algorithm optimisations" in practice are structure swaps, which is why Data Structures is the sibling half of this series.
  • Intractability is information, not failure. Proving your problem NP-hard does not end the work — it redirects it. You then choose deliberately among an exact exponential algorithm on small inputs, an approximation with a proven ratio, a randomized method, or a heuristic with no guarantee, and you know which of those you picked.
