NP-Completeness & Approximation

NP-completeness is the theory of where efficient algorithms stop: a precise sense in which thousands of natural problems — satisfiability, the travelling salesman, vertex cover, subset sum, graph colouring — are equally hard, so that a polynomial-time algorithm for any one of them would give one for all, and the widely-held belief that none exists. This page defines P, NP, NP-hard and NP-complete from the notion of a checkable certificate, builds polynomial-time reductions as the tool that transfers hardness, works the classic chain from satisfiability through clique, vertex cover, Hamiltonian cycle, the travelling salesman, subset sum and colouring with every gadget drawn and every small instance brute-forced, and then turns to what one does next: approximation algorithms with proven ratios (vertex cover, metric TSP, set cover, MAX-3-SAT, the knapsack FPTAS), exact exponential search with pruning, parameterised algorithms, local search and the population heuristics that give up the guarantee, and the SAT and integer-programming solvers that make hard problems routine in practice — every count measured by running the real routine, every answer cross-checked against exhaustive search.

01 What NP-completeness is

NP-completeness is a proved equivalence among thousands of apparently unrelated computational problems: each of them can be converted into each of the others by a polynomial-time translation of instances, so that a polynomial-time algorithm for any single one of them would yield a polynomial-time algorithm for every one of them — and despite fifty years of effort on hundreds of these problems, no such algorithm has been found. The statement is not "these problems are hard"; nobody has proved that. The statement is "these problems are exactly as hard as one another, and the class of problems they are hardest in contains everything whose solutions can be checked quickly." That is a theorem, and it is the single most useful theorem an engineer can know about the limits of algorithms: it converts the private question "am I not clever enough to solve this?" into the public question "is this problem NP-complete?", which has an answer, a proof technique, and a well-stocked catalogue.

the four definitions the whole page turns onP the decision problems a deterministic algorithm SOLVES in time polynomial in the number of BITS of the input (§04) NP the decision problems whose YES-answers have a certificate that a deterministic algorithm CHECKS in polynomial time (§05) NP-hard a problem X such that EVERY L ∈ NP reduces to it in polynomial time: ∀ L ∈ NP, L ≤ₚ X — X need NOT be in NP, need not even be decidable (§07) NP-complete a problem that is NP-hard AND in NP (§07) P ⊆ NP is immediate (an algorithm that decides L ignores the certificate). Whether the inclusion is STRICT is open. the transfer rule — the one mechanism behind every hardness proof on this pageA ≤ₚ B ("A reduces to B") means: a polynomial-time computable map f with x ∈ A ⟺ f(x) ∈ B (§06) then B ∈ P ⟹ A ∈ P (solve A by translating and calling B's algorithm) — the USEFUL direction and A ∉ P ⟹ B ∉ P (contrapositive) — the HARDNESS direction so A NP-hard and A ≤ₚ B ⟹ B NP-hard (transitivity) — the CATALOGUE direction the consequence that gives the class its nameif ANY NP-complete problem X has a polynomial-time algorithm, then P = NP: take any L ∈ NP; by X's NP-hardness, L ≤ₚ X by some f computable in time O(nᶜ); X decides in O(mᵏ); the composite decides L in O(nᶜ) + O((nᶜ)ᵏ) = O(nᶜᵏ) — polynomial, because polynomials compose. conversely if P ≠ NP then NO NP-complete problem has one. So the thousands of problems stand or fall together.

Where it sits

Everything earlier in this series assumed the problem in front of it was solvable, and asked how fast. Algorithm Analysis built the language — O, Θ, Ω, recurrences, the growth hierarchy — and §04 and §26 of this page are that language applied to the gap between nᵏ and 2ⁿ; its §10 table of how large an input each growth rate admits is the practical content of the word "intractable". Dynamic Programming gave O(nW) for the 0-1 knapsack and O(n²·2ⁿ) time — over Θ(n·2ⁿ) subset states — for the travelling salesman; §02 explains why the first of those is not a polynomial-time algorithm and §14 and §26 explain exactly what it is instead. Greedy Algorithms showed where a local rule provably succeeds — matroids, the cut property, Huffman — and where it provably fails, and its §22 on set cover and §23 on load balancing are the first two approximation guarantees in the series — set cover is re-proved and measured here in §23, and both are placed in the taxonomy of §20. Shortest Paths, MST & Flow is the largest collection of problems in the series that are in P, and §16 uses it as the far side of the boundary: shortest path is easy and longest simple path is NP-hard on the same graph, minimum cut is easy and maximum cut is NP-hard, 2-colouring is easy and 3-colouring is NP-hard. Graph Representations & Traversal supplies the objects almost every hard problem on this page is stated over, and §02 uses its §08 cost table to make the point that "the size of the input" is a matter of representation. Sorting & Searching contains the series' only unconditional lower bound — Ω(n log n) comparisons, proved by a decision-tree counting argument — and §07 contrasts it sharply with what this page can and cannot prove: there is no unconditional lower bound for any NP-complete problem, and producing one would settle P vs NP.

The thesis: hardness is a relation, not a property

The organising idea is that the page never proves a problem hard in absolute terms. It proves that one problem is at least as hard as another, by exhibiting a translation, and then chains those statements. Cook and Levin supplied the root of the chain independently in 1971 and 1973: satisfiability is at least as hard as every problem in NP, proved not by a translation from one particular problem but by a translation from an arbitrary polynomial-time verifier, whose computation is encoded as a Boolean formula (§08). Karp supplied the next twenty branches in 1972 — his list of twenty-one NP-complete problems counts satisfiability itself — and the catalogue has grown to thousands since. Each branch is a gadget: a local construction that turns a variable into a triangle, a clause into a widget, a choice into a pair of parallel edges, in such a way that solutions correspond. So the skill this page teaches is not "recognising hard problems by their look"; it is (a) recognising the catalogue entry your problem resembles, (b) constructing the map and proving both directions of the ⟺, and (c) knowing what to do afterwards — which is the second half of the page, and in working life much the larger half.

The second organising idea is that "NP-complete" is not a verdict of impossibility. It is a statement about worst-case, exact, general-instance solving of the decision version. Every one of those four qualifiers is a door: worst case opens onto instances that are easy in practice and onto the phase transition that explains why (§30); exact opens onto approximation algorithms with proved ratios (§20–§25); general instance opens onto parameterised algorithms that are polynomial once a structural parameter is bounded (§27); and decision version reminds you that the optimisation problem you actually face may be attacked by branch and bound, by a solver, or by local search that carries no guarantee at all and works (§26, §28–§30). A proof of NP-completeness is therefore best read as a routing decision: it tells you to stop hunting for an exact polynomial algorithm and start choosing among four well-understood responses (§19, §32).

What is and is not claimed

  • Claimed, and proved: that every problem in NP reduces to 3-SAT, to clique, to vertex cover, to Hamiltonian cycle, to subset sum, to 3-colouring, and so on down the catalogue; that a polynomial algorithm for any of them collapses the whole class into P; that certain approximation ratios are achievable, with the proofs given here; and that certain ratios are not achievable unless P = NP.
  • Believed, and unproved: that P ≠ NP. This page never writes "no polynomial-time algorithm exists" for an NP-complete problem. It writes "none is known, and its existence would imply P = NP", which is what the mathematics supports. The belief is strong — it is the standard working hypothesis of cryptography, of complexity theory, and of the whole literature of approximation lower bounds — but it is a hypothesis.
  • Not claimed: that NP-complete instances are hard in practice. Modern SAT solvers dispatch industrial formulas with millions of variables (§30), integer programming solves scheduling problems with hundreds of thousands of binaries, and the instances that defeat them are usually engineered. NP-completeness is a statement about the worst case over all instances, and the worst case is a small and peculiar corner of instance space.
  • Frequently confused, and separated here: NP-hard is not NP-complete (an NP-hard problem need not be in NP — the halting problem is NP-hard and not even decidable, §18); "NP" does not abbreviate "non-polynomial" (it abbreviates nondeterministic polynomial, §05); an optimisation problem is normally NP-hard rather than NP-complete, while its decision version is NP-complete (§03); and O(nW) knapsack is pseudo-polynomial, not polynomial (§02, §14).

32 Choosing a response, as a decision procedure

A proof of NP-hardness is a routing decision, and the route is determined by four questions about your instances and your caller — not by which technique is fashionable. §19 laid out the four responses as trades; this section turns them into a procedure you can actually run, in the order that eliminates the most work soonest. The order matters: the first question is free and occasionally ends the enquiry, the second is cheap, the third is a conversation with whoever asked, and only the fourth is an engineering decision. Running them in the reverse order — picking an algorithm first and discovering afterwards that the problem was never the general one — is the commonest way to waste a month.

Three notes on running this in practice

  • The responses compose, and in production they always do. A realistic pipeline is: presolve and kernelise (§27, §30), construct an initial solution with an approximation algorithm (§20–§25), improve it with local search (§28), hand both the improved solution and the instance to a branch-and-bound or MIP solver as an incumbent (§26, §30), and report the MIP gap. Each stage makes the next one cheaper, and the incumbent from stage three is what makes stage four's pruning bite from the first node.
  • Always build the measuring stick. Whatever you choose, compute a bound you can compare against: the exhaustive optimum on small instances, the MST or the assignment relaxation for TSP, the LP relaxation for anything you can write as an ILP, or the best-known solution for a public benchmark. A heuristic without a bound produces numbers no one can interpret; the same heuristic with a bound produces a measured gap, which is a real result about your instances.
  • Re-run Q0 when the answer disappoints. The most common reason a hard problem stays hard is that the formulation carries a constraint nobody needed, an objective nobody measured, or a generality nobody has. That is §19's fifth response, it is on no textbook's list, and it resolves more projects than any algorithm in this half of the page.

33 Pitfalls

The errors below are the ones this page's own construction produced, plus the ones the field repeats. Several of them are invisible at run time: the program is faster, the output looks reasonable, and nothing reports a problem. They are ordered roughly by how much damage they do relative to how easy they are to make.

Errors of statement

  • Reducing in the wrong direction. To prove B hard you reduce a known-hard A to B: A ≤ₚ B, an instance of A turned into an instance of B. Showing that your problem reduces to 3-SAT proves your problem is in NP, which is usually obvious and never what you wanted. The mnemonic that survives pressure: the reduction runs from the problem you already know is hard, and the new problem sits on the right of the ≤. §06 states both directions of the correctness condition; the trap is why it does.
  • "NP-complete" said of an optimisation problem. NP-complete is a class of decision problems, because membership in NP is about certificates for YES answers. "Find the shortest tour" is NP-hard; "is there a tour of length ≤ k?" is NP-complete. Both statements are useful and they are not interchangeable. §03 gives the machinery — self-reducibility — that makes the two versions equivalent in power while keeping them distinct in classification.
  • "NP-hard" used as a synonym for "not in P". NP-hard does not imply membership in NP, does not imply decidability, and does not imply "outside P" — if P = NP then every NP-complete problem is in P. The halting problem is NP-hard and undecidable (§18). Saying "NP-hard, therefore no algorithm exists" compounds three errors in five words.
  • "No polynomial-time algorithm exists." It is not known. P ≠ NP is unproven, and a proof would be the most important result in the field. The correct sentence is "no polynomial-time algorithm is known, and one would imply P = NP". This page writes it that way everywhere, and the discipline is not pedantry: the two sentences license completely different engineering decisions, because the second one tells you that the obstruction is a conjecture and the first tells you to stop looking.
  • "P = NP would break all cryptography." Too strong, in the two ways §07 sets out. An efficient, practical algorithm for NP would break public-key schemes whose security rests on an NP problem — factoring, discrete logarithm, lattices — and symmetric ciphers, because recovering a key from a known plaintext–ciphertext pair is an NP search problem with the key as its certificate. A proof of P = NP that is non-constructive, or whose algorithm has an unusable exponent or constant (an n¹⁰⁰), would break neither in practice — and that caveat applies to RSA-2048 exactly as much as to AES-128, since each is one fixed-size instance of an asymptotic family. It would not touch the one-time pad, which is information-theoretically secure and needs no assumption at all. The honest summary is "a practical algorithm would break essentially all deployed computational cryptography, public-key and symmetric alike; P = NP by itself would not".

Errors of arithmetic and of hypothesis

  • Treating pseudo-polynomial as polynomial. Knapsack's O(nW) is not polynomial in the input size, because W is written in ⌈log₂ W⌉ bits, so O(nW) = O(n·2^(bits)) — doubling the digits of W squares the running time (§02, §14). §02's figure measures exactly this. The diagnostic question is: does the running time depend on the magnitude of a number in the input, or only on how many symbols it takes to write?
  • Quoting a metric-TSP result for general TSP. Three different problems with three different answers: general TSP has no constant-factor approximation unless P = NP; metric TSP has 3/2 from Christofides (and, since 2021 randomised and since 2023 deterministic, very slightly better); Euclidean TSP has a PTAS. The 2 and the 3/2 both require the triangle inequality and both are false without it, by §22's gap reduction. Before quoting a ratio, name the hypothesis and check it — the figure of §22 checks the triangle inequality over all n³ triples for exactly this reason.
  • Dropping an approximation algorithm's hypothesis or its step 0. The knapsack FPTAS needs items heavier than the capacity discarded first, or v_max ≤ OPT fails and the guarantee evaporates (§25). Greedy set cover's bound is H(d) where d is the largest set size — and ln n is not H(n): ln(n + 1) < H(n) ≤ ln n + 1, so ln n sits strictly below it and ln n + 1 is the upper bound (§23). The 2-approximation for vertex cover needs a maximal matching, not a maximum one, and the distinction is where the proof lives (§21). A ratio stated without its hypothesis is not a theorem.
  • Computing a ratio from the wrong pair of numbers. An approximation ratio is ALG/OPT for a minimisation, and OPT means the true optimum, not the best you found, not the LP bound, and not the previous algorithm's answer. Every ratio on this page is measured against exhaustive search on instances small enough to exhaust, which is the only way to be sure — and where exhaustion is impossible, a ratio against a bound must be labelled as such, since it is an upper bound on the true ratio, not the ratio.

Errors that make the program faster and the answer wrong

  • A branch-and-bound "lower bound" that is not a lower bound. This is the worst one on the list, because its symptom is improved performance. §26 measures it: on the eight-city instance, the invalid bound runs the search in 49 nodes against the valid bound's 116 — less than half the work — and returns 33.0944 against the true optimum of 32.1721. There is no exception, no warning, no anomalous statistic; the search simply prunes the branch containing the optimum and reports what is left. The direct diagnosis takes one loop: on that instance the valid bound is ≤ the true cheapest completion on 260 partial paths out of 260, and the invalid one on 29 out of 260, overshooting by as much as 4.0036. Test the bound against exhaustive completion on small instances. Correctness of the search is not evidence.
  • A pruning rule with a strict/non-strict slip. Pruning on cost + LB ≥ best is correct; pruning on cost + LB > best is also correct but slower; pruning on cost + LB ≥ best when best is stored as the value of a solution you have not yet verified is a bug. The same class of slip in an FPT search — decrementing the budget on a branch that does not take a vertex — silently changes which instances answer YES.
  • Memoising on an incomplete state. Held–Karp is correct because the future of a partial tour depends only on (visited set, current endpoint). Drop the endpoint from the key and the table is smaller, the algorithm is faster, and the answers are wrong. Any DP over subsets must have the argument for why the state is sufficient written down, because the failure is silent in exactly the same way.

Errors of measurement and reporting

  • Benchmarking a heuristic only where it wins. §28's 2-opt reaches the global optimum on the eight-city instance from the nearest-neighbour start, in two moves. Reporting that as evidence for 2-opt would be indefensible: it is one instance, one starting tour, and the same code with best-improvement instead of first-improvement lands on 33.0944 after more work. The measured facts to report alongside any heuristic result are the instance family, the number of instances, the starting solution, the variance across seeds, and the bound the gap was measured against.
  • Reading an asymptotic threshold off a small-n curve. Random 3-SAT's conjectured threshold is m/n ≈ 4.267 as n → ∞. The measured 50% crossing is 4.721 at n = 14 and still 4.379 at n = 40 (§30). A figure of the small-n curve captioned "the threshold is 4.267" states a number the picture does not show. Both numbers are right; they answer different questions, and the caption has to say which one it is answering.
  • Confusing a state count with a time bound. Held–Karp has Θ(n·2ⁿ) states and Θ(n²·2ⁿ) running time; the bounded search tree has at most 2^(k+1) − 1 nodes and O(2ᵏ·E) running time. Putting the time bound in a column of state counts, or quoting the node count as if it were the cost, understates the work by a factor of n or of E. Keep them in separate columns, as §26 and §27 do.
  • Claiming a guarantee a local search does not have. 2-opt, simulated annealing, WalkSAT and genetic algorithms carry no approximation guarantee whatsoever. 2-opt on metric instances has a proved, tight worst-case ratio of √(n/2) for a local optimum — so it is not merely that no bound is known; a constant bound is known to be false. Describing a heuristic's typical performance as an "approximation" is the single most common abuse of the vocabulary this page builds.
  • Assuming a hardness proof describes your instances. The proof is about the worst case over all instances. Industrial SAT instances with millions of variables are solved in seconds while random instances with a few hundred variables at the threshold are not (§30). The theorem forbids a polynomial worst case; it says nothing about your data, and the only way to find out about your data is to measure.
