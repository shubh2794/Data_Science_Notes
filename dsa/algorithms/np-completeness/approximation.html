19 Four responses to a hardness proof

A proof that a problem is NP-hard is a statement about the worst case of the general problem, solved exactly, in polynomial time — and every practical response to such a proof works by giving up exactly one of those four words. The proof does not say your instances are hard; it says that some instance, of some size, is hard, for every polynomial algorithm, unless P = NP. That leaves four doors. Give up exactness and you get approximation algorithms with a proved ratio (§20–§25) or heuristics with none (§28–§29). Give up generality and you get special cases, structural restrictions and bounded parameters (§27, and the tractable islands of §16). Give up the polynomial bound and you get exact exponential algorithms whose exponent is small enough, or whose pruning is good enough, that real instances finish (§26, §30). Give up determinism, or the worst-case criterion itself, and you get randomised algorithms and average-case analysis. This section states the four responses as trades, so that the rest of the page reads as a catalogue of the price each one charges.

What the hardness proof actually forbids — and what it leaves alone

Read the statement of §07 literally. "3-SAT is NP-complete" says: if some algorithm decides every 3-CNF formula correctly in time bounded by a polynomial in the encoded length of the formula, then P = NP. Four quantifiers do the work, and each one is a place to stand.

Two things the proof does not forbid, and both matter more in practice than anything it does forbid. First, it does not forbid an algorithm that is fast on your instances: SAT solvers routinely dispatch industrial formulas with millions of variables (§30) while choking on random formulas with a few hundred, and nothing in the theorem is violated — the theorem only says that some family defeats every polynomial bound. Second, it does not forbid a good exponential: a bound of O(1.3·2ⁿ) and a bound of O(1.31ⁿ) are both exponential and the second solves instances the first cannot look at, which is the whole content of §26.

Response 1 — give up optimality

Return a solution that is provably within a factor of the best one. An algorithm with approximation ratio ρ is a theorem, not a hope: for every instance, the cost it returns is within ρ of the optimum, and you never need to know the optimum to trust it. §20 fixes the definitions; §21–§24 prove four such theorems (vertex cover at 2, metric TSP at 3/2, set cover at H(d), MAX-3-SAT at 7/8); §25 shows a scheme whose ratio you dial. The catch is that the ratio is itself subject to hardness: for many problems there is a threshold below which approximating is as hard as solving, and §20 names the theorem (PCP) that produces those thresholds. The other catch is that a proved ratio is often loose — the figure of §20 measures how loose, on this page's own instances.

If you do not need a proof, drop the ratio and keep the answer: a heuristic. Nearest neighbour for TSP (§22), 2-opt and simulated annealing (§28), genetic algorithms (§29) all return tours, none comes with a bound worth quoting, and on the instances people actually solve they routinely beat the algorithms that do come with bounds. Machine learning runs almost entirely on this response: finding the smallest decision tree consistent with a training set is NP-complete, so every practical learner grows the tree greedily one split at a time and abandons optimality without apology (Decision Trees). That is not a paradox: a worst-case ratio bounds the worst instance, not the typical one, and the measured column in the §20 figure separates the two.

Response 2 — give up generality

Solve a sub-family exactly. Three shapes, in increasing order of cleverness. (i) A syntactic restriction: 2-SAT is in P while 3-SAT is NP-complete; Horn-SAT is in P; 2-colouring is bipartiteness-testing and 3-colouring is NP-complete; matching is polynomial on any graph while its "vertex" twin, independent set, is not. §16 collects these boundary pairs, and the useful reflex is to look at your instances and ask which side of a known boundary they sit on. (ii) A structural restriction: many NP-hard graph problems are polynomial — often linear-time — on trees, on series-parallel graphs, on interval graphs, or on graphs of bounded treewidth, because the dynamic programming of Dynamic Programming has somewhere to run. Planarity alone is not on that list: planar 3-colouring (§15, §16), planar vertex cover and planar Hamiltonian cycle all stay NP-complete. What planarity buys is weaker but real — approximation schemes by Baker's layering technique and exact algorithms in 2^O(√n) time, because a planar graph has treewidth O(√n). (iii) A parameter that is small in practice: vertex cover is solvable in O(1.2738ᵏ + k·n) time where k is the cover size, which is polynomial — linear, even — in n for any fixed k. §27 develops this into fixed-parameter tractability, which is the most under-used of the four responses.

Response 3 — give up the polynomial worst case

Solve it exactly, and be honest about the exponent. Brute force over all 2ⁿ subsets is one such algorithm and usually the worst one; branch and bound prunes it with a bound computed at each node; dynamic programming over subsets (Held–Karp) solves TSP in O(n²·2ⁿ) instead of O(n!), which is the difference between 20 cities and 12; and a modern SAT or ILP solver is an exact exponential algorithm with two decades of engineering inside the constant. §26 and §30 are the two halves of this response. The measurement that matters is not the exponent in the bound but the size of the search tree the solver actually builds, and every figure in those sections counts nodes rather than quoting a bound.

Response 4 — give up determinism, or the worst case as the criterion

Randomise. §24's MAX-3-SAT algorithm is the clean example: flip a fair coin for every variable and you satisfy 7m/8 clauses in expectation, which is within 8/7 of the optimum, from an algorithm that does not look at the formula. Randomisation buys three different things — a guarantee in expectation (as there), a guarantee that holds with high probability, or merely a way to escape the adversarial instance that a deterministic rule would always meet — and in the first case it can often be removed afterwards, which is what the method of conditional expectations does in §24. Separately, one can keep exactness and change the criterion: average-case analysis under an instance distribution, or smoothed analysis, both of which say that the hard instances are rare rather than absent. The phase-transition picture of §30 is exactly this: random 3-SAT is easy at most clause densities and hard in a narrow band around m/n ≈ 4.27.

The fifth response, which is not on anyone's list

Solve a different problem. Much of the time the NP-hard formulation is an artefact of how the question was written down, not of what was asked. If the real requirement is "a good schedule by Friday", the NP-hard "an optimal schedule" was never the requirement. If the objective is a guess — a hand-tuned cost function, a proxy metric — then an exactly optimal solution to the guess is not obviously better than an approximate solution to it, and the modelling error may dwarf the optimality gap. If the constraint that makes the problem hard was added defensively, relax it. Concretely: fractional knapsack is solved by a greedy rule in O(n log n) while 0-1 knapsack is NP-hard (Greedy Algorithms); bipartite matching is polynomial while 3-dimensional matching is NP-complete; minimum spanning tree is polynomial while the Steiner tree that it is often mistaken for is NP-hard. Checking whether the hardness is in the problem or in the formulation costs an afternoon and occasionally saves the project.

20 Approximation ratios, and the APX / PTAS / FPTAS taxonomy

An algorithm has approximation ratio ρ(n) if, on every instance of size n, the cost C it returns and the optimal cost C* satisfy max(C/C*, C*/C) ≤ ρ(n) — one formula for both minimisation and maximisation, normalised so that ρ ≥ 1 always and ρ = 1 means exact. That convention is the one this page uses everywhere: every ratio quoted below is at least 1, and a bigger number is a worse guarantee. On top of it sit three classes, each a strictly stronger promise than the last: APX, the problems with a polynomial ρ-approximation for some constant ρ; PTAS, the problems where ρ can be pushed to 1 + ε for any ε > 0 you name, in time polynomial in n for each fixed ε; and FPTAS, where that time is polynomial in n and in 1/ε together. This section fixes the definitions, states the containments and why each is believed strict, names the theorem (PCP) that produces inapproximability thresholds, and explains the one structural property — strong NP-hardness — that rules an FPTAS out.

Two small traps in that definition, both of which this page's figures check rather than assume. First, C* must be positive for the ratio to be defined; problems whose optimum can be zero (minimum number of edges to delete, say) need an additive convention instead, and a "ratio" quoted for them is a different object. Second, the ratio is a worst-case quantity over all instances of a size, so measuring it on one instance can only ever produce a lower bound on it. Algorithm versus scheme

A ρ-approximation algorithm is one program with one ratio baked in: §21's vertex-cover algorithm is a 2-approximation and there is no dial on it. An approximation scheme takes the instance and a parameter ε > 0, and for each fixed ε it is a (1 + ε)-approximation algorithm — a whole family of algorithms indexed by how good you want the answer. The only question left is what the price of a small ε is.

Where the thresholds come from: the PCP theorem, stated carefully

Until 1992 there was no general technique for proving a problem hard to approximate; a reduction that preserves yes/no answers says nothing about near-optimal solutions. The PCP theorem supplied one. In its complexity form it says NP = PCP(log n, 1): every language in NP has a proof system in which the verifier tosses O(log n) random coins, reads a constant number of bits of the proof, accepts every correct proof of a true statement, and rejects every proof of a false statement with probability at least 1/2. Its equivalent form is the one used here — a gap statement.

Strong NP-hardness: the reason some problems can never have an FPTAS

§14 drew the distinction the argument needs. A problem is strongly NP-hard if it stays NP-hard when every number in the instance is bounded by a polynomial in the instance length — that is, when the numbers are written in unary. Subset sum and knapsack are not strongly NP-hard: their O(nW) and O(nV) dynamic programs are polynomial once the numbers are small, which is exactly the hook §25's FPTAS hangs on. TSP, 3-colouring, 3-SAT, vertex cover and 3-Partition are strongly NP-hard — their hardness survives tiny numbers, because they have no big numbers to shrink.

theorem — strong NP-hardness excludes an FPTASlet Π be an optimisation problem whose objective is INTEGER-valued, and suppose OPT(I) ≤ p(|I|, max(I)) for some polynomial p, where max(I) is the largest number appearing in I. If Π is STRONGLY NP-hard, it has no FPTAS unless P = NP. proof sketch: suppose an FPTAS runs in time q(|I|, 1/ε) for a polynomial q. Choose ε = 1 / (p(|I|, max(I)) + 1) < 1/OPT(I). then the relative error is below 1/OPT, so |C − OPT| < 1, and both are integers: C = OPT — the scheme is EXACT. its running time is q(|I|, p(|I|, max(I)) + 1) — polynomial in |I| and max(I), i.e. a PSEUDO-POLYNOMIAL exact algorithm. A strongly NP-hard problem has none unless P = NP (that is what "strongly" means). ∎ reading it backwards: an FPTAS is, in disguise, a pseudo-polynomial algorithm with the numbers scaled down. so LOOK FOR AN FPTAS exactly where §14 found a pseudo-polynomial DP, and nowhere else.

What a proved ratio is worth in practice — the measurement

The figure runs every approximation algorithm on this page against the exhaustive optimum of its own instance and plots the achieved ratio beside the proved bound. Three things come out of it, and the third is the one worth internalising. (i) The bounds hold, every time — that is the agree flag. (ii) Most of them are loose by a wide margin on ordinary instances: Christofides' 3/2 guarantee produces a tour 0.14% above optimal on the eight-city instance of §22, and greedy set cover's H(6) = 2.45 guarantee produces a cover 1.33 times the optimum. (iii) The bounds are not loose on the instances built to break them: the maximal-matching vertex cover hits its bound of 2 exactly, on both the seven-vertex instance and the fifty-vertex adversarial family, and greedy set cover reaches 3.5 on the family of §23. A proved ratio describes the adversary, and the adversary is rarely who you are dealing with — which is precisely why §28's unguaranteed local search can beat the guaranteed algorithms and still be the right choice.

Vocabulary the rest of the page uses without comment

  • NPO — the optimisation analogue of NP: instances and solutions are polynomially bounded and recognisable, the objective is computable in polynomial time, and one asks for a maximum or a minimum. Every problem in this section's taxonomy is in NPO.
  • APX-hard — every problem in APX reduces to it by an approximation-preserving reduction; by the PCP theorem it therefore has no PTAS unless P = NP. The implication runs one way only: "no PTAS unless P = NP" does not by itself make a problem APX-hard. Vertex cover, metric TSP, MAX-3-SAT and set cover are all APX-hard.
  • Gap-preserving reduction — a reduction that carries a "either ≥ a or ≤ b" gap in one problem to a gap in another, so that a good-enough approximation for the second would resolve the first. Ordinary Karp reductions (§06) do not do this; approximation hardness needs the stronger kind.
  • Tight — a ratio is tight when some instance family achieves it. §21's 2 is tight (a single edge already does it), §22's 3/2 is tight for Christofides, §23's H(d) is tight to within lower-order terms. A tight bound cannot be improved by a better analysis; only a better algorithm can help.
  • Asymptotic ratio — some guarantees hold only for large optima, as in bin packing's first-fit-decreasing rule, which uses at most (11/9)·OPT + 6/9 bins (and that additive constant is tight). This page states the additive term whenever there is one; a bare ratio here means the bound holds on every instance.

21 Vertex cover: 2 from a maximal matching

Take any maximal matching of the graph and output both endpoints of every matched edge: that is a vertex cover, and it is at most twice the minimum, because every cover must contain at least one endpoint of each matched edge and the matched edges are disjoint. The algorithm is three lines, it never computes or estimates the optimum, and its proof is two sentences — and yet, more than forty years after it was published, no polynomial algorithm with a constant ratio below 2 is known. That combination makes it the standard first example of an approximation algorithm, and also the standard example of how far a trivial algorithm can be from improvable. This section gives the algorithm, the proof in full, the instance on which the bound is exactly attained, the reason the other obvious greedy — repeatedly take the highest-degree vertex — is not a 2-approximation despite looking smarter, and the current state of the hardness results, with the conditionality of each stated precisely.

The algorithm

APPROX-VERTEX-COVER(G = (V, E))C ← ∅ E′ ← E while E′ ≠ ∅: pick any edge (u, v) ∈ E′ ← ANY edge. No rule, no heuristic, no tie-breaking. Any. C ← C ∪ {u, v} delete from E′ every edge incident on u or on v return C running time Θ(V + E) with adjacency lists and a "used" flag per vertex — one pass over the edge list. what it is really doing: the edges it PICKS form a MAXIMAL MATCHING M (no two share an endpoint, and no edge outside M can be added), and it returns the 2|M| endpoints of M.

Two observations before the proof, because they are where the algorithm's power actually lives. First, the maximality of the matching is what makes the output a cover: if some edge (x, y) had neither endpoint in C, then neither x nor y was ever matched, so (x, y) could have been added to M — contradicting maximality, and equally contradicting the loop condition, since (x, y) would still be in E′. Second, the matching is a lower bound you can hold in your hand: the algorithm produces, alongside its answer, a certificate |M| with OPT ≥ |M|. That is unusual and valuable. Most approximation algorithms prove their ratio by an argument about an optimum nobody computes; this one hands you the witness, so on any particular instance you can report "my cover has 6 vertices and the optimum is at least 3" without further work. §26 uses exactly this kind of computable lower bound to prune a branch-and-bound search.

The proof, in full

theoremAPPROX-VERTEX-COVER runs in polynomial time and returns a vertex cover C with |C| ≤ 2·|C*|, where C* is a minimum vertex cover. It is therefore a 2-approximation algorithm (ρ = 2 in the ρ ≥ 1 convention of §20). proof(1) C IS A COVER. The loop exits only when E′ = ∅, and an edge leaves E′ only when one of its endpoints enters C. So every edge of E has an endpoint in C. (2) |C| = 2|M|. Each iteration adds exactly two vertices, both previously absent — the chosen edge (u, v) is still in E′, so neither u nor v is in C yet (if either were, the edge would have been deleted). M is the set of chosen edges; the iterations are |M| in number; hence |C| = 2|M|, exactly, never less. (3) |C*| ≥ |M|. ◄── the only step with content. The edges of M are pairwise VERTEX-DISJOINT: when (u, v) is chosen, every other edge touching u or v is deleted, so no later chosen edge shares an endpoint with it. A vertex cover must contain at least one endpoint of (u, v), for every (u, v) ∈ M. Those |M| obligations are on |M| DISJOINT pairs of vertices, so they are satisfied by |M| DISTINCT vertices. Hence any cover, and in particular C*, has at least |M| vertices. (4) chain (2) and (3): |C| = 2|M| ≤ 2|C*|. ∎ note what the proof never does: it never computes C*, never bounds |C*| from ABOVE, and never uses any property of WHICH edge the loop picks. Every maximal matching gives a valid 2-approximation; the choices only change WHICH one.

Worked on the seven-vertex instance, and the bound attained exactly

The instance is the graph of §12: vertices a … g and the eight edges ab, bc, cd, ce, de, df, dg, ef. Degrees are a 1, b 2, c 3, d 4, e 3, f 2, g 1, summing to 16 = 2·8 as they must. Taking the edges in that stored order, the algorithm chooses ab (both endpoints free), skips bc and ce and de and df and dg (each already touches a matched vertex), and chooses cd and ef. The matching is M = {ab, cd, ef}, |M| = 3, and the returned cover is {a, b, c, d, e, f} — six vertices, every edge covered (dg through d, bc through b, and so on), with g left out. Exhaustive search over all 2⁷ = 128 subsets says the minimum cover has 3 vertices and is unique: {b, d, e}.

It is the worst case, it is attained by an infinite family, and no sharper ANALYSIS of this algorithm can beat it. Only a different algorithm could.

The complete-bipartite example is worth a second look, because it shows the algorithm failing for a structural reason rather than an unlucky one. In K_{n,n} a maximal matching must saturate every vertex, so the algorithm is forced to output all 2n vertices — and the optimum is one whole side. There is nothing the edge-selection rule could have done. The information the algorithm never uses is that this graph is bipartite; on a bipartite graph the minimum vertex cover equals the maximum matching (König's theorem) and is computable exactly in polynomial time by the flow reduction of Shortest Paths, MST & Flow. That is response 2 of §19 in miniature: vertex cover is NP-hard in general and polynomial on bipartite graphs, and detecting bipartiteness costs one breadth-first search.

The "smarter" greedy is worse: maximum degree is Θ(log n), not 2

The rule everyone proposes on first hearing the problem is: repeatedly take a vertex of maximum remaining degree, delete its edges, repeat. It is a better heuristic — on the seven-vertex instance it picks d (degree 4), then b (degree 2 among what is left), then e, returning exactly {b, d, e}, the unique optimum, where the matching algorithm returned six vertices. It is also not a 2-approximation, and the gap is unbounded.

The reason is that this greedy is the greedy set-cover algorithm of §23 in disguise: cover the set E of edges by the sets S_v = {edges incident on v}. So it inherits set cover's guarantee, H(Δ) where Δ is the maximum degree, and it inherits set cover's lower bound too — there are graphs on which it really does take Θ(log n) times the optimum. The family the figure builds is the standard one: a left side L of k vertices, and for each i = 2, 3, …, k a right group of ⌊k/i⌋ vertices, each joined to i distinct left vertices with the groups partitioning L within each i. Every right vertex of group i has degree exactly i, so the greedy sweeps the right side from i = k downwards and takes ∑ᵢ₌₂ᵏ ⌊k/i⌋ ≈ k(ln k − 1) vertices, while L — which covers every edge, since every edge has a left endpoint — has k.

Why the complement trick does not give an independent-set approximation

§12 proved that C is a vertex cover if and only if V \ C is an independent set, so the two problems are the same problem read in a mirror and an exact algorithm for one is an exact algorithm for the other. Approximation is not preserved by the mirror, and this is the cleanest illustration on the page of why approximation classes are finer than complexity classes. Take a graph on n = 2k vertices with minimum cover k − 1, so the maximum independent set has k + 1 vertices. A 2-approximate cover may have 2k − 2 vertices — a fine cover, ratio under 2 — and its complement is an independent set of 2 vertices against an optimum of k + 1: ratio (k+1)/2, unbounded. The arithmetic is the point: a multiplicative error on |C| becomes an additive error of the same size, and an additive error of k is nothing next to n but everything next to an optimum of size k + 1. Consistently, vertex cover is in APX with ratio 2, while maximum independent set (equivalently maximum clique, §11) has no n^(1−ε)-approximation for any fixed ε > 0 unless P = NP.

What is known about doing better than 2 — with every hypothesis stated

  • No constant below 2 is known. The best published ratios are all of the form 2 − o(1): 2 − (ln ln n)/(2 ln n), improved to 2 − Θ(1/√(log n)). Every one of these tends to 2 as n grows, so none is a ρ-approximation for any constant ρ < 2. "No better than 2 is known" is the accurate sentence; "2 is optimal" is not a theorem.
  • Vertex cover is APX-hard, so it has no PTAS unless P = NP. Concretely, achieving any ratio below 1.3606… = 10√5 − 21 is NP-hard, and that was strengthened by way of the 2-to-2 games theorem: achieving √2 − ε ≈ 1.4142 is NP-hard for every fixed ε > 0, so no ratio below √2 is reachable. Both are unconditional NP-hardness results — they assume nothing beyond P ≠ NP.
  • Under the unique games conjecture — an unproven hypothesis, not a theorem, and not known to follow from P ≠ NP — achieving 2 − ε is NP-hard for every fixed ε > 0, so no ratio below 2 would be reachable, which would make this three-line algorithm optimal. The honest statement of the field's position is: the truth is somewhere in [√2, 2], most people expect 2, and nobody has proved it.
  • Weighted vertex cover — minimise ∑ w(v) over covers — is not solved by this algorithm at all: taking both endpoints of a matched edge can take an arbitrarily heavy vertex when a light one would do. A ratio of 2 survives by a different route: write the integer program, relax the integrality of x_v ∈ {0, 1} to 0 ≤ x_v ≤ 1, solve the linear program in polynomial time — it is a convex problem, so a local optimum is a global one (Optimization) — and round every x_v ≥ 1/2 up. Every edge constraint x_u + x_v ≥ 1 forces at least one of its endpoints to 1/2, so the rounded set is a cover; and rounding at most doubles each variable, so the weight at most doubles the LP optimum, which is itself a lower bound on the integer optimum. §30 returns to this LP-relaxation-and-round pattern, which is the single most productive source of approximation algorithms.
  • On special graph classes the problem is not hard at all. Bipartite: exact by matching (König). Trees: exact by a linear-time dynamic program over the tree, as in Dynamic Programming. Bounded treewidth: exact. Planar: a PTAS exists. Small cover: the FPT algorithm of §27 finds a cover of size k in O(1.2738ᵏ + k·n) — exactly, not approximately.

22 The travelling salesman: 2, then 3/2, and why general TSP has none

There are three travelling-salesman problems, and they have three different answers: the general problem, with arbitrary non-negative costs, admits no polynomial ρ-approximation for any constant ρ unless P = NP; the metric problem, whose costs obey the triangle inequality, has a 2-approximation from a spanning tree and a 3/2-approximation from a spanning tree plus a matching; and the Euclidean problem, whose cities are points in the plane, has a PTAS. Conflating them is the single most common error made about approximation, and it is easy to make, because all three are called "TSP" and all three have a decision version of the same shape, "is there a tour of cost at most B?" — NP-complete for general and for metric costs, and NP-hard for Euclidean distances, where it is not even known to be in NP, because comparing a sum of square roots against B exactly is not known to be possible in polynomial time. The difference is not in how hard the problems are to solve — all three are NP-hard — but in how much structure an approximation algorithm has to exploit. This section derives both metric algorithms with their proofs, measures both on an eight-city instance against an exhaustive optimum over all 2 520 tours, and then runs the impossibility argument for the general problem on the gap instance that §13 constructs — measuring that too.

The 2-approximation: build a tree, walk it, shortcut

APPROX-TSP-TOUR(G, c) — "double tree"1. pick any root r; compute a minimum spanning tree T of the complete graph under c (Prim or Kruskal — see Shortest Paths, MST & Flow) 2. let H = the list of vertices in PREORDER of a depth-first walk of T from r 3. return the Hamiltonian cycle that visits the vertices in the order H running time O(n² ) dominated by the MST on a complete graph (O(V²) Prim with an array, O(E log V) with a heap). proof that the ratio is 2 — three inequalities, and only the third uses the triangle inequality(1) c(T) ≤ c(H*) where H* is an optimal tour. The achieved ratio is 1.2172 against a promised 2 — the guarantee is loose here by a factor of more than 1.6, which is the general experience with this algorithm.

Is the 2 tight? Essentially yes: there are metric families on which the double-tree tour approaches 2·OPT. The reason is visible in the measured tour above — the shortcut D→G costs 9.0554, crossing the whole instance, because the preorder walk finished the B-C-D branch and had to jump to the other side of the tree. The algorithm's weakness is that doubling every tree edge is wasteful: only the odd-degree vertices actually need help.

Christofides' 3/2: double only what must be doubled

The double-tree algorithm doubles every edge in order to make every vertex degree even, which is what an Eulerian circuit needs. But a tree's degrees are already even at most vertices; the ones that need fixing are exactly the odd-degree ones, and there is an even number of those (the handshake lemma: degrees sum to 2|E|, so the odd ones are even in number). Pair them up with a minimum-weight perfect matching instead of doubling everything.

On the 3/2, and on not overstating the 2021 improvement. The 3/2 algorithm dates from 1976 (found independently twice that year) and its ratio stood as the best known for metric TSP for forty-five years. In 2021 it was improved — to a randomised 3/2 − ε for a constant ε of order 10⁻³⁶ — and in 2023 the same authors derandomised it, so a deterministic 3/2 − ε algorithm now exists too. That is a landmark because it broke a barrier, not because it produces better tours: no instance you will ever run distinguishes 3/2 from 3/2 − 10⁻³⁶. The practical statement remains "3/2 is the best ratio anyone can deliver", and the theoretical statement is "3/2 is no longer known to be the limit". Note also that 3/2 is tight for this algorithm — there are metric families on which Christofides' tour approaches (3/2)·OPT — so any further improvement must change the algorithm, not sharpen its analysis. The gap between the achievable 3/2 and the proved-hard 123/122 is one of the best-known open gaps in the field.

Loose ends, each stated with its variant

  • The decision version is NP-complete; the optimisation version is NP-hard but not NP-complete — it is not a decision problem, so it is not in NP at all (§07). This distinction is not pedantry here: every hardness statement in this section is about the optimisation problem, and every one of them is phrased as "unless P = NP", not "because TSP is NP-complete".
  • Asymmetric TSP — c(u, v) ≠ c(v, u), as with one-way streets — is a fourth problem. Neither algorithm above survives, since a spanning tree has no orientation. A constant-factor approximation for the asymmetric metric case was first obtained in 2018, with a very large constant that has since been reduced substantially; the practical algorithms are the solvers of §30. Approximating asymmetric metric TSP better than 75/74 is NP-hard.
  • Euclidean TSP's PTAS partitions the plane recursively and argues that a near-optimal tour can be forced to cross the cut lines only at O(1/ε) designated points per line, which makes a dynamic program over the partition tractable. It is a genuine PTAS, and it is also genuinely impractical for the ε anyone would ask for; the constant hidden in n·(log n)^O(1/ε) is large. It is a theorem about what structure buys, not a recommendation.
  • What people actually run for large Euclidean instances is a constructive heuristic followed by local search — 2-opt, 3-opt, or the Lin-Kernighan family (§28) — which routinely lands within 1–2% of optimal on instances with millions of cities, with no bound of any kind. For exact answers up to tens of thousands of cities, the tool is an integer-programming formulation with cutting planes (§30), not any algorithm on this page.
  • The MST is not a tour and is not a lower bound for everything. c(T) ≤ OPT is the bound this section uses; the reverse is false and the tree is not a solution. The one-tree (Held-Karp) bound sharpens c(T) considerably and is what branch-and-bound codes prune with (§26).

23 Set cover: greedy and the ln n bound

Repeatedly take the set that covers the most still-uncovered elements, and you finish with at most H(d) times the optimal number of sets, where d is the size of the largest set and H(d) = 1 + 1/2 + ⋯ + 1/d is the d-th harmonic number — and no polynomial algorithm can do better than (1 − ε)·ln n unless P = NP, so this three-line greedy is essentially optimal. That pairing is what makes set cover the standard example of a logarithmic approximation: the ratio grows with the input, which looks like a weak result until you learn that nothing better is possible. Two things need care and this section gives both. The bound is H(d), not ln n: H(d) ≤ H(n) ≤ ln n + 1, so "ln n + 1" is the convenient looser restatement and "ln n" its asymptotic shorthand — strictly, ln n slightly underestimates H(n), since ln(n + 1) < H(n). Neither is the theorem. And the charging argument that proves it — every element pays, at the moment it is covered, the reciprocal of how many new elements its set brought in — is the proof technique to learn here, because it recurs throughout approximation.

The problem, and where it has already appeared

SET COVERgiven a finite universe X with |X| = n, and a family F = {S₁, …, S_m} of subsets of X with ⋃F = X find a subfamily C ⊆ F of MINIMUM SIZE with ⋃C = X (the unweighted version; §21's LP note covers weights) decision version "is there a cover of size ≤ k?" NP-complete (3-SAT ≤ₚ VERTEX-COVER ≤ₚ SET-COVER, §12 — vertex cover IS set cover with X = the edges and S_v = the edges incident on v) optimisation NP-hard. Sets may overlap; an element may be covered many times and is paid for once. d = max |Sᵢ| the largest set. For the vertex-cover special case, d = Δ, the maximum degree.

Greedy Algorithms introduces this problem as the canonical case where the greedy choice property fails — there is no exchange argument, the greedy answer is genuinely not optimal on the instance below — and defers the guarantee to here. This is that guarantee. The problem is also the abstract form of a great many real questions: choose the fewest sensors covering every zone, the fewest test cases exercising every branch, the fewest features covering every training example, the fewest data-centre regions covering every latency requirement.

The proof, by charging

The whole proof is the idea of a price. When greedy picks a set that brings in g new elements, it spends one set and buys g elements, so charge each of those g elements a price of 1/g. The matching lower bound, and what it makes greedy

  • No (1 − ε)·ln n-approximation unless P = NP, for every fixed ε > 0 — so no polynomial algorithm beats ln n even by a constant factor. The result was first proved in 1998 under the weaker hypothesis NP ⊄ DTIME(n^(log log n)) and strengthened in 2014 to plain P ≠ NP. Since greedy achieves ln n − ln ln n + Θ(1), the algorithm and the lower bound agree to within lower-order terms: greedy set cover is optimal, as far as anyone can ever prove without settling P versus NP. That is an unusual and satisfying state for a three-line algorithm.
  • Set cover is not in APX — no constant ratio is achievable, unless P = NP — which is why the double-counting in §20's taxonomy puts it outside the constant-factor world entirely, alongside maximum clique.
  • Bounded set sizes rescue it. If every set has at most d elements, the bound is H(d), a constant, so the problem is in APX for each fixed d. The vertex-cover special case is the extreme: every "set" is a vertex's edge list, d = Δ, and on a bounded-degree graph greedy is a constant-factor approximation. The other special structure that helps is the element's frequency f — the maximum number of sets containing any one element — which gives an f-approximation by LP rounding; for vertex cover f = 2, and that is §21's ratio of 2, arrived at from the other side.
  • Weighted set cover — minimise ∑ w(S) — keeps the same bound with the greedy rule changed to "maximise the number of new elements per unit weight", i.e. pick S minimising w(S) / |S ∩ U|. The charging proof goes through verbatim with c_x = w(S)/|S ∩ U|.
  • Maximum coverage — pick k sets covering as many elements as possible — is the maximisation twin, and the same greedy gives 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 of the optimum, i.e. ratio e/(e−1) ≈ 1.582, which is also the best possible unless P = NP. That result, and its submodularity proof, is the reason greedy feature selection has a guarantee at all.

24 MAX-3-SAT: 7/8 by coin flip, then derandomised

Set every variable by an independent fair coin flip. Each clause of three distinct literals fails only if all three of its literals come out false, which happens with probability (1/2)³ = 1/8, so each clause is satisfied with probability exactly 7/8 and the expected number satisfied is exactly 7m/8 — an 8/7-approximation from an algorithm that never reads the formula. Two further facts turn that party trick into one of the most striking results on this page. The expectation can be achieved deterministically, by walking the variables and at each step taking the value with the larger conditional expectation — the method of conditional expectations, which converts the probabilistic argument into a simple polynomial algorithm. And the ratio cannot be improved: satisfying more than (7/8 + ε)·m clauses in polynomial time, for any fixed ε > 0, is NP-hard. So the best polynomial algorithm for MAX-3-SAT, up to ε, is flip coins and do not look at the input — unless P = NP.

The problem, and the hypothesis the argument needs

MAX-3-SATgiven a 3-CNF formula φ with m clauses over n variables, where EVERY CLAUSE HAS EXACTLY THREE LITERALS ON THREE DISTINCT VARIABLES (so no clause contains both x and ¬x, and none repeats a variable) find a truth assignment satisfying as many clauses as possible. MAXIMISATION, so §20's live ratio is OPT / C. ► the distinctness hypothesis is not decoration. A clause (x ∨ ¬x ∨ y) is satisfied with probability 1, which is harmless. A clause (x ∨ x ∨ y) is really the 2-clause (x ∨ y) and is satisfied with probability only 3/4 — BELOW 7/8 — so a formula made of such clauses has E[satisfied] = 3m/4, and the 8/7 guarantee fails (only 4/3 survives). So the BOUND, and not only the exact value 7m/8 and the tightness of the whole section, needs the hypothesis, and the figure checks it on every instance it loads before it quotes a number. ► the decision version "are ALL m clauses satisfiable?" is 3-SAT, NP-complete (§09). If φ is satisfiable the optimum is m — but FINDING the satisfying assignment is still NP-hard, and, remarkably, the 7/8 threshold below holds even when you are PROMISED the formula is satisfiable.

The instance where the bound is exactly attained

Take all eight clauses of three literals over three variables: (¬x₁∨¬x₂∨¬x₃) ∧ (¬x₁∨¬x₂∨x₃) ∧ (¬x₁∨x₂∨¬x₃) ∧ (¬x₁∨x₂∨x₃) ∧ (x₁∨¬x₂∨¬x₃) ∧ (x₁∨¬x₂∨x₃) ∧ (x₁∨x₂∨¬x₃) ∧ (x₁∨x₂∨x₃). Every assignment falsifies exactly one clause — the one whose three literals are precisely the negations of the assignment's three values — and satisfies the other seven. So this formula is unsatisfiable, the optimum is 7, the distribution of Y is a single spike at 7 rather than a spread, and E[Y] = 7 = 7·8/8 = 7m/8 exactly. The 7/8 is attained, not merely approached: on this instance every run of the randomised algorithm returns an optimal assignment, and there is no assignment that does better. Exhaustive search over all 2³ = 8 assignments confirms all of it, and the figure re-derives it at load.

A richer instance shows the distribution the analysis is really about. A random 3-CNF on six variables with twenty clauses (seeded, and checked to be well-formed) has E[Y] = 7·20/8 = 17.5, and the exact distribution over all 2⁶ = 64 assignments is 14: 2, 15: 2, 16: 7, 17: 18, 18: 23, 19: 10, 20: 2 — mean 1120/64 = 17.5 exactly, matching the theorem, with a spread of six clauses around it. Two of the 64 assignments satisfy all twenty, so this formula is satisfiable and its optimum is 20; the randomised algorithm's expectation of 17.5 is 87.5% of that, and 4 000 seeded trials of the real routine give an empirical mean of 17.52.

Derandomisation by the method of conditional expectations

The randomised algorithm gives a guarantee in expectation. Converting it into a deterministic algorithm with the same guarantee takes one idea: the expectation, conditioned on some variables already fixed, is computable exactly in polynomial time; and since the two children average to the parent, at least one child is at least as good as the parent. Walk down the tree, always taking the better child, and the expectation never falls below where it started — 7m/8. At the leaf nothing is random any more, so the "expectation" is the number of clauses satisfied.

Why this is one of the most striking facts on the page

Look at what the threshold says next to the algorithm. Håstad's theorem: for every fixed ε > 0 it is NP-hard to find an assignment satisfying more than (7/8 + ε)·m clauses of a 3-CNF whose clauses have three distinct variables — and this holds even on instances promised to be satisfiable. The algorithm above: satisfies 7m/8 in expectation, deterministically after derandomisation, in O(nm) time, without using any structure of the formula beyond counting its clauses. The two meet exactly. There is no room between them — not a constant factor, not a lower-order term, nothing.

The consequence is worth stating plainly. For MAX-3-SAT, everything an algorithm could possibly learn by examining the formula is worth nothing, asymptotically, beyond what a coin already gives you — unless P = NP. All the propagation, all the clause learning, all the structural insight that makes the solvers of §30 devastating on real instances buys exactly zero guaranteed improvement in the worst case. That is the sharpest illustration available of the difference between worst-case approximability and practical performance, and it is the reason §28–§30 exist as separate sections rather than as footnotes to this one.

It also settles MAX-3-SAT's place in §20's taxonomy: it is in APX (ratio 8/7) and it has no PTAS unless P = NP (a (1 + ε)-approximation for small enough ε would beat 7/8). MAX-3-SAT is therefore the canonical APX-complete problem, and gap-preserving reductions from it are how most other inapproximability thresholds are obtained.

Neighbouring problems, each with its own number

  • MAX-SAT with clauses of arbitrary length is harder to approximate, not easier: a unit clause is satisfied by a coin with probability only 1/2, so the naive bound degrades to 1/2. Better algorithms — combining the random assignment with a linear-programming relaxation and randomised rounding, then taking the better of the two — reach 3/4 of the optimum in the classic treatment, and semidefinite relaxations push that to about 0.797; and since MAX-E3-SAT is a special case of MAX-SAT, nothing can beat 7/8 unless P = NP.
  • MAX-2-SAT: a random assignment satisfies each two-literal clause with probability 3/4. That is beatable — a semidefinite-programming relaxation with hyperplane rounding reaches about 0.940 — and the threshold is 21/22 ≈ 0.955, so a gap remains. Note the boundary this straddles: deciding 2-SAT is in P (§16), while maximising over 2-CNF is NP-hard. Optimisation versions can be hard when the decision version is easy.
  • MAX-Ek-SAT for k literals per clause: the same coin gives 1 − 2^(−k), and that too is exactly the threshold for every k ≥ 3. The trivial algorithm is optimal for the whole family — unless P = NP.
  • The method of conditional expectations is general. Any time a randomised algorithm's objective has a conditional expectation you can evaluate in polynomial time, the same walk derandomises it — it is not a trick specific to satisfiability. The obstacle in other settings is evaluating the conditional expectation, which is why pessimistic estimators (an efficiently computable bound that behaves like the expectation) exist.
  • The probabilistic method more broadly. "The average is at least t, therefore something is at least t" proves the existence of objects nobody can construct — Ramsey-graph lower bounds, error-correcting codes, expanders. This section is the case where the existence proof happens to be constructive with one extra idea, which is rarer than it looks.

25 The knapsack FPTAS: rounding the values

Knapsack has a dynamic program whose table is indexed by value and whose running time is Θ(n·V) — pseudo-polynomial, because V is exponential in the number of bits used to write the values down. Divide every value by K = ε·v_max/n and round down, and the table shrinks to O(n²/ε) columns while the answer loses at most n·K = ε·v_max ≤ ε·OPT: a (1 − ε)-guarantee in O(n³/ε) time, polynomial in n and in 1/ε together. That is a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme, the strongest class in §20's taxonomy, and this is the standard example of one. The mechanism is worth stating as a slogan, because it is the only known route to an FPTAS: an FPTAS is a pseudo-polynomial algorithm with the numbers scaled down. It follows that a problem has an FPTAS essentially only where §14 found a pseudo-polynomial dynamic program — which is why knapsack and subset sum have one and TSP, 3-colouring and vertex cover cannot (§20's strong-NP-hardness theorem).

Two dynamic programs, and why the second one is the useful one here

Dynamic Programming builds the weight-indexed table: m[i][w] = the best value achievable from the first i items within capacity w, filled in Θ(n·W) time. That is the one everyone learns, and it is the wrong one to scale, because the guarantee we want is about value and rounding the weights would break feasibility — a rounded-down weight can let a set into the knapsack that does not fit. So transpose the table.

What generalises, and what does not

  • Subset sum gets the same treatment in a slightly different dress: keep a list of achievable sums and repeatedly trim it, deleting any element within a factor 1 + δ of one already kept, with δ = ε/(2n). The list stays of length O((n/ε)·log(target)) and the answer loses at most a factor 1 + ε. Trimming and rounding are the same idea — collapse values that are close — and §14's pseudo-polynomial DP is the thing being collapsed in both cases.
  • The cheap alternative, when ε is not the question. A 2-approximation for knapsack needs no dynamic program at all: sort by value density vᵢ/wᵢ, take the greedy prefix that fits, and return the better of that prefix and the single most valuable item. One of the two is always at least half the optimum, because the fractional relaxation's optimum is the greedy prefix plus a fraction of the next item (Greedy Algorithms), and that optimum bounds OPT above. Use the FPTAS when the caller names an ε; use the greedy when they do not.
  • Why no FPTAS for TSP, colouring or vertex cover. §20's theorem: a strongly NP-hard problem with a polynomially bounded integer objective has no FPTAS unless P = NP, because running the scheme at ε < 1/OPT would make it exact in pseudo-polynomial time. Those three problems are strongly NP-hard — their hardness does not come from big numbers — so there is nothing to scale. Knapsack's hardness does come from big numbers, which is precisely §14's pseudo-polynomial observation read forwards.
  • The knapsack FPTAS is not how anyone solves large knapsacks. Branch and bound with the fractional relaxation as the bound (§26), or an integer-programming solver (§30), routinely handles instances with hundreds of thousands of items exactly. The FPTAS is the structural result — it locates knapsack in the easiest approximation class there is — and the algorithm you would actually deploy only when a hard accuracy contract has to be met with a provable bound.
  • Where the ε goes in practice. The running time O(n³/ε) is polynomial in 1/ε, but ε = 10⁻⁶ still means a million-fold multiplier. "Fully polynomial" is a statement about the shape of the dependence, not a promise that small ε is cheap. Measure before promising.
