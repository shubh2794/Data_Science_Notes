16 Where the boundary runs

The boundary between P and the NP-complete problems does not run between problems that look different; it runs between problems that look almost identical, and in every case the crossing is caused by one structural property collapsing. Two literals per clause is in P and three is NP-complete. Two colours is in P and three is NP-complete. Every edge once is in P and every vertex once is NP-complete. Shortest path is in P and longest simple path is NP-hard. Matching in two dimensions is in P and in three dimensions it is NP-complete. A spanning tree is in P and a Steiner tree is NP-hard. Real-valued linear programming is in P and integer-valued linear programming is NP-complete. Knapsack with unary numbers is in P and with binary numbers it is NP-complete. This section is the table of those pairs with the reason in each, because "which side of this line is my problem on?" is the question the rest of the page exists to answer, and the pairs are the fastest way to learn to see it.

What the pairs have in common

Read down the third column and the same three mechanisms recur.

  • Forcing. On the easy side, fixing one piece of the solution determines the next piece, so the algorithm is a propagation with no branching: 2-SAT's implications, 2-colouring's parity, Horn-SAT's unit propagation. On the hard side there is always a free choice left, and free choices compose multiplicatively.
  • A local certificate of global optimality. Berge's lemma for matching, the cut property for MST, convexity for LP, the relaxation optimality conditions for shortest paths. Each says "if no small local improvement exists, you are globally optimal", which is what makes a polynomial local search correct. Every hard-side entry is a problem where a local optimum need not be global.
  • Additivity or independence of subproblems. Shortest paths decompose; longest simple paths do not. Euler's condition is a sum over vertices; Hamiltonicity is not. This is the same "optimal substructure" property that Dynamic Programming, §02 requires, and its absence is what rules out the obvious dynamic program over polynomially many states.

None of the three is a decision procedure — there is no algorithm that takes a problem statement and reports which side of the line it falls on, and there cannot be one in general. But they are what an experienced person is checking when they look at a new problem for thirty seconds and say "this smells NP-hard". The productive follow-up is to try to build the gadget: if you can encode a Boolean variable and an OR, you are almost certainly done, and the attempt usually either succeeds within an hour or shows you the forcing structure that makes the problem easy.

How to use this table on a real problem. Find the closest entry. If your problem is on the easy side of a pair, look for the forcing structure or the local certificate, because that is what the polynomial algorithm will be built from — and check that your problem really has it and not merely something that resembles it. If it is on the hard side, do not spend a week on an exact polynomial algorithm; spend an hour on a reduction to confirm the hardness, and then read §19. And if your problem sits between two entries — feasibility easy, optimisation unknown; numbers small, combinatorics large — the parameter that decides it is usually visible in the table's third column, and the right move is often to fix that parameter and reach for §27.

17 co-NP, NP ∩ co-NP, and problems of unknown status

co-NP is the class of languages whose complements are in NP — equivalently, the class of problems whose no-instances have short, efficiently checkable certificates. NP is not symmetric under complement, and the asymmetry is built into the definition: a certificate for "yes, this formula is satisfiable" is one assignment, three lines long; there is no known short certificate for "no, this formula is satisfiable under none of its 2ⁿ assignments". Whether one exists is the question "NP = co-NP?", and it is a different open question from "P = NP?" — related, since P = NP would imply NP = co-NP, but not known to be equivalent. This section makes the asymmetry concrete, names the co-NP-complete problems, and then deals with the genuinely open middle: NP ∩ co-NP, and integer factorisation, which is the single most frequently mis-stated entry in this whole subject.

The asymmetry, made concrete

Take the page's formula φ, which is satisfiable, and its unsatisfiable companion φ₈. To convince a sceptic that φ is satisfiable, hand over the assignment x₁ = x₂ = x₃ = TRUE; checking it means evaluating at most 3m = 9 literals and confirming that each of the 3 clauses contains a true one. The check does not depend on how large the search space is; it is linear in the formula. To convince the same sceptic that φ₈ is unsatisfiable, there is nothing to hand over but the search itself: all 2³ = 8 assignments, each rejected. The figure scales this up with a control on n, generating a satisfiable instance (planted around a hidden assignment) and an unsatisfiable one (containing all eight clauses on three of the variables, which is unsatisfiable regardless of the rest) at each size, and measuring both costs.

At n = 20, with 4n = 80 clauses, the verifier does 126 literal evaluations on the yes-instance and the exhaustive search does 2²⁰ = 1 048 576 assignments — 8.9 million literal evaluations — on the no-instance. The ratio grows without bound, and that is the shape of the open question: is the second column necessarily exponential, or is there a certificate for unsatisfiability we have not found? A proof system for unsatisfiability does exist — resolution — but the refutations can be exponentially long: the pigeonhole principle, expressed in CNF, requires resolution refutations of size 2^Ω(n). That is a lower bound for one proof system, not for all of them, and proving super-polynomial lower bounds for strong enough proof systems is exactly the programme that would separate NP from co-NP.

NP ∩ co-NP: the problems with good characterisations

A problem in NP ∩ co-NP has short certificates both ways: whatever the answer, it can be justified in polynomial time. Edmonds called this a "good characterisation", and the striking historical pattern is that problems with good characterisations have, one after another, turned out to be in P — often decades later.

Factoring: the claim to get right

Integer factorisation is not known to be NP-complete, it is not known to be in P, and it is widely believed to be neither. The three statements that matter:

  • It is in NP ∩ co-NP. The decision form "does N have a non-trivial factor less than k?" has a short certificate either way: the complete prime factorisation of N, together with a Pratt certificate of primality for each prime factor, proves both what the factors are and that there are no others, and the whole object is polynomial in log N. This is the sense in which factoring is "certifiable both ways" — and it is only possible because primality itself has short certificates.
  • If it were NP-complete, NP would equal co-NP. That is the general fact stated above: an NP-complete problem in NP ∩ co-NP forces the two classes to coincide, because every NP language then reduces into co-NP. Since NP = co-NP is widely disbelieved, so is the NP-completeness of factoring. This is an inference from a conjecture, not a theorem, and should be stated as such.
  • Its presumed hardness is a cryptographic assumption, not a complexity theorem. RSA's security rests on factoring being hard on average for randomly chosen semiprimes — a much stronger requirement than NP-hardness, which is a worst-case notion. The best known classical algorithm, the general number field sieve, runs in time roughly exp(c·(log N)^(1/3)·(log log N)^(2/3)): sub-exponential in log N, super-polynomial, and nothing like the 2ⁿ of brute force. And Shor's algorithm factors in polynomial time on a quantum computer, which is a statement about BQP and says nothing about P versus NP — no quantum polynomial algorithm for an NP-complete problem is known, and none is expected.

The same three-part story applies, with different details, to discrete logarithm and to graph isomorphism. Graph isomorphism is in NP, is not known to be in P, and is not known to be NP-complete; it is not known to be in co-NP either, but graph NON-isomorphism has a short interactive proof and lies in AM — so graph isomorphism lies in coAM — from which it follows that if graph isomorphism were NP-complete the polynomial hierarchy would collapse to its second level — which is the strongest evidence available that it is not. Babai's 2015 algorithm runs in quasi-polynomial time, exp((log n)^O(1)) — faster than any exponential, slower than any polynomial. These are the natural inhabitants of the region that Ladner's theorem guarantees is non-empty: if P ≠ NP, there exist NP-intermediate languages, in NP but neither in P nor NP-complete. The theorem's construction is artificial; factoring and graph isomorphism are the natural candidates.

What a proof of NP ≠ co-NP would give

It would settle P ≠ NP immediately, by the contrapositive above — and it is, in one sense, the more tractable target, because it is a statement about proof length rather than about algorithms. To show NP ≠ co-NP it suffices to show that TAUTOLOGY has no polynomially-bounded proof system: no way of writing down, for every tautology, a proof that is polynomial in the formula's size and checkable in polynomial time. This reframing, due to Cook and Reckhow, turned the question into proof complexity, and the programme has produced real results — exponential lower bounds for resolution (Haken, 1985), for bounded-depth Frege systems, for cutting planes — each one a proof system knocked out. No super-polynomial lower bound is known for Frege systems or for extended Frege, and those remain the frontier.

Two practical morals for someone who is not going to prove any of this. First, the asymmetry is real and it shapes tools: a SAT solver that reports "satisfiable" hands you an assignment you can check in seconds, while one that reports "unsatisfiable" is asserting something you must trust its search to have done correctly — which is why modern solvers emit resolution proof logs that an independent checker replays, and why those logs are sometimes gigabytes long for instances the solver settled in minutes. Second, when you build a verification system, notice which direction you need: "find me a bug" is an NP-shaped question with a short witness, and "prove there is no bug" is a co-NP-shaped question with no short witness in general. That difference is why bug-finding tools scale and full verification does not.

The three sentences most often got wrong here, corrected. (1) "Factoring is NP-complete" — false as a claim of knowledge; it is not known to be, and if it were, NP = co-NP. (2) "PRIMES was proved to be in NP ∩ co-NP in 2002" — wrong date and wrong result; PRIMES was known to be in NP ∩ co-NP from 1975 (Pratt certificates for the yes side, a factor for the no side), and 2002 is when AKS put it in P. (3) "NP = co-NP is the same question as P = NP" — false; one implication is known (P = NP ⟹ NP = co-NP) and the converse is open, so a world with P ≠ NP and NP = co-NP is not ruled out by anything currently proved.

18 Beyond NP: PSPACE, EXPTIME, and the undecidable

NP is not the top of the hierarchy, and it is not even the place where the first proved separation happens: the time hierarchy theorem establishes that P ⊊ EXPTIME unconditionally, which means that at least one of the inclusions P ⊆ NP ⊆ PSPACE ⊆ EXPTIME is strict — we simply do not know which. Above NP sit PSPACE, whose complete problems are quantified Boolean formulas and two-player games of polynomially bounded length; EXPTIME, whose complete problems include generalised chess and Go on an n × n board; and above everything computable, the undecidable problems, of which the halting problem is the root and Rice's theorem is the generalisation that makes program analysis permanently approximate. This section draws the containment diagram, marks exactly which inclusions are proved strict and which are merely believed, and is careful about the difference throughout, because this is the part of the subject where confident-sounding false statements are most common.

The time hierarchy theorem: the separations we do have

Almost nothing is proved about the classes in this subject, and it is worth knowing where the exceptions come from. They come from diagonalisation, the same argument Cantor used on the reals and Turing used on the halting problem. Sketch, for time: build a machine D that, on input ⟨M⟩, simulates M on ⟨M⟩ for f(n) steps and then outputs the opposite of whatever M did. D runs in time about f(n)·log f(n) — the logarithmic overhead is the cost of a universal machine simulating an arbitrary machine with an arbitrary alphabet and tape count. And D's language cannot be decided in time f(n) by any machine, since any such machine M disagrees with D on the input ⟨M⟩. The machine M being simulated contributes its own constant factor, so the separation needs a little slack beyond f·log f: hence DTIME(f(n)) ⊊ DTIME(g(n)) whenever f(n)·log f(n) = o(g(n)) and g is time-constructible — for instance g = f·log²f — and taking f(n) = n^k against 2ⁿ gives P ⊊ EXPTIME.

Why does the same argument not separate P from NP? Because it relativises: it works unchanged if every machine is given access to an oracle, and Baker, Gill and Solovay showed in 1975 that there are oracles A with Pᴬ = NPᴬ and oracles B with Pᴮ ≠ NPᴮ. Any proof technique that survives the addition of an arbitrary oracle therefore cannot settle P versus NP in either direction. Two further barriers are known — natural proofs (Razborov–Rudich, 1994) and algebrization (Aaronson–Wigderson, 2008) — and between them they rule out most of what is currently known how to do. That is the real reason the problem is open: not that nobody has tried, but that the available techniques are provably insufficient.

PSPACE: the same space reused exponentially often

PSPACE is where the quantifiers alternate. NP asks "does there exist a certificate?" — one existential quantifier. The canonical PSPACE-complete problem allows arbitrarily many alternating quantifiers:

TQBF, the PSPACE-complete problemTQBF = { ⟨Ψ⟩ : Ψ = Q₁x₁ Q₂x₂ … Qₙxₙ φ(x₁ … xₙ) is TRUE }, each Qᵢ ∈ {∃, ∀}, φ a Boolean formula SAT is the special case with EVERY quantifier ∃; TAUTOLOGY is the case with every quantifier ∀ — so TQBF generalises both NP-complete and co-NP-complete problems. the recursive evaluator: EVAL(i): if i > n return φ(x₁ … xₙ) if Qᵢ = ∃ return EVAL(i+1 with xᵢ = 0) OR EVAL(i+1 with xᵢ = 1) if Qᵢ = ∀ return EVAL(i+1 with xᵢ = 0) AND EVAL(i+1 with xᵢ = 1) TIME up to 2^(n+1) − 1 recursive calls — EXPONENTIAL SPACE recursion depth n, one bit of assignment per level — Θ(n), LINEAR the space is REUSED: the ∃-branch's workspace is released before the ∀-branch runs. That reuse is exactly what "polynomial SPACE, unbounded time" buys, and it is why PSPACE contains problems believed far above NP while still being a class of feasible-MEMORY computations. measured in the figure at n = 3 on φ: ∃x₁∃x₂∃x₃ φ = TRUE (5 calls) · ∃x₁∀x₂∃x₃ φ = TRUE (7) · ∀x₁∃x₂∀x₃ φ = FALSE (7) · ∀x₁∀x₂∀x₃ φ = FALSE (4) — depth 3 in every case.

The quantifier alternation is what makes TQBF the natural model of a two-player game: "does White have a move such that for every Black reply White has a move such that …". Generalised versions of two-player games whose play length is bounded by a polynomial in the board size are typically PSPACE-complete — generalised Hex, generalised geography. One-player puzzles such as Sokoban and Rush Hour are PSPACE-complete for a different reason: their shortest solutions can need exponentially many moves, but a position is polynomial-size, and whether a goal position is reachable is decidable in polynomial space (Savitch's theorem applied to the graph of positions). Two-player games whose play can last exponentially long are typically EXPTIME-complete, not PSPACE-complete: generalised chess, generalised checkers and generalised Go (with the Japanese ko rule) are EXPTIME-complete. That distinction is routinely got wrong and is worth holding onto: for two-player games the class follows from how long the game can last, not from how hard the game feels. Because P ⊊ EXPTIME is proved, the EXPTIME-complete games are provably not solvable in polynomial time — one of the very few natural problems for which "no polynomial algorithm exists" can be asserted outright rather than conditionally.

Other PSPACE-complete problems worth recognising: equivalence of two ordinary regular expressions, the NFA universality problem ("does this automaton accept every string?"), and — the one most likely to arrive in a real job — planning in the STRIPS sense, where a state is a set of propositions and the question is whether a sequence of operators reaches a goal. Plan existence is PSPACE-complete precisely because the plan may need to be exponentially long even though each state is polynomial to write down. This is also the formal shape of model checking against a large state space, and it is why symbolic methods that manipulate sets of states rather than enumerating them are the only ones that scale.

The polynomial hierarchy in one paragraph

Between NP and PSPACE sits a tower. Σ₁ᵖ = NP is "∃ certificate, checkable in P". Σ₂ᵖ is "∃ y ∀ z, checkable in P" — for instance MINIMUM-EQUIVALENT-DNF, "is there a formula of size ≤ k that agrees with this one on every input?". Π₂ᵖ reverses the quantifiers. Each level is NP with an oracle for the level below, and PH = ⋃ₖ Σₖᵖ ⊆ PSPACE. The hierarchy is believed infinite, but if any two adjacent levels coincide it collapses to that level — and in particular P = NP collapses the whole of PH to P. "The polynomial hierarchy collapses" is therefore the standard way of saying "this would be very surprising", and it is the form the evidence against graph isomorphism's NP-completeness takes (§17). Whether PH = PSPACE is open, and there is a neat argument for believing it is not: PSPACE has complete problems (TQBF is one), so PH = PSPACE would give PH a complete problem — and a complete problem for PH would have to lie in some finite level Σₖᵖ, forcing the hierarchy to collapse to that level. Anyone who believes PH is infinite must therefore believe PH ⊊ PSPACE.

Below P: L and NL

It is easy to forget that there is a hierarchy below polynomial time, and it matters for streaming and for very large inputs. L is deterministic logarithmic work space: enough for a constant number of pointers into the input, and nothing else. NL is its nondeterministic version, and directed s-t connectivity is NL-complete under log-space reductions — you can guess the path one vertex at a time, holding only the current vertex. Undirected s-t connectivity was the famous open case, known to be in NL and conjectured to be in L, until Reingold proved it in L in 2004 by a derandomised random walk. L ⊆ NL ⊆ P, all inclusions open; L ⊊ PSPACE is proved. And note the asymmetry that space enjoys and time does not: NL = co-NL is a theorem, while NP = co-NP is open. Nondeterministic space is closed under complement; nondeterministic time is not known to be.

The floor: undecidability

the halting problem, and the diagonal argument in three sentencesHALT = { ⟨M, w⟩ : the Turing machine M halts on input w }. THEOREM (Turing, 1936): HALT is UNDECIDABLE — no machine decides it on every input. 1. Suppose H decides HALT. Build D(⟨M⟩): if H says M halts on ⟨M⟩, loop forever; otherwise halt. 2. Run D on its own description ⟨D⟩. If D halts on ⟨D⟩ then H said "halts", so D loops — contradiction. If D loops then H said "loops", so D halts — contradiction. 3. Therefore no such H exists. The self-reference is the whole argument; it is Cantor's diagonal with programs in place of reals. RICE'S THEOREM (1951) generalises it: EVERY non-trivial property of the LANGUAGE a program recognises is undecidable. "non-trivial" = some program has the property and some does not; "of the language" = it depends only on WHAT the program computes, not HOW. so each of these is undecidable: does this program ever output 7? is it equivalent to that one? does it terminate on all inputs? is this line reachable? is it free of null dereferences? what is NOT covered: SYNTACTIC properties — "does the source contain a goto?", "are there more than 500 lines?" — those are decidable, because they are not properties of the language computed.

The practical consequence is the reason every static analyser behaves the way it does. Since "does this program have a null dereference?" is undecidable, no analyser can be simultaneously sound (never misses a real bug), complete (never reports a false one) and total (always terminates with an answer). Every real tool gives up one. Type checkers and sound analysers keep soundness and totality and accept false positives — they reject programs that would in fact have been fine. Bug-finders like most linters and fuzzers keep completeness-ish behaviour and totality and accept false negatives — they miss real bugs. Interactive theorem provers keep soundness and completeness and give up totality — they may need human help or may not terminate. When a tool's documentation says "may report spurious warnings", that sentence is Rice's theorem showing through, not an implementation shortcut.

Note the relationship to NP-hardness, which is the pairing this page has been building toward. NP-hard does not mean decidable. HALT is NP-hard — given a 3-CNF formula, emit the program "try all assignments; halt if one satisfies", which is computable in polynomial time and halts iff the formula is satisfiable, so 3-SAT ≤ₚ HALT — and HALT is undecidable, hence not in NP, hence not NP-complete. An NP-hard problem is at least as hard as everything in NP; it may be very much harder, and it may not be solvable at all. §07 defines the two terms; this is the example that shows why they must be kept apart.

The containment diagram, with the proved-strict separations drawn differently from the merely-believed ones, and a control that toggles between what is proved and what is believed solid boundary = a containment that is PROVED strict; dashed boundary = a containment that is not known to be strict — the two classes may be equal for all anyone can prove; the "believed" view redraws the picture as most researchers expect it to be, and the readout runs the recursive TQBF evaluator to show exponential time against linear space
What to say and what not to say. Say: "P ⊊ EXPTIME is proved, so at least one of P ⊆ NP, NP ⊆ PSPACE, PSPACE ⊆ EXPTIME is strict." Say: "generalised chess is EXPTIME-complete, and therefore provably not in P." Say: "TQBF is PSPACE-complete; SAT is the all-∃ special case." Do not say: "PSPACE is bigger than NP" — that is believed, not known. Do not say: "NP-hard problems are undecidable" or "undecidable problems are NP-hard-but-worse" — NP-hardness is a lower bound and carries no upper bound at all, and HALT is the example. Do not say "Savitch's theorem shows NP = PSPACE"; Savitch shows NPSPACE = PSPACE, which is a statement about space and has no known consequence for NP.

31 NP-hardness in machine learning

Machine learning is one of the largest consumers of the coping strategies in this half of the page, and it is not obvious from the outside, because the field almost never says "this problem is NP-hard" — it says "we train with stochastic gradient descent", "we grow the tree greedily", "we run Lloyd's algorithm until convergence", "we use the ℓ₁ relaxation". Each of those is a response to a hardness result, and knowing which result it responds to tells you exactly what the method is and is not promising. This section lists the hardness theorems that actually bite in practice, attributes each correctly, and names the standard response beside it. The recurring shape is: the exact combinatorial problem is NP-hard; the field uses a convex relaxation, a greedy rule, or a local search; and the guarantee — where there is one — is conditional on an assumption about the data rather than universal.

Training

  • Training a three-node neural network is NP-complete (Blum and Rivest, 1992). The problem: given labelled examples in {0,1}ⁿ, do there exist weights for a network with two hidden linear-threshold units and one output unit that classifies every example correctly? That is as small as a network gets, the data are binary, and deciding consistency is already NP-complete — by reduction from set splitting. The result is about exact empirical risk minimisation, and it is the reason nobody looks for an algorithm that provably fits a network: backpropagation is a local search (Neural Network Training) over a non-convex surface, with no guarantee of reaching a global minimum and no claim that it does. What makes it work in practice is a separate and much-studied phenomenon — over-parameterisation, which changes the geometry of the loss surface (Neural Networks) — and not a repeal of the theorem.
  • Learning is hard in a second, different sense. Even where the optimisation is easy, proper PAC learning of some concept classes is NP-hard: finding the smallest consistent k-term DNF, or the smallest consistent decision tree, is hard even though a hypothesis from a larger class can be found in polynomial time. This is why the field so often learns an improper hypothesis — a bigger, messier model that predicts well — rather than the smallest one that fits, and why "find the simplest explanation" is a modelling aspiration rather than an algorithm. The bias-variance account of why a bigger hypothesis class is not automatically worse is Bias–Variance Trade-off.

Model structure

  • The minimum-size decision tree consistent with a sample is NP-hard (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976) — and so are most of its variants, including minimising the expected number of tests. The universal response is the greedy one: choose the split that maximises an impurity reduction, recurse, then prune. Decision Trees gives the splitting criteria and the pruning that follows them, and it is explicit that this is a heuristic with no optimality claim: greedy splitting can miss a small tree that requires a first split which looks useless in isolation, exactly the failure mode Greedy Algorithms analyses. Two footnotes worth carrying: the greedy rule has no approximation guarantee for tree size, and modern MIP-based optimal-tree solvers (§30's technology) now build provably optimal trees for small depths on moderate datasets — one of the clearest cases where a solver has moved the frontier of what "we must be greedy here" means.
  • Learning the structure of a Bayesian network is NP-hard (Chickering, 1996), and remains so with in-degree bounded by 2. Score-and-search methods (greedy hill climbing over DAGs with add/delete/reverse moves — local search, §28) and constraint-based methods (conditional-independence tests) are the standard responses; exact solvers based on dynamic programming over subsets (§26's Held–Karp pattern, applied to variable orderings) or on ILP (§30) handle a few dozen variables exactly.
  • Feature selection is subset selection. Choosing the best k features, or the sparsest regression with a given error, is NP-hard — this is the ℓ₀-constrained problem, hard by reduction from exact cover (Natarajan, 1995). Two responses dominate, and they have completely different guarantee structures. Convex relaxation: replace the ℓ₀ count by the ℓ₁ norm and solve a convex programme — the lasso — which recovers the true support under conditions on the design matrix (restricted isometry, irrepresentability) and gives no guarantee when those conditions fail; Regularization shows the geometry that makes ℓ₁ produce exact zeros. Greedy with submodularity: when the objective is monotone and submodular — coverage-like, with diminishing returns — greedy forward selection is within 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 of optimal (Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher, 1978), and that factor is optimal unless P = NP. This is §23's set-cover analysis in maximisation form, and the caution is the same: the guarantee needs submodularity, which for a criterion such as validation accuracy is an assumption, usually a false one.

Clustering

  • k-means is NP-hard — in general dimension even for k = 2, and in the plane for general k. Lloyd's algorithm is a local search, and saying so explains everything about its behaviour: it alternates two exact minimisations (assign points to the nearest centre; move each centre to its cluster's mean), each of which weakly decreases the objective, so it converges — to a local minimum whose quality depends entirely on the initialisation, which is why it is restarted and why k-means++ seeding exists. k-means++ is the one piece of this with a theorem: its seeding gives a solution within O(log k) of optimal in expectation, before Lloyd's iterations even begin. Clustering has the algorithm and the failure modes; §19's "give up optimality" is precisely the door being used.
  • k-centre has a clean 2-approximation and it is tight. Minimise the largest distance from a point to its assigned centre: the farthest-first traversal — repeatedly open a centre at the point furthest from all current centres — is a 2-approximation under the triangle inequality, by an argument in the same shape as §21's and §22's, and no (2 − ε)-approximation exists unless P = NP. This is the clustering objective for which the approximation theory is cleanest, and it is worth knowing that it exists, because the k-means objective's corresponding results are far weaker.
  • Correlation clustering, hierarchical objectives, and choosing k. Most alternative formulations are NP-hard too, and the responses are the same three: LP or SDP relaxation with rounding, greedy with a proved ratio where the objective is submodular, local search where it is not. Single-linkage hierarchical clustering is the exception that proves the rule: it is Kruskal's algorithm stopped early (Clustering), it is polynomial, and it is polynomial because the objective it optimises — maximising the minimum inter-cluster spacing — has a matroid-flavoured exchange argument behind it.

Inference

  • Exact MAP inference in a general graphical model is NP-hard, and computing marginals is #P-hard — a counting class above NP (§03). The tractable islands are exactly the ones §27 predicts: inference is polynomial when the graph has bounded treewidth, by dynamic programming over a tree decomposition, and the chain case of that is the Viterbi algorithm on a trellis, which is the DAG shortest-path algorithm in disguise (Hidden Markov Models & CRFs). Off the islands, the responses are loopy belief propagation (a fixed-point iteration with no convergence guarantee on a general graph), variational approximation (optimise a tractable surrogate, obtaining a bound rather than the answer), and sampling.
  • One important special case is exactly solvable, and it is a minimum cut. A pairwise binary energy whose pairwise terms are submodular can be minimised exactly, in polynomial time, by a single minimum cut — which is why interactive image segmentation is solved to global optimality and multi-label segmentation is not (Image Segmentation). Submodularity plays the role here that the triangle inequality plays in §22: a hypothesis that turns an intractable problem into a tractable one, and that has to be checked rather than assumed.
  • Planning under uncertainty. A fully observed Markov decision process is not NP-hard — it is solvable in polynomial time by linear programming, and value iteration and policy iteration are the practical algorithms (Reinforcement Learning). Partial observability changes that completely: finite-horizon POMDP planning is PSPACE-complete (§18), which is believed to be strictly harder than NP-complete. So the wall that reinforcement learning actually runs into is usually not NP-hardness but the size of the state space, and the response is function approximation rather than any technique in this half of the page.

Elsewhere, briefly

  • Tensor problems. Computing the rank of a 3-tensor, finding a best rank-1 approximation, and most related questions are NP-hard — in sharp contrast to the matrix case, where the SVD answers all of them in polynomial time (Eigenvalues & SVD). That single discontinuity explains a great deal about why tensor methods come with alternating-least-squares heuristics attached.
  • Optimal codebooks, quantisation and nearest-neighbour indexing. Optimal vector quantisation is a clustering problem and inherits its hardness; exact nearest-neighbour search in high dimension is subject to the curse of dimensionality rather than to NP-hardness, and the practical response — approximate search with a recall/latency trade — is the same shape of response as an approximation algorithm, with an empirical rather than a proved guarantee (k-Nearest Neighbours).
  • Hyperparameter search is the black-box case of §29: the objective is a validation score with no structure to exploit, so random search, successive halving and Bayesian optimisation are the sensible responses and exhaustive grid search is the brute force (Hyperparameter Tuning). Nothing here is NP-hard in the technical sense — the space is finite and the cost is in the evaluations — but the design pressures are identical.
  • The reading that generalises. When a machine-learning method is described as greedy, alternating, relaxed, variational, sampled or heuristic, the useful reflex is to ask which exact problem it is a substitute for, and what is known about that problem. Usually the answer is a named NP-hardness result and a named response from §19's four, and the method's guarantees are whatever that response carries — often nothing at all, occasionally a ratio, occasionally a ratio conditional on an assumption about the data. That is not a criticism of the field; it is the correct way to read it.
