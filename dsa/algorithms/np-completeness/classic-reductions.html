10 The reduction map: how the classic problems connect

The reduction map is the directed graph whose vertices are decision problems and whose arrow A → B means "there is a polynomial-time reduction A ≤ₚ B", so that hardness flows along the arrows: if A is NP-hard and A ≤ₚ B then B is NP-hard, and the whole catalogue of NP-complete problems is the set of vertices reachable from one root. That root is circuit satisfiability, which §08 proves NP-hard directly from the definition of a verifier; §09 pushes it into 3-CNF form; and every arrow after that is a construction — a gadget — that turns an instance of the problem at the tail into an instance of the problem at the head. Nobody proves a second problem hard from scratch. The whole discipline is: find the problem in the catalogue that most resembles yours, build the gadget, check both directions of the "if and only if", and check that the construction runs in polynomial time.

Why the map has one root

Cook–Levin does the only piece of work that cannot be delegated. To show that every language in NP reduces to circuit satisfiability, it does not look at any particular problem; it looks at the definition of NP itself — a polynomial-time verifier V(x, c) — and compiles that verifier, for the fixed input x and the fixed length bound on c, into a Boolean circuit whose inputs are the bits of the certificate and whose single output is V's verdict. The circuit is satisfiable exactly when some certificate is accepted. Because the verifier runs in polynomial time, the circuit has polynomially many gates, and building it is a mechanical transcription of the verifier's computation. That is the root, and it is the only vertex of the map whose hardness is established without reference to another vertex.

Everything downstream is comparison. Once CIRCUIT-SAT is NP-hard and 3-SAT inherits it, a new problem joins the catalogue by exhibiting one gadget. This is why the subject feels like a catalogue: the hard intellectual work happened once, in 1971, and the thousands of NP-completeness proofs since are each a single arrow added to a graph whose root was fixed then. It is also why a practitioner's job is pattern-matching. You do not prove your scheduling problem hard from the definition of NP; you notice that it contains 3-PARTITION, or that its objective is a disguised set cover, and you write the arrow.

Reading the map when you are trying to solve something

The backwards reading is the one that changes what you do on a Tuesday. If your problem sits at the head of an arrow whose tail is NP-hard, a polynomial algorithm for your problem would give a polynomial algorithm for the tail, hence for 3-SAT, hence — by §08 — for every problem in NP. That is not a proof that no such algorithm exists; §07 is explicit that P ≠ NP is unproven. It is a statement about company: your problem is now at least as hard as a problem that has resisted fifty years of attention by people who wanted very much to solve it. The correct inference is not "impossible" but "stop looking for an exact polynomial algorithm and read §19", which lists the four things to do instead.

The forwards reading matters too, in a way that is easy to miss. An arrow A → B also says that a good algorithm for B is a good algorithm for A. This is not idle: modern SAT solvers (§30) are fast enough that encoding a hard scheduling or verification problem into CNF and calling a solver is a standard engineering move. The reduction that proves your problem hard is often, run in the other direction, the reduction that lets you solve it. Hardness proofs and practical encodings are the same constructions with opposite intent.

The blow-up: reductions compose, and the sizes multiply

A reduction is only required to be polynomial, and polynomials compose: if f is O(nᵃ) and g is O(nᵇ) then g ∘ f is O(n^(ab)), still polynomial. That is all the theory needs. But the exponents are real, and a chain of four reductions applied to a tiny formula produces an instance nobody would want to hand a solver. The discipline, in four steps. To prove a new problem B NP-complete: (1) show B ∈ NP by naming the certificate and the polynomial verifier — skip this and you have proved NP-hardness only, which is a different and weaker claim (§07); (2) choose a known NP-complete A that resembles B structurally; (3) give a map f from instances of A to instances of B and argue it runs in polynomial time — which almost always means "the output has polynomial size and each piece is computed locally"; (4) prove x ∈ A ⟹ f(x) ∈ B and f(x) ∈ B ⟹ x ∈ A, both directions, because the forward direction alone is satisfied by the reduction that maps everything to a fixed yes-instance. Step 4's second half is where real proofs fail, and it is the half this page writes out every time.

11 3-SAT → Clique

CLIQUE asks, given an undirected graph G and an integer k, whether G contains k mutually adjacent vertices; it is NP-complete, and the proof reduces 3-SAT to it by making one vertex per literal occurrence, joining two vertices exactly when they lie in different clauses and their literals are not complementary, and asking for a clique of size k = m, the number of clauses. The reduction is the cleanest in the catalogue because the two "if and only if" directions are almost the same sentence read twice: a clique of size m must take exactly one vertex from each clause (vertices in the same clause are never adjacent), and the literals it takes are pairwise non-complementary, so they can all be made true at once — which is precisely a satisfying assignment. This section carries out the construction on the page's formula φ, gets a 9-vertex, 21-edge graph, and verifies by brute force over all C(9,3) = 84 triples that its maximum clique is exactly 3.

Both directions of the proof

If φ is satisfiable then G has a clique of size m. Take a satisfying assignment a. Every clause has at least one literal made true by a; pick one such literal per clause and let S be the corresponding m vertices, one from each clause. Any two of them lie in different clauses, so the first edge condition holds. Any two of them are literals made true by the same assignment, so they cannot be complementary — a cannot make both xᵢ and ¬xᵢ true — so the second condition holds. Every pair is joined; S is a clique of size m.

If G has a clique of size m then φ is satisfiable. Let S be such a clique. No two vertices of S lie in the same clause, because same-clause vertices are never adjacent; since there are m clauses and |S| = m, S has exactly one vertex in each. Now define an assignment: for each vertex of S carrying literal l, set the underlying variable so that l is true. This is consistent — two vertices of S carrying complementary literals would not be adjacent, so no clique contains such a pair — and any variable not mentioned by S may be set arbitrarily. Under this assignment each clause has the literal at its chosen vertex true, so every clause is satisfied.

Notice what each half of the edge rule buys. The "different clauses" half makes the clique's size a counter: a clique of size m is forced to touch every clause. The "not complementary" half makes it a consistency check: the literals it touches can be simultaneously true. The reduction is exactly the statement that a satisfying assignment is a choice of one true literal per clause with no self-contradiction, and CLIQUE is the graph-shaped way of saying "choose one from each group, pairwise compatible".

Reading the assignments back out is the reverse map of the proof. Each of the 12 triples names three literals; setting their variables accordingly gives a partial assignment, and the free variables may be filled in arbitrarily. The 12 triples produce 9 distinct partial patterns — 11*, 110, 1*1, 101, 010, *10, 111, 001, *01, where * is a variable no chosen literal mentions — and completing the stars in every possible way yields exactly {001, 010, 101, 110, 111}, which is exactly the set of 5 satisfying assignments of φ found by exhaustive search over all 2³ = 8 assignments. The figure checks that identity at load and flags it. The correspondence is not a bijection in either direction — several cliques give the same assignment, and one clique with a star covers two — but the sets match, which is what the theorem claims.

What the reduction does on an unsatisfiable formula

Run the same construction on φ₈, the conjunction of all eight clauses over three variables, which is unsatisfiable. It has m = 8, so G has 24 vertices and k = 8. The candidate edges number C(8,2) × 9 = 252; the figure measures 204 edges, so 48 pairs were complementary. The maximum clique, computed by a Bron–Kerbosch search under a node counter, is 7 — one short of k = 8, so the reduction correctly reports "no". And 7 is not an accident: the largest number of clauses of φ₈ that any single assignment can satisfy is 7 (this is the instance §24 uses for MAX-3-SAT), and a clique of size j in this graph is precisely a consistent choice of one true literal from each of j clauses. The maximum clique of the reduced graph equals the maximum number of simultaneously satisfiable clauses — a stronger statement than the reduction needs, and one worth noticing because it is the seed of the approximation-preserving reductions in §20.

Three things the reduction is often misread as saying

  • It does not say CLIQUE is harder than 3-SAT. A ≤ₚ B says B is at least as hard as A, up to polynomial factors. Since 3-SAT is NP-complete and CLIQUE is in NP, the reduction plus membership makes them equivalent: each reduces to the other, and a polynomial algorithm for either gives one for both. All NP-complete problems are interreducible; the map of §10 is a tree only because that is the order the proofs were found in, not because the relation is asymmetric.
  • It does not give an efficient algorithm for either problem. The reduction is polynomial; the search on the far side is not. Reducing 3-SAT on n variables and m clauses to CLIQUE and then running a brute-force clique search costs C(3m, m) candidate subsets, which is about 6.75^m by Stirling — worse than the 2ⁿ of direct search for every m above roughly 0.36·n, which is to say for essentially every formula anyone cares about. Reductions relocate a problem; they do not shrink it.
  • The vertex count is 3m, not 2n. One vertex per literal occurrence. A formula may repeat a literal across clauses and each repetition gets its own vertex — x₁ in C₁ and x₁ in C₃ are two vertices, joined by an edge. Building one vertex per literal instead breaks the reduction, because then a clique could not pick one vertex per clause.
  • Maximum clique and maximum independent set are the same problem in complementary graphs, which is the content of §12 — and the reason CLIQUE is usually the first stop on the way to the whole vertex-cover family.
Complexity of the reduction itself. Building G takes O(m²) time and O(m²) space: 3m vertices and at most 9·C(m,2) edges, each decided by one literal comparison. Reading a satisfying assignment back out of a clique takes O(m). Both are comfortably polynomial in |⟨φ⟩|, which is what the definition of ≤ₚ in §06 requires — and the requirement is on the reduction, not on anything that happens afterwards. A construction that produced an exponentially large graph would not be a polynomial-time reduction even if it were otherwise correct; this is exactly the trap that §14's pseudo-polynomial discussion turns on.

12 Clique → Vertex Cover → Independent Set

For any graph G = (V, E) and any subset S ⊆ V, these three statements are equivalent: S is an independent set of G; V ∖ S is a vertex cover of G; S is a clique of the complement graph Ḡ. One combinatorial fact, three problems — and so three NP-completeness proofs for the price of one. Maximum independent set, minimum vertex cover and maximum clique are the same optimisation wearing different clothes: the answers are related by |max independent set| = |V| − |min vertex cover| and by complementation of the graph, and any algorithm for one solves the other two after O(V + E) or O(V²) of rewriting. This section proves the cover half of the equivalence — the clique half is the complementation identity already proved in §06 — uses it to get CLIQUE ≤ₚ VERTEX-COVER from §11, and works the trio on a 7-vertex graph whose optimum is confirmed by enumerating all 2⁷ = 128 subsets.

The proof written out

Forward. Suppose G has a clique S with |S| = k. Every pair inside S is an edge of G, hence a non-edge of Ḡ, so S is independent in Ḡ. Take any edge (u, v) of Ḡ. It cannot have both endpoints in S, so at least one endpoint lies in V ∖ S. Therefore V ∖ S covers every edge of Ḡ and has size |V| − k = k′.

Backward. Suppose Ḡ has a vertex cover C with |C| ≤ k′ = |V| − k. Let S = V ∖ C, so |S| ≥ k. If some pair u, v ∈ S were joined in Ḡ, that edge would have neither endpoint in C, contradicting the cover. So S is independent in Ḡ, hence a clique in G, of size at least k; discard vertices to get exactly k if you want the sizes to match. Both directions hold, the construction is polynomial, and since §11 made CLIQUE NP-hard and VERTEX-COVER is visibly in NP, VERTEX-COVER is NP-complete. The same two paragraphs with Ḡ replaced by G prove INDEPENDENT-SET NP-complete without touching the graph at all.

What "take the complement" costs

The complement is cheap in theory and expensive in practice, and the difference is worth stating precisely. A graph with V vertices and E edges has a complement with C(V,2) − E edges, so a sparse graph — E = O(V), the usual case — has a dense complement with Θ(V²) edges. The 7-vertex instance below has 8 edges and a complement with C(7,2) − 8 = 21 − 8 = 13. The clique graph of §11 has 9 vertices and 21 edges, and its complement has C(9,2) − 21 = 36 − 21 = 15; the figure measures both and its vertex-cover optimum on the complement is 6, which is 9 − 3, exactly as α + τ = |V| requires.

Two operational consequences. First, an adjacency-list representation, which is the right one for sparse graphs, is the wrong one on the far side of a complement: the reduction turns a linear-size input into a quadratic-size one, which is polynomial and therefore legal, but means "the same problem" now needs Θ(V²) memory. Second, this is why practitioners solve independent set and vertex cover directly on the sparse graph rather than complementing into a clique solver — the two problems are equivalent in complexity but wildly different in the constants, and a state-of-the-art clique solver on Ḡ will often lose to a mediocre vertex-cover solver on G. Equivalence up to polynomial factors is a statement about class membership, not about which code to write.

Why this trio is the workhorse of the catalogue

  • Vertex cover is the standard source for further reductions because "hit every edge with few vertices" is a shape that recurs: SET-COVER generalises it (an element per edge, a set per vertex — the greedy analysis is §23), DOMINATING-SET is its close cousin, and the HAM-CYCLE construction of §13 reduces from it rather than from 3-SAT because the "k chosen vertices" translate naturally into k selector vertices on a cycle.
  • Vertex cover is the standard example for two coping strategies at once. It has a clean 2-approximation from a maximal matching (§21) — and, unusually, that factor 2 is essentially the best known: under the unique games conjecture no polynomial (2 − ε)-approximation exists, and, assuming only P ≠ NP, no polynomial approximation with ratio below √2 ≈ 1.414 exists (by way of the 2-to-2 games theorem, which superseded the earlier threshold of about 1.36). It also has a textbook fixed-parameter algorithm running in O(1.2738ᵏ + k·n) time, which is polynomial in n for every fixed k (§27). The same problem is a poster child for "approximate it" and for "the parameter is small", and which route you take depends on which of k and the required accuracy is the binding constraint.
  • Independent set is much harder to approximate than vertex cover, even though the two are complementary. This is the standard warning that approximation ratios do not transfer across a reduction that preserves exact answers: a cover of size 2·τ gives an independent set of size |V| − 2τ, which may be far below α = |V| − τ and may even be zero. Unless P = NP, maximum independent set cannot be approximated within n^(1−ε) for any fixed ε > 0. Exact-equivalence and approximation-equivalence are different relations, and §20 introduces the stricter reductions that do preserve ratios.
  • On special graph classes the problems split apart. Minimum vertex cover is polynomial on bipartite graphs, where König's theorem makes it equal to the maximum matching and a max-flow computation solves it; maximum independent set is polynomial on bipartite graphs for the same reason, and on trees by a simple tree dynamic program. Maximum clique is polynomial on chordal and on perfect graphs. Hardness is a statement about the general problem; the first question to ask about a real instance is what class it lies in.
Optimisation versus decision, stated carefully here because it is easy to slip. The decision problems VERTEX-COVER, INDEPENDENT-SET and CLIQUE — each with a bound k in the input — are NP-complete. The optimisation problems "find a minimum vertex cover", "find a maximum independent set" and "find a maximum clique" are NP-hard, and are not NP-complete, because they are not decision problems and so are not members of NP at all: NP is a class of languages. Saying "minimum vertex cover is NP-complete" is loose shorthand that this page avoids. §03 shows the two versions are polynomial-time equivalent by binary search on k plus self-reducibility, so nothing is lost by proving hardness for the decision version — but the two labels are not interchangeable, and §07 gives the definitions they come from.

13 Hamiltonian cycle, and Hamiltonian cycle → TSP

A Hamiltonian cycle of an undirected graph is a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once; deciding whether one exists is NP-complete, and the travelling-salesman decision problem — "is there a tour of all cities of total weight at most B?" — inherits that hardness through a reduction so simple it fits in one line: put weight 1 on the edges of G, weight 2 on every other pair, and set B = |V|. The contrast with the Euler tour, which visits every edge exactly once and is decided in O(V + E) by counting odd-degree vertices, is the sharpest "same-looking pair, different cost" on this page: one local condition per vertex versus a global condition on the whole ordering. This section states the Hamiltonian-cycle reduction honestly — it comes from VERTEX-COVER, with a 12-vertex widget per edge whose three admissible traversals the figure verifies exhaustively — then does the TSP reduction in full, and finally sharpens the weights into the version that §22 uses to rule out any constant-factor approximation of general TSP.

Why Euler is easy and Hamilton is not

Both ask for a closed walk with a covering property, and they were even posed in the same century, so the difference in cost deserves a reason rather than a shrug. Euler's condition is local and additive: a closed walk enters and leaves each vertex the same number of times, so every vertex's degree is used up in pairs, so every degree must be even — and conversely, even degrees plus connectivity are enough, because Hierholzer's algorithm can always splice a new closed sub-walk into the current one at any vertex with unused edges. The property decomposes over vertices; checking it is V independent tests.

The Hamiltonian condition does not decompose. Whether a vertex can be visited exactly once depends on which of its neighbours the rest of the tour has already consumed, and there is no quantity per vertex whose values can be checked independently. Every attempt at a local criterion fails on some graph: the Petersen graph is 3-regular, 3-connected, vertex-transitive and has girth 5 — as homogeneous as a graph can be — and has no Hamiltonian cycle at all, while having a Hamiltonian path. It is exactly the kind of counterexample that a plausible degree-based criterion dies on. The sharpened weights, and why §22 needs them

Widen the gap and the argument becomes an inapproximability proof. Fix any constant ρ ≥ 1 and build the same complete graph with

Second, the conclusion is about constant-factor approximation of general (non-metric) TSP and is conditional on P ≠ NP. 14 Subset sum, partition, knapsack — and pseudo-polynomial time

SUBSET-SUM asks, given a set S of positive integers and a target T, whether some subset of S sums to exactly T; it is NP-complete, and the reduction from 3-SAT writes each number as a string of decimal digits with one column per variable and one column per clause, arranged so that no column can ever carry — which turns "does a subset sum to T?" into "is there a consistent choice of one polarity per variable that lights up every clause?". It is the most beautiful gadget in the catalogue and also the one that most repays care, because SUBSET-SUM has a dynamic program running in O(nT) time, and understanding exactly why that does not put it in P is the single most useful thing on this page. T is written in ⌈log₂(T + 1)⌉ bits, so O(nT) is exponential in the length of the input — pseudo-polynomial, not polynomial. Everything §02 said about encodings is cashed in here.

That is what PSEUDO-POLYNOMIAL means, and it is the whole of it. a polynomial-time algorithm must be polynomial in L. An O(nT) algorithm is polynomial in L only if T is bounded by a polynomial in L, i.e. only if the numbers are SMALL.

The figure makes this visible by sliding the target's magnitude and plotting two curves from the same measured cell counts: area against T, which is a straight line, and area against ⌈log₂(T + 1)⌉, the number of bits actually written down, which is a doubling staircase. One picture, two axes, and the whole of pseudo-polynomiality.

Three consequences follow, and each is a place where the distinction earns its keep:

  • SUBSET-SUM in unary is in P. Write the numbers as strings of tally marks and the input length becomes Θ(∑sᵢ + T), so Θ(nT) is polynomial in that length. The problem did not change; the encoding did. This is not a curiosity — it is the formal content of the phrase "the hardness lives in the magnitudes, not in the combinatorics", and it is exactly why §02 insists that a problem is a language over an alphabet and not an abstract question.
  • SUBSET-SUM is NP-complete but not strongly NP-complete. A problem is strongly NP-complete when it stays NP-complete even if all numbers in the input are bounded by a polynomial in the input length — equivalently, when no pseudo-polynomial algorithm can exist unless P = NP. SUBSET-SUM, PARTITION and 0-1 KNAPSACK are not strongly NP-complete: their O(nT) and O(nW) tables settle them whenever the numbers are small. 3-PARTITION and BIN-PACKING are strongly NP-complete, and general TSP is too, which is the deeper reason §13's gap construction works and why no analogue of the knapsack table exists for TSP.
  • Knapsack has an FPTAS precisely because it is only weakly NP-hard. Rounding the values divides them by a scale factor, which shrinks V — the axis the table is indexed by — and turns the pseudo-polynomial table into a genuinely polynomial one at the cost of a controlled error. §25 builds it. A strongly NP-hard optimisation problem with a polynomially bounded objective cannot have an FPTAS unless P = NP, and that is the same observation read backwards.

The Dynamic Programming page develops the same table as a design exercise — the state, the transition, the traceback, the rolling-row space optimisation — and its subset-DP section gives the other exponential route, O(2ⁿ·n) over subsets, which is better than O(nT) when the numbers are huge and the set is small. Neither is polynomial; they are exponential in different parameters, and choosing between them is the first and cheapest form of "coping" on the list in §19. There is also the meet-in-the-middle algorithm, which splits S in half, sorts the 2^(n/2) subset sums of each half and matches them, running in O(2^(n/2)·n) time and O(2^(n/2)) space — still exponential, but with the exponent halved, and enough to settle n ≈ 60 instances that both other methods choke on.

The sentence to remember, and the one to never write. Write: "SUBSET-SUM has an O(nT) dynamic program, which is pseudo-polynomial — polynomial in the numeric value of T but exponential in the number of bits T is written in, and therefore not a polynomial-time algorithm." Never write: "SUBSET-SUM is solvable in polynomial time by dynamic programming, so P = NP" — a claim that has been submitted in earnest more than once, and whose error is entirely contained in §02. The same caveat attaches to knapsack's O(nW), to the O(V·W) "pseudo-polynomial" scheduling tables, and to every algorithm whose running time mentions a number from the input rather than the length of the input.

15 Graph colouring: 3-colouring is hard, 2-colouring is easy

A proper k-colouring of a graph assigns one of k colours to each vertex so that no edge joins two vertices of the same colour; deciding whether a proper 2-colouring exists is exactly deciding bipartiteness and takes O(V + E), while deciding whether a proper 3-colouring exists is NP-complete. Nothing about the statement changes between k = 2 and k = 3. What changes is that a 2-colouring is forced: fix one vertex's colour and every other vertex in its component is determined, so a single traversal either succeeds or exhibits an odd cycle. With three colours nothing is forced — each vertex left un-coloured has two options after a neighbour is fixed — and the choices interact globally. The reduction from 3-SAT makes that interaction do arithmetic: a palette triangle names TRUE, FALSE and BASE, a two-vertex gadget per variable makes a literal and its negation take opposite truth colours, and a pair of OR-gadgets per clause forces at least one of its three literals to be TRUE.

Both directions again. Satisfiable ⟹ 3-colourable: colour the palette with the three colours, colour each literal vertex by the assignment (true literals get T's colour, false literals get F's colour) — legal because xᵢ and ¬xᵢ differ and neither is B-coloured — and then, clause by clause, use the lemma to give each OR-gadget's output the T colour, which is possible because each clause has a true literal; the internal p, q, r of each gadget can always be completed. 3-colourable ⟹ satisfiable: read the assignment off the literal vertices, which is consistent because xᵢ and ¬xᵢ are adjacent and both avoid B's colour. Each clause's r₂ is adjacent to both B and F, so it holds T's colour; by the contrapositive of the lemma its inputs are not both F-coloured, and following that back through the two gadgets gives at least one true literal in the clause.

The lemma is the only step with any content, and it is small enough to verify by hand as well as by machine. Suppose u and v are both F-coloured. Then p ≠ F and q ≠ F, and since {p, q, r} is a triangle it uses all three colours, so {p, q} = {T, B} and r = F: the output is forced false. Suppose instead u is T-coloured and v is F-coloured. Setting q = B (allowed: q ≠ F) and p = F (allowed: p ≠ T) leaves r = T: the output can be true, and taking p = B, q = T instead leaves r = F, so in the mixed case the output is free. The case u false, v true is the mirror image. And if both inputs are T-coloured then p ≠ T and q ≠ T, so the triangle leaves only T for r — the output is forced true, which is also harmless. The count 24 deserves a moment. There are 3! = 6 ways to permute the three colours, and every proper colouring's permutations are also proper, so the 24 colourings fall into 24/6 = 4 classes under colour renaming — the graph has four essentially different 3-colourings. Any count of proper colourings is divisible by k! divided by the symmetry of unused colours; here all three colours are used in every one of the 24, so the divisor is exactly 6. This is also why practical colouring solvers fix the colours of a clique at the start: it kills the symmetry and shrinks the search space by a factor of k! before the search begins.

Greedy colouring, and why it is not an approximation

The obvious heuristic: take the vertices in some order and give each one the smallest colour not already used by a coloured neighbour. It runs in O(V + E) and it never uses more than Δ + 1 colours, because when a vertex is reached at most Δ of its neighbours are coloured and one of the first Δ + 1 colours must be free. That is a genuine bound and it is worth having. What it is not is an approximation guarantee.

Greedy is not a ρ-approximation for any constant ρ. (the crown graph is also the standard example that greedy is order-dependent: the order u₁…u_n, w₁…w_n on the SAME graph uses 2 colours.)

The failure is not fixable by a cleverer ordering rule, and the reason is a theorem rather than a lack of imagination: unless P = NP, no polynomial-time algorithm approximates the chromatic number within n^(1−ε) for any fixed ε > 0. Colouring is one of the problems where the honest advice is that approximation does not help either, and the routes that remain are the exact-exponential ones of §26, the parameterised ones of §27 when the graph has small treewidth, and the solvers of §30. Good orderings — largest-degree-first, DSATUR, smallest-last — are still worth using, because they are cheap and they do well on the graphs that arise in practice; they simply carry no guarantee. This is the pattern Greedy Algorithms §27 describes in general: a greedy rule with no matroid or exchange argument behind it is a heuristic, and calling it an approximation requires a proof it does not have.

Where colouring comes from

  • Register allocation. A compiler builds an interference graph — a vertex per live value, an edge between two values that are live at the same time — and a proper k-colouring with k = the number of machine registers is exactly an assignment of values to registers with no two simultaneously-live values sharing one. The problem is NP-complete in general, so real allocators use Chaitin-style graph colouring with heuristic simplification and spilling: when no vertex of degree < k remains, some value is written to memory and removed from the graph. The spill is what "the problem is NP-complete" looks like in a production compiler — not failure, but a fallback that costs performance rather than correctness.
  • Scheduling and frequency assignment. Exams that share students, jobs that share a machine, radio transmitters whose ranges overlap — each is a conflict graph whose chromatic number is the number of time slots or frequencies needed. Timetabling systems solve these with the same mixture of heuristics, local search and integer programming that §30 describes.
  • Sudoku is a 9-colouring. The 81 cells are vertices, joined when they share a row, a column or a box; the givens are pre-coloured vertices. Generalised n²×n² Sudoku is NP-complete, which is a fact about the family, not about the 9×9 puzzles in a newspaper — those have 81 vertices and are solved instantly by constraint propagation.
  • Bipartiteness is checked far more often than colourability. Two-colouring is the test behind "is this graph a valid bipartite matching instance?", behind detecting odd cycles in consistency checks, and behind the parity arguments in Graph Representations & Traversal, §14, which gives the BFS version in full. The gap between §14 there and this section is the gap between k = 2 and k = 3, and it is the whole subject in miniature.

The pattern to take away. The jump from 2 to 3 is not about the number of colours; it is about whether the constraint propagates deterministically. With two colours, fixing one vertex determines its whole component, so the algorithm is a traversal. With three, each step leaves a free choice, choices interact across the whole graph, and the reduction of this section shows that those interactions are expressive enough to encode arbitrary Boolean satisfiability. The same pattern explains 2-SAT versus 3-SAT (§16), matching versus 3-dimensional matching, and unit propagation on Horn clauses (Greedy Algorithms, §24) versus general SAT. When you meet a new problem, the useful question is not "how big is the search space" — it is always exponential — but "does fixing one piece force the next?".
