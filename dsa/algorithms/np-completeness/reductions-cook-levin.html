06 Polynomial-time reductions: the tool that transfers hardness

A polynomial-time reduction from A to B is a single function f, computable in polynomial time, that turns every instance of A into an instance of B with the same answer — yes to yes and no to no — and its whole purpose is to move a fact about B onto A, or a fact about A onto B, depending on which way the fact points. Written A ≤ₚ B and read "A is no harder than B", it is the only technique this page uses to prove anything hard, and it is the technique that most first attempts get backwards. The arrow points from the problem you already know about to the problem you are asking about, and which of those is "already known" depends entirely on whether you are proving hardness or building an algorithm.

The direction trap, stated as loudly as it deserves

To prove that your problem B is hard, you must reduce a known-hard problem A to B. You take an arbitrary instance of 3-SAT and build from it an instance of your problem. You do not take an instance of your problem and build a formula from it; that proves B ≤ₚ 3-SAT, which says your problem is no harder than 3-SAT — true of every problem in NP and therefore worth nothing as a hardness proof. The mnemonic that survives contact with a whiteboard: the hard problem is the input, your problem is the output. Your reduction algorithm must be able to handle any 3-SAT formula, however large and however structured, and must emit an instance of your problem; it may not choose which formulas to accept.

Two further traps ride along with the first. The no-direction: having built a construction that turns satisfying assignments into cliques, it is tempting to declare victory, but the theorem also requires that a clique in the constructed graph can only have come from a satisfying assignment. Most broken reductions in the wild are broken exactly there — the gadget admits a spurious solution that corresponds to nothing. The size trap: a map may be perfectly correct and still not be a reduction, if computing it takes exponential time or produces an exponentially large output. Converting a CNF formula to an equivalent DNF formula is a correct transformation and not a polynomial reduction, because the output can be exponentially larger (§09); Tseitin's trick in §08 exists precisely to avoid that blow-up at the price of new variables.

A reduction in full, on every instance of a small family

The cleanest possible example, and the one the figure runs: INDEPENDENT-SET ≤ₚ CLIQUE. An independent set in G is a vertex set with no edge inside it; a clique is a vertex set with every edge inside it. Those are the same condition in complementary graphs, so define f(⟨G, k⟩) = ⟨Ḡ, k⟩, where Ḡ has the same vertices and exactly the non-edges of G. Computing Ḡ takes Θ(n²) — read every pair once — so f is polynomial-time. Correctness, both halves at once: a set S has no edge of G inside it precisely when every pair inside it is a non-edge of G, that is, an edge of Ḡ; so S is independent in G ⟺ S is a clique in Ḡ. Taking |S| ≥ k on both sides gives the ⟺ the definition demands, and because the equivalence is about the same set S, the reduction also converts a witness into a witness with no work at all.

The figure enumerates the entire family of graphs on four vertices — 2⁶ = 64 of them, one per subset of the six possible edges — decides "has an independent set of size 3" on each by brute force, decides "has a clique of size 3" on each image by brute force, and shades the two columns accordingly. 23 of the 64 graphs are yes-instances of the independent-set question and 41 are no-instances, and the map agrees on all 64: every yes maps to a yes and every no to a no. Switching the control to the bogus map f(G) = G — a map that is polynomial-time and utterly wrong — makes the picture disagree on 46 of the 64 instances, the very first of them being the empty graph, which has an independent set of size 3 (any three vertices) and no clique of size 3 at all. That is what a failed reduction looks like: not a subtle inefficiency, but pairs of instances whose answers disagree, and finding one such pair is how a reduction is refuted.

Many-one reductions against Turing reductions

The reduction defined above is a many-one or Karp reduction: one call, at the end, with no post-processing — the answer to f(x) is the answer to x. The more permissive notion is a Turing or Cook reduction: A ≤ᵀ B when a polynomial-time algorithm decides A given free access to an oracle for B, calling it as often as it likes and doing what it likes with the answers. Every many-one reduction is a Turing reduction. The converse fails in an important way, and the reason the theory standardises on many-one is exactly that failure: a Turing reduction may negate the oracle's answer, so L ≤ᵀ complement of L always holds, and under Turing reductions NP and co-NP would become indistinguishable, collapsing the distinction §17 is built on. Cook's original 1971 paper used Turing reductions; Karp's 1972 paper introduced the many-one restriction, and it is Karp's version that the word "reduction" means on this page unless stated otherwise.

Turing reductions are still the right tool in two places, and both appear later. The optimisation-to-decision binary search of §03 is a Turing reduction — it calls the oracle O(log V) times and post-processes — and so is the self-reduction that recovers a witness. And the statement "TSP-OPT is NP-hard" is a statement about Turing reducibility, since an optimisation problem cannot be the target of a many-one reduction at all: what a many-one reduction maps to is a yes/no question. So the precise version of the definition in §07 is: X is NP-hard if every L ∈ NP satisfies L ≤ₚ X for decision X, and L ≤ᵀ X in general. This page uses many-one whenever the target is a decision problem, which is everywhere except the optimisation statements.

What a reduction preserves, and what it destroys

A polynomial-time reduction preserves membership in P, membership in NP, and NP-hardness. It does not preserve running time beyond a polynomial — a reduction that squares the instance size turns an O(m²) algorithm for B into an O(n⁴) algorithm for A, which is fine for the theory and can be fatal in practice; this is why reducing your problem to SAT and calling a solver (§30) is an engineering decision with a real cost, not a free lunch. It does not preserve approximability either, which is the reason §20 needs its own machinery: a reduction that maps optimal solutions to optimal solutions may map near-optimal ones to arbitrarily bad ones, so a 2-approximation for B gives nothing for A unless the reduction was built to be approximation-preserving. And it does not preserve the parameter that §27's fixed-parameter algorithms exploit — the standard reductions blow the parameter up polynomially, which is exactly why parameterised complexity needs its own notion of reduction.

The checklist for a reduction you have just written. (1) Which problem is the input and which is the output — is the known-hard one the input? (2) Does the construction run in polynomial time, and is the output polynomially bounded? (3) Yes to yes: take an arbitrary solution of the input instance and exhibit the corresponding solution of the output instance. (4) No to no, in its contrapositive form: take an arbitrary solution of the output instance and show it must have come from a solution of the input. (5) Test it on a small family by brute force, both sides, and compare — which is exactly what the figure above does, and what every reduction figure in §11–§15 repeats.

07 NP-hard, NP-complete, and what P = NP would mean

A problem is NP-hard when everything in NP reduces to it — it is at least as hard as the whole class — and NP-complete when it is NP-hard and itself in NP, so that it is simultaneously a hardest member and an ordinary member. The second condition is the one that gets dropped in conversation, and dropping it changes the meaning: NP-hard problems need not be in NP, need not be decision problems, and need not be decidable at all. The NP-complete problems are the ones that sit exactly at the boundary, and because a reduction chain can only move hardness upward, they are all equivalent: any one of them in P puts all of them in P, and all of NP with them.

What P = NP would and would not mean

The headline is genuine: if P = NP by a practical algorithm, then finding is as easy as checking, everywhere. Every problem whose solutions can be recognised quickly could be solved quickly — optimal schedules, optimal circuit layouts, optimal protein foldings in their combinatorial idealisations, and, most strikingly, proofs: "does this statement have a proof of at most n symbols in this formal system?" is in NP, because a proof is a certificate a proof-checker verifies in linear time, so mathematics itself would be mechanised up to proof length. That last consequence is the strongest informal argument people offer for believing P ≠ NP.

The cryptographic consequence deserves stating exactly, because it is routinely overstated in both directions. Overstatement one: "P = NP would break all cryptography." Almost right, with two qualifications. Information-theoretically secure schemes — the one-time pad, Shamir secret sharing — are unaffected, because their security is a counting argument and not a computational assumption. And the break requires an efficient algorithm: a proof that P = NP by a non-constructive argument, or a constructive one running in n¹⁰⁰ with galactic constants (§04), would leave every deployed system running exactly as before, while demolishing the theoretical foundation under it. With those two caveats, the statement holds and holds broadly: given an efficient, practical algorithm, factoring, discrete logarithm and lattice problems — all with decision versions in NP — would fall, taking RSA, Diffie–Hellman, elliptic-curve cryptography and the post-quantum lattice schemes with them; and recovering a symmetric key from a known plaintext–ciphertext pair is an NP search problem too — the key is the certificate — so AES would fall as well. The second caveat cuts the same way for both kinds of scheme: RSA-2048 and AES-128 are each a single fixed-size instance, so an impractical polynomial, or a proof with no algorithm in it, leaves both exactly as secure in practice as before, while a practical algorithm breaks both. There is no version of P = NP that breaks the public-key schemes and spares the symmetric ones. Overstatement two: "P ≠ NP means cryptography is safe." False, and in a way worth internalising. P ≠ NP says some problem in NP has no polynomial algorithm on its worst-case instances. Cryptography needs problems that are hard on random instances drawn from a distribution you can sample, which is a strictly stronger requirement; one-way functions imply P ≠ NP, but nobody can prove the converse, and it is entirely consistent with current knowledge that P ≠ NP and no one-way functions exist. The average-case theory is a different theory, and the phase-transition results of §30 are the practical face of the same gap: random SAT instances away from the critical clause ratio are easy, and worst-case hardness says nothing about them.

The evidence, and the three barriers

Nobody has proved either side, so the belief that P ≠ NP rests on evidence of three kinds. Failure at scale: thousands of NP-complete problems, attacked independently for fifty years by people with every incentive to succeed, and not one polynomial algorithm. Structural coincidence: those thousands of problems arose separately — in logic, scheduling, graph theory, number theory, statistical physics, biology — and turned out to be a single problem in disguise, which is the kind of unexpected unification that usually indicates something real. The absence of a proof is itself explained: three theorems show that broad, natural families of proof technique cannot settle the question, which is why fifty years of effort have produced so little.

  • Relativisation (Baker, Gill and Solovay, 1975). Give every machine access to the same oracle A and ask the question again. There is an oracle A with P^A = NP^A and an oracle B with P^B ≠ NP^B. Any proof technique that still works when both sides are given an arbitrary oracle — which includes simulation and diagonalisation, the techniques behind the hierarchy theorems of §18 — therefore cannot decide P versus NP, because it would have to prove both a statement and its negation.
  • Natural proofs (Razborov and Rudich, 1994). Most known lower-bound arguments for circuits are "natural": they exhibit a property of Boolean functions that is easy to check, is shared by most functions, and is not shared by any function with small circuits. Razborov and Rudich showed that a natural proof of a super-polynomial circuit lower bound would itself break the pseudo-random generators that cryptography is built on — so if strong one-way functions exist, no natural proof can separate the classes.
  • Algebrisation (Aaronson and Wigderson, 2008). Interactive-proof results such as IP = PSPACE do not relativise, and for a while looked like the technique that might break through; algebrisation is the extension of relativisation that captures them, and it too is shown to be insufficient for P versus NP.

Two smaller facts complete the picture. First, an exact consequence of a collapse that is easy to state and easy to misstate: if P = NP, then every language in P except the two trivial ones — the empty language and the set of all strings — is NP-complete. The reason is that with P = NP a reduction from any L' ∈ NP to such an L is trivial: decide x ∈ L' in polynomial time and output a fixed string known to be in L, or a fixed string known not to be, which is possible exactly when L has at least one of each. The two trivial languages are excluded because they have no such pair of witnesses, and they are the reason the statement carries an exception at all. Second, the question carries a Clay Millennium Prize, informal polls of the research community have consistently found a large majority expecting P ≠ NP, and the minority view is not that P = NP is likely but that the question may be independent of the standard axioms — a possibility nobody has been able to rule out either.

Proving NP-completeness in practice: the four-step recipe

Every completeness proof from §09 onward has the same four steps, and it is worth having them in one place.

  • 1. Show X ∈ NP. Name the certificate, name the verifier, argue it runs in polynomial time, and check soundness as well as completeness (§05). This step is usually three lines and is usually the one omitted, which is how "NP-complete" gets attached to problems that are merely NP-hard.
  • 2. Choose the known-hard problem A. Pick the catalogue entry whose structure resembles yours: 3-SAT when your problem has independent binary choices with local constraints; clique or independent set when it is about mutually compatible selections; Hamiltonian cycle when it is about ordering or routing; subset sum or partition when numbers are involved; 3-colouring when it is about conflicting labels. §10's map exists to make this choice fast.
  • 3. Build f and argue it is polynomial. Describe the construction locally — per variable, per clause, per vertex — and count: how many vertices, edges, numbers or clauses does one input symbol generate?
  • 4. Prove x ∈ A ⟺ f(x) ∈ X, both directions. Forward: convert a solution of x into a solution of f(x), explicitly. Backward: take an arbitrary solution of f(x) and show it induces a solution of x. The backward direction is where gadgets earn their keep, because it is where you must rule out solutions the construction did not intend.

And the standard hedge, which this page uses without further comment from here on: results of the form "no polynomial-time algorithm for X" are always stated as "unless P = NP", and results of the form "no (1 + ε)-approximation" as "unless P = NP" too. That is not timidity; it is the exact strength of what is proved. The unconditional statement — that no polynomial algorithm exists — has never been proved for any problem in NP, and proving it for one would prove it for all.

Three sentences to keep straight. "X is in NP" — yes-instances have short checkable certificates; says nothing about hardness. "X is NP-hard" — everything in NP reduces to X; X may be an optimisation problem, may be undecidable, may lie far above NP. "X is NP-complete" — both of the above; X is a decision problem in NP and a hardest one. When a source says "the travelling salesman problem is NP-complete" it means the decision version; when it says "TSP is NP-hard" it means either version, and only the second phrasing is safe without further qualification.

08 Circuit satisfiability and the Cook–Levin theorem

Reductions can only move hardness from one problem to another, so the catalogue needs a root: one problem proved hard directly, from the definition of NP rather than from another problem — and the proof is that a polynomial-time verifier is a machine, its computation on an input of size n is a rectangular table of poly(n) cells, and the question "is there a certificate that makes this table end in acceptance?" is a Boolean satisfiability question about the cells. That is the Cook–Levin theorem, proved independently by Cook in 1971 and Levin in 1973, and it is the only theorem on this page whose proof reaches all the way down to the machine. Everything else is a gadget.

Step (b): the tableau, in full

Let L ∈ NP. By the certificate definition (§05) there is a verifier V and constants such that x ∈ L iff some certificate y with |y| = O(nᵏ) makes V(x, y) accept within T(n) = O(nᵏ) steps. Model V as a single-tape machine — the model costs at most a polynomial (§04) — with state set Q, tape alphabet Γ, and a transition function δ. The reduction must produce, from x alone and in polynomial time, an object that is satisfiable exactly when such a y exists.

The object is the tableau: a table whose i-th row is the machine's configuration after i steps, written as the tape contents with the current state symbol inserted immediately to the left of the head's cell, and a delimiter # at each end. Two facts bound its size. The machine runs for at most T(n) steps, so the start row plus one row per step — T(n) + 1 rows — suffice; and in T(n) steps the head can visit at most T(n) + 1 tape cells, so those cells plus the inserted state symbol and the two delimiters — T(n) + 4 columns — suffice. The table is therefore (T(n) + 1) × (T(n) + 4), which is O(T(n)²) cells; textbooks usually tidy this to a square T × T by assuming the machine halts within T(n) − 3 steps, and nothing below depends on the exact shape. Every cell holds one symbol from the finite set C = Q ∪ Γ ∪ {#}, whose size is a constant depending on the machine and not on n.

the variablesfor each row i, column j, and symbol s ∈ C: a Boolean variable x[i, j, s] with intended meaning "cell (i, j) contains s" count: |C| · T(n)² = O(n^2k) variables — POLYNOMIAL in n, which is the whole reason this works. the four clause families — φ = φcell ∧ φstart ∧ φaccept ∧ φmoveφcell "every cell holds exactly one symbol" ⋀ᵢⱼ [ ( ⋁ₛ∈C x[i,j,s] ) ∧ ⋀ₛ≠ₜ ( ¬x[i,j,s] ∨ ¬x[i,j,t] ) ] at least one, and no two size: T² clauses of width |C|, plus T²·C(|C|,2) clauses of width 2 = O(n^2k) φstart "row 0 is the start configuration on input x, with the certificate cells FREE" x[0,0,#] ∧ x[0,1,q₀] ∧ x[0,2,x₁] ∧ … ∧ x[0,n+1,xₙ] ∧ ( nothing at all about the y cells ) ∧ x[0,·,␣] … ∧ x[0,T−1,#] size: one unit clause per FIXED cell = O(n^k). Leaving the y cells unconstrained is exactly how "∃ certificate" becomes "∃ satisfying assignment" — the y cells ARE the free choice. φaccept "the machine accepts somewhere in the tableau" ⋁ᵢⱼ x[i, j, q_accept] one clause of width O(n^2k) φmove "every row follows legally from the row above it" stated LOCALLY, as a condition on every 2 × 3 window of adjacent cells: ⋀ over window positions (i, j): "the window at (i, j) is legal" a window is LEGAL iff it can occur in some valid pair of consecutive configurations. Two equivalent CNF renderings: (1) ⋁ over legal 6-tuples (a₁…a₆) of ( x[i,j−1,a₁] ∧ … ∧ x[i+1,j+1,a₆] ) — a DNF per window, needs distributing (2) ⋀ over ILLEGAL 6-tuples of ( ¬x[i,j−1,a₁] ∨ … ∨ ¬x[i+1,j+1,a₆] ) — already CNF, 6 literals per clause either way the number of clauses per window is a CONSTANT (at most |C|⁶) depending only on the machine, and there are (T−1)(T−2) windows, so φmove has O(n^2k) clauses.

Why locality is enough — the one claim that carries the proof

The whole construction turns on a claim that looks too strong: if row 0 is the start configuration and every 2 × 3 window is legal, then every row is the configuration that legally follows the row above it. The reason it holds is that a machine step is itself local. Away from the head, a cell's symbol is simply copied down, and a window entirely away from the head sees three symbols above and the same three below — legal. At the head, the transition function determines what the three cells around the head become, and any 2 × 3 window that straddles the head position sees enough of the neighbourhood to certify that what happened is what δ prescribes. Since a configuration row contains exactly one state symbol — which φcell and φstart together force, and which the legal windows preserve — there is exactly one place in each row where anything non-trivial can happen, and the windows covering it pin it down. The 3 in "2 × 3" is chosen to be wide enough to see a head moving one cell in either direction; a 2 × 2 window is too narrow to distinguish some illegal head motions.

On x = 00 the accepting certificates are exactly those containing a 1, so the constructed formula ought to be satisfiable — and the tableau for y = 10 reaches the accepting state in three steps while the tableau for y = 00 never does. Polynomial-size, enormous constant. No one ever runs this construction; it exists to be a theorem.

Correctness of the reduction, and why it is polynomial

Forward. If x ∈ L, take an accepting certificate y, run V(x, y), and read the tableau off the run; setting x[i,j,s] = TRUE exactly for the symbol actually in each cell satisfies all four families by construction. Backward. If φ is satisfiable, take a satisfying assignment. φcell makes it a well-defined table of symbols; φstart makes row 0 the start configuration on x, with some bits in the certificate cells — call them y; φmove plus the locality claim makes every subsequent row the genuine next configuration, so the table is the actual run of V(x, y); and φaccept says that run accepts. Hence a certificate exists and x ∈ L. Polynomial time. The reduction algorithm never simulates anything: it emits O(n^2k) variables and O(n^2k) clauses by a mechanical loop over (i, j), using only n, the constants of V, and the bits of x. Writing that output is the entire cost.

Step (c): a circuit into CNF, without the exponential blow-up

Compiling a circuit into a formula naively — substitute each gate's definition into its consumers, recursively — duplicates a shared sub-circuit once per consumer, and a chain of k fan-out-2 gates produces a formula of size 2^k. That is a correct transformation and not a polynomial reduction (§06's size trap). Tseitin's encoding avoids it by refusing to eliminate anything: introduce a fresh variable for every gate's output wire and write down, for each gate, the constant number of clauses asserting that the new variable equals the gate's function of its inputs. Nothing is duplicated, so the output is linear in the circuit size.

What the theorem actually established, historically and logically

  • It created the field. Before 1971 there was no way to say "this problem is hard" beyond "nobody has solved it". Cook's paper — and Levin's, written independently and published in 1973 in a different research tradition — gave the notion of completeness and the first complete problem. Karp's 1972 paper then listed twenty-one NP-complete problems — satisfiability and twenty more reached from it by reduction — and by doing so demonstrated that the phenomenon was not a curiosity of logic but a property of combinatorics, graph theory, scheduling and number theory alike.
  • It is the only proof on this page that mentions a machine. Every subsequent hardness proof reduces from a problem already known complete, so the machine model never reappears. That is the division of labour the theorem buys: one proof pays the cost of talking about computation in general, and every later proof talks only about gadgets.
  • It is robust to the machine model. Single-tape, multi-tape, random-access: each is simulated by the others with polynomial overhead (§04), and the tableau construction goes through for each with a different, still constant, window size. Nothing in the theorem depends on the choice.
  • Circuits are the more natural root. The tableau construction is really building a circuit — one copy of the "one step of the machine" circuit per time step, wired in series, with the certificate bits left as free inputs. Stating the root as CIRCUIT-SAT makes that explicit and makes step (c) a separate, clean, linear-size compilation. It also connects directly to circuit complexity, where the hope of proving P ≠ NP by exhibiting a problem needing super-polynomial circuits has been pursued (and blocked by the natural-proofs barrier) for forty years.
  • Practical descendants. Tseitin's encoding is not a museum piece: it is how every modern toolchain hands a hardware or software verification query to a SAT solver (§30). Bounded model checking unrolls a transition relation for k steps and Tseitin-encodes the result — which is the Cook–Levin tableau, built deliberately, and then solved.
The one-sentence version. A polynomial-time verifier is a finite machine whose run on a fixed input is a polynomial-size grid of symbols; the grid is legal exactly when every little window in it is legal; "legal window" is a finite condition; so "does a certificate exist?" is "is this polynomial-size Boolean formula satisfiable?" — and therefore satisfiability is at least as hard as everything in NP.

09 SAT, CNF-SAT and 3-SAT

3-SAT is satisfiability restricted to formulas that are a conjunction of clauses of at most three literals each, and it is NP-complete — the restriction throws away none of the difficulty, because any CNF formula can be rewritten, in linear time, as a 3-CNF formula that is satisfiable exactly when the original is. That rewriting is one gadget, clause by clause, and it is the first gadget on the page: long clauses are cut into a chain with fresh variables acting as carry bits, short clauses are padded with fresh variables that cancel out. The payoff is that every reduction from §11 onward starts from a formula with a fixed, tiny clause shape, which is what makes the gadgets drawable.

The gadget: cutting a long clause into a chain

Take a clause C = (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ … ∨ ℓₖ) with k ≥ 4. Introduce k − 3 fresh variables y₁, …, y_{k−3}, used nowhere else in the formula, and replace C by the chain

the splitting gadget, for k ≥ 4(ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ y₁) ∧ (¬y₁ ∨ ℓ₃ ∨ y₂) ∧ (¬y₂ ∨ ℓ₄ ∨ y₃) ∧ … ∧ (¬y_{k−4} ∨ ℓ_{k−2} ∨ y_{k−3}) ∧ (¬y_{k−3} ∨ ℓ_{k−1} ∨ ℓ_k) k − 2 clauses, k − 3 fresh variables, every clause of width exactly 3 and for the short clauses, padding with fresh variables that cancel: width 2 (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂) → (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ p) ∧ (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ ¬p) 2 clauses, 1 fresh var width 1 (ℓ₁) → (ℓ₁ ∨ p ∨ q) ∧ (ℓ₁ ∨ p ∨ ¬q) ∧ (ℓ₁ ∨ ¬p ∨ q) ∧ (ℓ₁ ∨ ¬p ∨ ¬q) 4 clauses, 2 fresh vars width 3 unchanged 1 clause, 0 fresh vars

Why it preserves satisfiability, forward. Suppose an assignment satisfies C, so some ℓᵢ is true. Set the carries to y₁ = … = y_{i−2} = TRUE and the rest FALSE. Every clause before the one containing ℓᵢ is satisfied by its positive carry y_j; the clause containing ℓᵢ is satisfied by ℓᵢ itself; every clause after it is satisfied by ¬y_{j−1}, whose carry is false. (The two boundary cases work out the same way: if i ∈ {1, 2} set every y false, and if i ∈ {k−1, k} set every y true.) Why it preserves satisfiability, backward — the half that matters. Suppose every ℓᵢ is false and ask whether any setting of the carries can satisfy the chain. The first clause becomes (F ∨ F ∨ y₁), forcing y₁ = TRUE. The second becomes (¬y₁ ∨ F ∨ y₂) = (F ∨ F ∨ y₂), forcing y₂ = TRUE. Inductively every carry is forced true, until the last clause becomes (¬y_{k−3} ∨ F ∨ F) = (F ∨ F ∨ F) — unsatisfiable. So the chain is satisfiable only when some original literal is true. The fresh variables can absorb a true literal somewhere but cannot manufacture one; that asymmetry is the whole gadget, and it is exactly the "no maps to no" obligation of §06.

The padding gadgets are the same argument in miniature. (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ p) ∧ (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ ¬p): whichever value p takes, one of the two clauses loses its third literal and demands ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂. The width-1 case uses all four combinations of two fresh variables, so no choice of p, q escapes a clause in which both are false. And the new variables are local — a distinct pair per clause — so the gadgets cannot interfere with one another, which is what lets the argument be made clause by clause and then conjoined.

Equisatisfiable is not equivalent, and the size accounting

The output formula is not logically equivalent to the input: it has more variables, so it is a function on a bigger space, and it is not even true that every satisfying assignment of the original extends to the new one uniquely — the carries have slack, and a long clause with several true literals has several valid carry settings. What is preserved is exactly what §06 requires: satisfiable ⟺ satisfiable, and more usefully, the set of assignments to the original variables that satisfy the original formula equals the set of restrictions to those variables of the assignments satisfying the new one. The figure checks that second, stronger statement by brute force on both formulas.

Sizes, for a CNF with n variables, m clauses and L total literals: a clause of width k ≥ 4 contributes k − 2 clauses and k − 3 variables, width 3 contributes 1 and 0, width 2 contributes 2 and 1, width 1 contributes 4 and 2. Summing, the output has at most 4m + L clauses and n + 2m + L variables — linear in the input either way, so the map is computable in linear time and is a legitimate polynomial-time reduction. Why DNF-SAT being easy is not a contradiction

A DNF formula is a disjunction of terms, each term a conjunction of literals. It is satisfiable iff some term is satisfiable, and a single conjunction of literals is satisfiable iff it contains no variable together with its negation. So DNF-SAT is decided by one scan: for each term, check for a complementary pair; accept if any term has none. That is linear time, and the problem is in P.

The instinct that something must be wrong — "but every formula can be put in DNF, so convert the CNF and solve it" — is defused by §06's size trap. The conversion is correct and exponential. Distribute (x₁ ∨ y₁) ∧ (x₂ ∨ y₂) ∧ … ∧ (xₖ ∨ yₖ) and you get every way of choosing one literal from each clause: 2ᵏ terms, and no smaller DNF for this function exists, since the function has 2ᵏ minimal satisfying patterns. At k = 20 that is 1 048 576 terms from a formula of 40 literals. The hardness has not vanished; it has moved into the translation. The mirror image confirms the diagnosis: tautology checking — is this formula true under every assignment? — is trivial for CNF (check each clause for a complementary pair) and co-NP-complete for DNF. Each normal form makes one question easy and the dual question hard, and the two hard questions are hard in dual classes (§17).

Where the width boundary sits

  • 2-SAT is in P, in linear time. A clause (a ∨ b) is the pair of implications ¬a ⇒ b and ¬b ⇒ a, so a 2-CNF is a directed implication graph on the 2n literals, and the formula is unsatisfiable exactly when some variable x has x and ¬x in the same strongly connected component. Tarjan's algorithm from Graph Representations & Traversal, §22 settles it in O(n + m), and a satisfying assignment is read off the reverse topological order of the components. Greedy Algorithms, §24 makes the same point from the other side: two-literal clauses and Horn clauses are the two largest natural fragments on the easy side of the line. §16 draws the boundary in full.
  • 3-SAT is NP-complete, and stays complete under severe restrictions. Exactly three distinct literals per clause; each variable occurring at most three times, provided clauses of two and three literals are both allowed (impose both of the first two restrictions at once and every instance is satisfiable, by a Hall's-theorem matching of clauses to variables, so that particular combination is trivial); the formula's variable–clause incidence graph planar; monotone clauses (all-positive or all-negative) in the NAE variant — all still complete. That robustness is why 3-SAT is the workhorse source problem for the whole catalogue.
  • The optimisation versions cross the line earlier. MAX-2-SAT — satisfy as many 2-clauses as possible — is NP-hard even though deciding whether all of them can be satisfied is in P. Being able to answer "all or not all" in linear time tells you nothing about "how many", and §24 measures exactly that gap for MAX-3-SAT, where the best assignment on the eight-clause instance φ₈ satisfies 7 of 8 while the formula as a whole is unsatisfiable.
  • Practice runs the other way round. Modern solvers take CNF as their input format precisely because the Tseitin encoding of §08 puts everything into CNF cheaply, and they handle clauses of any width natively; the conversion to width 3 is a theoretical convenience for building gadgets, not something a solver wants (§30). A real toolchain encodes to CNF and stops.
