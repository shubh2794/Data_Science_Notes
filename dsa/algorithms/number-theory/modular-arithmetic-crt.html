09 Modular arithmetic: the groups Zₙ and Zₙ*

Arithmetic mod n works on the n residue classes {0, 1, …, n − 1}, and addition and multiplication are well defined on them: reducing before or after an operation gives the same class. Under addition the classes form the group Zₙ. Under multiplication, only the classes coprime to n have inverses, and they form the group Zₙ*, of size φ(n). These two groups are the arena for everything that follows. Their sizes, and the sizes of their subgroups, are what make Fermat's theorem, Miller–Rabin and RSA work.

Closure of Zₙ* is property (5) of §04: a product of two numbers coprime to n is coprime to n. Inverses exist by Bézout, as §11 makes concrete. The elements left out, the non-units that share a factor with n, can never be multiplied to 1. If d = gcd(a, n) > 1, every multiple a·x mod n is itself a multiple of d. This is the one place where modular arithmetic differs from ordinary arithmetic, and algorithms that divide mod n must check it. A useful by-product is that finding a non-unit factors n: gcd(a, n) is then a non-trivial divisor. Pollard's rho (§22) is a method for stumbling on one.

10 Euler's φ: how many residues are invertible

φ(n) counts the integers in [1, n] coprime to n, which is the size of Zₙ*. It is easy to compute from the factorisation of n and, as far as anyone knows, hard to compute without it. RSA depends on exactly that asymmetry.

the formulaφ(p) = p − 1 p prime φ(pᵏ) = pᵏ − pᵏ⁻¹ = pᵏ⁻¹(p − 1) remove the pᵏ⁻¹ multiples of p φ(m·n) = φ(m)·φ(n) when gcd(m, n) = 1 MULTIPLICATIVE — a corollary of the CRT (§14): Zₘₙ* ≅ Zₘ* × Zₙ* ⟹ φ(n) = n · ∏ over primes p | n of (1 − 1/p) φ(15) = 15·(2/3)·(4/5) = 8 φ(36) = 36·(1/2)·(2/3) = 12 φ(p·q) = (p − 1)(q − 1) two facts about itΣ over d | n of φ(d) = n e.g. n = 12: φ(1)+φ(2)+φ(3)+φ(4)+φ(6)+φ(12) = 1+1+2+2+2+4 = 12 (checked for every n ≤ 300) φ(n) > n / (e^γ · ln ln n + 3/ln ln n) for n ≥ 3 — never much smaller than n: a random residue is a unit with good probability

For an RSA modulus N = pq, knowing φ(N) is as good as knowing the factors. From φ(N) = N − (p + q) + 1 you get p + q, and with pq = N, p and q are the roots of z² − (p + q)z + N. Computing φ for an RSA modulus is therefore exactly as hard as factoring it. This is the first of several places where "compute a group's size" and "factor the modulus" turn out to be the same problem.

11 Modular inverses and linear congruences

a has an inverse mod n if and only if gcd(a, n) = 1, and extended Euclid computes it in O(β²) bit operations. More generally, a·x ≡ b (mod n) has a solution exactly when d = gcd(a, n) divides b, and then it has exactly d solutions mod n, spaced n/d apart.

the inversegcd(a, n) = 1 ⟹ extended Euclid gives a·x + n·y = 1 ⟹ a·x ≡ 1 (mod n): a⁻¹ = x mod n gcd(a, n) = d > 1 ⟹ a·x mod n is always a multiple of d, never 1: no inverse cost O(β²) bit operations (§06). alternative for prime p: a⁻¹ = aᵖ⁻² mod p (Fermat, §12) — O(β³), but branch-free 7⁻¹ mod 40: 40 = 5·7 + 5, 7 = 1·5 + 2, 5 = 2·2 + 1 ⟹ 1 = 3·40 − 17·7 ⟹ 7⁻¹ ≡ −17 ≡ 23 (7·23 = 161 = 4·40 + 1) solving a·x ≡ b (mod n)d = gcd(a, n), from extended Euclid a·x' + n·y' = d. if d ∤ b: NO solution (a·x − b is then never a multiple of n, since d divides a·x and n but not b) if d | b: x₀ = x'·(b/d) mod n is a solution, and ALL solutions mod n are xᵢ = x₀ + i·(n/d) for i = 0, 1, …, d − 1 — exactly d of them (checked against brute force for every n ≤ 60) 6x ≡ 4 (mod 10): d = 2 | 4, 6·2 + 10·(−1) = 2 ⟹ x₀ = 2·2 = 4, solutions 4, 9 (6·4 = 24 ≡ 4, 6·9 = 54 ≡ 4)

Why exactly d solutions: divide everything by d. The congruence becomes (a/d)·x ≡ b/d (mod n/d) with a/d now coprime to n/d, so it has exactly one solution mod n/d. That one class splits into d classes mod n. When n is prime every non-zero a is invertible and the congruence behaves exactly like a linear equation over the rationals. That is why so much cryptography, coding theory and hashing works mod a prime: Zₚ is a field, and algebra works in it without exceptions.

Inverses in bulk. To invert k residues mod the same n, one extended Euclid call suffices. Form the prefix products a₁, a₁a₂, …, a₁⋯aₖ, invert the last, and walk backwards: aₖ⁻¹ = (a₁⋯aₖ)⁻¹ · (a₁⋯aₖ₋₁), and so on. That is 3(k − 1) multiplications and one inversion instead of k inversions. Elliptic-curve libraries use this trick, often called batch inversion, to normalise many points at once.

12 Powers of an element: Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, orders, primitive roots

Take a unit a and list a, a², a³, … mod n. The list must repeat, and the first power that returns to 1 is the order of a. The powers of a form a subgroup of Zₙ*, so by Lagrange the order divides |Zₙ*| = φ(n), and therefore a^φ(n) ≡ 1. That is Euler's theorem, and Fermat's little theorem is the case n prime.

Discrete logarithms. Given a primitive root g mod a prime p and a residue y, the exponent x with gˣ ≡ y is the discrete logarithm. Computing gˣ takes O(β) multiplications (§13). Going back is believed hard: the best general methods are sub-exponential for Zₚ* and fully exponential, O(√p), for well-chosen elliptic-curve groups. Diffie–Hellman key exchange, DSA and elliptic-curve signatures rest on that one-way gap, as RSA rests on factoring.

13 Modular exponentiation by repeated squaring

To compute aᵇ mod n, read b in binary. Square once per bit, multiply once per 1-bit, and reduce mod n after every operation. That is at most 2⌊log₂ b⌋ + 1 modular multiplications, O(β) for a β-bit exponent, and every intermediate value stays below n². Without the binary trick, b − 1 multiplications for a 2048-bit b is 2²⁰⁴⁸ of them. Without reducing along the way, the number aᵇ has about b·log₂ a bits, more bits than there are atoms in the universe to store them.

left-to-right (scan the bits from the top)b = (b_{β−1} … b₁ b₀)₂ with b_{β−1} = 1 r ← a mod n for i = β − 2 down to 0: r ← r² mod n SQUARE: doubles the exponent built so far if bᵢ = 1: r ← r·a mod n MULTIPLY: adds 1 to it INVARIANT after processing bit i, r = a^(b >> i) mod n (the exponent is the prefix of b read so far) COUNT ⌊log₂ b⌋ squarings + (popcount(b) − 1) multiplications right-to-left (scan the bits from the bottom)r ← 1, s ← a mod n while b > 0: if b is odd: r ← r·s mod n MULTIPLY in the current power a^(2ⁱ) b ← ⌊b/2⌋; if b > 0: s ← s² mod n INVARIANT r · s^b ≡ a^(original b) (mod n) at the top of every iteration COUNT ⌊log₂ b⌋ squarings + popcount(b) multiplications (the first multiply is by r = 1) the costmultiplications: between ⌊log₂ b⌋ + 1 and 2⌊log₂ b⌋ + 1; ≈ 1.5·log₂ b for a random b (checked on 300 random exponents) bit operations with β-bit a, b, n and schoolbook arithmetic: O(β) · O(β²) = O(β³) naive: b − 1 multiplications = Θ(2^β) — exponential in the exponent's size

The two orders do the same number of squarings and differ by one multiplication. They differ in memory access pattern and in what can be precomputed. Left-to-right always multiplies by the same a, so it extends naturally to windowed exponentiation. Precompute a, a³, a⁵, …, a^(2ʷ−1) and consume w bits per multiplication, which cuts the multiplication count to about β/(w + 1) plus the table. With w = 5 at 2048 bits, total cost drops by about a fifth. Right-to-left computes the powers a^(2ⁱ) independently of b, so they can be precomputed when the base is fixed and the exponent varies, as in Diffie–Hellman with a fixed generator.

Two engineering notes. (1) The branch "if the bit is 1, multiply" makes the running time and the memory-access pattern depend on the secret exponent. A timing or power trace then reads the exponent bit by bit. Cryptographic code uses a constant-time schedule, the Montgomery ladder, which does one squaring and one multiplication on every bit whatever its value. It also uses constant-time table lookups for windowed methods. (2) The reduction "mod n" after each product is itself a division unless it is done in Montgomery form, where residues are stored as a·R mod n for R = 2^(word·k) and each reduction is shifts and one multiplication. Every RSA and Diffie–Hellman implementation in wide use combines the two.

14 The Chinese remainder theorem

If n = n₁·n₂·…·nₖ with the nᵢ pairwise coprime, then knowing x mod n is the same as knowing all the remainders x mod nᵢ. The map x ↦ (x mod n₁, …, x mod nₖ) is a bijection from Zₙ onto Zₙ₁ × … × Zₙₖ that respects + and ·. So arithmetic mod a big n can be done as k independent computations mod small nᵢ, and the answer recombined.

the theoremn₁, …, nₖ pairwise coprime, n = n₁·n₂·…·nₖ. The map x ↦ (x mod n₁, x mod n₂, …, x mod nₖ) Zₙ → Zₙ₁ × Zₙ₂ × … × Zₙₖ is a BIJECTION, and it is a ring isomorphism: sums and products can be computed component-wise. 15 What the CRT is used for

The CRT splits one problem mod n into independent problems mod its coprime factors. That turns big-number arithmetic into small-number arithmetic, turns proofs about composite moduli into proofs about prime powers, and makes RSA decryption about four times faster.

  • RSA-CRT decryption. The key holder knows p and q, so instead of c^d mod N it computes m_p = c^(d mod (p−1)) mod p and m_q = c^(d mod (q−1)) mod q and recombines them: m = m_q + q·((q⁻¹ mod p)·(m_p − m_q) mod p). This mixed-radix form of the reconstruction needs one precomputed inverse. Each half-size exponentiation has half as many bits in the exponent and a modulus half as wide. With schoolbook arithmetic each multiplication costs a quarter as much, so each half costs about ⅛ of the full exponentiation, and the two together about ¼. That gives the roughly 4× speed-up every RSA library uses. The toy RSA figure of §19 counts both.
  • Multi-modular (residue number) arithmetic. To compute an integer result whose size you can bound (a determinant, a resultant, the exact solution of an integer linear system), compute it mod many word-sized primes, independently and in parallel, and reconstruct with the CRT. No intermediate number is ever larger than a word. Exact linear algebra in computer algebra systems works this way, and so do fast multiplication by number-theoretic transform, which multiplies mod three or so NTT-friendly primes and recombines, and residue-number-system hardware for DSP.
  • Structure theorems. Zₙ* ≅ Z*_{p₁^e₁} × … × Z*_{pₖ^eₖ}, so φ is multiplicative (§10). An element's order is the lcm of its orders in the factors. x² ≡ 1 has 2ᵏ solutions mod a product of k distinct odd primes, one choice of ±1 per factor. That count is where Miller–Rabin's non-trivial square roots of 1 come from (§17), and it makes square roots mod N as hard as factoring N.
  • Scheduling and cycles. Two periodic events with coprime periods m and n coincide exactly once every mn steps. The CRT grid's walk is this statement. Calendars, gear trains and interleaved hash probes are all cases of it.
  • Secret sharing and error correction. Split a secret into its residues mod several coprime moduli, so that any large enough subset of residues reconstructs it and a smaller subset does not. This is the CRT twin of polynomial secret sharing. Redundant residue number systems add extra moduli so that a corrupted residue can be detected and corrected.
A fault in one CRT half leaks the key. If a glitch corrupts m_p but not m_q while signing, the faulty signature s' is still correct mod q and wrong mod p. Then gcd(s'^e − m, N) = q, and one faulty signature factors the modulus. Implementations verify sᵉ ≡ m before releasing a signature. This is a direct consequence of the CRT's independence of the components: the property that makes the method fast also makes it fragile.
