16 Testing primality: trial division, the Fermat test, and Carmichael numbers

Trial division is exact and exponential. The Fermat test, which checks whether aⁿ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod n), is fast and usually right. It is fooled completely by Carmichael numbers, composites that satisfy Fermat's congruence for every base coprime to them. Understanding exactly why it fails is what leads to Miller–Rabin.

On a Carmichael number the only Fermat witnesses are the bases that share a factor with n. There are n − 1 − φ(n) of them, a vanishing fraction when the prime factors are large. For 561 it is 240 of 560 bases, but a Carmichael number with three 100-digit prime factors has essentially none. Repeating the Fermat test does not help: every round is fooled for the same reason. The Fermat test is still useful as a quick filter, and it is what the Miller–Rabin census figure draws in orange: bases that pass Fermat's check and are caught only by the extra square-root step.

17 Miller–Rabin: the strong probable-prime test

Miller–Rabin looks inside the Fermat exponentiation. It computes a^(n−1) by repeated squaring from a^d, where n − 1 = 2ˢ·d, and checks that the last value before reaching 1 is −1, as it must be mod a prime. For every odd composite n > 9, at most a quarter of the bases pass. So t independent random bases wrongly accept a composite with probability at most 4⁻ᵗ, and a prime is never rejected. This is a one-sided Monte Carlo algorithm in the sense of Online & Randomized Algorithms. That page's amplification argument applies unchanged.

Why the bound is ¼ and not ½: the Fermat liars form a subgroup, which gives ½ whenever there is at least one witness. Strong liars do not form a subgroup in general. The proof of ¼ takes more care: it splits by the CRT into the prime-power factors of n and counts the bases whose square-root chains agree across every factor. For a Carmichael number with k ≥ 3 prime factors, a base survives only if all k components hit −1 at the same step, and the non-trivial square roots of 1 that the CRT supplies (§15) give the test 2ᵏ − 2 ways to catch it. Reading 4⁻ᵗ correctly. The ¼ is a worst case over composites. It says nothing about how likely a random number is to be prime. For a random odd β-bit candidate, the probability that it is composite and passes one round is far below ¼, since most composites have almost no strong liars, as the census shows. So the error after t rounds on random candidates is much smaller than 4⁻ᵗ, and standards allow fewer rounds when the candidate is random rather than chosen by an adversary. The deterministic 12-base set is the right choice for 64-bit integers. For numbers that an adversary may have constructed to fool fixed bases, use random bases, and many of them.

18 How many primes there are, and how to generate a large one

By the prime number theorem, about 1 in every ln n integers near n is prime. A random β-bit odd number is prime with probability about 2/(β·ln 2). To get a 1024-bit prime, draw random odd 1024-bit numbers and test each: about 355 draws on average, most of them rejected by a small-prime sieve before any exponentiation is done.

the prime number theoremπ(x) = #{ primes ≤ x } ~ x / ln x (the ratio → 1 as x → ∞) x = 10⁶: π = 78 498, x/ln x ≈ 72 382 the approximation li(x) = ∫₂ˣ dt / ln t is far closer density near x: ≈ 1 / ln x near 2^β: 1 / (β·ln 2) β = 1024: 1 in ≈ 710, 1 in ≈ 355 among odd numbers generating a random β-bit primerepeat: draw a uniformly random β-bit odd number c (set the top bit — or the top two, so p·q has exactly 2β bits) discard c if a small prime divides it (sieve by the first few hundred primes: cheap, rejects most c) run Miller–Rabin with t rounds (§17) until c passes; return c expected draws ≈ (β·ln 2)/2 = O(β); each costs O(t·β³) bit operations ⟹ O(t·β⁴) expected, much less with the sieve

The output distribution matters. "Pick a random start and take the next prime" is faster but biased. It favours primes that follow long prime gaps, so some primes are much more likely than others. For RSA the bias has not been shown to be exploitable, and some libraries use incremental search from a random start for speed. The uniform method above is simpler to reason about. What has broken real keys is bad randomness. Devices that generated keys right after boot, with little entropy, produced many RSA moduli that share a prime factor with some other key. A single gcd of the two moduli then factors both (§21). A generator is only as good as its random source.

Why the method is fast enough. The density of primes falls only like 1/β, so the number of candidates grows linearly with the key size, while each test is polynomial. Had primes been exponentially rare, like the fraction 2^(−β/2) of numbers that are perfect squares, random generation would be hopeless, and so would RSA. The prime number theorem is an analytic result, but here it is doing algorithmic work: it bounds the expected running time of the generator.

19 RSA: key generation, encryption, decryption

Pick two large primes p, q and publish N = p·q with an exponent e coprime to λ(N) = lcm(p − 1, q − 1). Encryption is c = mᵉ mod N. The private exponent d = e⁻¹ mod λ(N) undoes it: c^d ≡ m. Everything uses the tools of this page: prime generation (§18), extended Euclid for d (§11), repeated squaring (§13), and CRT for fast decryption (§15).

20 Why decryption works: Euler, Fermat and the CRT

Decryption works because e·d ≡ 1 (mod λ(N)) makes m^(ed) ≡ m, and it does so for every m in [0, N), not only for m coprime to N. The clean proof goes one prime at a time and reassembles the result with the CRT.

the proofe·d = 1 + k·λ(N) for some k ≥ 0, and (p − 1) | λ(N), (q − 1) | λ(N). mod p: if p | m: both m^(ed) and m are ≡ 0. if p ∤ m: m^(ed) = m · (m^(p−1))^(k·λ(N)/(p−1)) ≡ m · 1 (mod p) (Fermat) mod q: the same argument. so p | (m^(ed) − m) and q | (m^(ed) − m); p, q distinct primes ⟹ N = pq divides it: m^(ed) ≡ m (mod N) for EVERY m ∈ Z_N. ∎ (CRT, §14) the Euler-theorem version, and its gapgcd(m, N) = 1: m^(ed) = m · (m^φ(N))^k′ ≡ m (Euler, with d taken mod φ(N)). this misses the p + q − 2 messages divisible by p or q. (For N with a SQUARED prime factor the claim fails — N must be squarefree.) why λ(N) is enough, and when d mod φ(N) is usedλ(N) = lcm(p − 1, q − 1) is the exponent of Zₙ*: a^λ(N) ≡ 1 for every unit, and it is the least such number. any d with e·d ≡ 1 (mod λ(N)) decrypts; d mod φ(N) is one such d (φ is a multiple of λ), often larger. toy key: λ = 780 gives d = 413; φ = 3120 gives d = 2753 — both decrypt every message (checked for all key pairs in the figure)

Correctness needs p ≠ q and N squarefree. With N = p², the message m = p has m² ≡ 0, so every power is 0 and p is never recovered. The figure refuses p = q for that reason, and because √N would give the factor away at once. It also needs gcd(e, λ(N)) = 1. Otherwise m ↦ mᵉ is not injective and no d exists: try e = 3 with p = 61, where 3 | 60. Messages sharing a factor with N decrypt correctly, but a sender who produced one could compute gcd(m, N) and factor the modulus. For random messages that happens with probability about 1/p + 1/q, which is negligible for real key sizes.

21 Why RSA is believed secure, and why textbook RSA is not

Anyone who can factor N can compute λ(N) and then d, so RSA is at most as hard as factoring. Nobody has proved it is as hard. Its security is a belief, grounded in decades of failed attempts to factor fast. Factoring itself is almost certainly not NP-complete. Separately, "textbook" RSA, the bare map m ↦ mᵉ, is insecure in several simple ways, and real systems never use it unpadded.

what is known about the hardnessFACTOR N ⟹ compute λ(N), d breaking RSA ≤ factoring (trivially) KNOW d ⟹ FACTOR N in randomised polynomial time (e·d − 1 is a multiple of λ(N); split it as 2ˢ·t and look for a non-trivial square root of 1 — exactly Miller–Rabin's idea) compute e-th roots mod N WITHOUT d ⟸?⟹ factoring OPEN — the "RSA problem" might be easier than factoring decision version of factoring ("does N have a factor ≤ k?") is in NP ∩ co-NP: YES certified by the factor; NO certified by the full prime factorisation (primality is checkable in P). if it were NP-complete, then NP = co-NP — considered very unlikely. So factoring is believed NOT NP-complete, and also believed NOT in P. Best known classical algorithm: number field sieve, exp( O( β^(1/3) · (log β)^(2/3) ) ) quantum: Shor's algorithm factors in polynomial time — hence the move to post-quantum schemes. record for a general RSA-style modulus: 829 bits (2020). 2048-bit moduli are the common minimum today.

The NP ∩ co-NP argument is the one in NP-Completeness & Approximation: if any problem in NP ∩ co-NP were NP-complete, NP would equal co-NP. So cryptography does not rest on P ≠ NP in the way it is sometimes described. It rests on a narrower and stronger assumption, that one particular problem, which is probably not NP-hard, is nevertheless hard on average for moduli built from random primes. NP-hardness would not help anyway, because it is a worst-case notion. A cryptosystem needs instances that are hard on average, and a random RSA modulus is believed to be one.

Textbook-RSA pitfalls (conceptual, no recipes)

  • It is deterministic. The same m always gives the same c. Anyone can encrypt guesses with the public key and compare. If the message space is small (yes/no, a PIN, a salary band), textbook RSA hides nothing. Randomised padding fixes this: OAEP for encryption, PSS for signatures.
  • It is malleable. (m₁·m₂)ᵉ = m₁ᵉ·m₂ᵉ, so from c an attacker can make a valid encryption of m·r for any r without knowing m. Unpadded signatures can be combined the same way. Padding with structure checks breaks the multiplicative relation.
  • Small e with a small message. If mᵉ < N, no reduction happens and m is the ordinary integer e-th root of c. If one message is sent to e recipients with the same small e, the CRT combines the ciphertexts into mᵉ mod the product of the moduli, which again needs no reduction. e = 65537 with proper padding avoids both.
  • Shared moduli, shared primes. Two users who share N with different e can each derive the other's d, since knowing one's own d factors N (above). If the same message goes to both, Bézout on e₁, e₂ recovers it without any d. Keys generated with poor randomness that share one prime are broken by a single gcd (§18).
  • A small private exponent. Choosing a short d to speed up decryption is unsafe. Below about N^0.25, and in practice higher, d can be recovered from (N, e) alone. Use CRT for speed instead (§15).
  • Side channels and faults. A non-constant-time exponentiation leaks d through timing (§13). A fault in one CRT half leaks a factor (§15). Padding-check error messages that differ let an attacker decrypt by adaptive queries. These attacks target the implementation, not the mathematics.

22 Integer factorisation: trial division and Pollard's rho

Trial division finds a factor p in about p/2 steps. Pollard's rho finds it in about √p steps, by the birthday paradox, using almost no memory. For N = p·q with balanced factors that is N^(1/4) instead of N^(1/2): a large improvement, and still exponential in β. It is the right tool for the small factors of ordinary numbers and useless against an RSA modulus.

The rest of the factoring toolbox, in one paragraph. Pollard's p − 1 method finds p quickly when p − 1 has only small prime factors, which is why RSA primes were once required to be "strong", though random large primes are now considered fine. The elliptic-curve method is the p − 1 idea with a random group order, and its running time depends on the size of the smallest factor. It finds 30–60-digit factors of huge numbers. The quadratic sieve and the number field sieve are sub-exponential and depend only on the size of N. They are what actually threatens RSA, and their records set the key sizes. All of these use the tools of this page (gcd, modular exponentiation, CRT) as inner loops.
