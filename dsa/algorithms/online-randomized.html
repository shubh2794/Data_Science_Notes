Online & Randomized Algorithms

Every algorithm earlier in this series was handed its whole input before it started and did the same thing every time it ran. This page drops both assumptions. An online algorithm must answer each request as it arrives, without seeing the rest of the input, and is judged by its competitive ratio: how much worse it does than an all-knowing offline optimum on the worst input an adversary can build. A randomized algorithm flips coins on purpose, and is judged by its expected cost or its error probability on the worst input, with the coins as the only source of chance. The two ideas meet at once: against an adversary who must fix the input in advance, coin flips are what let an online algorithm beat every deterministic one, from ski rental's 2 down to e/(e − 1), and in paging from k down to about 2·ln k. The page works ski rental, paging with Belady's offline optimum, the marker algorithm, move-to-front and its potential-function proof, and online load balancing and matching. It then turns to probabilistic analysis with indicator random variables, the hiring and secretary problems, Las Vegas and Monte Carlo algorithms with amplification, Karger's min cut and Miller–Rabin, the Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff tail bounds, balls into bins, and the machinery of randomness itself: linear congruential generators and how they fail, Fisher–Yates and the classic shuffle bug, and reservoir sampling. Every fault count, ratio and success rate is measured by running the real routine on seeded random input.

01 Two ways to lose certainty: not knowing the future, and flipping coins

An online algorithm doesn't know what comes next. A randomized algorithm picks its next step with a coin flip. The first is something the problem forces on you. The second is a choice you make. The two ideas belong on one page because the choice is often the best answer to the constraint. A cache has to decide what to evict now, before it knows which page will be asked for next. A ski renter has to decide whether to buy today without knowing when the season ends. A hiring manager has to accept or reject each candidate before meeting the rest. None of them can be optimal on every input, because the input that punishes a given decision is always possible. What they can do is guarantee a bounded loss compared with someone who knew everything in advance. Where a deterministic rule can be exploited by an adversary who knows it, a randomized rule can hide its next move and push that bounded loss lower.

02 Online problems and the competitive ratio

An online algorithm is c-competitive if, on every request sequence, its cost is at most c times the optimal offline cost plus a constant that does not depend on the sequence. The comparison is with OPT because the absolute cost says nothing: an adversary can always force an online algorithm to pay a lot, but on those inputs OPT pays a lot too. The ratio isolates the cost of not knowing the future. It is a worst-case measure over inputs, just like the worst-case running time of Algorithm Analysis, but what it measures is the value of information rather than time.

Why the additive constant is there, and when it matters

Paging shows why the constant is needed. A cache starts empty, so any algorithm pays k cold misses to fill it, and OPT pays them too. But take a sequence long enough to fill the cache and no longer. OPT pays k, the online algorithm pays k, and the ratio is 1. Now add requests: OPT may pay nothing more while the online algorithm pays k more. Without an additive term, the ratio of a short sequence would be decided by warm-up effects that have nothing to do with the policy. The constant a absorbs those start-up costs. What it may not do is grow with the sequence: a is fixed first, and then the inequality must hold for all σ, however long. That order of quantifiers is the whole content of the definition. When the problem lets an adversary scale costs (multiply every price by 1 000), any fixed a becomes negligible, and the strict and non-strict ratios coincide. For ski rental they coincide. For paging, the lower-bound argument of §08 is built to make OPT's cost grow without bound, so it defeats any fixed a.

What the ratio measures, and what it misses

  • It is pessimistic by design. The ratio is a maximum over all inputs, and the worst input for paging is a cyclic scan of k + 1 pages that no real program issues in a loop forever. LRU's ratio of k is tight, yet on real traces LRU usually faults within a few percent of Belady. This gap between competitive ratio and observed behaviour is the best-known criticism of the measure. The refinements that address it change the model: resource augmentation gives the online algorithm more memory than OPT (§08), access graphs restrict which sequences are legal, and diffuse adversaries draw inputs from a restricted family of distributions.
  • It ignores running time. An online algorithm may take exponential time per request and still be competitive. The model charges only for decisions. In practice every algorithm on this page runs in O(1) or O(k) per request, and the practical variants of §24 exist to make that O(1).
  • It compares with the offline optimum, not with the best online rule. "LRU is k-competitive and so is FIFO" says they have the same worst case, not that they behave alike. §08's figure measures how different they are on typical inputs, and §09 shows a behaviour of FIFO that LRU cannot have.
  • It is not the regret of online learning. Online learning (bandits, expert advice, online gradient descent) compares against the best fixed action in hindsight, and measures the additive gap, called regret, which should grow sub-linearly in T. Competitive analysis compares with the best adaptive sequence of actions in hindsight, and measures a ratio. The two frameworks answer different questions, and §24 comes back to where each belongs.
A three-step recipe you will see in every section. (1) Upper bound: show that on every sequence ALG ≤ c·OPT + a, usually by charging each of ALG's costs to something OPT must also pay. The tools are phases for paging and a potential function for list update. (2) Lower bound: exhibit an adversary that forces every algorithm of the class to pay c·OPT. (3) If the two match, the ratio is tight and the problem is settled. If they don't, the gap is a research question. Ski rental, deterministic paging and list update are settled. Randomized paging is settled by a different algorithm than the marker (§10). Online bipartite matching with a randomized algorithm is settled at 1 − 1/e (§12).

03 Deterministic against randomized, and the three adversaries

For a deterministic algorithm there is only one adversary: whoever knows the algorithm can simulate it, so the whole worst-case input can be written down in advance. For a randomized algorithm, the answer depends on what the adversary sees of the coin flips, and three standard models give three different competitive ratios for the same algorithm. Getting the model right is not a formality. The marker algorithm is 2H_k-competitive against one model and no better than k-competitive against another.

the three adversary models, weakest firstOBLIVIOUS writes the whole sequence σ in advance, knowing ALG's code but NOT its random bits. Measure: E[ALG(σ)] ≤ c·OPT(σ) + a. The standard model; the default whenever a paper says "randomised". ADAPTIVE ONLINE chooses σₜ after seeing ALG's responses to σ₁…σₜ₋₁ (so it has learnt the coins used so far), but must serve σ itself ONLINE, committing as it goes. ADAPTIVE OFFLINE chooses σₜ adaptively as above, and is charged the OPTIMAL OFFLINE cost of the finished σ. the two facts that make the models matter(1) against an ADAPTIVE OFFLINE adversary randomisation is useless: if a randomised algorithm is c-competitive against it, then some DETERMINISTIC algorithm is c-competitive. (2) if ALG is c-competitive against ADAPTIVE ONLINE adversaries and some algorithm is d-competitive against OBLIVIOUS ones, then some DETERMINISTIC algorithm is (c·d)-competitive.

Why an oblivious adversary is the right model for a cache

An adaptive adversary can see the cache contents and ask for whatever just left the cache. In that model, a randomized cache does exactly as badly as a deterministic one: by fact (1), nothing is gained. But a program's memory accesses aren't chosen by an opponent who watches the replacement hardware's random number generator. They are fixed by the program and its input. So the oblivious model describes the real situation. The one real exception is security. A timing attack that probes which lines were evicted is an adaptive adversary, and caches designed to resist such attacks are analysed in that stronger model. Hash tables have the same split: a seeded random hash function defeats an adversary who knows the code but not the seed, and fails against one who can recover the seed. That is why universal hashing keeps its seed secret, and why a fixed hash function in a web server is a denial-of-service risk (§18).

Yao's principle: the one tool for randomized lower bounds

An upper bound for a randomized algorithm is an expectation calculation. A lower bound must hold against every possible randomized algorithm, which looks like a statement about an infinite family of probability distributions. Yao's minimax principle reduces it to a statement about deterministic algorithms on one input distribution chosen by you:

Yao's principle (minimax for online algorithms)for ANY distribution D over request sequences, and ANY randomised online algorithm R: max over σ of E_R[ R(σ) ] / OPT(σ) ≥ min over deterministic A of E_D[ A(σ) ] / E_D[ OPT(σ) ] so: pick a hard distribution D, show every DETERMINISTIC algorithm pays ≥ c·E_D[OPT] on it, and c is a lower bound for every RANDOMISED algorithm against the oblivious adversary. (holds up to the additive constant, for long sequences)

Choose the distribution well and the deterministic calculation becomes simple. For paging it is "each request is a uniformly random page among k + 1". Whatever a deterministic algorithm holds, the requested page is missing with probability exactly 1/(k + 1), so it faults on a 1/(k + 1) fraction of requests. OPT, meanwhile, faults once per phase, the stretch until all k + 1 pages have been requested. Starting from one page, collecting the other k is a coupon-collector time (§20) of (k + 1)·(1/k + 1/(k − 1) + … + 1) = (k + 1)·H_k requests on average. So OPT faults once per (k + 1)·H_k requests, the deterministic algorithm faults H_k times in the same stretch, and the ratio is H_k. No randomized paging algorithm beats H_k ≈ ln k against an oblivious adversary. The same principle gives the e/(e − 1) lower bound for ski rental (§05) and the 1 − 1/e bound for online matching (§12). The principle is the online analogue of von Neumann's minimax theorem: in a zero-sum game between the algorithm designer and the input chooser, a mixed strategy on one side can be met with a pure strategy on the other.

Read "randomized competitive ratio" with the model attached. "Marker is 2H_k-competitive" means against an oblivious adversary. Against an adaptive online adversary it is no better than k, because that adversary can always request an evicted page. Every randomized ratio on this page is for the oblivious adversary unless the text says otherwise, and the paging figure of §08 builds its adversarial sequence against the deterministic policy it is attacking. When the policy under test is the marker, it builds the sequence against LRU, because an oblivious adversary can't see the marker's coins.

04 Ski rental: the break-even rule, and why 2 − 1/B is the best any deterministic rule can do

Renting skis costs 1 a day and buying them costs B. You don't know how many days you will ski, and the season ends when an adversary says so. The rule "rent until the rent paid would reach the price, then buy" pays at most 2 − 1/B times the optimum, and no deterministic rule can guarantee less. Ski rental is the model for every rent-or-buy decision. Should a laptop spin down its disk after an idle period (spinning up costs energy, staying up costs power)? Should a TCP connection be kept open or closed? When should a thread stop spinning on a lock and block? When should a cloud workload move from on-demand instances to a reserved one? Should a key-value store keep a hot object in fast memory? All have the same structure: a small recurring cost, a large one-off cost that ends the recurring one, and an unknown horizon.

The lower-bound argument is short and is the typical online lower bound. A deterministic rule is fully described by one number, t. The adversary can simulate it, so it knows t and ends the season exactly when the purchase has become a waste. The figure's "worst" mode evaluates that argument directly. For every t from 1 to 3B, it tries every stop day d, records the worst ratio, and plots it. The curve has a single minimum, at t = B, with height 2 − 1/B. Buying too early (t < B) is punished by a season that ends the day you buy. Buying too late (t > B) is punished by a season that goes on: you've paid more rent than the skis cost, and then you buy them anyway. The break-even point balances the two regrets. The balance is what gives the ratio of about 2: the worst case pays for the purchase twice, once in rent and once at the till.

What the problem teaches beyond skis

  • Balance the regrets. Nearly every good deterministic online rule is found by setting the cost of acting too early equal to the cost of acting too late. Break-even for ski rental, "spin down after the idle time whose power cost equals a spin-up", "block after spinning for one context switch's worth of time": all three are ski rental with different prices.
  • Continuous time changes nothing essential. With a continuous horizon and rental rate 1, buying at time B is exactly 2-competitive, and the lower bound is 2. The −1/B comes from discrete days. The rule and the proof are the same.
  • Prediction helps, if you are careful. With a forecast of the season length, a rule can buy earlier when the forecast says "long". Learning-augmented algorithms do this while keeping a worst-case guarantee. For ski rental, a tunable parameter trades a ratio near 1 when the forecast is right against a bounded ratio when it is wrong. It is the same trade between trusting data and staying robust that runs through the ML pages.
The general shape. Rent-or-buy is the simplest problem where an online algorithm must decide when to make an irreversible commitment. Once you see ski rental in "when to stop waiting", "when to rebuild the index", "when to migrate the page" or "when to buy the reserved instance", you also know the answer: act once the waiting has cost about what acting would cost, and expect at worst twice the optimum.

05 Randomized ski rental: e/(e − 1) ≈ 1.582 against an oblivious adversary

If the buy day is drawn from a carefully shaped distribution, an adversary who knows the distribution but not the draw can't pick a bad stop day, because every stop day costs the same in expectation. The resulting expected ratio is 1/(1 − (1 − 1/B)^B), which decreases towards e/(e − 1) ≈ 1.582 as B grows, and no randomized rule does better. The improvement from 2 to 1.582 is the first concrete example of what hiding your strategy is worth.

The "distribution" mode of the §04 figure draws pᵢ and then evaluates the expected ratio against every stop day from 1 to 4B. The readout prints the minimum and maximum of those ratios, and they agree to the last digit shown. That is the equalising property: a distribution that makes the adversary indifferent is the optimal randomized strategy, just as in a mixed-strategy equilibrium of a two-player game. The "ratio" mode draws the same expected ratio as a flat violet line under the break-even rule's two-level step. The rule gives up a little on short seasons, where it sometimes buys too early, to save a lot on the stop day the adversary would otherwise choose.

What the oblivious assumption is doing

An adaptive adversary who watches the rule's decision day by day beats randomization completely. It ends the season on the day the rule buys, whichever day that turns out to be, and the ratio goes back to at least 2 − 1/B. The oblivious adversary must fix d before the coin is flipped, and that fixing is what the expectation averages over. For real rent-or-buy decisions the oblivious model fits: the weather does not observe your purchase, and a program's idle periods do not depend on when the disk spun down. But the model is doing real work, and a paper that claims a randomized competitive ratio should always say which adversary it assumes.

Where the number e/(e − 1) keeps coming back. The same constant 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 (its reciprocal) is the optimal ratio for randomized online bipartite matching (§12), the greedy guarantee for monotone submodular maximisation, and the randomized competitive ratio of TCP acknowledgement and of snoopy caching. It keeps appearing because of the geometric distribution that equalises two linear costs. The secretary problem's 1/e (§15) is related but different, and the two are easy to confuse.

06 Paging: the model, and the policies worth knowing

A fast memory holds k pages and a slow memory holds all of them. Each request names a page: if it is in the cache the request is a hit and costs nothing, and if not it is a fault, costs 1, and the page must be brought in, evicting another if the cache is full. The eviction rule is the whole algorithm. The same model covers CPU caches (lines), virtual memory (pages), database buffer pools (blocks), web and CDN caches (objects), and the key-value cache in front of any slow service. The only differences are the unit and the cost of a miss.

the paging problemcache of k slots; request sequence σ = p₁, p₂, …, pₙ of page ids cost = number of FAULTS (requests for a page not in the cache) DEMAND paging: a page is loaded only when it is requested, and at most one page is evicted per fault (every algorithm can be made demand-paging without increasing its cost, so this loses nothing) the policies this page simulatesLRU evict the page whose last request is OLDEST deterministic, k-competitive (§08) FIFO evict the page that was LOADED earliest (hits do not refresh it) deterministic, k-competitive, anomalous (§09) LFU evict the page with the FEWEST requests since it was loaded deterministic, NOT competitive FWF flush-when-full: on a fault with a full cache, evict EVERYTHING deterministic, k-competitive (!) Two families that organise the zoo

Marking algorithms. Divide the sequence into phases. A phase begins with a request and ends just before the request that would make it contain k + 1 distinct pages. Mark a page when it is requested and clear all marks at the start of each phase. A marking algorithm never evicts a marked page. LRU is a marking algorithm: a page requested in the current phase is among the k most recently used distinct pages, so it is never least recently used while unmarked pages remain. FWF is the crudest marking algorithm. The randomized marker is the marking algorithm that picks which unmarked page to evict by coin flip. Every marking algorithm faults at most k times per phase, and that single fact gives k-competitiveness (§08). Conservative algorithms. On any consecutive stretch of requests with at most k distinct pages, the algorithm faults at most k times. LRU, FIFO and CLOCK are conservative. FIFO is not a marking algorithm, since it can evict a page requested moments ago, but it is conservative, and conservativeness also gives k-competitiveness.

Why LFU fails, precisely

LFU looks sensible: keep what is popular. It fails because counts never forget. Request pages 1, …, k − 1 each M times. Then alternate two fresh pages x, y, x, y, … for 2M requests. Each of x and y arrives with count 1, below every stale page, and is the only candidate for eviction when the other arrives. LFU faults on every one of the 2M alternating requests. OPT evicts one stale page once and then faults on nothing, so it pays k + 1 in total. LFU's cost grows with M while OPT's stays fixed, so no constant c works. The "phases" pattern in the §08 figure, a working set that drifts, shows the same failure on a less contrived input: LFU keeps the old working set because of its high counts and faults on almost everything new. This is why production caches either decay their counts or combine frequency with recency (§24).

The cost model is a simplification, and it is the right one. Every fault costs the same. Real systems have different miss costs (a CDN object of 1 KB against one of 1 GB), different object sizes (the generalised caching problem), and write-backs (evicting a dirty page costs twice). Each generalisation has its own competitive theory, and the ratios mostly keep their shape: k for deterministic algorithms, O(log k) for randomized ones. The unit-cost model is where the ideas are cleanest, and it is the one this page proves things in.

07 Belady's rule: evicting the page needed furthest in the future is optimal offline

If the whole request sequence is known, the fewest faults come from a simple greedy rule: on each fault, evict the page whose next request lies furthest in the future, or a page never requested again if there is one. The proof is an exchange argument of exactly the kind in the greedy chapter. No online algorithm can run this rule, because it needs the future. Its value is as the yardstick: every competitive ratio on this page is measured against it, and every figure computes it by simulation on the same sequence.

the rule (furthest in future, FF)on a fault for page p with a full cache: evict argmax over q in cache of next(q) where next(q) = the index of q's next request after now (= ∞ if q is never requested again) cost: with the next-use indices precomputed in one right-to-left pass, O(k) per fault naively, O(log k) with a heap the theorem, and the exchange lemma that proves itTHEOREM FF incurs the minimum number of faults over all eviction schedules. LEMMA Let S be any schedule that makes the same decisions as FF on the first j requests. Then there is a schedule S' that makes the same decisions as FF on the first j + 1 requests, and faults(S') ≤ faults(S). Induction on j, starting from an optimal S, turns it into FF with no increase in faults.

The exchange, step by step

At request j + 1 both schedules have the same cache. If the request is a hit, or both evict the same page, set S' = S for this step. Otherwise FF evicts e and S evicts some f ≠ e. Let S' evict e instead. From then on the two caches differ in exactly one page: S holds e where S' holds f. S' copies S's decisions exactly until the first time this difference matters, and at that moment makes the caches identical without paying more than S. There are three possibilities. (a) A request for f arrives. S faults and S' hits. If S evicts e to make room, the caches are now equal and S' has saved a fault. If S evicts some other page g, then S' evicts g and loads e in its place. That is one load against S's one fault, and the caches are now identical at equal cost. The load is not on demand, but any schedule can be converted to a demand-paging one without adding faults (§06). (b) S evicts e of its own accord, to serve some other page. S' evicts f in the same step, and the caches become identical at the same cost. (c) A request for e arrives before any request for f. This is the case that cannot happen: FF chose e because its next request is furthest away, so f is requested first, or neither is ever requested again, in which case the difference never matters. In every case S' pays no more than S and agrees with FF for one more step.

This is the "greedy stays ahead / exchange" template of Greedy Algorithms, with one feature worth noting. The exchange is done locally in time: the argument never compares whole schedules, only the stretch from step j + 1 to the first request that separates e from f. The same shape of proof shows that earliest-deadline-first minimises maximum lateness in that chapter's §08.

What Belady's rule is used for in practice

  • As the ceiling in trace studies. Given a recorded trace of a real workload, FF gives the best hit rate any cache of size k could have had. A new policy is judged by how much of the gap between LRU and FF it closes. Every row of §08's figure prints its ratio to FF for this reason.
  • As a training target. Learned cache-replacement policies in CPUs and CDNs are often trained to imitate FF's decisions on past traces. The model's features predict "how far in the future is this object's next request", which is exactly the quantity FF sorts by.
  • When the future really is known. Compilers allocating registers over a basic block, schedulers that know a job's full I/O plan, and out-of-core matrix algorithms with a fixed access pattern can all run FF exactly. Register spilling in a straight-line block is often done with this rule, sometimes known by its compiler-community name.
A caution about "OPT" in sizes and costs. FF is optimal for the unit-cost, unit-size problem only. When pages have different sizes, or different miss costs, offline optimal caching becomes NP-hard, since it contains a knapsack-like choice of which set of objects to keep (NP-Completeness, §14). Evicting "the one needed furthest in future" can then be arbitrarily bad, for example evicting a small object needed soon rather than a huge one needed slightly sooner. The CDN literature compares against approximations of the offline optimum for this reason, not against FF.

08 LRU and FIFO are k-competitive, and no deterministic policy is better

Every marking or conservative policy (LRU, FIFO, CLOCK, even flush-when-full) faults at most k times for each fault that Belady's rule must make, plus a constant. There is a sequence on which every deterministic policy faults k times as often as Belady. So k is the exact deterministic competitive ratio of paging. The upper bound depends on the right way to split the sequence, and the lower bound is an adversary who always asks for the one page you do not hold.

Three consequences worth stating separately

  • The ratio does not tell LRU and FIFO apart, but workloads do. Both are k-competitive, and on the cyclic loop both fault on every request. On a skewed workload LRU usually wins, because a hit refreshes a popular page under LRU but not under FIFO. On a drifting working set, compare their bars: FIFO keeps evicting the hot pages of the current working set just because they were loaded early.
  • The cyclic loop is not exotic. It is what a program that scans an array one page larger than the cache does, and LRU is exactly wrong for it: the page it evicts is always the next one needed. Databases deal with this by giving sequential scans their own small ring of buffers so they cannot flush the main pool. The "hot set + one-shot scans" pattern shows scan pages ("s" in the timeline) pushing the hot set out of LRU.
  • Resource augmentation is the practical reading. "LRU with twice the memory is within 2 of optimal" is a guarantee engineers can use, and it matches the observation that LRU's miss rate approaches the optimum as caches grow. When sizing a cache, compare LRU at size k with FF at size k/2. That comparison is at most 2, whatever the workload.
Why CLOCK is what systems actually run. Exact LRU moves an entry to the head of a list on every hit. That is a pointer update under a lock on the hot path of every memory access. CLOCK sets a single reference bit on a hit, often in hardware, and pays for eviction only on a miss, by sweeping a hand past pages whose bit is set and clearing it. It is conservative, so it keeps the k guarantee, and on real workloads it behaves close to LRU. Its bar in the figure shows this. Operating-system page replacement and PostgreSQL's buffer manager both use CLOCK-style sweeps rather than exact LRU (§24). The doubly linked list that exact LRU needs is described in Linked Lists, Stacks & Queues.

09 Belady's anomaly: under FIFO, a bigger cache can fault more

On the request string 1 2 3 4 1 2 5 1 2 3 4 5, FIFO with 3 frames faults 9 times and FIFO with 4 frames faults 10 times. LRU and Belady's rule can never do this: their cache contents at size k are always a subset of their contents at size k + 1, so more memory can only help. The anomaly is a curiosity with a clear lesson. A policy's decisions at one size need not be consistent with its decisions at another, and a property that holds for some policies (more memory never hurts) is not automatically true of all.

the stack (inclusion) propertya policy is a STACK ALGORITHM if for every σ, every time t and every k: Cache_k(t) ⊆ Cache_{k+1}(t) — the k-frame contents are a subset of the (k+1)-frame contents ⟹ faults(k+1) ≤ faults(k) for every σ: whenever the k+1 cache misses, so did the k cache LRU: stack algorithm — Cache_k = the k most recently used distinct pages, which are among the k+1 most recent OPT: stack algorithm — Cache_k = the k pages ranked "soonest next use" among those loaded FIFO: NOT a stack algorithm — "loaded earliest" depends on the fault history, which differs between sizes

The stack property also has a practical payoff. For a stack algorithm, one pass over a trace gives the fault count for every cache size at once. Keep the pages in a recency stack, and for each request record its depth in the stack (its stack distance). The request is a hit in a cache of size k exactly when its depth is at most k. So the histogram of stack distances is the whole miss-ratio curve. This is how cache designers produce the "hit rate against cache size" curves used for sizing. It is not possible for FIFO, because FIFO's contents at different sizes aren't nested.

Why the anomaly does not contradict FIFO's k-competitiveness

The competitive bound compares FIFO at size k with OPT at size k. It says nothing about FIFO at size k against FIFO at size k + 1. The anomaly needs a string on which FIFO's larger cache keeps exactly the wrong pages, and such strings are uncommon: the seeded search behind the "found" option tries thousands of random strings before one shows it. How large can the anomaly get? It was long conjectured that FIFO's faults at size k + 1 could never exceed twice its faults at size k. That conjecture was later disproved: with enough frames and a long enough constructed string, the ratio is unbounded. It is still a curiosity. The engineering point is the one above: stack algorithms allow single-pass miss-ratio curves and FIFO does not.

Where FIFO is nonetheless the right choice. FIFO is making a comeback in large web caches. On a hit it does nothing at all: no pointer move, no lock, no reference bit. That makes it the cheapest policy to run at millions of requests per second. Recent designs put a small FIFO queue in front of a larger one, promoting an object only if it is hit again while in the small queue. That filters out the one-hit wonders that make up most CDN traffic and matches or beats LRU's hit rate (§24). The anomaly is real, but on real traces it rarely shows up.

10 The randomized marker algorithm: 2H_k ≈ 2 ln k, against a lower bound of H_k

Mark each page when it is requested, and on a fault evict an unmarked page chosen uniformly at random. When every cached page is marked, clear the marks and start a new phase. Against an oblivious adversary, the expected number of faults is at most 2H_k times optimal, where H_k = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/k ≈ ln k. So randomization improves paging from k to O(log k). For a thousand-slot cache the guarantee is 2H_k ≈ 15 instead of 1 000, against a floor of H_k ≈ 7.5. The analysis counts, phase by phase, how many pages are new to the phase and how likely a stale page is to have been evicted before it is requested again.

Why the cyclic loop is harmless to the marker

On the loop 0, 1, …, k, 0, 1, …, each phase requests k distinct pages and then the page that starts the next phase. Only one page per phase is new (mᵢ = 1), and the others are stale. LRU evicts exactly the next page needed, every time. The marker evicts a random unmarked page, and the j-th stale page it meets has been evicted with probability about 1/(k − j + 1). Summing gives H_k faults per phase against OPT's one. The sequence is the same, but the deterministic policy is being steered by an adversary who can predict it, and the randomized one is not.

Randomized eviction in real systems. Some processor caches use pseudo-random replacement within a set: it needs no per-line state, and it has no pathological cyclic case. Redis's approximate LRU samples a handful of keys at random and evicts the least recently used among the sample. That is a randomized policy chosen for memory efficiency, and its behaviour approaches true LRU as the sample size grows. Neither is the marker algorithm exactly, but both rely on its lesson: an eviction the workload can't predict can't be systematically exploited by the workload.

11 List update: move-to-front is 2-competitive, by a potential function

Keep n items in a linked list. Accessing the item at position i costs i, and after the access the item may be moved forward for free. Move-to-front (MTF) moves every accessed item to the head. Against any offline algorithm, even one that sees the whole sequence and rearranges the list at will, MTF pays at most twice as much, minus the number of requests. The proof uses the potential method from Algorithm Analysis, with the number of inversions between MTF's list and the offline algorithm's list as the potential. It is the classic example of amortized analysis applied to an online algorithm.

What each pattern shows

  • Locality is what MTF exploits. On bursts of repeats MTF beats even the best static list, because after the first access of a burst every repeat costs 1. The static list can't adapt to a burst. This is why self-organising lists appear inside compressors: move-to-front coding turns the locality in the output of the Burrows–Wheeler transform into a stream of small integers that entropy-code well.
  • Transpose can be trapped. Alternate requests for the last two items. Transpose swaps them with each other at the tail on every request and pays n each time. MTF brings both to the front and pays 1 or 2. The ratio of transpose to the optimum grows with n, and with longer adversarial sequences it is unbounded. Frequency count is caught by the reverse trick: a burst of requests for an item that was cold earlier leaves it deep in the list for a long time.
  • Cyclic access is MTF's worst case. Requesting n − 1, n − 2, …, 0 repeatedly makes every MTF access cost n. The static list pays about (n + 1)/2 on average. The ratio approaches 2, and the theorem says it can go no higher.
Why the proof uses a potential and not a phase argument. For paging the phases give a clean block structure. For list update there is none: MTF's cost on a request depends on the entire history of the list. The potential function records exactly the part of that history that matters, which is how differently the two lists are ordered, and makes every step's amortized cost bounded. It's the same move as the dynamic-array proof in Algorithm Analysis, §20. There, the potential banks credit before a doubling. Here, it banks credit whenever MTF's list agrees with A's more than the costs would suggest. The doubly linked list is what makes MTF's move constant-time once the item is found.

12 Online load balancing and bipartite matching, briefly

Two more online problems show the same pattern: a greedy rule with a clean guarantee, an adversary showing the guarantee is tight, and, for matching, a randomized rule that beats every deterministic one against an oblivious adversary. Greedy list scheduling is (2 − 1/m)-competitive for makespan on m machines. Greedy matching is ½-competitive, and the randomized RANKING algorithm is (1 − 1/e)-competitive, which is optimal.

The load-balancing bound is the same argument as the list-scheduling 2-approximation in Greedy Algorithms, and it works online because it never used the order of the jobs. Only the last job's size and the average load enter the proof. Sorting the jobs first (longest first) improves the offline guarantee to 4/3, but that needs the whole input, which is exactly what an online algorithm lacks. The deterministic online lower bound for this problem is about 1.88 for large m, and the best known algorithms reach about 1.92 by keeping some machines deliberately lightly loaded. So greedy's 2 − 1/m is close to the truth.

Matching is the problem behind ad allocation. Advertisers are the offline side, search queries arrive online, and each query must be shown an ad immediately. Deterministic greedy can be tricked. Give the first arrival two neighbours, let greedy pick either one, and have the adversary send the next arrival to the one it picked. That arrival goes unmatched, and repeating the construction on a larger graph holds greedy to half the optimum. RANKING hides its preferences in a random order drawn once, so the adversary can't aim the next arrival at the vertex it will choose. The weighted, budgeted version (each advertiser has a daily budget) has the same 1 − 1/e ratio for an algorithm that discounts bids by how much of the budget has been spent. The trade-off function that achieves it is the same geometric shape as ski rental's optimal distribution (§05).

A single idea connects §04–§12. Every deterministic online rule is steered by an adversary who can predict it, and every randomized one is protected by what the adversary can't see. The guarantees come from charging arguments (phases, potentials, the last job) that are independent of the order of the input. The lower bounds come from an adversary, or from Yao's principle with a well-chosen input distribution. Everything from here on changes the viewpoint: randomness stops being a defence against an adversary and becomes a tool for analysis and for design.

13 Probabilistic analysis, randomized algorithms, and indicator random variables

Probabilistic analysis assumes the input is random and averages a deterministic algorithm's cost over it. A randomized algorithm makes its own randomness and averages over its coins, so its guarantee holds for every input. The main calculation tool for both is the same: write the cost as a sum of 0/1 indicator variables and use linearity of expectation, which holds even when the indicators are dependent. Almost every expected-value result on the rest of this page is two lines once the right indicators are chosen.

Three quick examples

  • Fixed points of a random permutation. Let Iᵢ indicate that item i stays in position i. Then P = 1/n, so the expected number of fixed points is n · 1/n = 1 for every n. The indicators are dependent (if n − 1 items are fixed, so is the last one), and it doesn't matter.
  • Collisions among n keys in m slots. One indicator per pair, each with probability 1/m under uniform hashing, gives E[collisions] = C(n, 2)/m. Set it to 1 and you get the birthday threshold n ≈ √(2m) (§20). The same sum gives the expected chain length in hash-table chaining.
  • Clauses satisfied by a random assignment. A clause with three distinct literals is false with probability 1/8, so the expected number of satisfied clauses is 7m/8. That one line is the approximation guarantee in NP-Completeness, §24.

The two kinds of analysis give the same formula for insertion sort's average and for randomized quicksort's expectation. The difference is who guarantees the randomness. Algorithm Analysis, §06 averages insertion sort over random inputs. The result is true of random data and says nothing about the nearly sorted or reverse-sorted data real systems produce. Randomized quicksort's 2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n is an average over its own pivot choices. It holds on sorted input, on adversarial input, on every input, because the algorithm makes the randomness itself. When you can shuffle the input yourself (§22), the weaker kind of analysis turns into the stronger kind of guarantee.

The one discipline indicator arguments need. Name the sample space and the probability measure before writing P(Eᵢ). "The probability that candidate i is hired" means nothing until you say what is random. In §14 it is the order of arrival, assumed or enforced to be uniform over all n! orders. Most wrong expectations come from computing P(Eᵢ) under one model and applying it in another. The statistics series covers the underlying probability: expectation, the law of large numbers that makes Monte Carlo estimates converge, and the Monte Carlo method itself.

15 The secretary problem: observe n/e, then take the next best, and succeed with probability → 1/e

You may hire exactly one of n candidates who arrive in uniformly random order, you only learn how each compares with those before it, and every decision is final. The rule "reject the first r, then accept the first candidate better than all of those" picks the best candidate with probability (r/n)·Σᵢ₌ᵣ₊₁ⁿ 1/(i − 1). This is maximised near r = n/e, where it tends to 1/e ≈ 0.368. No rule of any kind does better. The constant is independent of n: whether there are ten candidates or ten million, you get the very best about 37% of the time.

Why no rule beats 1/e, and the variants that matter

Any optimal rule may be taken to accept only candidates who are the best seen so far, since accepting anyone else guarantees failure. Whether candidate i is a new record is independent of the relative order of the earlier candidates, so the optimal decision at i depends only on i. Backward induction then shows the optimal rule is a threshold: reject everyone up to some r, then accept the first record. The threshold rule is therefore optimal, and its best value tends to 1/e. Among the variants: the multiple-choice secretary (hire k, maximise the total value) achieves 1 − O(1/√k) of the optimum. The matroid secretary problem generalises to structured choices, like the matroids of Greedy Algorithms. The prophet inequality (values drawn from known distributions, arriving in adversarial order) guarantees half the expected maximum with a single threshold. That result underlies posted-price mechanisms in online markets.

Secretary against ski rental. Both are online, and both have an e in the answer, but the models are opposite. Ski rental faces an adversary and uses randomness to hide; its e/(e − 1) comes from equalising costs against every stop day. The secretary problem assumes a random order, and its algorithm is deterministic; its 1/e comes from maximising −x ln x. With an adversarial order the secretary problem is hopeless, with success at most 1/n. The random-order assumption is the model, and on real hiring or house-hunting data it is exactly the part to question.

16 Las Vegas and Monte Carlo: random time or random correctness, and amplification by repetition

A Las Vegas algorithm is always correct, and only its running time is random. A Monte Carlo algorithm always finishes within its time bound, but may be wrong with some bounded probability. Repeating a Monte Carlo algorithm independently drives its error down exponentially, and a Las Vegas algorithm stopped at a multiple of its expected time becomes Monte Carlo. The two classes are interchangeable once you can either check an answer or bound a running time.

Why a small error probability is not a compromise

An error probability of 2⁻¹⁰⁰ is far below the probability that a cosmic ray flips a bit in the machine during the computation, or that the compiler has a bug. Past that point, "deterministic" and "correct with probability 1 − 2⁻¹⁰⁰" are the same in engineering terms, and the randomized algorithm is usually simpler and faster. What makes the small error trustworthy is independence of the repetitions. Each run must use fresh random bits, and the error bound must hold for every input, not only on average over inputs. That is why a Monte Carlo guarantee is stated as max over x of P_r(wrong on x). The input can't be chosen to make the repetitions fail together, because the only randomness is the algorithm's own.

Complexity classes, for orientation

  • ZPP: problems with Las Vegas algorithms running in expected polynomial time. Equivalently, RP ∩ co-RP.
  • RP: polynomial-time Monte Carlo with one-sided error. A no-instance is always rejected, and a yes-instance is accepted with probability ≥ ½. Polynomial identity testing is the famous member not known to be in P.
  • BPP: two-sided error ≤ 1/3 (any constant below ½, by amplification). It is widely conjectured that BPP = P: every efficient randomized algorithm can be derandomized with only polynomial slowdown, given sufficiently good pseudo-random generators. Primality testing moved from co-RP (Miller–Rabin, §18) to P (AKS) and is the flagship example. In practice, randomized algorithms remain faster and simpler, which is why they are used. See NP-Completeness, §18 for the surrounding classes.
Say which one you have. A code review of a randomized routine should get a one-line answer to "what is random: the time, or the answer?" If it is the answer, the next question is "what is the error per run, is it one-sided, and how many repetitions does the caller rely on?" A probabilistic data structure that silently returns a wrong answer, such as a Bloom filter's false positive (Hash Tables), is a Monte Carlo component, and its error rate is part of the interface.

18 Fingerprints, Miller–Rabin primality, and randomized hashing

The most-used randomized algorithms check equality or membership by comparing short random fingerprints instead of the objects themselves. What makes each of these correct, and what breaks them

  • Primality testing is exponential by trial division. Checking divisors up to √n takes 2^(b/2) steps for a b-bit number, exponential in the input size, as NP-Completeness, §02 explains. Miller–Rabin is polynomial in b, and it is what every cryptographic library uses to generate RSA primes. The deterministic AKS test proved primality is in P but is far slower in practice. The randomized test with 40 or so rounds is the engineering answer.
  • The Fermat test alone is not enough. Checking aⁿ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod n) is fooled by Carmichael numbers, such as 561, for every base coprime to n. The square-root step of Miller–Rabin is what makes the "¼ of bases" bound hold for every composite.
  • Universal hashing needs a secret seed. The guarantee is over the random choice of h. If an attacker knows h, they can send keys that all collide, and a hash table degrades to a list with Θ(n) per operation. This is the algorithmic-complexity attack that led language runtimes to seed their string hashes randomly at start-up. The analysis and the constructions are in Hash Tables, universal hashing, and perfect hashing uses a Las Vegas retry loop on top.
  • Fingerprints are everywhere. Content-addressed storage, deduplication, rsync's rolling checksum, Git's object ids and database join filters all compare short hashes instead of whole objects. When the hash is cryptographic the "random choice" is replaced by the hardness assumption. When it isn't, the failure probability comes from the analysis above.
One pattern behind all four. A random evaluation point makes two different objects look different with high probability. Two different polynomials of degree d agree on at most d points (Schwartz–Zippel). Two different matrices send a random 0/1 vector to different images with probability ≥ ½. Two different keys land in different buckets of a random universal hash function with probability ≥ 1 − 1/m. A composite number fails a random base with probability ≥ ¾. Each gives a cheap test with one-sided error, and §16's amplification turns it into a test as reliable as you need.

19 Tail bounds: Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff, and when each applies

An expectation tells you the average and nothing about how often you are far from it. Tail bounds do. Markov needs only non-negativity and gives a weak linear bound. The progression from each to the next is what turns "quicksort takes O(n log n) in expectation" into "quicksort takes O(n log n) with probability 1 − 1/n^c", which is the statement you actually want when a service runs it a billion times a day.

How each is used in algorithm analysis

  • Markov: Las Vegas to Monte Carlo. Stopping a Las Vegas algorithm at 2·E[T] fails with probability ≤ ½ (§16). It is also the first step of every other bound.
  • Chebyshev: when only pairwise independence is available. Hash families that are only pairwise independent (cheap to evaluate) still give Chebyshev-strength concentration. That is enough for the median-of-means estimators in streaming algorithms, which take the median of independent Chebyshev-bounded means and get exponential confidence back.
  • Chernoff plus the union bound: "with high probability". Randomized quicksort's recursion depth is O(log n) with probability 1 − 1/n. Along any root-to-leaf path, each level is "good" (the pivot splits within the middle half) with probability ½ independently, and a path needs only about log_{4/3} n good levels. Chernoff bounds the chance that a path of c·log n levels has too few, and the union bound over the n leaves finishes it. The same two steps bound the maximum load in balls into bins (§20), the error of a sampled estimate, and the generalisation gap of a learned model.
The hypotheses are where these bounds go wrong in practice. Chernoff needs independence. Requests from one user, samples from one time series and clicks from one session are not independent, and a Chernoff interval computed as if they were is too narrow, sometimes absurdly so. Markov needs non-negativity. Chebyshev needs a finite variance, which heavy-tailed latencies and file sizes may not have. Before quoting "with probability 1 − 10⁻⁹", check which of the three hypotheses the data actually satisfies. The statistics series takes up the same issue for confidence intervals.

Where each threshold decides a design

  • Birthday: id and hash sizes. A 32-bit random request id collides after about 77 000 requests, a Tuesday morning's traffic, so random ids are 64 or 128 bits. Hash-based deduplication uses cryptographic-length digests for the same reason. A hash table must handle collisions even when nearly empty, because a table with m slots sees its first collision around √m keys (Hash Tables, "collisions are guaranteed").
  • Coupon collector: coverage. Random sampling needs about n ln n draws to see all n items. It sets the length of a random walk that covers a graph, the number of random probes to reach every server, and the lower bound in §03's Yao argument (the length of OPT's phase in random paging).
  • Max load: load balancing. Sending each request to a uniformly random server gives a busiest server with about ln n / ln ln n times the average load when the load is one request per server. Probing two servers and choosing the less busy one cuts that to ln ln n, which is effectively constant. Load balancers ("power of two random choices"), cuckoo hashing (two possible buckets per key, in Hash Tables) and distributed schedulers all use it.
Why two choices help so much. With one choice, a bin's load is approximately Poisson(1), and the maximum of n Poisson(1) variables is ln n / ln ln n. With two choices, a ball lands in a bin of load ≥ i + 1 only if both probes found load ≥ i. So the fraction of bins with load ≥ i + 1 is about the square of the fraction with load ≥ i. Squaring at each level makes the fractions fall doubly exponentially, and the maximum load is reached after only log log n levels. Every "random" choice on this page was made by a deterministic program: a pseudo-random generator (PRNG) that expands a small seed into a long sequence that passes statistical tests for randomness. What to use, and how to seed it

  • For simulation and ML: a modern small generator (the PCG family, xoshiro/xoroshiro, or a counter-based generator such as Philox), or the Mersenne Twister in older libraries. The Mersenne Twister has period 2¹⁹⁹³⁷ − 1 and good equidistribution, but a 2.5 KB state and detectable linear structure. Counter-based generators compute output k directly from (seed, k).   • For anything an adversary can see: a cryptographically secure generator from the operating system, or a stream cipher keyed from it. Session tokens, password-reset links, nonces, and the seed of a universal hash (§18) all belong here. A statistical PRNG is predictable from a few outputs: an LCG from three, the Mersenne Twister from 624.
  • Seeding: seed once, at start-up, and record the seed with the results so a run can be reproduced exactly. Don't reseed inside a loop, and never reseed from the clock in a tight loop, which makes consecutive "random" draws identical. For parallel work, give each worker an independent stream (jump-ahead, a split generator, or a counter-based generator with distinct keys) rather than seeds 1, 2, 3 of the same LCG, whose streams can be correlated.
  • Turning words into ranges: rand() % n is biased unless n divides the generator's range, and with an LCG it also uses the worst bits. Use rejection sampling (discard the top partial block), or multiply-and-shift using the high bits. Floats: take the top 53 bits and scale by 2⁻⁵³.
State size bounds what a generator can reach. A generator with b bits of state produces at most 2ᵇ distinct sequences, and so at most 2ᵇ distinct shuffles of a deck. A 52-card deck has 52! ≈ 2²²⁶ orderings. A shuffle driven by a 32-bit seed reaches at most a 2⁻¹⁹⁴ fraction of them, which is fine for a card game but not for a fairness guarantee. The generator on this page, mulberry32, has exactly 32 bits of state. That is ample for reproducible figures, and it is the reason it would be the wrong choice for a production shuffle that has to be unpredictable.

22 Shuffling: Fisher–Yates, and the one-character bug that biases it

To shuffle an array uniformly, walk from the last position down to the second and swap each position i with a uniformly random position in 0…i. That makes exactly n! equally likely paths of random choices, one per permutation, in Θ(n) time and n − 1 random draws. Draw j from 0…n − 1 at every step instead, and there are nⁿ equally likely paths spread over n! permutations. For n ≥ 3, nⁿ is not divisible by n!, so the shuffle is biased. The bug is a single character, and it went unnoticed in shipped software that dealt real cards.

Why the correct version is correct, in one line of counting

Fisher–Yates makes n − 1 independent choices, with n, n − 1, …, 2 options, for n! equally likely paths. Distinct paths give distinct permutations: the choice at step i is determined by which item ends at position i, so the path can be recovered from the result. That makes it a bijection between paths and permutations, and each permutation has probability 1/n!. The naive version's nⁿ paths can't be split evenly, which proves bias without computing it. At n = 3, 27 paths fall over 6 permutations as 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 4, and the readout shows this count.

Where a correct shuffle carries a guarantee. Shuffling is the bridge from probabilistic analysis to a randomized algorithm (§13). Shuffle the input and the hiring problem's Hₙ, quicksort's expected cost, and a random-order online algorithm's guarantee (§15) hold for every input. In ML, every epoch of stochastic gradient descent begins with a shuffle of the training set. A biased shuffle, or shuffling within fixed shards only, systematically presents some examples earlier than others, and the order effect shows up in the loss curve. Neural Network Training describes the minibatch loop that relies on it.

23 Reservoir sampling: a uniform sample of k from a stream of unknown length

Keep the first k items. For each later item i (numbering from 1), keep it with probability k/i, replacing a uniformly random member of the reservoir. When the stream ends, whenever that is, every item seen is in the reservoir with probability exactly k/N, and the reservoir is a uniform random k-subset. The algorithm uses O(k) memory and one pass. It is an online algorithm in the strictest sense: it doesn't know N until the stream ends, and it must be correct at every moment in between.

The two biased rules fail in opposite ways. Replacing with probability ½ is a recency sample: an item survives only if few replacements follow it, so the last few items dominate and the first ones almost never survive. Replacing with the fixed probability k/N needs N in advance, and even with N known it is wrong, because early items face many more chances of eviction than late ones. The first k items sit in the reservoir for free and survive with probability about 1/e, far above k/N. Every later item enters with probability only k/N and can still be evicted afterwards, so all of them are under-represented, those just after the first k most of all. Only the decreasing probability k/i exactly balances "joined later, so fewer evictions to survive" against "joined later, so less likely to join". Uses include sampling log lines for debugging, keeping a fixed-size random sample of a training stream, and maintaining a uniform replay buffer over an unbounded stream of experience in reinforcement learning.

A family of one-pass randomized algorithms. Reservoir sampling belongs with other streaming sketches that trade a small, controlled error for sublinear memory. Bloom filters answer membership with one-sided error (Hash Tables). Count-min sketches estimate frequencies with additive error. HyperLogLog counts distinct items from the maximum number of leading zeros among hashed values, a record statistic like §14's. Each is an online algorithm and a randomized one, and each is analysed with the tools on this page: indicators, linearity of expectation, and a Chernoff or Chebyshev bound.

25 Pitfalls

  • Quoting a randomized ratio without its adversary. "Marker is O(log k)-competitive" is true against an oblivious adversary and false against an adaptive one (§03). Say which.
  • Forgetting the additive constant, or letting it grow. ALG ≤ c·OPT + a with a fixed before σ is chosen. A "constant" that depends on the sequence length makes the definition empty (§02).
  • Reading a competitive ratio as a prediction. LRU's k is a worst case over all inputs. On real traces LRU is typically within a small factor of Belady. Measure on traces, and use the ratio to understand the failure modes (§08).
  • Using LFU without decay. Counts that never forget keep a stale working set forever. LFU is not competitive at all (§06).
  • Assuming a bigger FIFO cache can't hurt. Belady's anomaly (§09). Only stack algorithms, such as LRU and OPT, are guaranteed to improve with more frames.
  • Confusing probabilistic analysis with a randomized algorithm. An average over assumed-random inputs is broken by the one input an adversary or a real system provides. Shuffle first if you want the guarantee (§13, §22).
  • Assuming independence without justification. Linearity of expectation needs none. Variance additivity needs pairwise independence. Chernoff needs full independence. Most "high-probability" claims about real data fail at this step (§13, §19).
  • Estimating a rare event by naive simulation. With T runs you can't see probabilities below about 1/T. Bound them, or use importance sampling (§19).
  • Repetitions that share randomness. Amplification needs independent runs. Reseeding each run with the same seed, or with the clock inside a loop, gives the same run repeated (§16, §21).
  • Using rand() % n or the low bits of an LCG. Both give biased or short-period values (§21).
  • A shuffle with j drawn from the wrong range. [0, n − 1] gives a biased shuffle, and [0, i − 1] gives only cyclic permutations. The range is [0, i] (§22).
  • Shuffling by sorting with a random comparator. It is biased, and it can violate the sort's contract (§22).
  • A statistical PRNG where security matters. Tokens, nonces and hash seeds need a cryptographically secure generator (§21).
  • A secretary rule on non-random arrivals. The 1/e guarantee rests entirely on the uniformly random order (§15).
