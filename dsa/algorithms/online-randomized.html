Online & Randomized Algorithms

Every algorithm earlier in this series was handed its whole input before it started and did the same thing every time it ran. This page drops both assumptions. An online algorithm must answer each request as it arrives, without seeing the rest of the input, and is judged by its competitive ratio: how much worse it does than an all-knowing offline optimum on the worst input an adversary can build. A randomized algorithm flips coins on purpose, and is judged by its expected cost or its error probability on the worst input, with the coins as the only source of chance. The two ideas meet at once: against an adversary who must fix the input in advance, coin flips are what let an online algorithm beat every deterministic one, from ski rental's 2 down to e/(e − 1), and in paging from k down to about 2·ln k. The page works ski rental, paging with Belady's offline optimum, the marker algorithm, move-to-front and its potential-function proof, and online load balancing and matching. It then turns to probabilistic analysis with indicator random variables, the hiring and secretary problems, Las Vegas and Monte Carlo algorithms with amplification, Karger's min cut and Miller–Rabin, the Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff tail bounds, balls into bins, and the machinery of randomness itself: linear congruential generators and how they fail, Fisher–Yates and the classic shuffle bug, and reservoir sampling. Every fault count, ratio and success rate is measured by running the real routine on seeded random input.

01 Two ways to lose certainty: not knowing the future, and flipping coins

An online algorithm doesn't know what comes next. A randomized algorithm picks its next step with a coin flip. The first is something the problem forces on you. The second is a choice you make. The two ideas belong on one page because the choice is often the best answer to the constraint. A cache has to decide what to evict now, before it knows which page will be asked for next. A ski renter has to decide whether to buy today without knowing when the season ends. A hiring manager has to accept or reject each candidate before meeting the rest. None of them can be optimal on every input, because the input that punishes a given decision is always possible. What they can do is guarantee a bounded loss compared with someone who knew everything in advance. Where a deterministic rule can be exploited by an adversary who knows it, a randomized rule can hide its next move and push that bounded loss lower.

Compared against OPT(σ), the cost of the best OFFLINE solution that saw σ whole. RANDOMISED the algorithm reads random bits r in addition to its input x; its cost (or its answer) is a random variable. Judged by max over x of E_r[ cost(x, r) ] — expectation over the COINS, worst case over the INPUT or max over x of P_r[ wrong answer on x ] — error probability, again worst case over the input PROBABILISTIC ANALYSIS (a different thing): a DETERMINISTIC algorithm, run on an input drawn from an assumed distribution — E_x[ cost(x) ]. Where it sits in the series

Algorithm Analysis introduced amortized cost and the potential method. §11 uses exactly that method to prove move-to-front 2-competitive, and the proof is a textbook application of the potential function. The same page's treatment of the average case is the probabilistic analysis of §13, done there for linear search and insertion sort. Greedy Algorithms gave the exchange argument, and §07 uses it to prove that evicting the page requested furthest in the future is optimal offline. Its list-scheduling 2-approximation becomes, unchanged, the (2 − 1/m)-competitive online load balancer of §12. It explains why the bound holds for every input, which is what makes the algorithm randomized rather than merely average-case. NP-Completeness & Approximation used a coin flip to satisfy 7/8 of the clauses of a 3-CNF formula in expectation, then derandomized it. That is the same expectation-and-indicator reasoning as §13, applied to an optimization problem. How to read the figures. Every number a figure displays comes from running the real routine: a cache simulator for the fault counts, a list that really moves items for move-to-front's cost, a shuffle whose random-choice paths are enumerated exhaustively for its bias heatmap. Every random experiment uses a seeded generator, so every reader sees the same picture. Wherever a figure checks a theorem (a competitive bound, a tail bound, the per-step potential inequality), it prints the check and a ✓ holds or ✗ VIOLATED marker, so a wrong claim on the page would show up as a failing check.

24 Where it shows up: caches, buffer pools, CDNs, and machine learning

The theory above picks out the right questions and bounds. Production systems then engineer around the model's gaps: exact LRU costs too much on every hit, pure recency is fooled by scans, pure frequency never forgets, and real workloads are neither adversarial nor uniform. The design lesson that recurs. The competitive ratio tells you what can go wrong: a cyclic scan, a trap for transpose, a stale frequency count. Practical policies are built to block exactly those inputs cheaply, with scan-resistant buffers, decaying counts and admission filters, while keeping close to LRU's behaviour on the common case. When a new policy is proposed, ask two questions: which adversarial pattern does it defend against, and what does it cost on every hit?

25 Pitfalls

  • Quoting a randomized ratio without its adversary. "Marker is O(log k)-competitive" is true against an oblivious adversary and false against an adaptive one (§03). Say which.
  • Forgetting the additive constant, or letting it grow. ALG ≤ c·OPT + a with a fixed before σ is chosen. A "constant" that depends on the sequence length makes the definition empty (§02).
  • Reading a competitive ratio as a prediction. LRU's k is a worst case over all inputs. On real traces LRU is typically within a small factor of Belady. Measure on traces, and use the ratio to understand the failure modes (§08).
  • Using LFU without decay. Counts that never forget keep a stale working set forever. LFU is not competitive at all (§06).
  • Assuming a bigger FIFO cache can't hurt. Belady's anomaly (§09). Only stack algorithms, such as LRU and OPT, are guaranteed to improve with more frames.
  • Confusing probabilistic analysis with a randomized algorithm. An average over assumed-random inputs is broken by the one input an adversary or a real system provides. Shuffle first if you want the guarantee (§13, §22).
  • Assuming independence without justification. Linearity of expectation needs none. Variance additivity needs pairwise independence. Chernoff needs full independence. Most "high-probability" claims about real data fail at this step (§13, §19).
  • Estimating a rare event by naive simulation. With T runs you can't see probabilities below about 1/T. Bound them, or use importance sampling (§19).
  • Repetitions that share randomness. Amplification needs independent runs. Reseeding each run with the same seed, or with the clock inside a loop, gives the same run repeated (§16, §21).
  • Using rand() % n or the low bits of an LCG. Both give biased or short-period values (§21).
  • A shuffle with j drawn from the wrong range. [0, n − 1] gives a biased shuffle, and [0, i − 1] gives only cyclic permutations. The range is [0, i] (§22).
  • Shuffling by sorting with a random comparator. It is biased, and it can violate the sort's contract (§22).
  • A statistical PRNG where security matters. Tokens, nonces and hash seeds need a cryptographically secure generator (§21).
  • A secretary rule on non-random arrivals. The 1/e guarantee rests entirely on the uniformly random order (§15).
