02 Online problems and the competitive ratio

An online algorithm is c-competitive if, on every request sequence, its cost is at most c times the optimal offline cost plus a constant that does not depend on the sequence. The comparison is with OPT because the absolute cost says nothing: an adversary can always force an online algorithm to pay a lot, but on those inputs OPT pays a lot too. The ratio isolates the cost of not knowing the future. It is a worst-case measure over inputs, just like the worst-case running time of Algorithm Analysis, but what it measures is the value of information rather than time.

Why the additive constant is there, and when it matters

Paging shows why the constant is needed. A cache starts empty, so any algorithm pays k cold misses to fill it, and OPT pays them too. But take a sequence long enough to fill the cache and no longer. OPT pays k, the online algorithm pays k, and the ratio is 1. Now add requests: OPT may pay nothing more while the online algorithm pays k more. Without an additive term, the ratio of a short sequence would be decided by warm-up effects that have nothing to do with the policy. The constant a absorbs those start-up costs. What it may not do is grow with the sequence: a is fixed first, and then the inequality must hold for all σ, however long. That order of quantifiers is the whole content of the definition. When the problem lets an adversary scale costs (multiply every price by 1 000), any fixed a becomes negligible, and the strict and non-strict ratios coincide. For ski rental they coincide. For paging, the lower-bound argument of §08 is built to make OPT's cost grow without bound, so it defeats any fixed a.

What the ratio measures, and what it misses

  • It is pessimistic by design. The ratio is a maximum over all inputs, and the worst input for paging is a cyclic scan of k + 1 pages that no real program issues in a loop forever. LRU's ratio of k is tight, yet on real traces LRU usually faults within a few percent of Belady. This gap between competitive ratio and observed behaviour is the best-known criticism of the measure. The refinements that address it change the model: resource augmentation gives the online algorithm more memory than OPT (§08), access graphs restrict which sequences are legal, and diffuse adversaries draw inputs from a restricted family of distributions.
  • It ignores running time. An online algorithm may take exponential time per request and still be competitive. The model charges only for decisions. In practice every algorithm on this page runs in O(1) or O(k) per request, and the practical variants of §24 exist to make that O(1).
  • It compares with the offline optimum, not with the best online rule. "LRU is k-competitive and so is FIFO" says they have the same worst case, not that they behave alike. §08's figure measures how different they are on typical inputs, and §09 shows a behaviour of FIFO that LRU cannot have.
  • It is not the regret of online learning. Online learning (bandits, expert advice, online gradient descent) compares against the best fixed action in hindsight, and measures the additive gap, called regret, which should grow sub-linearly in T. Competitive analysis compares with the best adaptive sequence of actions in hindsight, and measures a ratio. The two frameworks answer different questions, and §24 comes back to where each belongs.
A three-step recipe you will see in every section. (1) Upper bound: show that on every sequence ALG ≤ c·OPT + a, usually by charging each of ALG's costs to something OPT must also pay. The tools are phases for paging and a potential function for list update. (2) Lower bound: exhibit an adversary that forces every algorithm of the class to pay c·OPT. (3) If the two match, the ratio is tight and the problem is settled. If they don't, the gap is a research question. Ski rental, deterministic paging and list update are settled. Randomized paging is settled by a different algorithm than the marker (§10). Online bipartite matching with a randomized algorithm is settled at 1 − 1/e (§12).

03 Deterministic against randomized, and the three adversaries

For a deterministic algorithm there is only one adversary: whoever knows the algorithm can simulate it, so the whole worst-case input can be written down in advance. For a randomized algorithm, the answer depends on what the adversary sees of the coin flips, and three standard models give three different competitive ratios for the same algorithm. Getting the model right is not a formality. The marker algorithm is 2H_k-competitive against one model and no better than k-competitive against another.

the three adversary models, weakest firstOBLIVIOUS writes the whole sequence σ in advance, knowing ALG's code but NOT its random bits. Measure: E[ALG(σ)] ≤ c·OPT(σ) + a. The standard model; the default whenever a paper says "randomised". ADAPTIVE ONLINE chooses σₜ after seeing ALG's responses to σ₁…σₜ₋₁ (so it has learnt the coins used so far), but must serve σ itself ONLINE, committing as it goes. ADAPTIVE OFFLINE chooses σₜ adaptively as above, and is charged the OPTIMAL OFFLINE cost of the finished σ. the two facts that make the models matter(1) against an ADAPTIVE OFFLINE adversary randomisation is useless: if a randomised algorithm is c-competitive against it, then some DETERMINISTIC algorithm is c-competitive. (2) if ALG is c-competitive against ADAPTIVE ONLINE adversaries and some algorithm is d-competitive against OBLIVIOUS ones, then some DETERMINISTIC algorithm is (c·d)-competitive.

Why an oblivious adversary is the right model for a cache

An adaptive adversary can see the cache contents and ask for whatever just left the cache. In that model, a randomized cache does exactly as badly as a deterministic one: by fact (1), nothing is gained. But a program's memory accesses aren't chosen by an opponent who watches the replacement hardware's random number generator. They are fixed by the program and its input. So the oblivious model describes the real situation. The one real exception is security. A timing attack that probes which lines were evicted is an adaptive adversary, and caches designed to resist such attacks are analysed in that stronger model. Hash tables have the same split: a seeded random hash function defeats an adversary who knows the code but not the seed, and fails against one who can recover the seed. That is why universal hashing keeps its seed secret, and why a fixed hash function in a web server is a denial-of-service risk (§18).

Yao's principle: the one tool for randomized lower bounds

An upper bound for a randomized algorithm is an expectation calculation. A lower bound must hold against every possible randomized algorithm, which looks like a statement about an infinite family of probability distributions. Yao's minimax principle reduces it to a statement about deterministic algorithms on one input distribution chosen by you:

Yao's principle (minimax for online algorithms)for ANY distribution D over request sequences, and ANY randomised online algorithm R: max over σ of E_R[ R(σ) ] / OPT(σ) ≥ min over deterministic A of E_D[ A(σ) ] / E_D[ OPT(σ) ] so: pick a hard distribution D, show every DETERMINISTIC algorithm pays ≥ c·E_D[OPT] on it, and c is a lower bound for every RANDOMISED algorithm against the oblivious adversary. (holds up to the additive constant, for long sequences)

Choose the distribution well and the deterministic calculation becomes simple. For paging it is "each request is a uniformly random page among k + 1". Whatever a deterministic algorithm holds, the requested page is missing with probability exactly 1/(k + 1), so it faults on a 1/(k + 1) fraction of requests. OPT, meanwhile, faults once per phase, the stretch until all k + 1 pages have been requested. Starting from one page, collecting the other k is a coupon-collector time (§20) of (k + 1)·(1/k + 1/(k − 1) + … + 1) = (k + 1)·H_k requests on average. So OPT faults once per (k + 1)·H_k requests, the deterministic algorithm faults H_k times in the same stretch, and the ratio is H_k. No randomized paging algorithm beats H_k ≈ ln k against an oblivious adversary. The same principle gives the e/(e − 1) lower bound for ski rental (§05) and the 1 − 1/e bound for online matching (§12). The principle is the online analogue of von Neumann's minimax theorem: in a zero-sum game between the algorithm designer and the input chooser, a mixed strategy on one side can be met with a pure strategy on the other.

Read "randomized competitive ratio" with the model attached. "Marker is 2H_k-competitive" means against an oblivious adversary. Against an adaptive online adversary it is no better than k, because that adversary can always request an evicted page. Every randomized ratio on this page is for the oblivious adversary unless the text says otherwise, and the paging figure of §08 builds its adversarial sequence against the deterministic policy it is attacking. When the policy under test is the marker, it builds the sequence against LRU, because an oblivious adversary can't see the marker's coins.

04 Ski rental: the break-even rule, and why 2 − 1/B is the best any deterministic rule can do

Renting skis costs 1 a day and buying them costs B. You don't know how many days you will ski, and the season ends when an adversary says so. The rule "rent until the rent paid would reach the price, then buy" pays at most 2 − 1/B times the optimum, and no deterministic rule can guarantee less. Ski rental is the model for every rent-or-buy decision. Should a laptop spin down its disk after an idle period (spinning up costs energy, staying up costs power)? Should a TCP connection be kept open or closed? When should a thread stop spinning on a lock and block? When should a cloud workload move from on-demand instances to a reserved one? Should a key-value store keep a hot object in fast memory? All have the same structure: a small recurring cost, a large one-off cost that ends the recurring one, and an unknown horizon.

the modeleach day you ski, you either RENT (cost 1) or BUY (cost B, integer ≥ 1; after that every day is free). the adversary chooses d ≥ 1, the number of days, and reveals it only by stopping. OPT(d) = min(d, B) — rent every day if d < B, buy on day 1 otherwise a deterministic online rule is just a day t on which to buy: "rent on days 1…t−1, buy on day t" ALG_t(d) = d if d < t (the season ended before you bought) = (t − 1) + B if d ≥ t the break-even rule t = B, and its ratiod < B : ALG = d = OPT ratio 1 d ≥ B : ALG = (B − 1) + B = 2B − 1, OPT = B ratio (2B − 1)/B = 2 − 1/B so ALG_B(d) ≤ (2 − 1/B)·OPT(d) for every d — STRICTLY (2 − 1/B)-competitive, no additive constant needed the lower bound: every t loses at least that muchthe adversary stops on day d = t — the day the rule buys — so ALG pays (t − 1) + B for skis used once. t ≤ B : OPT = t, ratio (t − 1 + B)/t = 1 + (B − 1)/t ≥ 1 + (B − 1)/B = 2 − 1/B (decreasing in t, so smallest at t = B) t ≥ B : OPT = B, ratio (t − 1 + B)/B ≥ (2B − 1)/B = 2 − 1/B (increasing in t, so smallest at t = B) ⟹ min over t of max over d of ALG_t(d)/OPT(d) = 2 − 1/B, attained only at t = B. (never buying: ratio d/B → ∞)

The lower-bound argument is short and is the typical online lower bound. A deterministic rule is fully described by one number, t. The adversary can simulate it, so it knows t and ends the season exactly when the purchase has become a waste. The figure's "worst" mode evaluates that argument directly. For every t from 1 to 3B, it tries every stop day d, records the worst ratio, and plots it. The curve has a single minimum, at t = B, with height 2 − 1/B. Buying too early (t < B) is punished by a season that ends the day you buy. Buying too late (t > B) is punished by a season that goes on: you've paid more rent than the skis cost, and then you buy them anyway. The break-even point balances the two regrets. The balance is what gives the ratio of about 2: the worst case pays for the purchase twice, once in rent and once at the till.

What the problem teaches beyond skis

  • Balance the regrets. Nearly every good deterministic online rule is found by setting the cost of acting too early equal to the cost of acting too late. Break-even for ski rental, "spin down after the idle time whose power cost equals a spin-up", "block after spinning for one context switch's worth of time": all three are ski rental with different prices.
  • Continuous time changes nothing essential. With a continuous horizon and rental rate 1, buying at time B is exactly 2-competitive, and the lower bound is 2. The −1/B comes from discrete days. The rule and the proof are the same.
  • Prediction helps, if you are careful. With a forecast of the season length, a rule can buy earlier when the forecast says "long". Learning-augmented algorithms do this while keeping a worst-case guarantee. For ski rental, a tunable parameter trades a ratio near 1 when the forecast is right against a bounded ratio when it is wrong. It is the same trade between trusting data and staying robust that runs through the ML pages.
The general shape. Rent-or-buy is the simplest problem where an online algorithm must decide when to make an irreversible commitment. Once you see ski rental in "when to stop waiting", "when to rebuild the index", "when to migrate the page" or "when to buy the reserved instance", you also know the answer: act once the waiting has cost about what acting would cost, and expect at worst twice the optimum.

05 Randomized ski rental: e/(e − 1) ≈ 1.582 against an oblivious adversary

If the buy day is drawn from a carefully shaped distribution, an adversary who knows the distribution but not the draw can't pick a bad stop day, because every stop day costs the same in expectation. The resulting expected ratio is 1/(1 − (1 − 1/B)^B), which decreases towards e/(e − 1) ≈ 1.582 as B grows, and no randomized rule does better. The improvement from 2 to 1.582 is the first concrete example of what hiding your strategy is worth.

the optimal distribution over buy days, for integer BP(buy on day i) = pᵢ = ((B − 1)/B)^(B − i) / ( B · (1 − (1 − 1/B)^B) ) for i = 1, 2, …, B — geometric, growing towards day B: the rule is most likely to buy late, least likely on day 1. E[ALG(d)] / OPT(d) = 1 / (1 − (1 − 1/B)^B) for EVERY stop day d — the adversary is made indifferent → 1 / (1 − 1/e) = e/(e − 1) ≈ 1.5820 as B → ∞ (continuous version: density eˣ/(e − 1) on [0, 1] in units of B) why no randomised rule can do better (Yao)choose the stop day d at random, with P(d) shaped so that EVERY deterministic buy day t has the same expected cost ratio; that common value is again e/(e − 1) in the limit — the two distributions are the two halves of a minimax equilibrium.

The "distribution" mode of the §04 figure draws pᵢ and then evaluates the expected ratio against every stop day from 1 to 4B. The readout prints the minimum and maximum of those ratios, and they agree to the last digit shown. That is the equalising property: a distribution that makes the adversary indifferent is the optimal randomized strategy, just as in a mixed-strategy equilibrium of a two-player game. The "ratio" mode draws the same expected ratio as a flat violet line under the break-even rule's two-level step. The rule gives up a little on short seasons, where it sometimes buys too early, to save a lot on the stop day the adversary would otherwise choose.

What the oblivious assumption is doing

An adaptive adversary who watches the rule's decision day by day beats randomization completely. It ends the season on the day the rule buys, whichever day that turns out to be, and the ratio goes back to at least 2 − 1/B. The oblivious adversary must fix d before the coin is flipped, and that fixing is what the expectation averages over. For real rent-or-buy decisions the oblivious model fits: the weather does not observe your purchase, and a program's idle periods do not depend on when the disk spun down. But the model is doing real work, and a paper that claims a randomized competitive ratio should always say which adversary it assumes.

Where the number e/(e − 1) keeps coming back. The same constant 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632 (its reciprocal) is the optimal ratio for randomized online bipartite matching (§12), the greedy guarantee for monotone submodular maximisation, and the randomized competitive ratio of TCP acknowledgement and of snoopy caching. It keeps appearing because of the geometric distribution that equalises two linear costs. The secretary problem's 1/e (§15) is related but different, and the two are easy to confuse.
