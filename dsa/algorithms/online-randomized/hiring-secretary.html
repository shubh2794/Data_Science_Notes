13 Probabilistic analysis, randomized algorithms, and indicator random variables

Probabilistic analysis assumes the input is random and averages a deterministic algorithm's cost over it. A randomized algorithm makes its own randomness and averages over its coins, so its guarantee holds for every input. The main calculation tool for both is the same: write the cost as a sum of 0/1 indicator variables and use linearity of expectation, which holds even when the indicators are dependent. Almost every expected-value result on the rest of this page is two lines once the right indicators are chosen.

Three quick examples

  • Fixed points of a random permutation. Let Iᵢ indicate that item i stays in position i. Then P = 1/n, so the expected number of fixed points is n · 1/n = 1 for every n. The indicators are dependent (if n − 1 items are fixed, so is the last one), and it doesn't matter.
  • Collisions among n keys in m slots. One indicator per pair, each with probability 1/m under uniform hashing, gives E[collisions] = C(n, 2)/m. Set it to 1 and you get the birthday threshold n ≈ √(2m) (§20). The same sum gives the expected chain length in hash-table chaining.
  • Clauses satisfied by a random assignment. A clause with three distinct literals is false with probability 1/8, so the expected number of satisfied clauses is 7m/8. That one line is the approximation guarantee in NP-Completeness, §24.

The two kinds of analysis give the same formula for insertion sort's average and for randomized quicksort's expectation. The difference is who guarantees the randomness. Algorithm Analysis, §06 averages insertion sort over random inputs. The result is true of random data and says nothing about the nearly sorted or reverse-sorted data real systems produce. Randomized quicksort's 2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n is an average over its own pivot choices. It holds on sorted input, on adversarial input, on every input, because the algorithm makes the randomness itself. When you can shuffle the input yourself (§22), the weaker kind of analysis turns into the stronger kind of guarantee.

The one discipline indicator arguments need. Name the sample space and the probability measure before writing P(Eᵢ). "The probability that candidate i is hired" means nothing until you say what is random. In §14 it is the order of arrival, assumed or enforced to be uniform over all n! orders. Most wrong expectations come from computing P(Eᵢ) under one model and applying it in another. The statistics series covers the underlying probability: expectation, the law of large numbers that makes Monte Carlo estimates converge, and the Monte Carlo method itself.

14 The hiring problem: in random order, the number of hires is Hₙ ≈ ln n

Interview n candidates one at a time and hire each one who is better than everyone seen so far, firing the previous hire. In the worst order, increasing quality, every candidate is hired. In a uniformly random order the expected number of hires is Hₙ = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n ≈ ln n + 0.577, which is about 5 for a hundred candidates and about 7.5 for a thousand. The problem is a model for any "replace the current best" update: tracking a running maximum, updating a best-so-far model checkpoint, and the number of times a max-heap's root changes as items stream in.

The same count, in other places

  • Left-to-right maxima. The number of times the running maximum changes while scanning a random array is Hₙ. That is the number of writes to best in the textbook max loop, so the assignment is executed O(log n) times on average even though the comparison runs n − 1 times.
  • Records in data. In a series of independent, identically distributed measurements, the expected number of record highs in n observations is Hₙ, whatever the distribution. That is why "the hottest year on record" should arrive about ln n times in n years of a stable climate, and why many more records than that are evidence of a trend.
  • Treap and random BST depth. A node's depth in a random binary search tree is a sum of such record indicators, giving 2 ln n expected depth. The analysis of randomized quicksort's comparisons in Sorting & Searching is the same argument applied to pairs.
Online, but not competitive. The hiring problem is online (each hire happens on arrival), but the question asked is about cost in a random order, not about competing with an optimum. The next section keeps the same setting and asks the competitive question: with one irrevocable hire, how often can you get the best candidate?

15 The secretary problem: observe n/e, then take the next best, and succeed with probability → 1/e

You may hire exactly one of n candidates who arrive in uniformly random order, you only learn how each compares with those before it, and every decision is final. The rule "reject the first r, then accept the first candidate better than all of those" picks the best candidate with probability (r/n)·Σᵢ₌ᵣ₊₁ⁿ 1/(i − 1). This is maximised near r = n/e, where it tends to 1/e ≈ 0.368. No rule of any kind does better. The constant is independent of n: whether there are ten candidates or ten million, you get the very best about 37% of the time.

the success probability of cutoff rSUCCESS ⟺ for some i > r: candidate i is the overall best AND the best of the first i − 1 is among the first r (otherwise some j with r < j < i would have been a new record and been accepted first) P(candidate i is the best) = 1/n; given that, P(best of the first i − 1 lies in the first r) = r/(i − 1) P_success(r) = Σᵢ₌ᵣ₊₁ⁿ (1/n)·(r/(i − 1)) = (r/n)·Σᵢ₌ᵣ₊₁ⁿ 1/(i − 1) ≈ (r/n)·ln(n/r) write x = r/n: f(x) = −x ln x, f'(x) = −ln x − 1 = 0 ⟹ x = 1/e, f(1/e) = 1/e ≈ 0.3679 the model assumptions, all of them neededORDER uniformly random (an adversarial order defeats every rule: P(success) ≤ 1/n) DECISIONS irrevocable and immediate; ONLY RELATIVE RANKS observed (with known values, better rules exist) GOAL = the single best (maximising the expected RANK or VALUE is a different problem with different answers)

Why no rule beats 1/e, and the variants that matter

Any optimal rule may be taken to accept only candidates who are the best seen so far, since accepting anyone else guarantees failure. Whether candidate i is a new record is independent of the relative order of the earlier candidates, so the optimal decision at i depends only on i. Backward induction then shows the optimal rule is a threshold: reject everyone up to some r, then accept the first record. The threshold rule is therefore optimal, and its best value tends to 1/e. Among the variants: the multiple-choice secretary (hire k, maximise the total value) achieves 1 − O(1/√k) of the optimum. The matroid secretary problem generalises to structured choices, like the matroids of Greedy Algorithms. The prophet inequality (values drawn from known distributions, arriving in adversarial order) guarantees half the expected maximum with a single threshold. That result underlies posted-price mechanisms in online markets.

Secretary against ski rental. Both are online, and both have an e in the answer, but the models are opposite. Ski rental faces an adversary and uses randomness to hide; its e/(e − 1) comes from equalising costs against every stop day. The secretary problem assumes a random order, and its algorithm is deterministic; its 1/e comes from maximising −x ln x. With an adversarial order the secretary problem is hopeless, with success at most 1/n. The random-order assumption is the model, and on real hiring or house-hunting data it is exactly the part to question.
