16 Las Vegas and Monte Carlo: random time or random correctness, and amplification by repetition

A Las Vegas algorithm is always correct, and only its running time is random. A Monte Carlo algorithm always finishes within its time bound, but may be wrong with some bounded probability. Repeating a Monte Carlo algorithm independently drives its error down exponentially, and a Las Vegas algorithm stopped at a multiple of its expected time becomes Monte Carlo. The two classes are interchangeable once you can either check an answer or bound a running time.

Why a small error probability is not a compromise

An error probability of 2⁻¹⁰⁰ is far below the probability that a cosmic ray flips a bit in the machine during the computation, or that the compiler has a bug. Past that point, "deterministic" and "correct with probability 1 − 2⁻¹⁰⁰" are the same in engineering terms, and the randomized algorithm is usually simpler and faster. What makes the small error trustworthy is independence of the repetitions. Each run must use fresh random bits, and the error bound must hold for every input, not only on average over inputs. That is why a Monte Carlo guarantee is stated as max over x of P_r(wrong on x). The input can't be chosen to make the repetitions fail together, because the only randomness is the algorithm's own.

Complexity classes, for orientation

  • ZPP: problems with Las Vegas algorithms running in expected polynomial time. Equivalently, RP ∩ co-RP.
  • RP: polynomial-time Monte Carlo with one-sided error. A no-instance is always rejected, and a yes-instance is accepted with probability ≥ ½. Polynomial identity testing is the famous member not known to be in P.
  • BPP: two-sided error ≤ 1/3 (any constant below ½, by amplification). It is widely conjectured that BPP = P: every efficient randomized algorithm can be derandomized with only polynomial slowdown, given sufficiently good pseudo-random generators. Primality testing moved from co-RP (Miller–Rabin, §18) to P (AKS) and is the flagship example. In practice, randomized algorithms remain faster and simpler, which is why they are used. See NP-Completeness, §18 for the surrounding classes.
Say which one you have. A code review of a randomized routine should get a one-line answer to "what is random: the time, or the answer?" If it is the answer, the next question is "what is the error per run, is it one-sided, and how many repetitions does the caller rely on?" A probabilistic data structure that silently returns a wrong answer, such as a Bloom filter's false positive (Hash Tables), is a Monte Carlo component, and its error rate is part of the interface.

17 Randomized quicksort, quickselect and Karger's min cut

Randomized quicksort and quickselect are Las Vegas: always correct, with expected Θ(n log n) and Θ(n) comparisons on every input. Karger's contraction algorithm is Monte Carlo: it finds a minimum cut with probability at least 2/(n(n − 1)), which looks hopeless until amplification turns roughly n² ln n repetitions into success with probability 1 − 1/n. The first pair shows randomization removing a worst case. Karger shows randomization giving a much simpler algorithm than any deterministic one.

On two dense clusters joined by two edges, the single-run success rate is far above the guarantee: most random edges lie inside a cluster, so contracting them is harmless. On a cycle, every contraction sequence ends with a cut of size 2, and every one of the C(n, 2) pairs of edges is a minimum cut. That is the extreme case of the corollary, with the maximum possible number of minimum cuts.

Why Karger is worth knowing even though it is slow

A single run costs O(n²) with an adjacency-matrix implementation, so the full O(n² log n) repetitions cost O(n⁴ log n). That is worse than running a max-flow per vertex pair. The recursive variant (Karger–Stein) observes that the early contractions are safe and the late ones are dangerous. It contracts down to n/√2 vertices once, then recurses twice on the result, for a success probability of Ω(1/log n) per run and O(n² log³ n) total. The algorithm's lasting value is its argument. The counting corollary (at most C(n, 2) minimum cuts, and at most n^(2α) cuts within a factor α of the minimum) is used throughout network reliability and graph sparsification, and it was first proved by exactly this probabilistic reasoning. The deterministic polynomial-time min-cut algorithms (max-flow based, in Shortest Paths, MST & Flow) solve the problem; Karger explains its combinatorics.

For quicksort, see the dedicated page. The full derivation of 2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n, the recurrence and the pair argument, and a Monte Carlo check are in Sorting & Searching, §18. Pivot choice, median-of-three, and the introsort fallback that caps the worst case at O(n log n) are in its §16 and §19. What this page adds is the classification: randomized quicksort is Las Vegas, its bound is over the coins for every input, and that is why a production quicksort that picks pivots randomly (or with a seeded hash) can't be forced into Θ(n²) by a crafted input, while a fixed "first element" pivot can.

18 Fingerprints, Miller–Rabin primality, and randomized hashing

The most-used randomized algorithms check equality or membership by comparing short random fingerprints instead of the objects themselves. Two different objects collide only with small probability over the random choice. Miller–Rabin tests primality with error at most 4⁻ᵗ after t rounds. Freivalds checks a matrix product in O(n²) time instead of recomputing it. Universal hashing makes every sequence of keys behave, in expectation, like random keys. All are one-sided Monte Carlo algorithms, or Las Vegas ones built on them.

What makes each of these correct, and what breaks them

  • Primality testing is exponential by trial division. Checking divisors up to √n takes 2^(b/2) steps for a b-bit number, exponential in the input size, as NP-Completeness, §02 explains. Miller–Rabin is polynomial in b, and it is what every cryptographic library uses to generate RSA primes. The deterministic AKS test proved primality is in P but is far slower in practice. The randomized test with 40 or so rounds is the engineering answer.
  • The Fermat test alone is not enough. Checking aⁿ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod n) is fooled by Carmichael numbers, such as 561, for every base coprime to n. The square-root step of Miller–Rabin is what makes the "¼ of bases" bound hold for every composite.
  • Universal hashing needs a secret seed. The guarantee is over the random choice of h. If an attacker knows h, they can send keys that all collide, and a hash table degrades to a list with Θ(n) per operation. This is the algorithmic-complexity attack that led language runtimes to seed their string hashes randomly at start-up. The analysis and the constructions are in Hash Tables, universal hashing, and perfect hashing uses a Las Vegas retry loop on top.
  • Fingerprints are everywhere. Content-addressed storage, deduplication, rsync's rolling checksum, Git's object ids and database join filters all compare short hashes instead of whole objects. When the hash is cryptographic the "random choice" is replaced by the hardness assumption. When it isn't, the failure probability comes from the analysis above.
One pattern behind all four. A random evaluation point makes two different objects look different with high probability. Two different polynomials of degree d agree on at most d points (Schwartz–Zippel). Two different matrices send a random 0/1 vector to different images with probability ≥ ½. Two different keys land in different buckets of a random universal hash function with probability ≥ 1 − 1/m. A composite number fails a random base with probability ≥ ¾. Each gives a cheap test with one-sided error, and §16's amplification turns it into a test as reliable as you need.

19 Tail bounds: Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff, and when each applies

An expectation tells you the average and nothing about how often you are far from it. Tail bounds do. Markov needs only non-negativity and gives a weak linear bound. Chebyshev needs the variance and gives a quadratic one. Chernoff needs a sum of independent bounded variables and gives an exponential one. The progression from each to the next is what turns "quicksort takes O(n log n) in expectation" into "quicksort takes O(n log n) with probability 1 − 1/n^c", which is the statement you actually want when a service runs it a billion times a day.

How each is used in algorithm analysis

  • Markov: Las Vegas to Monte Carlo. Stopping a Las Vegas algorithm at 2·E[T] fails with probability ≤ ½ (§16). It is also the first step of every other bound.
  • Chebyshev: when only pairwise independence is available. Hash families that are only pairwise independent (cheap to evaluate) still give Chebyshev-strength concentration. That is enough for the median-of-means estimators in streaming algorithms, which take the median of independent Chebyshev-bounded means and get exponential confidence back.
  • Chernoff plus the union bound: "with high probability". Randomized quicksort's recursion depth is O(log n) with probability 1 − 1/n. Along any root-to-leaf path, each level is "good" (the pivot splits within the middle half) with probability ½ independently, and a path needs only about log_{4/3} n good levels. Chernoff bounds the chance that a path of c·log n levels has too few, and the union bound over the n leaves finishes it. The same two steps bound the maximum load in balls into bins (§20), the error of a sampled estimate, and the generalisation gap of a learned model.
The hypotheses are where these bounds go wrong in practice. Chernoff needs independence. Requests from one user, samples from one time series and clicks from one session are not independent, and a Chernoff interval computed as if they were is too narrow, sometimes absurdly so. Markov needs non-negativity. Chebyshev needs a finite variance, which heavy-tailed latencies and file sizes may not have. Before quoting "with probability 1 − 10⁻⁹", check which of the three hypotheses the data actually satisfies. The statistics series takes up the same issue for confidence intervals.

20 Balls into bins: the birthday bound, the coupon collector, and the maximum load

Throw balls independently and uniformly into m bins. Three questions about this one experiment give three thresholds that come up throughout algorithms. The first collision comes after about √m balls (the birthday bound). Every bin is non-empty after about m ln m balls (the coupon collector). With m balls, the fullest bin holds about ln m / ln ln m, and letting each ball choose the less loaded of two random bins drops that to about ln ln m / ln 2. Hash tables, load balancers, random sampling and distributed systems all run this experiment.

Where each threshold decides a design

  • Birthday: id and hash sizes. A 32-bit random request id collides after about 77 000 requests, a Tuesday morning's traffic, so random ids are 64 or 128 bits. Hash-based deduplication uses cryptographic-length digests for the same reason. A hash table must handle collisions even when nearly empty, because a table with m slots sees its first collision around √m keys (Hash Tables, "collisions are guaranteed").
  • Coupon collector: coverage. Random sampling needs about n ln n draws to see all n items. It sets the length of a random walk that covers a graph, the number of random probes to reach every server, and the lower bound in §03's Yao argument (the length of OPT's phase in random paging).
  • Max load: load balancing. Sending each request to a uniformly random server gives a busiest server with about ln n / ln ln n times the average load when the load is one request per server. Probing two servers and choosing the less busy one cuts that to ln ln n, which is effectively constant. Load balancers ("power of two random choices"), cuckoo hashing (two possible buckets per key, in Hash Tables) and distributed schedulers all use it.
Why two choices help so much. With one choice, a bin's load is approximately Poisson(1), and the maximum of n Poisson(1) variables is ln n / ln ln n. With two choices, a ball lands in a bin of load ≥ i + 1 only if both probes found load ≥ i. So the fraction of bins with load ≥ i + 1 is about the square of the fraction with load ≥ i. Squaring at each level makes the fractions fall doubly exponentially, and the maximum load is reached after only log log n levels.
