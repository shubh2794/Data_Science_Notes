21 Generating randomness: pseudo-random generators, linear congruential generators, and seeding

Every "random" choice on this page was made by a deterministic program: a pseudo-random generator (PRNG) that expands a small seed into a long sequence that passes statistical tests for randomness. The oldest family, the linear congruential generator x ← (a·x + c) mod m, is fast and tiny and fails in ways that matter. Its low bits have short periods, and its consecutive outputs lie on a small number of planes. Knowing how it fails tells you what to demand from any generator.

What to use, and how to seed it

  • For simulation and ML: a modern small generator (the PCG family, xoshiro/xoroshiro, or a counter-based generator such as Philox), or the Mersenne Twister in older libraries. The Mersenne Twister has period 2¹⁹⁹³⁷ − 1 and good equidistribution, but a 2.5 KB state and detectable linear structure. Counter-based generators compute output k directly from (seed, k). That makes them trivially parallel, which is why GPU frameworks use them for dropout masks and data augmentation.
  • For anything an adversary can see: a cryptographically secure generator from the operating system, or a stream cipher keyed from it. Session tokens, password-reset links, nonces, and the seed of a universal hash (§18) all belong here. A statistical PRNG is predictable from a few outputs: an LCG from three, the Mersenne Twister from 624.
  • Seeding: seed once, at start-up, and record the seed with the results so a run can be reproduced exactly. Don't reseed inside a loop, and never reseed from the clock in a tight loop, which makes consecutive "random" draws identical. For parallel work, give each worker an independent stream (jump-ahead, a split generator, or a counter-based generator with distinct keys) rather than seeds 1, 2, 3 of the same LCG, whose streams can be correlated.
  • Turning words into ranges: rand() % n is biased unless n divides the generator's range, and with an LCG it also uses the worst bits. Use rejection sampling (discard the top partial block), or multiply-and-shift using the high bits. Floats: take the top 53 bits and scale by 2⁻⁵³.
State size bounds what a generator can reach. A generator with b bits of state produces at most 2ᵇ distinct sequences, and so at most 2ᵇ distinct shuffles of a deck. A 52-card deck has 52! ≈ 2²²⁶ orderings. A shuffle driven by a 32-bit seed reaches at most a 2⁻¹⁹⁴ fraction of them, which is fine for a card game but not for a fairness guarantee. The generator on this page, mulberry32, has exactly 32 bits of state. That is ample for reproducible figures, and it is the reason it would be the wrong choice for a production shuffle that has to be unpredictable.

22 Shuffling: Fisher–Yates, and the one-character bug that biases it

To shuffle an array uniformly, walk from the last position down to the second and swap each position i with a uniformly random position in 0…i. That makes exactly n! equally likely paths of random choices, one per permutation, in Θ(n) time and n − 1 random draws. Draw j from 0…n − 1 at every step instead, and there are nⁿ equally likely paths spread over n! permutations. For n ≥ 3, nⁿ is not divisible by n!, so the shuffle is biased. The bug is a single character, and it went unnoticed in shipped software that dealt real cards.

Why the correct version is correct, in one line of counting

Fisher–Yates makes n − 1 independent choices, with n, n − 1, …, 2 options, for n! equally likely paths. Distinct paths give distinct permutations: the choice at step i is determined by which item ends at position i, so the path can be recovered from the result. That makes it a bijection between paths and permutations, and each permutation has probability 1/n!. The naive version's nⁿ paths can't be split evenly, which proves bias without computing it. At n = 3, 27 paths fall over 6 permutations as 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 4, and the readout shows this count.

Where a correct shuffle carries a guarantee. Shuffling is the bridge from probabilistic analysis to a randomized algorithm (§13). Shuffle the input and the hiring problem's Hₙ, quicksort's expected cost, and a random-order online algorithm's guarantee (§15) hold for every input. In ML, every epoch of stochastic gradient descent begins with a shuffle of the training set. A biased shuffle, or shuffling within fixed shards only, systematically presents some examples earlier than others, and the order effect shows up in the loss curve. Neural Network Training describes the minibatch loop that relies on it.

23 Reservoir sampling: a uniform sample of k from a stream of unknown length

Keep the first k items. For each later item i (numbering from 1), keep it with probability k/i, replacing a uniformly random member of the reservoir. When the stream ends, whenever that is, every item seen is in the reservoir with probability exactly k/N, and the reservoir is a uniform random k-subset. The algorithm uses O(k) memory and one pass. It is an online algorithm in the strictest sense: it doesn't know N until the stream ends, and it must be correct at every moment in between.

The two biased rules fail in opposite ways. Replacing with probability ½ is a recency sample: an item survives only if few replacements follow it, so the last few items dominate and the first ones almost never survive. Replacing with the fixed probability k/N needs N in advance, and even with N known it is wrong, because early items face many more chances of eviction than late ones. The first k items sit in the reservoir for free and survive with probability about 1/e, far above k/N. Every later item enters with probability only k/N and can still be evicted afterwards, so all of them are under-represented, those just after the first k most of all. Only the decreasing probability k/i exactly balances "joined later, so fewer evictions to survive" against "joined later, so less likely to join". Uses include sampling log lines for debugging, keeping a fixed-size random sample of a training stream, and maintaining a uniform replay buffer over an unbounded stream of experience in reinforcement learning.

A family of one-pass randomized algorithms. Reservoir sampling belongs with other streaming sketches that trade a small, controlled error for sublinear memory. Bloom filters answer membership with one-sided error (Hash Tables). Count-min sketches estimate frequencies with additive error. HyperLogLog counts distinct items from the maximum number of leading zeros among hashed values, a record statistic like §14's. Each is an online algorithm and a randomized one, and each is analysed with the tools on this page: indicators, linearity of expectation, and a Chernoff or Chebyshev bound.
