Parallel Algorithms

Every algorithm in this series so far has been priced on one processor: a single sequence of steps, one after the other, whose length is the running time. Parallel algorithms are priced on many processors at once, and the price has two numbers instead of one. The work T₁ is the total number of steps, exactly the serial cost; the span T∞ is the length of the longest chain of steps that must happen one after another, the time an unlimited number of processors would still need. Their ratio, the parallelism, is the most speedup the algorithm can ever show, and a greedy scheduler achieves close to it: T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞. This page builds that theory on the fork-join model (spawn, sync, parallel for), draws the computation DAG of real programs and schedules it on p processors, sets Amdahl's and Gustafson's laws beside it, shows why races make results depend on timing, and then works the classic parallel algorithms: reduction, the work-efficient prefix sum and what it unlocks, merge sort with a parallel merge, matrix multiplication by loops, by divide and conquer and on a systolic array, and level-synchronous BFS. The thread that runs through all of it is that span, not work, is what decides the speedup. Every work, span and step count on the page is measured by running the real routine under a tracer.

01 Why parallelism, and why "divide by p" is not the analysis

For about thirty years a program got faster every year without anyone touching it, because the clock got faster; that stopped in the mid-2000s, and since then the extra transistors each generation brings have been spent on more cores, wider vector units and more GPU lanes instead. Speed now comes from doing several things at once, and it has to be earned by the algorithm. The reason the clock stopped is power. Dynamic power in a chip scales roughly as capacitance × voltage² × frequency, and for decades each shrink let voltage fall along with feature size, so frequency could rise at constant power density. Once voltage could no longer fall — leakage current grows sharply as the threshold voltage drops — a higher clock meant a hotter chip, and a chip can only shed so many watts per square centimetre. Transistor counts kept doubling, so designers used them the only way that does not raise the clock: replicate the core.

The naive consequence is "with p cores, divide the running time by p". That is almost never what happens, and the reasons are the content of this page. First, not every step can run alongside every other: a step that needs another step's result must wait for it, so the dependency structure of the computation caps the speedup no matter how many processors there are. Second, someone has to decide which processor runs which step, and a bad schedule wastes processors even when the dependencies allow them to be used. Third, processors share memory, so two steps that each look correct can produce a wrong answer together if they touch the same location without coordination, and the coordination itself costs time. Fourth, some of every real program is serial, and that part does not shrink at all.

the analysis replaces one number with twoserial analysis T(n) one number: steps on one processor (Algorithm Analysis) parallel analysis T₁(n) = WORK total steps, i.e. the serial running time T∞(n) = SPAN longest chain of dependent steps T₁ / T∞ = PARALLELISM the most speedup any number of processors can give T_p the time on p processors, which depends on the SCHEDULE too the headline result (§07): max(T₁/p, T∞) ≤ T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞ for any greedy scheduler

Work tells you whether the algorithm is efficient at all — a parallel algorithm whose work is asymptotically larger than the best serial one loses on a small machine before it has a chance to win on a large one. Span tells you how far parallelism can take it. The two are computed by the same recurrence machinery as the serial analysis of Algorithm Analysis, with one change: where the serial recurrence adds the costs of two recursive calls, the span recurrence takes their maximum when the calls run in parallel. That single change is why a divide-and-conquer algorithm from Recursion, Divide & Conquer is the natural shape for a parallel algorithm: its subproblems are independent by construction.

What "processor" means here. The theory is stated for p identical processors sharing one memory, each executing one unit-cost instruction per time step. Real cores are close enough for the asymptotics; GPU lanes and SIMD units are processors that must execute the same instruction in lockstep (§23); a cluster adds communication costs the model leaves out (§21). Every bound on the page names the model it is proved in.

Two of these deserve a sentence each. GPUs are the purest test of the work/span idea: a GPU has tens of thousands of lanes, so an algorithm with parallelism below that simply cannot fill the machine, and an algorithm that is not work-efficient wastes it — the reason the work-efficient scan, not the naive one, is the GPU library primitive at block scale. Data-parallel training is Gustafson's law in action: training with more workers usually means a proportionally larger global batch, so each worker's compute stays constant and the question becomes whether the per-step all-reduce — the serial, communication-bound part — stays small relative to it. The page on distributed training takes that question from here.

The stragglers problem, in this page's terms. At the end of a parallel stage the slowest task is on the critical path, so one machine that is 3× slower than the rest makes the whole stage 3× slower whatever p is. That is the span law applied to a DAG whose vertex costs are not uniform, and the standard fix — launch a backup copy of a slow task and take whichever finishes first — spends extra work to shorten the span.
