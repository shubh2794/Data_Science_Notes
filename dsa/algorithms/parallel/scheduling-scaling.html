07 Greedy scheduling and Brent's bound

A scheduler is greedy if it never leaves a processor idle while a strand is ready: at each step it runs min(p, number of ready strands) of them. Every greedy scheduler, however it breaks ties, achieves T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞. Since the laws say T_p ≥ max(T₁/p, T∞) and a + b ≤ 2·max(a, b), a greedy schedule is always within a factor of 2 of the best possible one.

the theoremfor any DAG with work T₁ and span T∞, and any greedy schedule on p processors, T_p ≤ T₁ / p + T∞ (sharper: T_p ≤ (T₁ − T∞)/p + T∞ ) hence T_p ≤ 2 · T_p* where T_p* is the optimal schedule's length, and T₁/T∞ ≫ p ⟹ T_p ≈ T₁/p (near-perfect linear speedup) the proof: classify every step as complete or incompleteCOMPLETE step all p processors busy. Each executes p strands, and there are only T₁, so #complete ≤ ⌊T₁ / p⌋. INCOMPLETE step fewer than p ready strands, so the scheduler runs ALL of them. Let G' be the DAG of strands not yet executed. Every longest path in G' starts at a strand with no unexecuted predecessor — a READY strand — and all of those run, so the longest path in G' shrinks by one. Hence #incomplete ≤ T∞. TOTAL T_p = #complete + #incomplete ≤ T₁/p + T∞. ∎

The bound's history is worth one sentence. The original 1974 result was about PRAM computations with a known number of operations at each level: a computation of m operations and t levels runs on p processors in at most t + (m − t)/p steps — the sharper form above. The greedy-scheduler version for arbitrary DAGs is the same argument restated for a dynamic setting, and the reason fork-join programs can be written without mentioning p: the program fixes T₁ and T∞, and any greedy runtime converts them into a running time on whatever p turns up.

What the bound assumes. Unit-time strands (§04 makes this free), identical processors, and a scheduler that knows instantly which strands are ready and can hand them out at no cost. That last assumption is the unrealistic one — a centralised ready queue is a bottleneck on a many-core machine — and the next section is about the schedulers that give up exact greediness to remove it while keeping the bound in expectation.

08 Work stealing: the scheduler real runtimes use

In a work-stealing scheduler each worker keeps its own double-ended queue of ready tasks and works on the bottom of it like a serial program's call stack; only when its deque is empty does it become a thief, pick another worker uniformly at random, and steal the task at the top of that worker's deque. Randomised work stealing runs a fork-join computation in expected time T₁/p + O(T∞) — Brent's bound up to the constant on the span term — with no central queue at all.

the protocolon spawn of child C: push the CONTINUATION of the parent onto the bottom of my deque; run C now ("work-first": the child runs immediately, exactly as in the serial order) on return / sync: pop the bottom of my deque and resume it, if it is still there deque empty: choose a victim uniformly at random; steal the TOP of its deque; repeat on failure the guarantees, for fully strict fork-join computationstime E[T_p] = T₁/p + O(T∞) and the same with high probability, up to a log(1/ε) term steals E[#steal attempts] = O(p · T∞) — communication is proportional to the SPAN, not the work space S_p ≤ p · S₁ — each worker's stack is at most one serial stack deep

Why stealing from the top? The top of a deque holds the oldest continuation, which in a divide-and-conquer program is the one closest to the root of the spawn tree — the one with the most work still under it. A single steal therefore transfers a large piece of the computation, and the thief then runs it locally, pushing and popping its own deque without contention for a long time. Owners touch the bottom and thieves touch the top, so the common case needs no lock; only when the deque is nearly empty do the two ends meet, and that race is resolved with one atomic compare-and-swap. The upshot is that almost all of the T₁ work runs at serial speed with no synchronisation, and the coordination cost scales with T∞ — the same quantity that already bounds the running time.

The space bound is the other half of why the model is practical. A breadth-first scheduler that ran every ready task as soon as possible would, for P-FIB, materialise exponentially many live frames; work-first stealing keeps each worker on a depth-first path, so the total memory is at most p times what the serial program uses. p processors cost at most p stacks, and never more.

Where the model and the runtime part ways. The bound counts steal attempts as unit-time operations and ignores cache effects. In reality a stolen task's data is in another core's cache, so each steal costs a burst of cache misses — the reason grain size matters (§24) and the reason work stealing is paired with locality-aware tweaks such as stealing from a nearby core first. The asymptotics survive; the constants are where the engineering goes.

09 Amdahl's law and Gustafson's law

Amdahl's law is the span law for the crudest possible DAG: a fraction f of the serial running time cannot be parallelised at all, and the rest parallelises perfectly. Then T_p = f·T₁ + (1 − f)·T₁/p, and the speedup can never exceed 1/f however many processors you add. Gustafson's law asks a different question — not "how much faster is this problem?" but "how much bigger a problem can I solve in the same time?" — and gets a speedup that grows linearly in p.

Amdahl — fixed problem size (strong scaling)f = serial fraction of the ONE-processor running time S_p = T₁ / T_p = 1 / ( f + (1 − f)/p ) → 1/f as p → ∞ f = 0.05: S₁₆ = 1 / (0.05 + 0.95/16) ≈ 9.14 and never more than 20, even at a million cores Gustafson — problem grows with p (weak scaling)f = serial fraction of the p-PROCESSOR running time; the parallel part grows in proportion to p scaled speedup S_p = f + p·(1 − f) = p − f·(p − 1) — linear in p, slope 1 − f f = 0.05: S₁₆ = 16 − 0.05 · 15 = 15.25

The two laws do not contradict each other; they measure different things, and the difference is entirely in what f is a fraction of. Amdahl fixes the problem, so as p grows the parallel part shrinks and the serial part becomes an ever larger share of the time until it is all that is left. Gustafson fixes the time and lets the problem grow, which is how large machines are actually used: a weather model on a bigger computer runs at finer resolution, not faster; a larger GPU cluster trains a larger model. The serial part — reading the configuration, setting up, a final reduction — stays the same size while the parallel part grows with p, so its share of the work falls.

Amdahl is a special case of the span law, and a pessimistic one. In work/span terms, Amdahl's program has T∞ = f·T₁ + 1: the serial part is on the critical path and the parallel part adds one step. The span law gives S_p ≤ T₁/T∞ ≈ 1/f — the same ceiling. Real algorithms rarely have a fixed serial fraction; their span grows with n more slowly than their work, so their parallelism rises with problem size. That is Gustafson's observation in algorithmic form, and it is why work and span are the more useful pair of numbers.

10 Strong scaling and weak scaling

A scaling study is a graph of speedup or efficiency against p, and it comes in two kinds that answer the two laws' questions. Strong scaling fixes the total problem and asks how the time falls; weak scaling fixes the problem per processor and asks whether the time stays flat.

The work/span analysis predicts both from one pair of recurrences. For the parallel reduction, T₁ = Θ(n) and T∞ = Θ(log n). Strong scaling at fixed n: speedup rises until p ≈ n / log n and then flattens. Weak scaling with n = p·m: T_p ≈ m + log(p·m), so the time creeps up only logarithmically — near-perfect weak scaling. For merge sort with a serial merge, T∞ = Θ(n), so weak scaling is impossible: the time grows linearly in n = p·m whatever p is. Parallelism that does not grow with the problem is the signature of an algorithm that cannot scale.

Reporting a scaling result honestly. The baseline T₁ should be the best serial program, not the parallel program run on one core — the parallel version carries spawn overhead and extra work, and using it as the baseline inflates the speedup. Say which kind of scaling was measured, the problem size, and the efficiency, not just the speedup; an 80× speedup on 1 000 cores is 8 % efficiency.
