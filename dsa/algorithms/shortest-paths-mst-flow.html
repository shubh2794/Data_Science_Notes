Shortest Paths, MST & Flow

The weighted-graph problems are the three questions a graph with numbers on its edges is asked most: the cheapest route between vertices (shortest paths), the cheapest connected skeleton of the whole graph (the minimum spanning tree), and the most that can be pushed from a source to a sink through capacitated edges (maximum flow) — with the bottleneck that limits it (the minimum cut). This page builds each from one primitive, relaxation, and one invariant per algorithm: Dijkstra and exactly where it needs non-negative weights, Bellman-Ford and the negative cycle, one pass over a DAG, Floyd-Warshall and Johnson for all pairs, A* and what an admissible heuristic buys; the cut and cycle properties and the three MST algorithms with union-find built and its inverse-Ackermann bound explained; residual graphs, augmenting paths, the max-flow min-cut theorem, Ford-Fulkerson's pathology, Edmonds-Karp's bound, push-relabel, and the reductions — matching, disjoint paths, segmentation — that make flow the workhorse it is. Every count a figure shows is measured by running the real routine under a counter, and every answer is cross-checked against brute force in its readout.

01 What the weighted-graph problems are

A weighted graph is a graph G = (V, E) together with a weight function w : E → ℝ; the weight of a path is the sum of the weights of its edges; and the three weighted-graph problems are to find, from those sums, the cheapest path between vertices (shortest paths), the cheapest edge set that keeps every vertex connected (the minimum spanning tree), and the largest amount that can be routed from a source to a sink when each edge's weight is read as a capacity (maximum flow), together with the bottleneck that limits it (the minimum cut). Everything on the Graph Representations & Traversal page treated an edge as a unit step, so that "distance" meant edge count and BFS found it. One number per edge changes the questions, not the graph: the representations are the same adjacency lists with one more field, the traversals are the same loops with one more comparison, and the whole of this page is what that comparison — relaxation — does in the hands of a dozen algorithms.

The thesis: one primitive, one invariant per algorithm

The shortest-path algorithms differ far less than their reputations suggest. Each initialises d[s] = 0 and every other d[v] = ∞, then performs relaxations — nothing else ever changes a d value — and each is characterised entirely by the order in which it relaxes edges: Dijkstra relaxes the out-edges of vertices in increasing order of final distance, which its greedy extraction achieves only when weights are non-negative; Bellman-Ford relaxes every edge, V − 1 times over, in any order at all; the DAG algorithm relaxes each edge once, in topological order of its tail; A* relaxes in order of d + h. Because relaxation only ever lowers an estimate and never below the truth (§02, the upper-bound property), and because relaxing the edges of a shortest path in sequence forces the estimates along it to their true values whatever else happens in between (the path-relaxation property), a correctness proof for any of these algorithms is a proof that its order relaxes the edges of every shortest path in sequence. The spanning-tree algorithms have the same shape with a different lemma: each grows an edge set that is always contained in some minimum spanning tree, and the cut property says which edge is safe to add next. The flow algorithms have it too: each maintains a feasible flow and improves it along an augmenting path in the residual graph, and the max-flow min-cut theorem says that when no such path exists the flow is maximum. So the page has three invariants, not thirty, and each algorithm is an invariant plus a schedule plus a data structure that makes the schedule cheap.

What "weight" means, and what it does not

A weight is any real number attached to an edge; the algorithms do not care whether it is a distance in kilometres, a latency, a cost, a log-probability or a capacity, but they care a great deal about its sign and its type. Non-negative weights license Dijkstra (§04–§05); negative weights force Bellman-Ford (§07) or a DAG (§09) or Johnson's reweighting (§14), and a negative cycle makes shortest paths undefined (§03). Small integer weights admit bucket queues that beat every comparison-based heap (§11). Capacities are weights read as upper bounds on how much can pass, and flow is a different problem from shortest paths even though it is solved with the same traversals (§21). Undirected graphs are the directed graph with both arcs; a negative undirected edge is a negative cycle of length two, which is why the shortest-path sections are stated for directed graphs and the spanning-tree sections for undirected ones. Nothing here requires weights to be distinct, integer, or bounded, except where the text says so, and each such assumption is named where it is used: the Fibonacci-heap bound is amortised (§06), Ford-Fulkerson is guaranteed to terminate only for integer or rational capacities (§22) and terminates on any capacities once a shortest-path rule is imposed (§23), Edmonds-Karp's O(VE²) is for shortest augmenting paths only (§23), and union-find's inverse-Ackermann bound needs both of its heuristics (§18).

28 Choosing the algorithm: the decision procedure

The algorithm for a weighted-graph problem is decided by four questions asked in order — what is being asked (one source, all pairs, one target, a tree, a flow), what the weights are (unit, small integers, non-negative, arbitrary, capacities), what structure the graph has (a DAG, sparse or dense, implicit or explicit, bipartite), and how often the question will be asked — and every bound quoted in the answer carries the hypothesis it was proved under. The procedure below is the page's contents rearranged as a decision tree, with the cost of each branch stated as "bound, under hypothesis" rather than as a bare Big-O, because most of the wrong choices on this page are a correct bound applied outside its hypothesis: Dijkstra's O((V + E) log V) without w ≥ 0, Edmonds-Karp's O(VE²) for a depth-first path rule, the array's Θ(V²) on a sparse graph, union-find's α(n) with one heuristic, Ford-Fulkerson's termination on irrational capacities. Shortest paths: the sign of the weights first, then the structure, then the query pattern

The first question after "one source?" is not the algorithm but the weights, because the weights decide which invariants are available. Non-negative weights license the greedy invariant — the closest tentative vertex is final — and with it every Dijkstra variant, 0-1 BFS, Dial and A*; a single negative edge withdraws the licence, and §05's slider shows the first failure at w = −2 on a four-vertex DAG that has a perfectly well-defined δ. The second question is structure, and it can overrule the first: a DAG has an evaluation order that makes the sign irrelevant (§09), so the topological sort should be attempted before Bellman-Ford is reached for — it costs Θ(V + E), the same as the sweep that follows it, and it either succeeds or reports a cycle. The third question is what the weights are made of when they are non-negative: equal weights are BFS and a heap is waste; weights in {0, 1} are a deque; integers below log V are buckets; and only real or large-integer weights need a comparison-based queue, at which point density chooses between the heap and the array on the terms §06 measured — the array's Θ(V²) beats the heap's O((V + E) log V) on the bound's accounting from the first sampled density above the algebraic threshold, 0.15 at V = 128 (the threshold itself is E > 2,213, density ≈ 0.135), and never on the measured sift steps for random weights, so the honest advice is the heap unless the array is measured to win on the actual inputs. The fourth question is the query pattern. One target with a heuristic is A*, and a consistent heuristic is a potential (§15) that costs nothing in correctness; one target without one is Dijkstra stopped at the target's extraction, and bidirectional search halves the radius. Many sources, or all pairs, is §12–§14's table: V Dijkstras for non-negative sparse graphs, Johnson for negative sparse ones, Floyd-Warshall for dense ones, and the boolean closure when only reachability is wanted. And a source that is really a constraint system — inequalities xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ b — is Bellman-Ford from the zero-edge source with the negative cycle read as the answer (§10), which is the one case where a negative cycle is an output rather than an error.

All pairs: density decides, and the negative-cycle test comes free

The three all-pairs strategies differ by a factor of V/log V in either direction depending on density, so the choice is arithmetic: E log V against V². Below that crossover — road networks, meshes, most graphs that arise from data — run a single-source algorithm from every vertex: Dijkstra if the weights are non-negative, Johnson's reweighting first if they are not, and never V Bellman-Fords, which Johnson dominates at the cost of one Bellman-Ford. Above it, Floyd-Warshall's three loops with k outermost are hard to beat: no heap, no adjacency structure, a contiguous matrix, and a negative-cycle test that is one glance at the diagonal. Repeated squaring is the algorithm to know rather than run — a log factor slower than Floyd-Warshall, and the reason (§12) that no Strassen-type shortcut exists for distances while one does for reachability. Two practical notes. The output is Θ(V²) whatever the method, so at V = 10⁵ the question is moot and the answer is "queries on demand, cached", i.e. Dijkstra per query with potentials or landmarks. And when the graph will be queried many times but not for all pairs, Johnson's step 2 — one Bellman-Ford to get potentials — is worth doing once even for a handful of queries, since it converts every later query from O(VE) to O((V + E) log V).

Minimum spanning trees: how the edges arrive

All three MST algorithms are O(E log V) in their standard forms and all three are correct by the same lemma, so the choice is about representation and side effects. Kruskal wants an edge list and pays for a sort: if the edges are already sorted, or integer-weighted (counting sort), or arrive as a stream, the loop's O(E·α(V)) is the whole cost and nothing else comes close; it also produces the spanning forest of a disconnected graph for free and, stopped early, the single-linkage clustering (§20). Prim wants an adjacency structure and a queue: with an array it is Θ(V²), which beats every sort-based method on dense graphs, and it grows a connected tree from a root, which is what an implicit graph (edges generated on demand from a vertex) or an early-stopped partial tree needs. Borůvka wants neither a sort nor a heap, does its work in ⌈log₂ V⌉ rounds each of which is a parallel scan of the edges, and is the base of the randomised linear-time algorithm; it needs distinct weights or the (w, index) tie-break, and §20 shows the cycle that ties can otherwise close. The union-find inside Kruskal must have both heuristics for the α(n) bound — §18 measured 104 hops per operation with neither, 2.3 with rank alone, 2.7 with compression alone and 1.3 with both on the random workload, and 799 against 1.0 on the chain — and the "bottleneck", "second-best" and "clustering" questions are all answered from the tree in O(V²) or less rather than by a new algorithm.

Flow: size and structure, and when to leave the combinatorial world

Every flow algorithm on the page is Ford-Fulkerson with a rule, and the rule is chosen by size. On a graph small enough to enumerate its cuts — the page's instances — any rule works and the brute-force cut is the check. On thousands of vertices Edmonds-Karp's O(VE²) is fine in practice because the augmentation count sits far below VE (3 against 54 on six vertices; at most 8 against 128 over 300 random eight-vertex networks); on larger sparse graphs and on all unit-capacity networks Dinic's level graphs win, with O(E√V) on matching networks; and on dense or grid-structured networks — the segmentation problems of §26–§27 — push-relabel with the gap and global-relabelling heuristics, or a search-tree-reusing augmenting-path solver, is what the libraries run. Bipartite matching is Hopcroft-Karp, which is Dinic on the unit network, and the cover and the Hall violator come from the final residual reachability at no extra cost (§25). The step outside this page is taken when the problem acquires costs (assignment, transportation, the earth-mover distance of §27): minimum-cost flow, whose successive-shortest-path algorithm reuses Bellman-Ford or potential-reweighted Dijkstra from §07 and §14. And the step outside combinatorial optimisation is taken when the problem acquires several commodities sharing capacities, or side constraints that are not flow conservation, or when a fractional answer is acceptable: then the max-flow LP of §21 generalises and the flow algorithms do not — the integrality theorem is a theorem about one commodity, and a two-commodity instance can have no integral optimum at all. Knowing where that boundary lies is the last decision, and it is the one NP-Completeness & Approximation takes up from the other side.

The one-paragraph version. Ask what is wanted; then look at the weights (unit → BFS, 0/1 → deque, small integers → buckets, non-negative → Dijkstra with the queue chosen by density, negative → Bellman-Ford, a DAG → one pass whatever the weights); then at the query pattern (a heuristic → A*, all pairs → Johnson when sparse and Floyd-Warshall when dense). For a spanning tree, ask how the edges arrive (sorted or streamed → Kruskal, adjacency and dense → Prim's array, parallel → Borůvka), and build the union-find with both heuristics. For a flow, ask how big (Edmonds-Karp, then Dinic, then push-relabel) and whether there are costs (min-cost flow) or several commodities (linear programming). Attach the hypothesis to every bound you quote, and check the answer on a small instance against brute force before trusting the routine — which is what every figure on this page does.

29 Pitfalls

Every pitfall below was either exhibited by a routine on this page and caught by its brute-force comparison, or measured by running a deliberately wrong variant of a routine against the correct one on the page's own instances — the wrong answers are numbers, not hypotheticals. They fall into three kinds. A hypothesis was dropped: a bound or an invariant proved under w ≥ 0, "bipartite", "both heuristics", "integer capacities" or "shortest augmenting path" was applied without it, and the algorithm returned a plausible wrong number or ran for far longer than the quoted bound. A certificate was misread: a vertex was taken to be on the cycle when it was merely downstream of it, a finite estimate was taken for a distance, a tie was taken for a property of the problem. Or a step was skipped: the test round, the reverse arc, the stale check, the outermost loop. The cure is the habit the figures practise — run the routine beside an enumeration on an instance small enough to enumerate, and do not trust it until they agree — and the load-time audit of §30 does that for every family on the page.

  • A negative edge into S makes Dijkstra finalise a wrong estimate, and no implementation variant repairs it (§05). On the four-vertex graph s→a (2), s→b (1), a→b (−3), b→c (1), Dijkstra extracts b at key 1 and finalises it; the truth is δ(s, b) = −1. The variant that skips edges into S returns d = ⟨0, 2, 1, 2⟩ against δ = ⟨0, 2, −1, 0⟩, wrong at b and c; the variant that relaxes the edge anyway but never re-extracts fixes b by accident and still returns d[c] = 2. The slider's sweep finds the first failing weight at −2 for both variants — at −1 the route ties the direct edge and survives — which is exactly the boundary the proof's step (2) draws: a stretch of weight zero past a finalised vertex is harmless, a strictly negative one is not. The errors are always too high (upper-bound property), which is why "Dijkstra ran without complaint" proves nothing: nothing in its state records that a finalised vertex was later improved.
  • Adding a constant to every weight does not repair negative edges; adding a potential does (§03). On s→t (1), s→a (2), a→t (−2) the true distance to t is 0 by the two-edge route; shifting every weight by +2 makes that route cost 4 and the direct edge 3, and Dijkstra on the shifted graph returns the direct edge — original weight 1 — and does so for every shift c ≥ 2, because a path with k edges gains k·c and the shift is a per-edge toll. Johnson's reweighting ŵ = w + h(u) − h(v) is a per-endpoint toll that every s ⇝ v path pays equally: with h = ⟨0, 0, −2⟩ the three edges become 3, 2, 0, Dijkstra returns ⟨0, 2, 2⟩, and un-reweighting gives ⟨0, 2, 0⟩ = δ. The difference between the fallacy and the theorem is the telescoping sum of §14, and nothing else.
  • Bellman-Ford without the V-th round returns finite numbers where δ = −∞ (§07). On the five-vertex instance with 4→1 = −6, four rounds end with d = ⟨0, 1, −2, 5, 3⟩; a routine that stops there hands those back as distances. The fifth round lowers three more estimates, to ⟨0, −3, −6, 2, 3⟩, and that is the report: the true values for 1, 2, 3, 4 are −∞, the brute force over the 3 simple cycles reachable from s finds the lightest at −7, and the finite numbers are the state of a process that would descend forever. The early exit is the reason the omission survives: on every instance without a negative cycle the extra round changes nothing and looks redundant.
  • The vertex whose estimate fell in the test round need not be on the cycle, and the −∞ set is not just the cycle (§07–§08). The witness vertex is on the cycle or downstream of it, which is why the routine walks π back V times before collecting the cycle — on §07's instance the walk 1 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 4 lands on 4 and the cycle is read from there. And the set of vertices with δ = −∞ is everything reachable from a reachable negative cycle, computed as the forward closure of the vertices improved in the test round. On §07's graph the cycle's vertices and the −∞ set happen to coincide — {1, 2, 3, 4} both — so that instance cannot distinguish the right rule from the wrong one, and a test suite built from it would pass a routine that reports only the cycle's vertices; the audit's 36 random negative-cycle instances, where the two sets differ whenever a vertex hangs off the cycle, are what check the rule against brute force.
  • Floyd-Warshall's k must be the outermost loop (§13). The invariant is "after stage k, intermediates are drawn from {1 … k}", and it is the induction; with k innermost the cell (i, j) is finished before later intermediates are available to it. Measured by the checking script with the loops reordered: 2 of the 25 cells wrong on the §13 graph and 1 of 16 on the §12 graph, and a wrong matrix on 35 of 165 random graphs without negative cycles — never on the diagonal, never for row 0 of those two instances, which is why a spot check of one source can miss it. The order of i and j inside k is free (§13 proves the in-place update is safe because row and column k do not change during stage k); the order of k is not.
  • Johnson's potential must come from an added source that reaches everything, not from an existing vertex (§14). Any h satisfying h(v) ≤ h(u) + w(u, v) on every edge works, and the distances from an ordinary vertex s satisfy it wherever they are finite: on the §13 graph, which is strongly connected, h = δ(0, ·) = ⟨0, 1, −2, 2, 0⟩ gives ŵ = ⟨5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 9, 3, 1⟩, all non-negative, and the algorithm runs correctly — which is how the shortcut earns trust it does not deserve. The moment some vertex is unreachable from s its h is ∞, ŵ on its edges is ∞ − ∞, and the reweighted graph is not a graph. The added source with a zero-weight edge to every vertex is what makes every h finite, and it costs one vertex and V edges.
  • An inadmissible heuristic makes A* fast and wrong, and not reliably wrong (§15). Doubling the Manhattan distance on the 9 × 14 grid expands 49 cells instead of Manhattan's 50 and returns a 25-step path where BFS finds 21; on the second grid the same heuristic expands 43 cells and happens to return the optimal 19. A heuristic that fails only sometimes cannot be caught by a demo; the guarantee that survives is weighted A*'s cost ≤ ε·δ, here 25 ≤ 42, which is a different promise from optimality. Consistency is the property that lets the closed set be final; an admissible but inconsistent heuristic needs reopening, and the figure's routine counts reopenings — 0 for all three admissible heuristics on both grids — because that count is the test.
  • Union-find's α(n) needs both heuristics; either alone has a different bound, and neither alone is "almost constant" on the wrong workload (§18). On the random workload of 5 000 operations over 1 000 elements the measured hops per operation are 104.47 with neither heuristic, 2.27 with rank only, 2.65 with compression only and 1.28 with both; on the chain adversary — 999 sequential unions, then 4 001 finds of the deepest element — naive linking costs 799.40 hops per operation and every heuristic combination 1.00. The theorems are: rank alone Θ(m log n) worst case, compression alone Θ(m·log1+m/n n), both O(m·α(n)); the "as good as constant" reading rests on the table α(n) ≤ 4 for n ≤ A₄(1), which is a statement about both heuristics together. Kruskal on the shared instance cannot show the difference — 9 hops with both against 12 with neither over 18 finds — so a routine tested only on toy graphs will not reveal that its union-find has one heuristic.
  • Kruskal's test is find(u) ≠ find(v), not "is either endpoint already in the forest" (§17). The tempting shortcut — reject an edge whose endpoints are both already touched — rejects AB (4) at the fourth step on the shared instance, because A was touched by AD and B by BE, though they are in different trees. The script that runs that rule returns three edges {CF, AD, BE} of weight 6 and stops: a forest, not a spanning tree. The opposite shortcut, no test at all, accepts all nine edges (weight 41) and builds cycles. Only the union-find question "same tree?" is the cut property's condition, and it is asked 2E = 18 times on the instance.
  • Borůvka with tied weights and a per-vertex tie-break can add a cycle (§20). On a triangle with all weights 1, vertex x may choose xy, y choose yz and z choose zx — three edges, one cycle, and A is no longer a forest. The routine compares (w, index) lexicographically, which makes every comparison behave as if weights were distinct, and returns 2 edges on that triangle; the audit runs it on 57 tied-weight random graphs and finds a spanning tree of minimum weight every time. The tie-break is not a nicety: the proof that the chosen edges are acyclic uses "the heaviest edge on a cycle of chosen edges was chosen by a component that had a lighter option", which needs a strict order.
  • The minimum spanning tree is not the shortest-path tree, and from a different root it is a different comparison (§16, §20). From root A on the shared instance the MST is {AB, AD, BE, EF, CF} (15) and the shortest-path tree is {AB, AD, BE, BC, CF} (16); the MST's path to C is A–B–E–F–C = 13 against δ(A, C) = 10, a stretch of 1.30. From root C the SPT weighs 21; the largest excess is at D — 15 along the MST against δ = 11, 4 units — while the largest stretch ratio is at B, 9 against 6 (1.50): the two "worst" vertices need not coincide, and the figure's readout reports the largest excess. Prim and Dijkstra are one loop with one line changed (w(u, v) against d[u] + w(u, v)), and a program that uses the wrong key returns a spanning tree with a plausible weight — the SPT weighs 16, one more than the minimum — and no error. Compare against brute force over the 55 trees, or at least check the tree's weight against a second MST algorithm, as every MST figure does.
  • Forgetting the reverse residual arcs turns Ford-Fulkerson into a greedy heuristic (§21). Without cancellation the depth-first rule on the six-vertex network stops at value 19 after three augmentations, because its first path sent 4 units the long way through v₃→v₂ and only the reverse arc v₂→v₃ can take them back; with the reverse arcs it reaches 23 in four. On the pathology it stops at 199 of 200, and on the five-vertex push-relabel instance it happens to reach the optimum — three instances, three different behaviours, and the last is the dangerous one. The max-flow min-cut theorem's (2) ⇒ (3) is about the residual network with both kinds of arc; "no forward path left" is not "no augmenting path left".
  • Ford-Fulkerson's termination is a theorem about integer capacities, and its cost is a theorem about the path rule (§22). With M = 100 the "worst" rule needs 200 augmentations and 2 402 arc scans on four vertices to move 200 units, one at a time across the middle edge and back; depth-first needs 4 and BFS 2. The bound O(E·|f*|) is not pessimism — the figure attains it — and with irrational capacities the method can fail to terminate at all, converging to a value below the maximum. Every count on this page is for integer capacities, and the bound that does not depend on them is Edmonds-Karp's O(VE²), which needs the BFS rule and nothing else.
  • Antiparallel edges must each own their residual pair; a residual capacity stored per unordered pair conflates room with cancellation (§21, §26). The definition's convention subdivides one of an antiparallel pair; the routine gives every input edge its own forward and reverse arc, so c_f(u, v) on the forward arc of (u, v) is "room left" and on the reverse arc of (v, u) is "flow to cancel", and they are never added. The segmentation network of §26 is made of such pairs — 24 neighbour arcs on the 3 × 3 grid, λ each way — and its minimum cut of 22 at λ = 3 agrees with brute force over 512 labellings because each direction's capacity is severed separately. An implementation that keys residual capacity on {u, v} would count λ once for a cut that must pay it in one direction and would silently mis-price every boundary.
  • Kruskal and Prim are for undirected graphs; run on a directed graph they answer a question nobody asked (§16). Treating the arcs as edges and running either algorithm returns a spanning tree of the underlying undirected graph, which as a set of arcs is in general not an arborescence: some vertices have two incoming arcs and others none, so it is not a rooted out-tree from any root, and its weight is a lower bound on the arborescence weight that need not be attained (three vertices r, a, b with arcs r→a (10), a→b (1), b→r (1): Kruskal on the arcs as edges returns {a→b, b→r}, weight 2, in which a has no incoming arc and r has one — not an out-tree from r; the cheapest arborescence rooted at r is {r→a, a→b}, weight 11, found by brute force over the two candidate arc sets). The directed problem is the minimum spanning arborescence, solved by the contraction algorithm of §16's opening; the cut property, which every MST algorithm on this page rests on, is a theorem about undirected cuts.
  • König's theorem is for bipartite graphs only (§25). A triangle has a maximum matching of size 1 and a minimum vertex cover of size 2; max |M| = min |C| fails, and so does the flow reduction, which needs the two sides to place the source and sink. The augmenting-path characterisation of a maximum matching does hold in general graphs, but finding the paths there needs blossom handling that this page does not build. The routine's inputs are typed as {nL, nR, adj} for that reason, and Hall's condition — derived from König — inherits the restriction.
  • A tied minimum makes the returned labelling a convention, not a property of the energy (§26). On the 3 × 3 grid the minimum energy is unique at λ = 0, 3, 4, 5 but attained by two labellings at λ = 1 and three at λ = 2; the figure draws the source side of the final residual reachability, which is the minimum cut with the fewest foreground pixels, and a different max-flow implementation would legitimately draw another. Pixel 5 flips 1 → 0 → 1 → 0 as λ rises, so per-pixel labels are not monotone in the smoothness weight even though the energy and the boundary length are. Compare energies across implementations, or fix the tie rule; comparing labellings will fail on every tie.
  • Lazy deletion without the stale check keeps the distances and loses everything else (§06, §19). A popped pair whose key exceeds the vertex's current estimate, or whose vertex is already done, must be discarded. With weights ≥ 0 the distances survive its omission — a stale entry cannot lower an estimate below the truth — which is why the bug is invisible in a distance check; what it costs is work and correctness downstream. On the six-vertex instance the omission makes 17 relaxations instead of 10; on the dense random graphs of §06 it makes 2 241 relaxations against 1 024 at V = 64 and 10 449 against 4 096 at V = 128, a factor that grows with the stale count. And in Prim's lazy version the same omission adds an edge for a vertex already in the tree: on the shared instance it returns 10 edges of weight 47, with BC twice, instead of 5 edges of weight 15. The check is one comparison per pop; the figures count the stale pops (4 of 10 on §04's graph, 203 of 331 at V = 128) so that its necessity is visible.
  • The DAG figure's "shortest" toggle is not a scheduling quantity (§09). The sweep with min returns 10 for the task DAG — the length of the shortest chain from S to T, A–C–E–G or B–D–E–G — and no project finishes in 10 time units: the earliest finish is the longest chain, 12, and every task's earliest start is a longest path. The toggle exists to show that the code path is identical and only the comparison changes; reading its output as a schedule is the mistake, and it is easy to make because both numbers come from the same routine with the same relaxation count of 11 per sweep (22 for the two sweeps §09 runs).
  • Bellman-Ford's edge order changes the work, not the answer or the worst case (§07–§08). On the five-vertex instance the listed order — the shortest paths' edges in reverse — needs 5 rounds and 40 relaxations; the reversed list needs 2 rounds and 16. Both end at ⟨0, 4, 1, 5, 3⟩. A benchmark that happens to meet the shortest paths in order will report a near-linear algorithm; the DP view says the two-array recurrence takes exactly V − 1 rounds regardless (32 relaxations either way on that instance), and the queue-based variant's typical speed carries no guarantee. Quote O(VE), and measure the early exit as a bonus.
  • A bound for one queue is not "Dijkstra's bound", and the bound's own accounting is not a measurement (§06). Θ(V²), O((V + E) log V), O((V + E) log E) and O(E + V log V) amortised are four different theorems about four data structures. On the bound's accounting the array should win from the first sampled density above the algebraic threshold, 0.15 at V = 128 (the threshold itself is E > 2 213, density ≈ 0.135); counting the sift steps the heap actually took, it never does on random weights — 9 760 steps to the array's 24 512 at full density, with only 366 of 16 256 edges producing a decrease-key. The number the Fibonacci heap's bound charges for, E decrease-keys, was 708 of 16 384 on the dense family at V = 256. Recommendations that rest on the accounting are not wrong, but they are statements about adversarial inputs, and the page says so where it makes them.

Where this goes next in the series. The three problems this page named and did not solve — the Steiner tree of §20, the shortest simple path with negative cycles of §03, and the integral multi-commodity flow of §28 (its fractional form is the linear program §28 recommends, and is polynomial), all NP-hard where their single-commodity or all-vertex cousins are polynomial — are the subject of NP-Completeness & Approximation, together with the travelling salesman and the flow linear program's role in approximation by rounding. Upstream, the representations and traversals every algorithm here is built on, BFS's shortest-path theorem for unit weights and the topological sort of §09, are Graph Representations & Traversal; the priority queues that make Dijkstra's and Prim's schedules cheap, with the index map that decrease-key needs and the Fibonacci heap's amortised cut, are Heaps & Priority Queues; the cut property is stated as the greedy-choice lemma at Greedy Algorithms, §20; and Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall as dynamic programs with an extra parameter, and the DAG pass as the table-filling order every DP walks, are Dynamic Programming, §23.
Where it goes sideways, out of this domain. §27 gave the correspondences with their hypotheses; the pages are: geodesic distances on a neighbourhood graph among the nonlinear alternatives on Dimensionality Reduction, built from the k-nearest-neighbour graph; single-linkage clustering as Kruskal stopped early on Clustering, where spectral methods are the relaxation of a balanced cut, not an s–t cut; the minimum cut as exact MAP inference for binary submodular energies at Image Segmentation, §23; Viterbi as the DAG algorithm on the trellis at Hidden Markov Models & CRFs; value iteration as Bellman-Ford's rounds and Dijkstra as its priority order on Reinforcement Learning; the earth-mover distance as a minimum-cost flow behind the Wasserstein GAN and the one-dimensional closed form in drift monitoring; and the three things that are not shortest paths — PageRank in extractive summarisation, message passing in Graph Neural Networks, greedy routing in vector databases.
