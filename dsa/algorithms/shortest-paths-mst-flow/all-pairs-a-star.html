12 All pairs: V runs of Dijkstra, and repeated matrix squaring

The all-pairs shortest-paths problem asks for δ(i, j) for every ordered pair of vertices — a V × V distance matrix D, and usually a predecessor matrix Π from which any one shortest path can be read off. The obvious solution is V runs of a single-source algorithm: V Dijkstras when the weights are non-negative, O(V·(V + E) log V) with a binary heap, or V Bellman-Fords when they are not, O(V²E) — O(V⁴) on a dense graph. The all-pairs algorithms of this section and the next two are the ways to do better when V²E is too much: a matrix formulation whose repeated squaring gives Θ(V³ log V) for any weights without negative cycles (this section), Floyd-Warshall's Θ(V³) (§13), and Johnson's reweighting, which restores the V-Dijkstra bound in the presence of negative edges and wins on sparse graphs (§14).

Why (min, +) is a semiring, and why the fast matrix multiplication algorithms do not transfer

The product (A ⊗ B)ᵢⱼ = min_k (Aᵢₖ + Bₖⱼ) is a matrix product over the structure (ℝ ∪ {∞}, min, +): "addition" is min, with identity ∞; "multiplication" is +, with identity 0; both are associative and commutative, + distributes over min (a + min(b, c) = min(a + b, a + c)), and ∞ annihilates (∞ + a = ∞). That is exactly the list of axioms a semiring satisfies, and it is enough for matrix multiplication to be associative — which is the fact repeated squaring needs, since W⁴ = (W ⊗ W) ⊗ (W ⊗ W) only equals W ⊗ W ⊗ W ⊗ W if the bracketing does not matter. Associativity is also what makes the squaring meaningful: (L⁽ᵃ⁾ ⊗ L⁽ᵇ⁾)ᵢⱼ is the least weight of a path of at most a + b edges that splits into an at-most-a-edge prefix and an at-most-b-edge suffix at some vertex k, and every path of at most a + b edges splits that way. What the structure lacks is subtraction: min has no inverse (there is no x with min(3, x) = ∞). Strassen's algorithm and its successors (Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §19) multiply n × n matrices in fewer than n³ multiplications by computing clever sums and differences of submatrices and cancelling terms; without subtraction those cancellations are unavailable, and the Θ(V³) per product stands. The (min, +) product is in fact known to be equivalent in difficulty to all-pairs shortest paths itself, so a truly subcubic all-pairs algorithm on general real weights would need a new idea rather than a transplanted one; the best known bounds shave only sub-polynomial factors off V³. For the boolean semiring (or, and) — reachability rather than distance — the situation is different: boolean matrix product can be embedded in integer product and done in Strassen time, which is why transitive closure (§13) has a subcubic algorithm while all-pairs distances do not.

13 Floyd-Warshall, stepped, with path reconstruction and transitive closure

Floyd-Warshall computes all-pairs shortest paths in Θ(V³) time and Θ(V²) space by a dynamic program over a different parameter from §12's edge count: d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j) is the least weight of a path from i to j whose intermediate vertices all lie in {1, …, k}, and d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j) = min( d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, j), d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k) + d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, j) ) — either the best path avoids vertex k, or it passes through k exactly once. Three nested loops with k outermost, a one-line body, no data structure, negative edges allowed; a negative cycle shows up as a negative diagonal entry; a "next-hop" matrix updated alongside recovers any path; and replacing (min, +) by (or, and) gives the transitive closure.

Proof of the recurrence: the "intermediates ⊆ {1..k}" invariant

Fix k ≥ 1 and a pair (i, j), and let p be a shortest path from i to j whose intermediate vertices all lie in {1, …, k}; assume there are no negative cycles, so p can be taken simple. Two cases. k is not an intermediate vertex of p. Then all of p's intermediates lie in {1, …, k − 1}, so p is a candidate for d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, j), and conversely every candidate for d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, j) is a candidate for d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j); hence d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j) = d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, j). k is an intermediate vertex of p. Because p is simple, k appears once, and p splits as i ⇝ k ⇝ j into p₁ and p₂. Neither piece has k as an intermediate (it is their endpoint), so both have intermediates in {1, …, k − 1}; and both are shortest among such paths by cut and paste — a shorter i ⇝ k path with intermediates in {1..k − 1} spliced onto p₂ would give a shorter candidate for p. So w(p) = d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k) + d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, j). The two cases are exhaustive and each value is attained by a real path, so the minimum of the two is exactly d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j). Induction on k from the base d⁽⁰⁾ = W finishes it. Where the no-negative-cycle assumption was used: to make p simple, so that k occurs at most once on it and the splice argument does not produce a walk that repeats k. With a negative cycle present the recurrence still runs and still produces finite numbers; they are no longer shortest simple-path weights, but the diagonal test below detects the situation.

Why dropping the superscript is safe — the in-place argument

The recurrence as written reads d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾ and writes d⁽ᵏ⁾, suggesting two matrices, or V + 1 of them (Θ(V³) space) if every stage is kept. A single matrix updated in place is correct, and the reason is that the entries a stage-k update reads — row k and column k — are the entries it never changes. Consider d⁽ᵏ⁾(i, k): the recurrence gives min( d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k), d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k) + d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, k) ) = d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k), because d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, k) = 0 when there is no negative cycle (it is 0 initially and no path from k to itself is shorter). Symmetrically d⁽ᵏ⁾(k, j) = d⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, j). So during stage k, whether a cell (i, k) or (k, j) has "already been updated this stage" or not makes no difference — its value is the same either way — and every other cell (i, j) reads only those two, so it reads stage-(k − 1) values as the recurrence demands. Hence one matrix suffices: Θ(V²) space, and the loop order of i and j inside k is irrelevant (the order of k is not: it is the induction). The same argument covers the next matrix, since next[i][k] and next[k][j] are likewise untouched in stage k.

Reconstruction with the next matrix, and the negative-cycle test

Whenever D[i][j] improves to D[i][k] + D[k][j], the new best i → j path starts the way the best i → k path starts, so next[i][j] ← next[i][k]. Following next from i until j lists the path in forward order — 0 → 4: next[0][4] = 3, next[3][4] = 1, next[1][4] = 2, next[2][4] = 4, giving 0 → 3 → 1 → 2 → 4, whose edge weights re-sum to 2 − 1 − 3 + 2 = 0 = D[0][4]. The predecessor form π[i][j] ("the vertex before j on the best i → j path", updated as π[i][j] ← π[k][j]) is equivalent and lists the path backwards; both cost Θ(V²) extra space and O(1) per update. A negative simple cycle through i gives D[i][i] < 0: the first time the cycle's other vertices are all allowed as intermediates, the path i ⇝ i around it beats the initial 0 (the recurrence's induction is over simple paths with distinct intermediates, and a simple cycle through i is one). Adding the edge 4→1 (−2) to the example creates the cycle 1→2→4→1 of weight −3, and the routine's final diagonal reads 0, −3, −3, −2, −6: every vertex on the cycle (1, 2, 4) is negative, as the recurrence guarantees; 3, which is not on the cycle but reaches it (3→1) and is reached by it (2→3), happens to go negative too (D[3][4] + D[4][3] = −2 + 0 at k = 4), while 0, which likewise reaches the cycle (0→1) and is reached by it (4→0), stays at 0 (D[0][4] + D[4][0] = 0 + 3). Off-cycle diagonal entries carry no guarantee either way, so the test is "some D[i][i] < 0", read over the whole diagonal, and the off-diagonal entries are then meaningless. Under the < test the algorithm never crashes on such input; it must simply be told to check the diagonal, and the reconstruction must not be trusted (the next pointers can loop).

Transitive closure: the boolean version

Ask only "is there a path from i to j?" and every weight can be 1, so D[i][j] < ∞ is the answer — or, more economically, keep one bit per cell: t⁽⁰⁾(i, j) = 1 if i = j or (i, j) is an edge, and t⁽ᵏ⁾(i, j) = t⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, j) ∨ (t⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(i, k) ∧ t⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾(k, j)). Same three loops, Θ(V³) bit operations, and rows fit in machine words so the inner loop runs 64 columns at a time. This is the reachability matrix the graph page uses as its independent check on strongly connected components (Graph Representations & Traversal, §22: u and v are in the same component iff t(u, v) ∧ t(v, u)); on the five-vertex example the closure is all ones — the graph is strongly connected, as the row of finite distances in D already shows. When only the closure is wanted on a sparse graph, V traversals cost O(V(V + E)) and beat the matrix; the matrix wins when the graph is dense or when the closure is needed as a bit-matrix for further algebra. Its (∨, ∧) structure is the boolean semiring of §12, and unlike (min, +) it does embed in ordinary integer multiplication, which is why transitive closure has fast-matrix-multiplication algorithms and all-pairs distances do not.

14 Johnson's reweighting for sparse graphs

Johnson's algorithm computes all-pairs shortest paths on a graph with negative edges (and no negative cycle) in O(V² log V + VE) — the cost of V Dijkstra runs — by first computing a potential h(v) for every vertex with one Bellman-Ford run from an added source, then reweighting every edge to ŵ(u, v) = w(u, v) + h(u) − h(v), which is non-negative and changes the weight of every u → v path by the same constant h(u) − h(v), so that Dijkstra on ŵ finds the same shortest paths and the original distances are recovered by subtracting the constant back. On a sparse graph — E near V rather than V² — this beats Floyd-Warshall's Θ(V³) by a factor approaching V / log V, and it is the reason "negative edges ⇒ can't use Dijkstra" is only half true: one Bellman-Ford run buys permission to use it V times.

Why this is exactly what Dijkstra needs, and no more

Dijkstra's correctness (§05) rests on one inequality: a path cannot get shorter by being extended, so the unsettled vertex with the smallest estimate is final. Any non-negative weighting gives that; the reweighting supplies one that additionally has the same shortest paths as w. The proof of the second lemma is the telescoping sum above and nothing else, and it shows more than is needed: every potential function h preserves shortest paths, whether or not it comes from Bellman-Ford. What Bellman-Ford's h adds is the first lemma, non-negativity, and there the triangle inequality is doing the work — δ′(s*, v) ≤ δ′(s*, u) + w(u, v) holds because "the shortest way to u, then the edge" is one particular way to reach v. The added source is what makes h finite everywhere: with s* adjacent to all vertices, no δ′ is ∞, and since every original vertex has a zero-weight edge from s*, every h(v) ≤ 0, with h(v) < 0 exactly for vertices reachable by a negative-weight path from somewhere. The recovery step is the preservation lemma read backwards: δ(u, v) = δ̂(u, v) − h(u) + h(v). And the negative-cycle report is inherited, since G′ has a negative cycle if and only if G does (the new vertex has no incoming edge, so it lies on no cycle).

When it beats Floyd-Warshall, and when it does not

Compare O(V² log V + VE) with Θ(V³). With E = Θ(V) — road networks, meshes, most "real" sparse graphs — Johnson is O(V² log V) against V³, a factor of V / log V; at V = 10⁴ that is on the order of 10⁹ heap-driven operations (V² log₂ V ≈ 1.3 × 10⁹) against 10¹², the difference between seconds and hours. With a binary heap the bound is O(VE log V) = O(V² log V) on such graphs, the same story. At the other extreme, E = Θ(V²), Johnson is O(V³) with a Fibonacci heap and O(V³ log V) with a binary one, and Floyd-Warshall wins on the constant: its inner loop is an add, a compare and a store on a contiguous array, with no heap, no adjacency list and no pointer chasing, and it vectorises. The crossover is roughly where E log V ≈ V². Two further points. If the weights are already non-negative, h ≡ 0 and Johnson is just "run Dijkstra from every vertex" — step 2 costs one pass that changes nothing. And the potential idea outlives the algorithm: the same telescoping argument is what makes A*'s heuristic legitimate (§15), and potentials are the standard way to keep reduced costs non-negative in min-cost-flow and assignment algorithms, where every augmentation re-runs Dijkstra on reweighted residual edges.

15 A*: admissible and consistent heuristics, optimality, and what it saves

A* is Dijkstra's algorithm for a single target t, with the priority of a vertex changed from g(v) — its tentative distance from the source — to f(v) = g(v) + h(v), where h(v) is a cheap estimate of the distance still to go from v to t; it stops when t is popped. If h never overestimates (admissible: h(v) ≤ δ(v, t) for every v), the path returned is optimal. If moreover h obeys a triangle inequality along edges (consistent: h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v)), then no vertex is ever expanded twice, and A* is literally Dijkstra on the reweighted graph ŵ(u, v) = w(u, v) + h(v) − h(u) — the same potential trick as Johnson's algorithm (§14) with the sign flipped, which is why it is correct. What the heuristic buys is fewer expansions: Dijkstra explores a ball around the source until it happens to swallow the target; A* explores an ellipse-like region stretched toward it, and a better estimate makes the region thinner.

Optimality with an admissible heuristic, proved

Suppose A* (with reopening, as written above) pops t with g[t] > δ(s, t). Take a shortest path p = ⟨s = v₀, …, vₖ = t⟩ and let vᵢ be the first vertex on p that is not closed at that moment, or is closed with g[vᵢ] > δ(s, vᵢ); such a vertex exists because t itself qualifies. Its predecessor vᵢ₋₁ is closed with the exact value g[vᵢ₋₁] = δ(s, vᵢ₋₁) (by the choice of i; if i = 0 then vᵢ = s, which is open with the exact value g = 0 from the start, and the next sentence applies to it directly), and when it was expanded it relaxed the edge to vᵢ, so g[vᵢ] ≤ δ(s, vᵢ₋₁) + w(vᵢ₋₁, vᵢ) = δ(s, vᵢ), hence g[vᵢ] = δ(s, vᵢ), since g never drops below the true distance; and vᵢ is in the open set — it was inserted there at that relaxation, and by the choice of i it is not closed with an exact value, so it has not been popped since. Then f(vᵢ) = δ(s, vᵢ) + h(vᵢ) ≤ δ(s, vᵢ) + δ(vᵢ, t) = δ(s, t) < g[t] = f(t), using admissibility and the fact that p is shortest. So vᵢ has strictly smaller f than t and would have been popped first — contradiction. The argument needs reopening only to handle the case where an inadmissible-looking ordering closes a vertex with a stale g; consistency removes that case, and then the closed set can be final and the proof is simply Dijkstra's. Consistency implies admissibility by induction along any path from v to t: h(v) ≤ w(v, v′) + h(v′) ≤ … ≤ w(path) + h(t) = w(path), and taking the shortest path gives h(v) ≤ δ(v, t). The converse fails: an admissible but inconsistent h can force a vertex to be reopened, and without reopening the graph-search version can return a suboptimal path.

Manhattan distance is the exact distance on an empty 4-connected grid, so it is admissible (walls only make the true distance longer) and consistent (one move changes |Δr| + |Δc| by at most 1, the move's cost). Euclidean distance is at most Manhattan, hence also admissible, and consistent by the triangle inequality of the plane — but it is a weaker bound on a grid, where diagonal shortcuts do not exist, and it expands more nodes: 65 against 50. The zero heuristic expands 86, most of the 101 free cells, because it grows the frontier in every direction until the goal happens to be reached. The ordering 86 ≥ 65 ≥ 50 is the general fact that a heuristic that dominates another (h₂ ≥ h₁ pointwise, both consistent) expands no more nodes, up to ties — every node A* expands with h₂ it would also expand with h₁, because f₂(v) < δ(s, t) implies f₁(v) < δ(s, t). Counts depend on the tie-break; the figure's routine breaks equal f toward larger g (deeper first, the usual choice, since it pushes toward the goal) and then first-inserted, and the figure re-measures everything at load and checks each path length against the BFS distance. Doubling Manhattan makes it inadmissible: the search is greedier still (49 expansions) and returns a 25-step path — it dives toward the goal, hits the wall column, and commits to a detour that a 21-step path avoids. Weighted A* with factor ε has the guarantee cost ≤ ε · δ(s, t), here 25 ≤ 42, and is used deliberately when a quick near-optimal answer is worth more than an optimal one; the figure's second instance, a grid with two long walls, shows that the inadmissible heuristic sometimes lands on the optimum anyway (43 expansions, 19 steps, optimal) — it is not reliably wrong, which is exactly what makes it dangerous when optimality is required.

Where the saving comes from, and its limits

A* with a consistent heuristic expands every reachable vertex with f(v) < δ(s, t), no vertex with f(v) > δ(s, t), and some of those with f(v) = δ(s, t), depending on ties. Under the potential view that set is "the vertices within reweighted distance δ̂(s, t) of s", the same ball Dijkstra would explore on ŵ — but the reweighting has shrunk the edges that point toward the goal (h drops by the edge's length, so ŵ ≈ 0) and stretched the edges that point away (ŵ ≈ 2w), so the ball is elongated toward t and squashed behind s. With the perfect heuristic h = δ(·, t) every edge on a shortest path has ŵ = 0, every other edge is positive, and the search walks straight to the goal. The limits: the heuristic must be cheap (it is evaluated once per relaxation, so anything costing more than O(1) or a table lookup eats the saving); it must be informed (on a maze whose shortest path winds away from the goal, Manhattan distance is admissible but nearly useless, and the expansion count approaches Dijkstra's); and memory is the open set, which for implicit graphs (Graph Representations & Traversal, §09 — puzzle states, robot configurations) is the binding constraint, motivating the memory-bounded variants. Bidirectional search is the other standard saving, independent of heuristics: run Dijkstra from s forward and from t backward on the reversed graph, stop when the two frontiers' minimum keys sum to at least the best meeting-point path found; each side explores roughly a ball of half the radius, which on a graph whose balls grow polynomially in the radius cuts the work by a large constant, and on graphs where they grow exponentially cuts it to the square root. Combining the two — bidirectional A* — is possible but delicate: the naïve stopping rule can miss the optimum, and the consistent-potential formulation is what makes the correct rule expressible.
