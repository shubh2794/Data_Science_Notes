07 Bellman-Ford, stepped: V − 1 rounds, early exit, the negative cycle found and reported

The Bellman-Ford algorithm computes single-source shortest paths with edge weights of any sign by relaxing every edge of the graph, in any fixed order, V − 1 times over — and then relaxing every edge once more: if that extra round still lowers an estimate, a negative cycle is reachable from the source, and the algorithm reports it instead of distances. It needs no priority queue, no restriction on the weights and no structure in the graph, and it costs O(VE) for that generality: on a sparse graph a factor of V/log V more than Dijkstra. It is the algorithm of last resort for single-source shortest paths, the negative-cycle detector every other method relies on, the engine of difference-constraint solving (§10) and of Johnson's reweighting (§14), and — as §08 shows — a dynamic program in disguise.

Worked: the same graph with a negative cycle

Change the weight of 4→1 from 3 to −6. The cycle 1→2→3→4→1 now weighs −3 + 4 − 2 − 6 = −7, it is reachable from s, and it reaches every vertex but s; so δ(s, s) = 0 and δ = −∞ for 1, 2, 3, 4. The first three rounds are identical to the table above (the edge 4→1 has tail ∞ in round 1 and 11, 7 thereafter, and 11 − 6 = 5 ≥ 4 does not improve 1 in round 3). In round 4, 4→1 relaxes 7 − 6 = 1 < 4 and the cycle begins to turn: d = ⟨0, 1, −2, 5, 3⟩. The test round lowers three estimates more, to ⟨0, −3, −6, 2, 3⟩, and the algorithm reports a negative cycle. To name it, take the first vertex whose estimate fell in the test round — vertex 1 — and walk π back five times: 1 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 4, landing on 4; then follow π from 4 until it returns: 4, 3, 2, 1, which in edge direction is the cycle 1→2→3→4→1 of weight −7. The −∞ set is {1, 2, 3, 4}: the vertices improved in the test round and everything reachable from them. Brute force confirms both: the lightest of the 3 simple cycles reachable from s weighs −7, and the set of vertices reachable from a negative cycle is exactly {1, 2, 3, 4}. The finite values the algorithm holds for those vertices when it stops mean nothing — they are the estimates after five rounds of a process that would go to −∞ if allowed to continue — and an implementation must not return them as distances.

What the algorithm returns, and what to do with a negative cycle

Without a negative cycle the return value is the same d, π pair as Dijkstra's, and the predecessor-subgraph property makes π a shortest-paths tree; unreachable vertices keep d = ∞ and π = NIL by the no-path property. With a negative cycle three outputs are possible and an implementation should choose deliberately: a boolean ("there is a negative cycle reachable from s"), the cycle itself as a vertex list, or the partition of V into finite-δ vertices (still correct: a vertex no negative cycle can reach has its shortest path relaxed in order during the first V − 1 rounds regardless of what happens elsewhere), −∞ vertices, and unreachable ones. The third is what §10's constraint solver and §14's reweighting need. A negative cycle in the whole graph, not just the part reachable from s, is found by the usual trick of a virtual source joined to every vertex by a zero-weight edge, which makes everything reachable and adds nothing to any cycle. And in the arbitrage reading of §03 — weights −log(rate) — the reported cycle is the sequence of trades, its weight is −log of the round-trip gain, and the vertices it reaches are the currencies from which that gain is accessible.

08 Why Bellman-Ford is right: the path-relaxation property, and the DP view

Bellman-Ford is correct because a shortest path has at most V − 1 edges and each round relaxes every edge, so after round i the first i edges of every shortest path have been relaxed in path order — and the path-relaxation property of §02 then guarantees the estimate at the end of any shortest path with at most i edges is exact. The negative-cycle test is correct in both directions by a two-line argument each way, and the reported cycle is genuinely negative by the argument that already showed the predecessor subgraph is acyclic. Underneath, the algorithm is the dynamic program "shortest walk with at most k edges" evaluated for k = 1 … V − 1, which explains the round count, the space, and why the in-place one-array version is never worse than the recurrence it implements.

The dynamic-programming view: "at most k edges"

Dynamic Programming, §23 arrives at Bellman-Ford from the other side: shortest paths are a DP on a DAG, a graph with cycles has no evaluation order, and the cure is to add a parameter that strictly decreases — the number of edges allowed. Define dₖ(v) as the weight of the shortest s → v walk using at most k edges. Then d₀(s) = 0, d₀(v) = ∞ otherwise, and dₖ(v) = min( dₖ₋₁(v), min over edges (u, v) of dₖ₋₁(u) + w(u, v) ): a shortest walk of at most k edges either has fewer than k edges, or ends with some edge (u, v) preceded by a shortest walk of at most k − 1 edges to u — cut and paste on the last edge. The state space is V × V (vertex, edge budget), each state reads its in-edges, so the cost is (V − 1) × E: Bellman-Ford's bound is the DP's "states times choices". With no negative cycle reachable, a shortest walk is a simple path, d_{V−1}(v) = δ(s, v), and the recurrence is the algorithm, with round k computing row k from row k − 1. Two things the DP view makes obvious and the graph view does not. The rows are independent of edge order — dₖ is defined by a minimum over all walks, not by a schedule — so the two-array implementation always takes exactly V − 1 rounds and does not benefit from a good ordering; measured on the §07 instance, the rows are ⟨0 ∞ ∞ ∞ ∞⟩, ⟨0 4 5 ∞ ∞⟩, ⟨0 4 1 9 11⟩, ⟨0 4 1 5 7⟩, ⟨0 4 1 5 3⟩ for both the list order and its reverse, 32 relaxations either way. And the negative-cycle test is the statement that d_V ≠ d_{V−1}: a walk of V edges beats every walk of fewer, which is only possible if it goes round a cycle and the cycle pays.

Why the in-place version is at least as good as the two-array version

The algorithm of §07 keeps a single array and relaxes edges using values already updated in the current round; the recurrence reads only the previous row. The one-array version is not the recurrence, but it dominates it. Claim: after round k of the in-place algorithm, d[v] ≤ dₖ(v) for every v. Proof: induction on k; the base is initialisation. In round k, when the edge (u, v) is relaxed the in-place value of u is at most its value at the end of round k − 1, which is ≤ dₖ₋₁(u) by hypothesis, so the relaxation leaves d[v] ≤ dₖ₋₁(u) + w(u, v); and d[v] was already ≤ dₖ₋₁(v) at the round's start and never rises. Taking the minimum over in-edges, d[v] ≤ dₖ(v). Meanwhile the upper-bound property keeps d[v] ≥ δ(s, v) throughout, since the in-place algorithm is a sequence of relaxations. So the in-place estimates are squeezed between δ and the recurrence's rows: they reach δ no later than the two-array version, sometimes much earlier — on the §07 instance with the reversed edge order the in-place version is exact after one round while the recurrence still needs four — and they can be checked for stability to exit early, which the recurrence cannot. Space is Θ(V) either way (the recurrence keeps two rows), but the in-place form is the one to implement. This is the same observation as the DP page's rolling-array discussion, with one difference of substance: rolling a DP array is a space optimisation that must not change the answer, whereas relaxing in place changes the intermediate values and is licensed only because relaxation can never overshoot.

Ordering heuristics, and the queue-based variant

Because the round count depends on how the edge order meets the shortest paths, two heuristics are common and neither changes the worst case. The first fixes a vertex order, splits the edges into those that go forward in it and those that go backward, and relaxes all forward edges in vertex order then all backward edges in reverse vertex order; a shortest path alternates between forward and backward runs and each round handles one run of each kind, so ⌈V/2⌉ rounds suffice — half the work, same order of growth. The second is the queue-based variant, often called SPFA: keep a FIFO queue of vertices whose estimate has changed since their out-edges were last relaxed, initially just s; pop a vertex, relax its out-edges, and push any vertex whose estimate fell if it is not already queued. It relaxes only edges that might be tense, which on many graphs is a small fraction of E per "round", and it terminates when the queue empties. Its worst case is the same O(VE) — each vertex can be dequeued up to V − 1 times, once per edge count of its shortest path, and adversarial instances (certain grids with weights chosen so that estimates fall by one repeatedly) drive it there — and it detects a negative cycle when some vertex is dequeued a V-th time or, equivalently, when a π-chain grows to V edges. It is a heuristic with a good typical case and no better guarantee, and it is not measured here for that reason: an instance family on which it looks fast says nothing about the family on which it is slow. When the weights are non-negative, Dijkstra's ordering is the heuristic that does carry a guarantee — each edge relaxed once — and that is the whole difference between the two algorithms: one commits to an order and needs w ≥ 0 to justify it, the other commits to nothing and pays V − 1 rounds for the freedom.

What to carry forward. Bellman-Ford's proof is the path-relaxation property plus "a shortest path has ≤ V − 1 edges"; Dijkstra's is the same property plus a reason the first relaxation of each edge is already in path order. The negative-cycle test is exact in both directions, and the reported cycle is negative. The DP view gives the bound as states × choices and shows that rounds, not edge order, are what the recurrence counts; the in-place algorithm is a dominated-by-δ, dominating-the-recurrence sequence of relaxations, which is why it may exit early and why it is the version to write. The DAG algorithm of §09 is what remains when the graph has a topological order and the "at most k edges" parameter is no longer needed.

09 Shortest and longest paths in a DAG: one pass, critical paths

On a directed acyclic graph, single-source shortest paths are computed by relaxing every edge exactly once, taking the vertices in a topological order — Θ(V + E) time, no priority queue, and no restriction on the sign of the weights. The reason is the path-relaxation property of §02: relaxing the edges of a shortest path in the order they appear on the path makes the endpoint's estimate exact, whatever other relaxations happen in between. A topological order relaxes the edges of every path in path order at once, so one sweep suffices. The same sweep with max in place of min computes longest paths — which is intractable in general graphs and trivial here — and that is the critical-path computation of project scheduling.

Why one pass is enough: the path-relaxation property applied to every path at once

Take any vertex v reachable from s and a shortest path p = ⟨s = v₀, v₁, …, vₖ = v⟩. Every edge (vᵢ₋₁, vᵢ) of p is a directed edge, so vᵢ₋₁ precedes vᵢ in any topological order, and the sweep processes v₀ before v₁ before … before vₖ₋₁. Since the sweep relaxes all out-edges of a vertex when it processes that vertex, the edges of p are relaxed in the order (v₀, v₁), (v₁, v₂), …, (vₖ₋₁, vₖ) — possibly with many other relaxations in between, which is exactly the situation the path-relaxation property covers: after that sequence, d[v] = δ(s, v), and by the upper-bound property nothing later can lower it further or raise it at all. The argument used nothing about the weights' signs — only that p is a shortest path and that shortest paths are well defined, which in a DAG they always are, since a negative-weight cycle is the only thing that can make them undefined (§03) and a DAG has no cycle of any weight. Vertices not reachable from s keep d = ∞ because no relaxation from a finite estimate ever reaches them. The predecessor pointers form a shortest-path tree by the predecessor-subgraph property. The cost accounting is an aggregate argument: the inner loop runs once per out-edge of each vertex, and summing out-degrees over all vertices gives E, so exactly E relaxations happen regardless of the order chosen among the (possibly many) valid topological orders.

Longest paths, and why negation is legal here and not in general

Longest simple paths in a general directed graph are NP-hard (a longest simple path visiting every vertex is a Hamiltonian path). The reason the DAG case collapses to one pass is the same reason shortest paths needed no sign restriction: the cut-and-paste step "replace the prefix of the path by a better prefix" produces a walk that might repeat a vertex in a cyclic graph, and in a DAG cannot. So the argument above runs verbatim with max for min, −∞ for ∞, and "longest" for "shortest". The second route to the same answer is to negate every weight and run the shortest-path sweep unchanged: the longest path under w is the shortest under −w, and the value flips sign. The reason this is not a general recipe — "to get longest paths, negate and run Dijkstra" is a classic wrong answer — is that negation turns every positive cycle into a negative cycle, and a negative cycle makes shortest paths undefined and breaks Dijkstra's invariant (§05) besides. In a DAG there is no cycle to flip, so −w is as harmless as w. The routine behind figure 06 computes the longest values both ways on the task graph below and checks they agree.

Critical-path scheduling: seven tasks, worked in full

A project is a set of tasks with durations and precedence constraints, "u must finish before v starts". The earliest time the whole project can finish is the length of the longest chain of dependent tasks, the critical path; every task on it has no room to slip, and any other task can be delayed by its slack without delaying the project. The dynamic-programming view, and where this sits among the algorithms

The sweep is a dynamic program whose state is a vertex and whose evaluation order is the topological order — Dynamic Programming, §23 develops that view, shows that every table-filling DP is a shortest- or longest-path computation on the DAG of its subproblems, and derives Bellman-Ford and Floyd-Warshall as the same recursion with an extra parameter that restores acyclicity when the graph has cycles. Relative to the rest of this page: Dijkstra (§04) needs w ≥ 0 and pays O((V + E) log V) for its heap; Bellman-Ford (§07) allows negative edges and pays O(VE); the DAG sweep allows negative edges, pays Θ(V + E), and computes longest paths for free — but only on a DAG. On a graph that is not known to be acyclic, the topological sort's failure (a back edge, or fewer than V vertices output) is the test, and it costs the same Θ(V + E). Two edge cases worth naming: a vertex with no in-edges other than the source is exact after the source's out-edges are relaxed; and a DAG with several valid topological orders gives the same d[] under all of them, though the predecessor tree may differ where ties exist, as it does for the two shortest chains above.

10 Systems of difference constraints as a shortest-path problem

A system of difference constraints is a set of m inequalities of the form xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ bₖ over n unknowns, and it is feasible if and only if its constraint graph — one vertex per unknown, one edge vᵢ → vⱼ of weight bₖ per constraint, plus a source v₀ with a zero-weight edge to everything — has no negative-weight cycle; when it is feasible, xᵢ = δ(v₀, vᵢ) is a solution. This is the first place on the page where a shortest-path algorithm solves something that is not, on its face, a path problem, and it is the reason Bellman-Ford's negative-cycle report (§07) matters as an answer rather than as an error: the cycle is a certificate that the constraints contradict each other. Time windows in scheduling, clock-skew and timing analysis in circuits, and the feasibility half of a linear program whose every row has one +1 and one −1 are all instances.

Proof, both directions

(a) The distances satisfy every constraint. Take any constraint xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ bₖ, i.e. any edge vᵢ → vⱼ of weight bₖ. The triangle inequality for shortest-path weights (§02) says δ(v₀, vⱼ) ≤ δ(v₀, vᵢ) + w(vᵢ, vⱼ) — a shortest path to vⱼ is no longer than "a shortest path to vᵢ, then the edge". Substituting xᵢ = δ(v₀, vᵢ) and xⱼ = δ(v₀, vⱼ) gives xⱼ ≤ xᵢ + bₖ, which is the constraint. The v₀ edges are what make every δ finite (every vertex is one zero-weight edge from the source), so the substitution is always defined; without v₀, an unknown unreachable from the chosen source would get xᵢ = ∞. (b) A negative cycle is a contradiction. Let c = ⟨v₁, v₂, …, vₖ = v₁⟩ be a cycle of negative total weight; v₀ is not on it, since v₀ has no incoming edges, so every edge of the cycle is a constraint: x₂ − x₁ ≤ w(v₁, v₂), x₃ − x₂ ≤ w(v₂, v₃), …, x₁ − xₖ₋₁ ≤ w(vₖ₋₁, v₁). Suppose a solution existed and add these k − 1 inequalities. On the left every unknown appears once with + and once with −, so the sum telescopes to 0; on the right the sum is w(c) < 0. That is 0 ≤ w(c) < 0, a contradiction, so no solution exists. Note the asymmetry the proof exposes: a negative cycle is a local certificate of infeasibility, a handful of constraints that already contradict each other, and Bellman-Ford's predecessor walk (§07) produces it explicitly.

Infeasible: flip one constraint

Replace constraint 4 by x₃ − x₅ ≤ −3 (edge v₅ → v₃ of weight −3). Now the cycle v₅ → v₃ → v₄ → v₅ has weight −3 − 2 + 2 = −3 < 0, and the three constraints on it — x₃ − x₅ ≤ −3, x₄ − x₃ ≤ −2, x₅ − x₄ ≤ 2 — add to 0 ≤ −3: no assignment can satisfy all three, whatever the other five say. Bellman-Ford shows it dynamically: the rounds never stop changing (d[v₄] runs −2, −5, −5, −8, −8, −11, … — it drops by 3 every second round as the cycle turns; d[v₃] runs −3, −3, −6, −6, −9, −9, …), the sixth round — the V-th — still improves an estimate, and the predecessor walk from the improved vertex lands on exactly that cycle. 6 rounds × 13 = 78 relaxations, measured in the figure's "flipped" mode.

Where the systems come from

Scheduling with windows. Let xᵢ be the start time of event i. "j starts at least 2 hours after i" is xᵢ − xⱼ ≤ −2; "at most 5 hours after" is xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ 5; "j and i start within an hour of each other" is the pair xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ 1, xᵢ − xⱼ ≤ 1. Any mixture of minimum gaps, maximum gaps and simultaneity constraints is a difference system, and the shortest-path solution is the schedule in which every event happens as late as the constraints allow relative to the latest one (all xᵢ ≤ 0); reversing every edge (keeping its weight), solving from v₀ again, and negating the result gives the as-early-as-possible schedule (all xᵢ ≥ 0, componentwise minimal) — the substitution x = −x′ turns xⱼ − xᵢ ≤ b into x′ᵢ − x′ⱼ ≤ b, which reverses the edge and keeps its weight. Checked on two constraints, x₂ − x₁ ≤ 5 and x₁ − x₂ ≤ −2 ("event 2 at least 2 after event 1, at most 5 after"): the graph has v₁ → v₂ (5) and v₂ → v₁ (−2), so δ = (−2, 0) is the latest schedule; reversing gives v₂ → v₁ (5) and v₁ → v₂ (−2), δ′ = (0, −2), and negating gives the earliest schedule (0, 2). Negating the weights as well would give (11, 9), which violates x₁ − x₂ ≤ −2. Critical-path scheduling (§09) is the special case with only minimum gaps — no maximum gaps means no cycles, hence a DAG and one pass. Timing analysis. Signal arrival times along a circuit with minimum and maximum delays per stage, clock-skew bounds between registers, and retiming constraints are difference systems on thousands of unknowns; a negative cycle is a timing violation, and the cycle Bellman-Ford returns is the set of paths that cannot all meet their bounds. Linear programming. A difference system is a linear program with no objective, each row of the matrix holding one +1 and one −1. General LP needs the simplex method or an interior-point method; this special structure is solved by a graph algorithm in O(nm), and adding an objective "maximise Σ xᵢ" is still solvable by the same machinery (the δ solution is componentwise maximal among solutions with max xᵢ ≤ 0). Two things it does not extend to: constraints with a coefficient other than ±1, and constraints on three or more unknowns — either change destroys the telescoping that made the cycle sum vanish.

11 0-1 BFS and Dial's buckets: when the weights are small integers

When every edge weight is 0 or 1, single-source shortest paths take O(V + E) with a deque in place of Dijkstra's heap: a relaxation across a 0-edge pushes the vertex to the front, across a 1-edge to the back, and the deque then pops vertices in non-decreasing distance order exactly as a heap would. When the weights are integers in 0 … C, Dial's algorithm keeps C + 1 buckets indexed by distance and sweeps them in order, in O(E + V·C). Both are Dijkstra (§04) with the priority queue replaced by a structure that exploits what the keys are: the correctness proof of §05 only needs the vertices to be settled in non-decreasing order of distance, and a heap is merely the general-purpose way to obtain that order. When the keys take few distinct values, a cheaper structure gives the same order.

0-1 BFSd[s] ← 0, others ∞; deque ← ⟨(s, 0)⟩ while deque not empty: (u, ℓ) ← pop-front; if ℓ > d[u] or u already expanded: skip (stale) — each vertex is expanded once for each edge (u, v): if d[u] + w < d[v]: d[v] ← d[u] + w; w = 0 ⇒ push-front (v, d[v]) w = 1 ⇒ push-back (v, d[v]) INVARIANT the labels in the deque, front to back, are non-decreasing and take at most TWO distinct values, D and D + 1 ⇒ pops come out in non-decreasing label order, so this is Dijkstra's settling order, and the §05 proof applies unchanged COST every vertex expanded once, every edge relaxed once, each push/pop O(1) ⇒ O(V + E); at most one entry enters the deque per successful relaxation, plus the source Dial's algorithm (bucket queue), integer weights 0 … Cbuckets B[0 … C] as a circular array, B[d mod (C + 1)] holds vertices whose label is d; a pointer cur sweeps 0, 1, 2, … pop from B[cur mod (C+1)] until empty, then cur ← cur + 1; a relaxation to label d[v] ≤ cur + C goes into B[d[v] mod (C+1)] WHY C + 1 SUFFICE every label in the buckets lies in [cur, cur + C] (it was cur′ + w with cur′ ≤ cur, w ≤ C, and nothing below cur is left) COST O(E) inserts and pops + the pointer advances at most max distance ≤ (V − 1)·C times ⇒ O(E + V·C) (radix heaps refine the buckets to widths 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, …, giving O(E + V·log C) — a pointer, not built here)

Why pushing to the front is right

The heap in Dijkstra exists to answer one question: which unsettled vertex has the smallest tentative distance? With weights in {0, 1}, the answer has a shape. Suppose the vertex just popped has label D. A relaxation across a 0-edge gives its neighbour label D — the same as the current minimum, so it belongs ahead of everything at D + 1 and is tied with everything at D; pushing it to the front is safe because ties may be settled in any order. A relaxation across a 1-edge gives label D + 1, which is at least as large as every label already present (they are all D or D + 1), so it belongs at the back. Induction on the operations proves the invariant stated above: initially the deque holds one label; a pop-front removes the minimum and leaves a non-decreasing sequence of at most two values; a push-front of D when the minimum is D keeps both properties; a push-back of D + 1 when the maximum is at most D + 1 keeps both. Once the minimum has advanced to D + 1, the new pushes are D + 1 and D + 2, and the same two-value picture holds one step along. Because a vertex's tentative distance strictly decreases each time it is pushed, a vertex has at most one entry with its final label, and only that entry is expanded — the entries with larger labels are skipped as stale when they surface. So each vertex is expanded once and each of its edges relaxed once, and with O(1) deque operations the total is O(V + E): the bound of unweighted BFS, of which this is a strict generalisation (with all weights 1 the front is never used and the deque is a queue). The figure's readout verifies the invariant on the instance it draws — the largest number of distinct labels the deque ever held is printed — and the readout also repeats the check on 200 random 4 × 6 grids with 0/1 weights each time the page loads: two distinct labels at most, non-decreasing, every vertex expanded exactly once, distances equal to Dijkstra's, in all 200 (the same check on 500 grids was run while this page was written, with the same result).

Dial's buckets, worked

Directed edges 0→1 (2), 0→2 (3), 1→2 (1), 1→3 (3), 2→3 (1), 2→4 (2), 3→5 (2), 4→5 (3), C = 3, so four buckets B[0 … 3] and a label d lives in B[d mod 4]. Popping 0 at cur = 0 inserts 1 (label 2 → B[2]) and 2 (label 3 → B[3]); the pointer advances through the empty B[1] to B[2] and pops 1 at cur = 2; 1→2 offers 2 + 1 = 3, a tie with the label 2 already holds, so nothing changes (the relaxation is strict); 1→3 inserts 3 at label 5 → B[1]; cur = 3 pops 2, improving 3 to label 4 → B[0] and 4 to 5 → B[1]; cur = 4 pops 3 from B[0] and inserts 5 at label 6 → B[2]; cur = 5 pops the stale entry of 3 (its label 5 exceeds d[3] = 4) and then 4; cur = 6 pops 5. Distances ⟨0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6⟩; 7 inserts, 7 pops (one stale), 8 relaxations, and the pointer made 13 bucket inspections — one per pop plus one per advance of the pointer, i.e. pops + (label of the last pop) = 7 + 6, here equal to pops + max distance because the last pop was a live one; when the last entry popped is stale its label can exceed the largest finalised distance by up to C, which is the inequality the figure's readout checks alongside the exact count. The wrap-around is visible: label 4 sits in B[0] and label 5 in B[1] while cur is still 3, and nothing goes wrong because every live label is within C of the pointer.

When these beat a binary heap, measured

The figure's default instance is a 5 × 8 grid, 40 vertices and 67 undirected edges, each edge weighted 0 or 1 by a seeded coin (35 of the 67 are zero). 0-1 BFS from the corner: 49 pops, of which 9 stale, 26 front pushes and 22 back pushes, 134 relaxations = 2E (each undirected edge is two arcs, each relaxed once from its own endpoint), the deque never holding more than two distinct labels, all distances at most 3. Dijkstra with a binary heap and lazy deletion on the same instance (§06) does 47 pushes and 47 pops, 7 of them stale, and each of those heap operations costs O(log n) where the deque's cost O(1). Both give the same distances. With weights 1 … 4 (C = 4) on the same grid, Dial's algorithm does 45 inserts, 45 pops (5 stale) and 65 bucket inspections against Dijkstra's 45 heap pushes and 45 pops; the last pop's label is the largest distance, 20, so the pointer advanced 20 times and 65 = 45 + 20. On a 40-vertex grid nothing is slow, and the point is the bound, not the stopwatch: O(E + V·C) beats O((V + E) log V) when C is smaller than log V — road networks with weights in metres do not qualify (C is in the thousands), but hop counts, small penalties, unit costs with a few zero-cost shortcuts, and the "minimum number of edits/flips/moves" problems that arise as implicit graphs (Graph Representations & Traversal, §09) do. 0-1 BFS is the more common of the two in practice, because 0/1 weights arise whenever a problem asks "the fewest bad steps" and the good steps are free. The radix heap, mentioned above, closes the gap for larger C: O(E + V log C), which beats the binary heap whenever C < V and is the structure behind the fastest practical integer-weight Dijkstra variants.
