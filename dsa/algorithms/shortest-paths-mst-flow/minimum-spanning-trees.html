16 Minimum spanning trees: the cut property and the cycle property

A minimum spanning tree (MST) of a connected, undirected, edge-weighted graph is a spanning tree — an acyclic subgraph that connects every vertex, which forces it to have exactly V − 1 edges — whose total edge weight is as small as any spanning tree's. Everything in this section and the four after it is for undirected graphs: on a directed graph the analogous object is the minimum spanning arborescence — a rooted out-tree reaching every vertex, each vertex except the root with exactly one incoming arc — and the cut and cycle properties below do not carry over to it as stated, so it needs a different algorithm (Chu-Liu/Edmonds: take each vertex's cheapest incoming arc, contract every cycle those arcs form, adjust the weights of the arcs entering the contracted vertex, and repeat), not Kruskal or Prim run on the arcs. An MST exists if and only if the graph is connected (a disconnected graph has a minimum spanning forest, one tree per component, and every algorithm below produces that instead), it is the cheapest way to make everything reachable from everything, and it is found by greedy algorithms — Kruskal's, Prim's and Borůvka's — whose correctness rests on a single lemma about cuts and its mirror image about cycles. This section states the problem, proves both lemmas, derives uniqueness under distinct weights, sets out the generic algorithm the three named ones instantiate, and previews the contrast the page keeps returning to: an MST is not a shortest-path tree, and the two answer different questions on the same graph. The shared instance is the six-vertex, nine-edge graph on which Greedy Algorithms, §20 first states the cut property; that page states it, this one proves it, checks it by brute force over all 55 spanning trees, and builds the algorithms.

definitionsG = (V, E) undirected, connected, w : E → ℝ. A SPANNING TREE T ⊆ E is acyclic and connects all of V; |T| = V − 1 (either of "acyclic" or "connected" plus the edge count gives the other). w(T) = ∑ over e ∈ T of w(e). A MINIMUM spanning tree minimises w(T) over all spanning trees. The SPANNING FOREST version (G disconnected) is the union of one MST per component. a CUT (S, V − S) is a partition of V, S ≠ ∅, S ≠ V. An edge CROSSES the cut if exactly one endpoint is in S. An edge set A RESPECTS the cut if no edge of A crosses it. a LIGHT edge across a cut is a crossing edge of minimum weight (there may be several). An edge is SAFE for A ⊆ some MST if A ∪ {e} is still contained in some MST. the CUT property — the greedy-choice lemma for spanning treesHYPOTHESIS A ⊆ T for some MST T; (S, V − S) a cut that A respects; e = (u, v) a light edge crossing it. CONCLUSION e is safe for A: A ∪ {e} ⊆ T′ for some MST T′. proof — the exchange argumentif e ∈ T there is nothing to do (T′ = T). Otherwise T ∪ {e} contains exactly one cycle: the unique tree path P from u to v in T, closed by e. P starts in S (at u) and ends outside S (at v), so it crosses the cut an odd number of times — at least once. Let e′ be any edge of P that crosses the cut. e′ ∉ A, because A respects the cut and e′ crosses it. Let T′ = T − {e′} ∪ {e}. T′ is a spanning tree: removing e′ from T splits it into two components, one containing u and one containing v (e′ lies on the u–v path); e re-joins them; |T′| = V − 1 and it is connected. w(T′) = w(T) − w(e′) + w(e) ≤ w(T), because e is light across the cut and e′ crosses the same cut, so w(e) ≤ w(e′). T is minimum, hence w(T′) = w(T) and T′ is also an MST; and A ∪ {e} ⊆ T′ since A ⊆ T, e′ ∉ A, e ∈ T′. ∎ COROLLARY at any stage of any algorithm whose A is contained in an MST, a light edge across ANY cut respected by A can be added without loss.

The cycle property — the same fact seen from the other side

The cut property says which edges must be in: the lightest way out of any set of vertices. Its dual says which edges must be out: the heaviest way around any cycle. Both are the same exchange argument with the roles of "cheap crossing edge" and "expensive cycle edge" swapped, and the two together are what every MST algorithm and every MST verification rests on.

the CYCLE propertyHYPOTHESIS C is a cycle in G and f is an edge of C with w(f) > w(e) for every other edge e of C (f is the STRICTLY heaviest edge on C). CONCLUSION f lies in no minimum spanning tree. proof: suppose f = (x, y) ∈ T for an MST T. Remove f: T splits into two components, x on one side and y on the other — a cut (S, V − S) that f crosses. C is a cycle through f, so C − {f} is an x–y path, and it must cross the cut somewhere — at an edge e ∈ C, e ≠ f, e ∉ T (T − {f} does not cross the cut). T′ = T − {f} ∪ {e} is a spanning tree (same argument as above) with w(T′) = w(T) − w(f) + w(e) < w(T), contradicting minimality. ∎ with TIES if f is merely a heaviest (not strictly) edge on C, the same exchange gives w(T′) = w(T): f may be in some MST, but SOME MST omits it. The strict version is what "distinct weights" buys. on the shared instance — every non-MST edge is the heaviest on the cycle it closes (the figure re-derives this)BD (5) closes A–B–D with AB (4), AD (2) BC (6) closes B–E–F–C with BE (3), EF (5), CF (1) CE (7) closes C–F–E with CF (1), EF (5) DE (8) closes D–A–B–E with AD (2), AB (4), BE (3) so the four edges outside the MST {AD, AB, BE, EF, CF} are exactly the four that the strict cycle property bars from every MST; the five that remain form a spanning tree, so that tree is the unique MST — a complete certificate of optimality without enumerating a single other tree. VERIFICATION in general: a spanning tree T is minimum ⇔ every non-tree edge e is at least as heavy as every edge on T's path between e's endpoints (⇒ by the exchange; ⇐ because Kruskal's run would then accept exactly T's edges under a suitable tie order).

Uniqueness: distinct weights are sufficient, ties may or may not break it

THEOREM if all edge weights are distinct, the minimum spanning tree is uniqueproof: let T₁ ≠ T₂ be two MSTs. Their symmetric difference is non-empty (both have V − 1 edges and they differ), so let e be the LIGHTEST edge in exactly one of them — say e ∈ T₁, e ∉ T₂ (weights distinct ⇒ "lightest" is unambiguous). remove e from T₁: a cut (S, V − S) that e crosses. T₂ connects the two sides, so some e′ ∈ T₂ crosses the cut, and e′ ∉ T₁ (T₁ − {e} has no crossing edge). e′ is in exactly one of the trees and is not e, so by the choice of e, w(e) < w(e′) (strict — distinct weights). T₂ − {e′} ∪ {e} is a spanning tree of weight w(T₂) − w(e′) + w(e) < w(T₂): T₂ was not minimum. Contradiction. ∎ CONVERSE FAILS the shared instance has BD = EF = 5 and still exactly one MST (55 trees enumerated, one of weight 15): the tie is between an edge that closes a cycle of lighter edges (BD) and one that does not (EF), so it never has to be broken. NON-UNIQUE EXAMPLE a triangle with all three edges of weight 1 has three spanning trees, all of weight 2, all minimum; a 4-cycle with equal weights has four. In general the number of MSTs can be exponential in V: a chain of k triangles joined at shared vertices, all weights equal, has 3ᵏ minimum spanning trees — each triangle independently drops one of its 3 edges (checked by brute force: 3, 9, 27, 81 trees for k = 1 … 4). WHAT TIES COST THE ALGORITHMS nothing for correctness — each algorithm returns SOME MST, with the tie broken by whatever order it examines edges — but two algorithms (or one algorithm from two roots) may return different edge SETS of equal weight (§19 checks this).

The generic algorithm, and why three different loops are the same algorithm

Every MST algorithm on this page is the following loop: keep an edge set A that is always a subset of some MST (initially empty — trivially contained in any MST), and repeatedly add a safe edge until A has V − 1 edges. The cut property supplies the safe edges: choose any cut that A respects, take a light edge across it. Since the invariant "A ⊆ some MST" holds at the start and is preserved by every step, it holds at the end, and an edge set of size V − 1 contained in a spanning tree is that spanning tree. The three named algorithms differ only in which cut they choose at each step, and therefore in the data structure that finds the light edge across it:

GENERIC-MST(G, w)A = ∅ while A is not a spanning tree: find an edge e that is SAFE for A ← the cut property: any cut A respects, any light edge across it A = A ∪ {e} return A INVARIANT before each iteration, A ⊆ some MST. TERMINATION |A| grows by one per step; after V − 1 steps A is a spanning tree (connected: else a cut it respects would still have a crossing edge). the three instantiationsKRUSKAL (§17) A is a forest. Cut = (the tree containing u, everything else) for the lightest edge (u, v) whose endpoints are in different trees — light across that cut by construction. Needs: sorted edges + union-find. PRIM (§19) A is one tree grown from a root r. Cut = (vertices of A, the rest). Light edge = the lightest edge leaving the tree — a priority queue keyed by "lightest edge into A" per outside vertex. BORŮVKA (§20) A is a forest; EVERY tree adds its lightest outgoing edge in the same round — each is light across (that tree, the rest), a cut A respects; needs distinct weights (or a consistent tie-break) so the added edges cannot close a cycle. all three add only safe edges ⇒ all three are correct by the invariant; they differ in cost profile, not in what they compute.

Minimum bottleneck spanning trees: every MST is one, and not conversely

definition and theoremthe BOTTLENECK of a spanning tree is its heaviest edge; a MINIMUM BOTTLENECK spanning tree (MBST) minimises that maximum. THEOREM every minimum spanning tree is a minimum bottleneck spanning tree. proof: let T be an MST with bottleneck edge f = (x, y), w(f) = b, and suppose some spanning tree T* has every edge lighter than b. Remove f from T: a cut (S, V − S). T* connects the two sides, so it has an edge e crossing the cut, with w(e) < b = w(f). Then T − {f} ∪ {e} is a spanning tree lighter than T — contradiction. ∎ CONVERSE FAILS on the shared instance the MST's bottleneck is 5 (edge EF), and {AD, BD, BE, CF, EF} also has bottleneck 5 but weight 16 — an MBST that is not an MST. (The figure's readout lists every tree sharing the minimum bottleneck.) USE "the path between u and v whose heaviest edge is lightest" (the MINIMAX path — widest path, safest route) is the MST path between u and v, for every pair at once: one MST answers all V² bottleneck queries.

MST is not a shortest-path tree

The two trees are easy to confuse because Prim's algorithm and Dijkstra's algorithm (§04) are the same loop with a different key, and both produce a spanning tree rooted at a start vertex. But they optimise different things: the MST minimises the sum of the tree's edges, the shortest-path tree minimises every root-to-vertex path length, and neither objective implies the other. On the shared instance, from root A, the shortest-path tree is {AB, AD, BE, BC, CF} with total weight 16, and the MST is {AB, AD, BE, EF, CF} with weight 15; the MST's path from A to C runs A–B–E–F–C for 13, while the true distance is 10. §20 draws both trees side by side and measures the largest such stretch. The general facts: a shortest-path tree's weight is at least the MST's (it is a spanning tree), an MST path can be nearly V − 1 times longer than the shortest path (a cycle of unit edges with one edge of weight 1 + ε: the MST drops that edge, so its path between that edge's endpoints has length V − 1 against a distance of 1 + ε), and with non-negative weights the one edge the two trees are guaranteed to share is the root's lightest incident edge when it is unique — it is light across the cut ({r}, V − {r}) and it is the shortest path to its other endpoint.

17 Kruskal's algorithm, stepped

Kruskal's algorithm builds a minimum spanning tree by sorting the edges by weight and walking them in that order, accepting an edge if and only if its endpoints are currently in different trees of the forest built so far — so that it never closes a cycle — and stopping when V − 1 edges have been accepted. The test "different trees?" is a pair of find operations on a union-find structure, and each acceptance is a union; with the two heuristics of §18 the whole walk costs O(E·α(V)), which is negligible beside the Θ(E log E) sort. Since E ≤ V², log E ≤ 2 log V, and the bound is O(E log V): the sort is the algorithm's cost. Correctness is the cut property applied once per accepted edge, and the section hand-works the shared instance edge by edge — order, verdict, reason, forest — with every count the figure reports measured by running the routine.

KRUSKAL(G, w)A = ∅ for each vertex v: makeSet(v) V make-sets sort E by weight, non-decreasing Θ(E log E) comparisons — the dominant term for each edge (u, v) in sorted order: E iterations, 2E finds in all if find(u) ≠ find(v): the endpoints are in different trees of the forest A A = A ∪ {(u, v)}; union(u, v) exactly V − 1 of these succeed on a connected graph return A (may stop early once |A| = V − 1; the figure runs the whole loop) INVARIANT A is a forest, and A ⊆ some MST (the second half is what the cut property gives) costsort Θ(E log E) = Θ(E log V) since log E ≤ log V² = 2 log V (and E ≥ V − 1 on a connected graph, so log E ≥ log(V − 1)) union-find V make-sets + 2E finds + (V − 1) unions = O((V + E)·α(V)) with union by rank + path compression (§18) — O(E log V) with rank alone, still dominated by the sort total O(E log V) time; Θ(V + E) space beyond the input (the sorted edge array, the parent array) already sorted or weights small integers (counting/radix sort, Sorting & Searching, §22–§21): the loop's O(E·α(V)) is the whole cost — near-linear correctness — one application of the cut property per accepted edgelet (u, v) be the edge about to be accepted, and C₁ the tree of A containing u. The cut is (C₁, V − C₁). A respects it: every edge of A lies inside one tree of the forest, and C₁ is a whole tree. (u, v) crosses it (v ∉ C₁, since find(u) ≠ find(v)) and is LIGHT across it: suppose a strictly lighter edge (x, y) crossed it, x ∈ C₁, y ∉ C₁. It was examined earlier. if it was ACCEPTED, x and y have been in one tree ever since (trees only merge) — so y ∈ C₁; if it was REJECTED, x and y were already connected then, hence still are — so y ∈ C₁. Either way y ∈ C₁: contradiction. so (u, v) is safe for A, A ∪ {(u, v)} ⊆ some MST, and by induction the final A — a spanning tree with V − 1 edges — IS an MST. ∎ REJECTED edges need no argument: a rejected edge closes a cycle whose other edges are all lighter-or-equal (they were accepted earlier), so it is a heaviest edge on that cycle — the cycle property says some MST omits it.

Ties, stability and what "the" MST means

When several edges share a weight, the sorted order among them is whatever the sort produces, and Kruskal's output can depend on it: on a graph with several MSTs, different tie orders give different (equally minimum) trees. A stable sort (Sorting & Searching, §11) keeps tied edges in input order, which makes the output a deterministic function of the input list — the figure's merge sort breaks ties by edge index for exactly this reason, and it is why the worked table can say "BD precedes EF" rather than "one of them". On the shared instance the tie is harmless — BD is rejected whichever way it is ordered, because A–B–D is already connected by lighter edges — and the MST is unique despite the tie, as §16 proved by enumeration. When the sort is by a comparison that ignores ties, an implementation that wants reproducible output should add a secondary key.

Where the time goes, and when Kruskal is the right choice

The sort is Θ(E log E) and everything else is near-linear, so Kruskal's algorithm is the natural choice when the edges are already sorted, when they can be sorted in linear time (small integer weights: counting or radix sort), or when the graph is sparse (E close to V) so that E log E is small. On a dense graph (E = Θ(V²)) the sort costs Θ(V² log V), and Prim's algorithm with an array (§19) does Θ(V²); the crossover is the same density argument that decides adjacency lists against adjacency matrices (Graph Representations & Traversal, §08). Kruskal has two structural advantages the cost table does not show: it processes edges as a stream (no adjacency structure is needed — an edge list suffices), and it produces the minimum spanning forest of a disconnected graph with no special handling, since the loop simply runs out of edges with fewer than V − 1 accepted. A third: stopping the loop early — after k acceptances short of V − 1 — leaves a forest of V − k trees that is the single-linkage clustering into V − k clusters (§20). A fourth, on the analysis side: it is the matroid greedy algorithm of Greedy Algorithms, §18 run on the graphic matroid with increasing weights, which is a second, independent proof of its correctness.

18 Union-find built: union by rank, path compression, and the inverse-Ackermann bound

Union-find (the disjoint-set forest) maintains a partition of n elements into sets under two operations — find(x), which returns a canonical representative of the set containing x, and union(x, y), which merges the sets of x and y — by storing each set as a rooted tree in a single parent array: find climbs parent pointers to the root, and union links one root under the other. Naively, a chain of unions can make a tree of height n − 1 and a find cost Θ(n). Two heuristics fix this. Union by rank links the shorter tree under the taller and bounds every height by ⌊log₂ n⌋, with proof. Path compression makes every find re-point the nodes it visited straight at the root, so no path is climbed twice. Together they give the celebrated bound: any sequence of m operations on n elements costs O(m·α(n)), where α is the inverse of an Ackermann-type function and is at most 4 for every n that will ever be written down — not constant in theory, indistinguishable from constant in practice. This section builds the structure that Graph Representations & Traversal, §27 named and deferred here, gives the proofs it can give in full, states the ones it cannot with their exact hypotheses, and measures every claim.

the structure — three arrays of n entries, or twoparent[x] the parent of x in its tree; parent[x] = x ⇔ x is a ROOT = the representative of its set rank[x] an upper bound on the height of the subtree at x (exact height until path compression starts shortening paths; never decreases; only roots' ranks change) makeSet(x): parent[x] = x; rank[x] = 0 Θ(1) find(x): r = x; while parent[r] ≠ r: r = parent[r] ← climb; the cost of a find is the number of hops (path compression, second pass) while parent[x] ≠ r: next = parent[x]; parent[x] = r; x = next return r union(x, y): rx = find(x); ry = find(y); if rx = ry: return ← already one set (union by rank) if rank[rx] > rank[ry]: parent[ry] = rx else: parent[rx] = ry; if rank[rx] = rank[ry]: rank[ry] += 1 ← the only time a rank grows (naive linking) parent[rx] = ry — x's root under y's root, whatever their sizes UNION BY SIZE is the same idea with size[root] = number of nodes: link the smaller under the larger; the same O(log n) height bound with the same proof shape. what a find returns is a NAME for the set that is stable until the next union touching it: "same set?" ⇔ find(u) = find(v).

Why the naive version is Θ(n) per find, and why the fix is two lines

With parent[rx] = ry unconditionally, the sequence union(0, 1), union(1, 2), …, union(n − 2, n − 1) builds a chain: after union(0, 1) the root of 0 is 1; union(1, 2) links that root under 2; and so on, until 0 is at depth n − 1 and find(0) costs n − 1 hops — on the figure's twelve elements, 11. Repeat that find m times and the total is Θ(mn). Nothing in the structure is wrong; the linking direction is simply arbitrary, and an adversary picks the bad direction every time. The fix is to make the direction informed — link the smaller (by rank or by size) under the larger — and, independently, to make each find leave the tree flatter than it found it. The figure's "chain" preset shows the naive chain of height 11; switching on either heuristic alone collapses it to height 1, for different reasons (rank never lets it form; compression flattens it on the first find(0), which costs 11 hops once and 1 thereafter).

Union by rank alone: height ≤ ⌊log₂ n⌋, proved

LEMMA with union by rank (and no compression) a root of rank r has at least 2ʳ nodes in its treeproof by induction on the operations. Initially every root has rank 0 and 1 = 2⁰ node. a LINK of rx under ry with rank[rx] < rank[ry]: ry's rank is unchanged and its tree only grew — the claim holds. a LINK with rank[rx] = rank[ry] = r: ry's rank becomes r + 1 and its tree has ≥ 2ʳ + 2ʳ = 2ʳ⁺¹ nodes — the claim holds. non-roots never change rank, and (without compression) a root's rank IS its height: the height only changes at a tie link, where it becomes r + 1. ∎ COROLLARY every rank is ≤ ⌊log₂ n⌋ (a rank-r tree needs 2ʳ ≤ n nodes); every find costs ≤ ⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1 hops; m operations cost O(m log n) — worst case, not amortised. ALSO the number of nodes of rank EXACTLY r is at most n / 2ʳ, hence of rank > k at most n/2ᵏ⁺¹ + n/2ᵏ⁺² + … ≤ n / 2ᵏ. (charge each rank-r node the ≥ 2ʳ nodes in its tree at the moment it reached rank r; these charged sets are disjoint, because a node that once sat below a rank-r ancestor only ever acquires ancestors of higher rank — ranks strictly increase up every path, and compression re-points only upwards.) This counting fact survives path compression (ranks never change once a node is a non-root) and is what the log* and α analyses lean on. MEASURED the figure's random workload (n = 1,000) ends with height 4 under rank alone, well under ⌊log₂ 1000⌋ = 9; the pairs preset on n = 12 ends at height 3 ≤ ⌊log₂ 12⌋ = 3.

Path compression alone, and the bound that is stated rather than proved here

Path compression on its own — naive linking, but every find re-points the path at the root — has a worse worst case per operation (a chain can still be built and its first traversal costs Θ(n)) and a much better amortised one: any m ≥ n operations cost Θ(m·log1 + m/n n). The bound is tight — both an upper bound and an adversarial sequence achieving it are known — and for m = Θ(n) it is Θ(n log n), while for m ≥ n^{1+ε} it collapses to O(m). The proof is a potential-function argument in the same family as the one sketched below and is not reproduced here; the fact worth carrying is that compression alone is already near-linear whenever the finds outnumber the elements substantially, which is Kruskal's situation (2E finds on V elements). The figure measures it: on the random workload, compression alone averages 2.65 hops per operation against 104.47 with nothing, and 1.28 with both heuristics.

Why it is not constant, and why it is as good as constant — the shape of the proof

The proof is a potential-function argument (Algorithm Analysis, §19) and its idea can be understood without its bookkeeping. Ranks strictly increase along any path to a root, and a node's rank is fixed forever once it stops being a root, while its parent's rank only ever increases — every compression re-points a node at a strictly higher-ranked ancestor. So each non-root node x sits at some "level": the largest k such that rank[parent[x]] ≥ Ak(rank[x]). Because ranks are at most n − 1 and Aα(n)(1) ≥ n, every level is between 0 and α(n) − 1 — there are only α(n) possible levels. A node's level can only rise (its parent's rank only rises), and within a level a second counter — how many times Ak must be iterated on rank[x] to exceed the parent's rank — also only rises, up to rank[x] times before the level itself must tick over. Give each non-root node a potential of roughly (α(n) − level)·rank − iterations, and each root a potential of α(n)·rank.

Now take a find that follows a path of s hops. Charge the hops to the nodes on the path. A node whose next-higher-level ancestor is also on the path gets its potential reduced by the compression (after re-pointing at the root, its parent's rank jumped enough to advance its counter or its level), so that hop is paid by released potential — and nodes of this kind can be all but a few. The hops that are not paid by potential are the last node at each level on the path plus the root and its child: at most α(n) + 2 of them. So the amortised cost of a find is O(α(n)); a link costs O(α(n)) because at most one node's potential rises, by at most α(n); and a makeSet is O(1). Sum over the sequence: O(m·α(n)). The reason the bound is not O(1) is the count of distinct levels — the potential can be released only α(n) times "per unit of rank", and that is what the unpaid hops per find add up to. The reason it is as good as constant is the table above: the levels are so coarse that four of them cover every rank up to 10⁸⁰.

the simpler bound one CAN prove in a paragraph — O(m log* n), by the same idea with cruder levelslog* n = the number of times log₂ must be applied to n to bring it to ≤ 1: log* 2 = 1, log* 4 = 2, log* 16 = 3, log* 65536 = 4, log* 2⁶⁵⁵³⁶ = 5 (and log* 1000 = 4, log* 10⁸⁰ = 5) group the ranks into blocks {1}, {2}, {3, 4}, {5 … 16}, {17 … 65536}, {65537 … 2⁶⁵⁵³⁶}, … — block after {k+1 … 2ᵏ} is {2ᵏ+1 … 2^{2ᵏ}}; the number of blocks up to rank log₂ n is log* n. One-pass variants, and what the structure cannot do

Two-pass compression (climb to the root, then re-point) is the cleanest statement but does the walk twice. Path halving does one pass — at each node x on the way up, set parent[x] = parent[parent[x]] and step to that grandparent — so every other node on the path is re-pointed two levels up. Path splitting also does one pass, re-pointing every node at its grandparent and stepping to the old parent. Both keep the O(m·α(n)) bound with union by rank (the same proof goes through with a constant-factor change), both touch each path node once, and both are what production code tends to use; on the figure's random workload with rank, halving averages 1.16 hops per operation, splitting 1.29, full compression 1.28 — differences within the noise of one seed, all a small constant. What union-find cannot do is delete: a union cannot be undone, so "are u and v still connected after this edge is removed?" is outside its contract — the offline trick (process deletions backwards as insertions) or a fully dynamic connectivity structure is needed, as Graph Representations & Traversal, §27 says. It is also symmetric — nothing about direction, nothing about strong components — and it knows nothing about distances; it answers one question, "same set?", and answers it in effectively constant time.

19 Prim's algorithm, stepped — Dijkstra's skeleton with a different key

Prim's algorithm grows a minimum spanning tree from a single root, one vertex at a time: every vertex outside the tree carries a key, key[v] = the weight of the lightest edge joining v to the tree so far (∞ if none), and each step extracts the outside vertex of minimum key, adds it and its key edge to the tree, and lowers the keys of its neighbours that are still outside. The loop is Dijkstra's loop (§04) with one substitution: where Dijkstra relaxes with d[u] + w(u, v), Prim relaxes with w(u, v) alone. Everything else — the min-priority queue, extract-min then decrease-key, the settled set that only grows — is identical, and so is the cost: O(E log V) with a binary heap, Θ(V²) with a plain array, which wins on dense graphs. Correctness is the cut property applied to the cut (tree, everything else) at every extraction. Correctness: the cut is always (tree, rest)

Let A be the set of vertices extracted so far, and suppose by induction that the edges (π[v], v) for v ∈ A − {r} form a tree contained in some MST — true at the start, when A = {r} and the edge set is empty. The cut (A, V − A) is respected by the tree (all its edges have both endpoints in A). The invariant on keys says that for each outside v, key[v] is the lightest edge from A to v; so the minimum key over all outside vertices is the lightest edge crossing the cut — a light edge — and the vertex u with that key, together with (π[u], u), is exactly what the cut property says is safe. Adding it preserves "tree ⊆ some MST". The key invariant itself is preserved by the relaxation loop: after u joins, the only crossing edges that are new are those from u, and each is compared against the outside endpoint's key. The proof needs the graph to be connected for the loop to produce a spanning tree; on a disconnected graph the queue eventually returns a vertex with key ∞, and the routine stops there with the MST of the root's component (a second run from an unvisited vertex gives the next component — the figure's routine breaks on ∞ for this reason). The proof does not need non-negative weights: nothing is ever summed.

Prim or Kruskal?

The asymptotic bounds match at O(E log V) with a binary heap, so the choice is about the graph and the representation. Prim wants an adjacency structure (it walks u's neighbours) and pays per edge scan into the heap; Kruskal wants an edge list and pays once for the sort. On dense graphs Prim with an array is Θ(V²) and beats any sort-based method; on sparse graphs the two are close, and Kruskal wins outright when the edges arrive sorted or integer-weighted. Prim's tree is connected at every moment — useful when the algorithm is stopped early (a partial MST around a root) or when the graph is implicit and edges are generated on demand from a vertex; Kruskal's partial output is a forest — useful for clustering. In the vocabulary of Heaps & Priority Queues, §24, Prim is the second of the two graph algorithms whose inner loop is a decrease-key, and the discussion there of lazy versus indexed heaps applies unchanged. Borůvka (§20) is the third option, and the one that parallelises.

Borůvka's algorithm is the third instantiation of the generic MST loop: in each round every tree of the current forest selects its lightest outgoing edge, and all the selected edges are added at once — so the number of trees at least halves per round and the whole forest is one tree after at most ⌈log₂ V⌉ rounds, each costing O(E). It is the oldest of the three algorithms, the one whose rounds are naturally parallel, and the seed of the expected-linear-time randomised MST algorithm. The randomised linear-time algorithm, as a pointer

Two or three Borůvka rounds cut the vertex count by a factor of 4 or 8 while keeping the edge count — a graph with V/8 vertices is much easier, but the edges have not gone away. The expected-linear-time algorithm adds a sampling step: take a random half of the edges, recursively find their minimum spanning forest F, and discard every unsampled edge that is the heaviest on the cycle it closes with F (the cycle property, checked in linear time by a tree-path-maximum verification routine); a lemma shows only about 2V edges survive in expectation. Recurse on the survivors. The recurrence resolves to O(V + E) expected time. It is a beautiful construction and, on the machines and graphs one actually meets, slower than the three deterministic algorithms above; it belongs here as the answer to "is the sort in Kruskal or the heap in Prim really necessary?" — not in the comparison model deterministically (that is open), but in expectation with randomness, no.

Maximum spanning tree, and the bottleneck tree again

A maximum spanning tree is an MST of the graph with every weight negated (or, equivalently, Kruskal with the edges sorted in decreasing order, Prim with a max-queue): every proof above goes through with the inequalities reversed, so no new algorithm is needed. It is what the maximum-weight forest of Greedy Algorithms, §18 computes on a connected graph, and it has its own applications — the maximum spanning tree of a similarity graph is the basis of tree-structured approximations of a joint distribution, and its path between two vertices is the widest-bottleneck (maximin) path. The minimum bottleneck tree of §16 needs nothing beyond the MST either: every MST is an MBST, so Kruskal's output answers "the path whose heaviest edge is lightest" for every pair at once. When only the bottleneck value is wanted — not the tree — it can be found in linear time by a median-of-weights binary search that repeatedly discards half the edges, a divide-and-conquer trick (Recursion, Divide & Conquer) that beats the E log V of building the tree.

Every edge lighter than d* was examined before it and either accepted (so it lies inside a cluster) or rejected because its endpoints were already joined by accepted edges (so it lies inside a cluster too): no edge lighter than d* joins two different clusters, and the spacing of Kruskal's clustering is exactly d*. any other k-partition P must split some Kruskal cluster C (if every cluster sat inside one part of P, P would be the same partition); take p, q ∈ C on different sides of P. C's tree path from p to q uses only the first V − k accepted edges, all of weight ≤ d*, and crosses P's boundary somewhere: spacing(P) ≤ d*. ∎ on the shared instance: k = 2 cuts EF (5) → {A, B, D, E} and {C, F}; k = 3 cuts EF, AB → {A, D}, {B, E}, {C, F}; k = 4 cuts EF, AB, BE → {A, D}, {B}, {E}, {C, F} (computed by the figure's routine; these are the forests §17 said an early-stopped Kruskal leaves behind) the DENDROGRAM of single-linkage agglomerative clustering is Kruskal's merge sequence read as a tree; the "chaining" weakness of single linkage is the MST's willingness to take a long detour (the SPT contrast, again).

The clustering page in the machine-learning notes (Clustering, hierarchical clustering) presents single, complete and average linkage as choices of inter-cluster distance; the fact recorded here is that the single-linkage choice makes the whole agglomerative procedure a minimum spanning tree computation, O(n² log n) on the complete distance graph or O(n²) with Prim's array version, rather than the O(n³) of the naive agglomerative loop. Image segmentation by region merging, percolation thresholds, and the "connected components at threshold t" query for every t at once are the same computation under other names: sort the edges, union in order, and read the components off the union-find structure at whichever threshold is asked.

Steiner trees: the NP-hard cousin

Change the question slightly — connect only a given subset of terminal vertices as cheaply as possible, using any other vertices as optional intermediates — and the problem becomes the Steiner tree problem, which is NP-hard in general graphs and in the Euclidean plane (where optional points may be placed anywhere). The MST of the terminals alone, computed on the metric closure (shortest-path distances between terminals), is a 2-approximation: it weighs at most twice the optimal Steiner tree, because doubling the Steiner tree's edges gives an Eulerian walk through every terminal, and shortcutting that walk to visit each terminal once gives a spanning tree of the terminals no heavier than the doubled tree. The gap between "spanning tree of all vertices — polynomial, greedy, three algorithms" and "spanning tree of some vertices — NP-hard, approximable within a constant" is one of the sharpest in the field, and the reasons on the hard side are the subject of NP-Completeness & Approximation.
