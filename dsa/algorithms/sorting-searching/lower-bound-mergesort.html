10 The Ω(n log n) lower bound: decision trees, worst case and average case

Every sort so far is Θ(n²) or worse than n log n; the sorts from §11 on are Θ(n log n). Before improving further it is worth knowing that no comparison sort can do better than log₂(n!) ≈ n log₂ n − 1.44n comparisons in the worst case — nor in the average case. The proof is a counting argument about trees, it is one paragraph long, and it is the single most important theorem in this subject because it tells you when to stop looking for a faster algorithm and start looking for a different model.

What the theorem does and does not say

  • It is about a model. Counting sort (§22), radix sort (§23) and bucket sort (§24) run in linear time without contradiction, because they never ask "is aᵢ ≤ aⱼ" — they read the key's value as a number and use it as an index. The bound says nothing about them, and it says nothing about sorting integers in general. It says: if all you may do is compare, this is the floor.
  • It is about comparisons, not time. A sort that makes n log n comparisons but moves n² elements is quadratic. The bound is a lower bound on one resource; upper bounds have to account for all of them (§21).
  • It is a worst-case AND average-case statement, and the two are separate theorems with separate proofs. The worst-case bound follows from the height of a tree with n! leaves; the average-case bound from its mean leaf depth. Both are log₂(n!). A common misreading is that "some inputs must be slow but most could be fast" — they cannot: at most a vanishing fraction of the n! inputs can sit at depth much below log₂(n!) − c, since a tree has at most 2ᵈ leaves at depth d.
  • It is tight. Mergesort and heapsort achieve O(n log n) in the worst case, so Θ(n log n) is the true worst-case complexity of comparison sorting — the bound is not a gap in our knowledge but a closed question. The remaining gap between log₂(n!) and the best sort is a linear term, and closing that is what merge-insertion and its descendants do, at a cost in moves that makes them impractical.
The decision tree of a real sort, drawn from its comparison sequences on every permutation — leaves counted, height measured against log₂(n!) each internal node is one "x > y?" the routine actually asks; each leaf is the output order it produces; the bound is checked underneath

The tree is not drawn from a textbook picture; it is reconstructed by running the routine on every one of the n! inputs, logging each comparison it asks and the answer, and merging the logged paths. For insertion sort at n = 3 that yields 6 leaves, height 3, shortest path 2, mean depth 2.667 — against log₂(3!) = 2.585, so the worst case meets ceil(2.585) = 3 exactly and the mean sits just above the average-case bound. Selection sort's tree has the same 6 leaves but every path has length 3, because it asks all n(n − 1)/2 questions regardless: a mean depth of 3.000 against the same 2.585 bound is the cost of not being adaptive, visible as a tree with no short paths. At n = 4 insertion sort's height is 6 against ceil(log₂ 24) = 5 — one comparison above optimal, and the best possible sorting procedure for four keys does use 5. Where the bound is put to work. Every n log n sort on this page is, by this theorem, within a constant factor of optimal in comparisons; the competition between them (§21) is therefore entirely about the constant, the moves, the memory pattern and the worst case — never about the exponent. And the theorem is the reason §22–§24 exist: the only way past it is to leave the model.

11 Mergesort as a sort: the merge invariant, stability, and the buffer

Mergesort is the first sort on this page that meets the bound of §10, and it does so because of one linear-time primitive: merging two sorted runs into one sorted run in p + q − 1 comparisons at most. Sorted input is the precondition that makes it cheap — the smallest unconsumed element of the union is always at the front of one of the two runs, so one comparison decides the next output element. Everything mergesort is (stable, Θ(n log n) in every case, sequential, buffer-hungry) is a property of this loop. The recursion that calls it, and the recurrence T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n), are in Recursion, Divide & Conquer, §14; this section is about the merge.

the merge of a[lo..mid) with a[mid..hi), through a buffercopy a[lo..hi) into buf; i = lo; j = mid; k = lo while i < mid and j < hi: if buf[i] ≤ buf[j]: a[k] = buf[i]; i = i + 1 ← ≤, not <: a tie takes the LEFT element, so the sort is stable else: a[k] = buf[j]; j = j + 1 k = k + 1 copy the remainder of whichever run is left ← no comparisons here the loop invarianta[lo..k) holds the k − lo smallest elements of the two runs, in sorted order; buf[i] and buf[j] are the smallest UNCONSUMED elements of their runs; every element consumed from the left run is ≤ every element still in the left run, and likewise on the right THEOREM (cost of one merge). Hypothesis: runs of length p and q, m = p + q.comparisons = m − t, where t = the length of the final stretch of output that came from ONE side so min(p, q) ≤ comparisons ≤ m − 1 moves = 2m with a copy-in / merge-out buffer; m with alternating (ping-pong) buffers space = Θ(m) for the buffer — allocated once at the top and reused, never per call

The default replays the trace above and lands on 5 comparisons and 12 moves, with the identity m − t = 6 − 1 = 5 confirmed. The two extra presets are the ends of the range: two runs that do not overlap merge in the length of the LOWER run — here the left, min(p, q) = 4 comparisons — once that run is exhausted the other is copied in silence — and two perfectly interleaved runs cost m − 1 = 7, the maximum, because the very last element is the only one that goes out uncompared. That spread, min(p, q) to m − 1 per merge, is the whole distance between mergesort's best and worst cases.

The buffer, and what "in place" would cost

The merge above needs Θ(m) extra space, and mergesort therefore needs a buffer of n elements — allocated once, at the top, and threaded through the recursion, because allocating per call turns a constant into a log n factor of allocator traffic and, worse, a fresh cache-cold buffer at every level. That buffer is the standard objection to mergesort and the objection is weaker than it looks. Merging in place in linear time is possible in principle — stable in-place merge algorithms with O(m) moves and comparisons exist — but their constants are large enough that a simple in-place merge is usually implemented by rotations, which cost O(m log m) per merge and make the whole sort O(n log² n); and the linear-time constructions are so involved that they are essentially never used. The honest statement is: mergesort with a buffer is Θ(n log n) and stable; mergesort without one is either slower by a logarithmic factor or impractically complex; and when memory is the constraint the answer is heapsort or quicksort, not an in-place merge. The linked-list version (§12) is the exception that proves the rule — there the "buffer" is the pointers you already have.

Sequential access is the asset. The merge reads two runs front to back and writes one run front to back: three sequential streams, which is the best case for prefetching and for a cache line. That is why mergesort — not quicksort, not heapsort — is the algorithm of external sorting (§31) and of every library sort that has to be stable (§26). "It needs a buffer" and "its access pattern is ideal" are the same fact seen from two sides.

12 Mergesort's exact counts, and its variants: bottom-up, natural, linked-list

Because the recursion tree's shape depends only on n — never on the data — mergesort's cost can be written down exactly rather than bounded. Its worst case is n·ceil(log₂n) − 2^ceil(log₂n) + 1 comparisons for the top-down split at floor(n/2); its best case is about half that; its average on random input is n·log₂n − Θ(n). Those are statements about the top-down recursive version. Three other ways to schedule the same merges — bottom-up, natural, and on a linked list — have the same asymptotics and different exact counts, memory needs and adaptivity.

Mergesort on a linked list: O(1) extra space, honestly

On a singly linked list the merge needs no buffer at all: the two runs are already chains of nodes, and merging them is re-linking next pointers — each step detaches the smaller front node and appends it to the output chain. Splitting is the only cost that changes: finding the middle of a list takes a Θ(n) walk (the runner technique of Linked Structures, Stacks & Queues, §09), which adds a linear term per level and leaves the total at Θ(n log n). The bottom-up schedule avoids even that: walk the list once, merging runs of length 1 into 2, then 2 into 4, and so on, with no midpoint search at all. The result is the one classical case of a stable n log n sort in O(1) extra space — because the "extra space" is the n pointers the list already carries — and it is why mergesort is the sort for linked lists, where quicksort's random-access partition and heapsort's index arithmetic are unavailable. The cache cost is the usual one for linked structures: every node visit is a potential miss, so the constant is far worse than the array version's.

Which variant, when

  • Top-down is the reference implementation: simplest to prove, O(log n) stack, the exact counts above. Its one avoidable inefficiency is copying: alternating the roles of the array and the buffer level by level (merging from one into the other) halves the moves from 2n to n per level.
  • Bottom-up removes the recursion, which matters where a stack is unwelcome, and is the natural form for linked lists and for external sorting (§31). Pad the input to a power of two conceptually — or accept the lopsided tree's slightly larger worst case.
  • Natural buys adaptivity in the run measure: presorted, reverse-sorted-then-fixed, and "sorted with a block appended" inputs all sort in near-linear time. The price is a run-detection pass and unbalanced merges on random input. Library sorts (§26) take this idea and add a minimum run length built by insertion sort, so that the balanced and the adaptive behaviours are both kept.
  • The stability of all three rests on the same character: ≤ in the merge. Nothing else in mergesort can reorder equal keys, because no element ever moves past another except through a merge.
