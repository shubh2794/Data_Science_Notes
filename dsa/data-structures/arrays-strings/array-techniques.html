12 The two-pointer pattern and its invariant

A family of linear-time array algorithms that all have the same shape: maintain two indices, move them in a disciplined way that never backtracks, and carry an invariant strong enough that when the indices meet the answer is decided. The pattern is worth naming because it converts a great many naive double loops from Θ(n²) to Θ(n) with O(1) extra space, and because the conversion is only valid when the invariant holds — which is what makes it a technique rather than a trick.

The two configurations

  • Converging pointers — one array; lo starts at 0, hi at n − 1, and they move toward each other until they meet or cross. Reversal, palindrome testing, the sorted two-sum and the container-of-most-water problem all live here. Termination is free: the gap hi − lo strictly decreases every iteration.
  • Chasing pointers — one array; a read index sweeps it while a write index trails behind, marking the end of the part already processed. Removing duplicates from a sorted array in place, stable partitioning by a predicate, and compacting out deleted entries all live here. Termination is free again: read increases every iteration and write ≤ read always.
  • Parallel pointers — two sequences, one index into each, both moving only forward, with the choice of which to advance made by comparing the two elements they point at. The merge step of mergesort is the canonical instance, and so are the sorted-set intersection, union and difference routines. It is worth separating from the first two because the termination argument is different: neither index is bounded by the other, and the loop ends when either sequence is exhausted, so the body needs an explicit drain of whatever remains. Total movement is still at most n₁ + n₂, which is why merging is linear.

A hand-worked trace: a pair summing to 17

Take the sorted array A = [1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 13, 15] and target 17. Start with lo = 0, hi = 7 and compare the sum against the target at every step:

step 1A[0] + A[7] = 1 + 15 = 16 < 17 → 1 is too small to pair with even the largest element ⇒ lo = 1 step 2A[1] + A[7] = 3 + 15 = 18 > 17 → 15 is too large to pair with even the smallest survivor ⇒ hi = 6 step 3A[1] + A[6] = 3 + 13 = 16 < 17 → 3 is eliminated ⇒ lo = 2 step 4A[2] + A[6] = 4 + 13 = 17 = 17 → found, at indices (2, 6) cost4 sum evaluations, against the 8·7/2 = 28 pairs a double loop would test

The elimination argument is the whole proof. When the sum is too small, A[lo] has just been paired with the largest remaining element and still fell short; since every other candidate partner is smaller, A[lo] can be in no solution at all and may be discarded forever. The mirror argument discards A[hi] when the sum is too large. Each step therefore removes one element from consideration permanently, which is simultaneously the correctness proof (nothing that could be part of an answer is ever discarded) and the running-time proof (at most n − 1 steps before the pointers meet).

That argument has a standard shape, and it is worth naming because every correctness proof on this page uses it. A loop invariant is a statement about the program's variables that is true before the loop starts, is preserved by one iteration of the body, and — combined with the loop's exit condition — implies the thing you wanted. The three obligations have names: initialization (it holds on entry), maintenance (each iteration preserves it), and termination (when the loop stops, the invariant plus the exit condition give the result). Discharging all three is a proof; skipping the third is how an algorithm ends up correct on every test case and wrong on the empty input.

the loop invariant that makes it correctif a pair summing to the target exists anywhere in A, both of its elements lie in A[lo … hi] initializationlo = 0, hi = n−1 — the range is the whole array, so the invariant holds vacuously maintenancea discarded element is provably in no solution (the elimination argument above) terminationlo ≥ hi with nothing found ⇒ the range is empty ⇒ by the invariant, no pair exists the precondition the whole thing rests onA is SORTED. Without that, "every other partner is smaller" is false and the method is wrong.
Two pointers, stepped, with the invariant on screen every count in the readout is taken from this run

Step the two-sum trace and watch the shaded region shrink from both ends: the cells outside [lo, hi] are the ones the elimination argument has permanently discarded, and the invariant on the last line is what licenses discarding them. Switch to reverse for the other flavour, where the shading means something different — the cells outside [lo, hi] are finished rather than excluded — and where the invariant becomes "the two ends already hold their final mirrored values". Both routines are one pass and both use no extra memory beyond the two indices, which is the pattern's characteristic signature.

When the pattern applies, and when it does not

  • It needs a monotone consequence of moving a pointer. Two-sum works because the array is sorted, so moving lo right can only increase the sum and moving hi left can only decrease it. If no such monotonicity exists, advancing a pointer can skip an answer and the method is simply incorrect — no amount of care in the implementation repairs that.
  • Sorting first may still be worth it. Given an unsorted array, sorting for Θ(n log n) and then running two pointers for Θ(n) beats the Θ(n²) double loop for all but tiny n. A hash table solves the same problem in Θ(n) expected time without sorting, at the price of Θ(n) extra space and no worst-case guarantee — a good miniature of the recurring space-for-time trade.
  • Three pointers is the same idea one level up. The classic three-sum is a loop over the first element wrapping a two-pointer scan over the rest: Θ(n²) instead of Θ(n³). The three-way partition of §15 is a three-pointer routine in its own right.
  • Do not confuse the three forward-moving shapes. Converging pointers shrink one range from both ends. Parallel pointers walk two different sequences and the object of interest is the merged output. A sliding window's two indices walk the same sequence, both forward, and the object of interest is the region between them. All three share the "each index moves at most n times" accounting and nothing else, and it is that accounting — not any resemblance in the code — that makes all of them linear.

13 The sliding window, fixed and variable

A window is a contiguous range A[l … r] carried across the array together with a summary of its contents — a sum, a count, a character multiset, a maximum. The pattern works when the summary can be updated incrementally: O(1) to admit the element entering on the right, O(1) to evict the one leaving on the left. Then a question about all Θ(n²) contiguous sub-ranges collapses into one pass.

Fixed width: the easy case

With a window of fixed width k, both edges advance in lockstep. Compute the summary once for A[0 … k−1] in Θ(k), then slide: sum += A[r] − A[l−1]. Each of the n − k + 1 positions costs O(1), so the whole sweep is Θ(n) against the Θ(nk) of recomputing each window from scratch. This is the same algebraic move that Rabin-Karp's rolling hash makes on a window of characters and that a moving average makes on a time series; the summary just has to admit an inverse for the departing element.

a fixed window of width k, incrementallyS₀ = A[0] + A[1] + ⋯ + A[k−1] Θ(k) S_(s+1) = S_s − A[s] + A[s+k] O(1) per shift totalΘ(k) + Θ(n − k) = Θ(n) versus Θ(nk) recomputing each window the requirement — and where it failsthe summary must be invertible: sums and counts yes; maximum NO (the sliding-window maximum needs a monotone deque instead — still Θ(n))

Variable width: the interesting case

When the window's width is the thing being optimised, the right edge advances unconditionally in the outer loop and the left edge advances only as far as a feasibility condition requires. The structure looks quadratic — a while inside a for — and is not, for an amortization reason identical in spirit to the one behind amortized append:

the shapel = 0 for r = 0 … n−1: admit A[r] into the summary while the window violates the condition: evict A[l]; l = l + 1 record the answer for the window [l, r] the amortized argumentr increases exactly n times; l never decreases and is bounded by n ⇒ total edge movement ≤ 2n, so the nested loop runs in Θ(n) overall the two question shapes it answerslongest window satisfying a condition that is monotone under SHRINKING shortest window satisfying a condition that is monotone under GROWING
A window that grows on the right and shrinks on the left the readout counts both edges and checks the total against 2n

The trace on abcabcbb, written out. Indices 0…7 hold a b c a b c b b. The window is [l, r] and the eviction loop runs until the admitted character is no longer a duplicate:

r · character admitted · evictions forced · resulting window · best so farr=0 'a' none [0,0] "a" best 1 r=1 'b' none [0,1] "ab" best 2 r=2 'c' none [0,2] "abc" best 3 r=3 'a' evict A[0] [1,3] "bca" best 3 — width unchanged, the window SLIDES r=4 'b' evict A[1] [2,4] "cab" best 3 — slides again r=5 'c' evict A[2] [3,5] "abc" best 3 — slides again r=6 'b' evict A[3], A[4] [5,6] "cb" best 3 — TWO evictions, the window shrinks r=7 'b' evict A[5], A[6] [7,7] "b" best 3 — two again the answer3, and the total edge movement is 8 admissions + 7 evictions = 15 ≤ 2n = 16

Read the eviction column and the amortized argument becomes visible rather than asserted: three of the eight steps evict nothing, three evict once, and two evict twice — the per-step cost is not bounded by a constant, and the total is, because the left edge can only ever be pushed forward through eight positions in total no matter how the pushes are distributed. That is the same accounting as amortized append and not a coincidence.

On pwwkew the doubled w forces a two-step eviction at r = 2, and the best window is wke — also 3, but reached differently, and a useful reminder that the answer is a length, not a unique substring. The sum variant inverts the logic: it shrinks greedily whenever the window is already good enough, so that the recorded length is always minimal for the current right edge, giving 2 for [4, 3].

Three things that go wrong

  • Applying it where the condition is not monotone. "Longest window with no repeat" works because removing elements can never create a repeat, so shrinking always moves toward feasibility. If a condition can be broken by shrinking, the left edge has no safe direction and the pattern does not apply — a window over an array containing negative numbers breaks "shortest window with sum ≥ target" for exactly this reason, and that problem needs prefix sums plus a search structure instead.
  • A summary that cannot evict in O(1). Maintaining the window's maximum with a plain variable is wrong: when the maximum leaves on the left, there is no way to recover the new maximum without rescanning. The fix is a monotone deque holding indices in decreasing value order, which keeps the answer at the front and still amortizes to O(1) because each index is pushed and popped once.
  • Recording the answer at the wrong moment. For a longest window, record after the shrink loop restores feasibility. For a shortest window, record inside the shrink loop, before the eviction that would break feasibility. Swapping those two placements produces code that runs, terminates, and returns the wrong number.
The shared accounting. Two pointers and sliding windows are both instances of one argument: an index that only moves forward can move at most n times, however deeply nested the loop that moves it appears to be. That is the same aggregate reasoning that gives amortized bounds, and it reappears verbatim in KMP's proof that its backtracking loop runs O(n) times in total.

14 Prefix sums and difference arrays

A prefix-sum array is a precomputed table of cumulative totals: P[i] is the sum of the first i elements, so that the sum of any contiguous range is the difference of two of its entries. One linear pass converts every future range-sum query from Θ(length) into O(1). It is the smallest interesting example of a precomputation trade — spend Θ(n) once, save on every query afterwards — and the first tool to reach for when a problem mentions "sum over a subarray".

the table, with the convention that makes the arithmetic painlessP[0] = 0, P[i+1] = P[i] + A[i] for i = 0 … n−1 so P has n+1 entries, and P[i] = A[0] + A[1] + ⋯ + A[i−1] the querysum(A[l … r]) = P[r+1] − P[l] one subtraction, O(1) why it telescopesP[r+1] − P[l] = (A[0]+⋯+A[r]) − (A[0]+⋯+A[l−1]) = A[l] + ⋯ + A[r] cost of q queriesprefix: Θ(n) + Θ(q) direct: Θ(q·n) in the worst case

Use the n+1-entry convention. Defining P[i] as the sum of the first i elements — rather than the sum up to and including index i — makes P[0] = 0 a real entry and removes the special case at l = 0 entirely. The alternative convention forces a conditional in every query, and that conditional is where the off-by-one lives.

Build once, subtract forever — with both answers computed and compared the bars are measured arithmetic operations over q random queries

Note the half-cell offset between the two rows: it is drawn that way because P indexes the boundaries between elements, not the elements, which is the mental model that makes the r+1 stop being a fiddly correction and start being obvious. The query bracket spans from the boundary before A[l] to the boundary after A[r]. Drag q and watch the bars separate: the prefix cost is a fixed build plus one operation per query, so it is essentially flat, while direct summation grows in proportion to q × (average range length). Both answers are computed on every draw and compared — the readout says so explicitly — because a range-query identity that is off by one is exactly the kind of thing that is right in the caption and wrong in the code.

The inverse trick: difference arrays for range updates

Prefix sums make range queries cheap on a static array. The dual construction makes range updates cheap when all the queries come at the end. To add v to every element of A[l … r], do not touch the range — record the change at its two boundaries in a difference array D, and reconstruct A by taking the prefix sums of D once, at the end.

the update, O(1) instead of Θ(r − l + 1)add v to A[l … r] ⇒ D[l] = D[l] + v; D[r+1] = D[r+1] − v the size of D — the fencepost againD has n+1 entries, so that D[r+1] is a legal write when r = n−1; the last entry is never read back the reconstruction, once, at the endA[i] = D[0] + D[1] + ⋯ + D[i] — a single prefix-sum pass, Θ(n) u updates then one readoutΘ(u + n) instead of Θ(u · n)

A hand-worked example. Start with A = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0] and apply two updates: add 5 to A[1 … 3], then add 2 to A[2 … 4]. The difference array takes four writes in total — D[1] += 5, D[4] −= 5, D[2] += 2, D[5] −= 2 — leaving D = [0, 5, 2, 0, −5, −2]. Its running total is [0, 5, 7, 7, 2, 0]. Check it by hand: index 1 got only the first update (5 ✓); index 2 and 3 got both (7 ✓); index 4 got only the second (2 ✓); indices 0 and 5 got neither (0 ✓). Two range updates that between them touch six cells cost four writes — and because each update is two writes regardless of its width, the saving grows without bound with the range length: the same two updates over ranges of a thousand cells would still be four writes. The +v at l switches the contribution on and the −v at r+1 switches it off; the prefix sum is what integrates the switches back into values.

Where the idea generalises

  • Two dimensions. Build S[i][j] = the sum of the rectangle from (0,0) to (i−1, j−1), in one pass with S[i+1][j+1] = A[i][j] + S[i][j+1] + S[i+1][j] − S[i][j]. Any axis-aligned rectangle sum is then four table lookups by inclusion–exclusion — for the rectangle spanning rows l … r and columns k … c, it is S[r+1][c+1] − S[l][c+1] − S[r+1][k] + S[l][k]. The subtracted corner is added back because it was removed twice. This is the integral image that makes box filters and Haar-feature detectors constant-time per window regardless of window size.
  • Other operators. Any associative operation with an inverse works the same way — products (watch for zeros), XOR (its own inverse, which makes it especially clean), counts. Operators with no inverse, minimum and maximum being the usual culprits, cannot use the subtraction trick and need a sparse table or segment tree instead, which is exactly the point at which a flat array stops being enough.
  • Numerical caution. With floating-point elements the subtraction P[r+1] − P[l] subtracts two large nearly equal numbers and can lose most of its significant digits when the range is short and the prefix is long. For exact integer work this never arises; for accumulating floats it is a real concern and the reason numerical libraries offer compensated summation.
  • It is the base case of a bigger idea. "Store the answers to overlapping subproblems so you never recompute one" is the definition of dynamic programming, and a prefix-sum table is the smallest instance in which the recurrence, the table and the O(1) transition are all visible at once.

15 In-place rearrangement: reverse, rotate, partition

An operation is in place when it uses O(1) extra space beyond the input — a handful of index variables and one temporary, no second array. For arrays this is a class of algorithms worth knowing individually, because they are the primitives that larger routines are assembled from, and because "in place" is the property that decides whether an operation on a large array is possible at all rather than merely fast.

Reverse: the base primitive

Converging pointers, swap, close in. floor(n/2) swaps, Θ(n) time, O(1) space, and the invariant of §12. Everything below is built from it.

Rotate: three reversals

Rotating an array left by k looks like it needs a scratch buffer of k elements. It does not — and the reason is a small identity worth carrying: reversing two halves separately and then reversing the whole thing swaps the halves.

rotate A left by k, in placereverse(A[0 … k−1]); reverse(A[k … n−1]); reverse(A[0 … n−1]) why, in symbols, writing Xᴿ for the reversal of XA = X Y ⇒ after the first two: Xᴿ Yᴿ ⇒ after the third: (Xᴿ Yᴿ)ᴿ = Y X costfloor(k/2) + floor((n−k)/2) + floor(n/2) swaps ≈ n swaps = Θ(n) time, O(1) space

Worked by hand on A = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7] with k = 3. Reverse the first three: [3, 2, 1, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Reverse the rest: [3, 2, 1, 7, 6, 5, 4]. Reverse everything: [4, 5, 6, 7, 1, 2, 3] — the original rotated left by three, using 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 swaps and one temporary. The alternative cycle (juggling) algorithm moves each element directly to its destination by following the permutation's cycles, of which there are exactly gcd(n, k); it performs n element moves rather than about 2n, which is fewer writes, but it jumps through memory with stride k and therefore often loses to the three reversals on a real machine for exactly the reason §09 gives. Two algorithms, the same asymptotics, and the winner decided by the memory hierarchy.

Partition: two-way and three-way

Partitioning rearranges an array so that everything satisfying a predicate precedes everything that does not. The two-way version is one chasing-pointer pass — a write index trailing a read index, swapping whenever read finds a qualifying element — and it is the engine inside quicksort and quickselect. The three-way version, the Dutch national flag problem, is the one to know: it splits the array into three regions in a single pass, which is what makes quicksort robust on inputs with many duplicate keys.

three-way partition — one pass, three pointerslo = 0; i = 0; hi = n−1 while i ≤ hi: if A[i] < pivot: swap(A[lo], A[i]); lo = lo + 1; i = i + 1 elif A[i] == pivot: i = i + 1 else: swap(A[i], A[hi]); hi = hi − 1 // i does NOT advance invariant, maintained at the top of every iterationA[0 … lo−1] < pivot · A[lo … i−1] == pivot · A[hi+1 … n−1] > pivot · A[i … hi] unexamined terminationi + (n−1−hi) increases by one every iteration and is bounded by n ⇒ ≤ n iterations
Three-way partition, stepped — the four regions form as you advance the readout re-checks the output independently — sortedness and multiset

The single most error-prone line is the third branch: after swapping a 2 to the back, i must not advance, because the value that came back from position hi has never been examined. Advancing it there produces code that passes on many inputs and fails on some — try the exactly reversed preset, which is where the bug shows up soonest. The all equal to the pivot preset is the one that explains why three-way partitioning exists at all: the counter records zero swaps — its ten tests are the nine element dispatches plus the loop's final i > hi check — and the routine returns immediately with the whole array classified as "equal", so a quicksort built on it recurses on nothing.

What a two-way partition does on the same input depends on which two-way partition it is, and this is worth stating precisely because "quicksort is quadratic on duplicates" is repeated far more often than it is qualified:

  • The one-directional scheme — a single read index with a write index trailing it, testing A[read] ≤ pivot and swapping on success — sends every equal key to the low side. On an array of identical keys the split is n − 1 against 0, the recursion depth is n, and the total work is Θ(n²). This is the scheme most often written from memory, and it is the one the folklore is about.
  • The two-index scheme — pointers advancing from both ends that stop on a key equal to the pivot rather than scanning past it — swaps equal keys across the middle and splits an all-equal array almost exactly in half, giving Θ(n log n). The stopping-on-equal rule looks like wasted work on a single partition and is precisely what buys the balance; a version that scans past equal keys to "save a swap" reintroduces the quadratic case.
  • Three-way partitioning fixes it for both, and does more than fix it: by removing the entire equal-block from the recursion it makes an array with k distinct values sort in O(n log k) rather than O(n log n), which for something like a column of boolean or categorical values is a different complexity class rather than a constant-factor improvement.

What in-place buys, and what it costs

  • It buys feasibility at scale. An out-of-place operation on an array that is half of available memory cannot run at all. An in-place one can. This, not speed, is usually the reason it matters.
  • It buys locality. In-place routines touch one buffer instead of two, which halves the number of cache lines in flight — §09's ledger again.
  • It costs stability, sometimes. The swap-based two-way partition is not stable: equal elements can be reordered. A stable partition in Θ(n) time requires Θ(n) extra space, and doing it in O(1) space costs Θ(n log n) time. Where relative order carries meaning — sorting records by one field after another — that trade is the whole decision.
  • It costs the original. An in-place routine destroys its input by definition. If the caller needs the original as well, the copy you avoided inside has to be made outside, and nothing was saved.
  • Recursion is not free space. A routine that recurses is not O(1) space, however carefully its body avoids allocation: the call stack is auxiliary space, and it is Θ(depth). Quicksort is called "in place" by convention and still needs Θ(log n) stack when its recursion is balanced — a point the space section of Algorithm Analysis makes at length.

16 Binary search, and what it needs from the array

Binary search is the payoff for having both properties at once: an ordered sequence and constant-time access to any position. Either alone is not enough — you cannot halve a sorted linked list usefully, because finding its middle costs a traversal, and you cannot halve an unsorted array at all. It is therefore the clearest single demonstration of what contiguity is for, which is why it appears here; the family of variants and their proofs belong to Sorting & Searching.

the loop, on a sorted arraylo = 0; hi = n − 1 while lo ≤ hi: mid = lo + (hi − lo) / 2 // integer division, floor if A[mid] == x: return mid elif A[mid] < x: lo = mid + 1 else: hi = mid − 1 return NOT FOUND invariantif x occurs in A at all, its index lies in [lo, hi] the recurrence and its solutionT(n) = T(n/2) + Θ(1) ⇒ T(n) = Θ(log n) the exact worst-case countfloor(log₂ n) + 1 comparisons — the range shrinks from n to 1 by halving spaceO(1) iteratively; Θ(log n) stack if written recursively

Take the sorted array A = [2, 5, 8, 12, 16, 23, 38, 56, 72, 91], so n = 10 and the indices run 0 … 9. Search for x = 23:

step · lo · hi · mid = lo + floor((hi−lo)/2) · A[mid] · comparison · action1 0 9 mid = 0 + 4 = 4 A[4] = 16 16 < 23 ⇒ lo = 5 2 5 9 mid = 5 + 2 = 7 A[7] = 56 56 > 23 ⇒ hi = 6 3 5 6 mid = 5 + 0 = 5 A[5] = 23 equal ⇒ return 5 the range at the top of each step, and the invariant it satisfies[0,9] → [5,9] → [5,6]; at every step, if 23 occurs in A it occurs in A[lo … hi] now the same array, searching for x = 40, which is absent1 0 9 mid = 4 A[4] = 16 16 < 40 ⇒ lo = 5 2 5 9 mid = 7 A[7] = 56 56 > 40 ⇒ hi = 6 3 5 6 mid = 5 A[5] = 23 23 < 40 ⇒ lo = 6 4 6 6 mid = 6 A[6] = 38 38 < 40 ⇒ lo = 7 5 7 6 lo > hi — the range is empty, so by the invariant 40 is not in A count the probes against the bound4 probes; floor(log₂ 10) + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4 ✓ — the absent key attains the worst case and note where the search stoppedlo = 7 is exactly the index at which 40 would be inserted to keep A sorted

That last line is the whole reason to prefer the boundary formulation discussed below: the failing search did not merely fail, it computed the insertion point, and throwing that away is throwing away the only useful thing a negative answer carries. Notice also that the successful search took three probes and the failing one four — a successful search is on average cheaper, because it can stop early, and the worst case belongs to the keys that are not there.

The three details that are always the bug

  • The midpoint must not overflow. (lo + hi)/2 is wrong in fixed-width integer arithmetic: for large arrays the sum exceeds the integer range before the division happens. lo + (hi − lo)/2 computes the same value and cannot overflow, because hi − lo is never larger than hi. This bug survived in widely used library code and in a canonical textbook for two decades, which is the best available evidence that binary search is harder to write than it looks.
  • The loop must shrink the range on every iteration. With lo ≤ hi and the mid ± 1 updates above, it does. The variants that write while lo < hi with hi = mid must take care that mid is never equal to lo when the range has two elements, or the loop spins forever. Pick one formulation, prove it terminates, and use only that one.
  • "Found it" is usually not the question. The genuinely useful variants return a boundary: the first index whose value is ≥ x (lower bound), or the first whose value is > x (upper bound). Those compose into counting occurrences, into range extraction, and into insertion-point queries, and they return a meaningful answer when x is absent — which plain membership does not. Write the boundary version; derive membership from it.

What it costs on an array, honestly

The Θ(log n) is a count of comparisons, and on a large array each comparison is a jump to an unrelated address — a probable cache miss, per §02. Twenty comparisons on a million-element array may therefore be twenty DRAM round trips, while a linear scan of a few hundred contiguous elements is a handful of line fetches with prefetching on top. That is why B-trees exist — they widen the node so that each expensive fetch resolves many comparisons at once — and why "binary search is always better than scanning" is false for small n. It is the same family of constant-factor argument that makes library sorts hand small subarrays to insertion sort rather than recursing further, though that threshold is driven as much by call overhead and branch prediction as by the cache. The asymptotic claim is unaffected and remains the right basis for the decision at scale; the constant is where the surprises are.

A linear scan has one classical trick worth naming because it belongs to the same "arrays are arithmetic" family. A scanning loop normally tests two things per step — have I run off the end? and is this the element? — and the first test is pure overhead, since it succeeds every time but once. Write the sought value into one spare cell past the live data, a sentinel, and the search is now guaranteed to terminate on a value match, so the bounds test can be deleted from the loop entirely; whether the scan found a real element or the sentinel is decided once, afterwards, by comparing the final index against n. The same idea appears everywhere a boundary can be turned into a value: a sentinel node that removes the null check from a linked-list loop, a sentinel row of +∞ that removes the exhausted-run check from a merge. It buys a constant factor and nothing asymptotic, it costs one cell of slack and the right to write into it, and it is the reason a container that owns one spare slot is sometimes easier to write loops against than one that does not.

The other precondition, easy to forget. Binary search requires the array to be sorted by the key you are searching on. If the array is maintained sorted, every insertion costs Θ(n) for the shift of §05 — so a sorted array is an excellent read-mostly structure and a poor write-heavy one. That single sentence is the reason balanced search trees exist: they keep the ordering and the logarithmic search while making insertion logarithmic too, by giving up the contiguous block.
