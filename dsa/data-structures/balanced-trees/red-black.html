08 Red-black trees: the five properties, black-height, and the height lemma proved

A red-black tree is a binary search tree in which every node carries one bit, its colour, subject to five properties whose joint effect is that no root-to-leaf path is more than twice as long as any other. Where the AVL invariant is a rule about heights, the red-black invariant is a rule about counts of black nodes on paths — an approximate balance that is looser than AVL's (height up to 2·log₂(n+1) rather than 1.44·log₂(n+2)) but cheaper to maintain: an insertion needs at most two rotations and a deletion at most three, with the rest of the repair being recolouring, which never changes shape. The properties are stated over a tree whose empty subtrees are represented by a single shared black sentinel, nil, which is what makes "every leaf is black" a sentence rather than a special case.

the five properties1. every node is red or black. 2. the root is black. 3. every leaf (nil) is black. 4. a red node has two black children — "no two reds in a row" on any path. 5. for every node x, all simple paths from x down to a leaf contain the same number of black nodes. black-heightbh(x) = the number of black nodes on any path from x (NOT counting x itself) down to a leaf, counting the nil leaf. Why colour rather than height

The red-black invariant looks less natural than AVL's until you see what it encodes. Property 5 says that if you erase the red nodes — merge each red node into its black parent — every leaf ends up at the same depth: the black nodes form a perfectly balanced tree of variable fan-out, and the red nodes are the "extra" keys that let a black node hold two or three keys instead of one. That tree is the 2-3-4 tree of §11, and the red-black tree is its binary encoding: a way to get perfect depth balance on the multiway tree while storing plain binary nodes with one extra bit. Property 4 bounds how many extra keys a black node can absorb (at most two reds hang from one black, so a multiway node has at most 3 keys), and that is what keeps the encoding's binary height within twice the multiway height. Seen this way the repair rules of §09 and §10 stop being a list of cases to memorise and become "split an overfull multiway node" and "borrow from or merge with a sibling", the operations of §13.

09 Red-black insertion: colour the new node red, then fix "two reds in a row" — three cases, at most two rotations

A new key is inserted as in a plain BST and coloured red, which is the colour that cannot break property 5 (the black count on every path is unchanged) and can break only property 4 (its parent may be red) or property 2 (it may be the root). The fix-up loop walks upward while the parent is red, and at each step is in one of three cases distinguished by the colour of the uncle and the side the node is on. Case 1 recolours and moves the problem two levels up; cases 2 and 3 rotate and end the loop. Because only case 1 repeats, the loop performs O(log n) recolourings but at most two rotations in total — and the recolourings do not change the shape.

10 Red-black deletion: the "doubly black" node, four cases, at most three rotations

Deletion follows the BST deletion exactly — the node spliced out is either the target (fewer than two children) or its successor — and the question is what colour was removed. If the spliced node was red, nothing is broken: no black count changed, no two reds were made adjacent, the root is still black. If it was black, every path through its position has lost a black node, and the repair pushes an "extra black" up the tree, carried by the node x that took the spliced node's place, until it can be absorbed by a red node (recolour it black) or reaches the root (where it vanishes). The four cases are distinguished by the colour of x's sibling w and of w's children, and — the counting fact that matters — cases 1, 3 and 4 each rotate once and case 4 terminates, so a deletion performs at most three rotations.

11 A red-black tree is a 2-3-4 tree in disguise — the isomorphism, and the left-leaning variant

Take any red-black tree and merge every red node into its black parent. Because a red node's children are black (property 4), each black node absorbs at most two reds, giving a node with one, two or three keys and two, three or four children — a 2-3-4 tree. Because every root-to-nil path has the same number of black nodes (property 5), every leaf of the merged tree is at the same depth — the defining property of a 2-3-4 tree (§13). The correspondence goes both ways, up to the choice of which key of a 3-node is the black one, and it explains every red-black rule as a multiway rule: insertion case 1 is splitting a 4-node, cases 2 and 3 are rearranging within a node, the deletion cases are transfer from a sibling and fuse with a sibling.

Insertion becomes three local fixes applied on the way back up: if right child red and left black → rotate left; if left child red and its left child red → rotate right; if both children red → flip colours (= split). Deletion is longer. same O(log n) bounds, same 2·log₂(n+1) height; the code is shorter and the invariant easier to state, at the cost of giving up the O(1)-rotations guarantee: the fix-ups can fire at every level on the way back up, so an insertion may cost Θ(log n) rotations.

Slide back to k = 3 and then 4: after 10, 34, 14 the tree is a single 4-node [10 14 34] (black 14 with two red children), and inserting 30 — red-black case 1 — splits it: 14 rises to become a new root 2-node, 10 becomes a 2-node, and 30 joins 34 to make the 3-node [30 34]. That is the one 2-3-4 operation that grows the tree, and it is the one red-black operation that increases the black-height.

Which to implement. The 2-3-4 tree makes the idea clear; the red-black tree makes the code uniform (every node has the same layout, one bit of colour), and that is why the libraries store the binary encoding. Left-leaning red-black trees are the compromise that keeps the binary layout but reduces the insertion cases to three one-line fixes. The 2-3-4 tree taken seriously, with a much larger node, is the B-tree of §14.

12 AVL versus red-black: what each buys, measured on the same workload

The received wisdom is "AVL for lookup-heavy, red-black for update-heavy": AVL's tighter invariant gives a shorter tree and faster searches, red-black's looser one gives fewer rotations on updates. The measurements below test that wisdom on the same keys, the same searches and the same deletions, and the result is more nuanced than the slogan — on random input the two trees are nearly identical in every count, and the slogan is really about worst cases.

On random input the two structures are indistinguishable to two decimal places where it matters: mean node depth 10.26 (AVL) against 10.30 (red-black), comparisons per successful search 11.24 against 11.25, height 14 and 14. Rotations per insertion favour red-black slightly, 0.59 against 0.69; rotations per deletion are level, 0.39 against 0.38 — but red-black pays 2.3 recolourings per insertion and 2.0 per deletion on top, each a memory write the AVL tree does not make (its height updates along the path are the analogous cost, and are not free either). Switch to sorted input and the pictures separate: the AVL tree ends at height 12, the red-black at 21 — the long spine of the opening figure — yet their mean depths are 10.0 and 10.5, because the red-black spine holds few nodes; and both perform almost exactly 1.0 rotation per insertion. So: AVL wins the worst case for height (1.44 vs 2 in the constant, 12 vs 21 here), red-black wins the worst case for rotations per deletion (3 vs O(log n)), and on typical input neither wins anything measurable. The choice in practice is made by the second fact and by engineering convenience — an O(1)-rotation guarantee is what a concurrent or intrusive implementation wants — which is why the standard libraries picked red-black (§33).

The claim to retire. "Red-black trees are faster than AVL trees" — or the reverse — is not a statement with a truth value until the workload is named. Lookup on a tree built adversarially: AVL, by up to 1.4×. Deletions with a hard bound on rotations: red-black. Random workload: a coin toss decided by cache behaviour and node size, not by asymptotics.
