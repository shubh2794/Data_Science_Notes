19 Splay trees: no invariant at all — move every accessed node to the root

A splay tree is a binary search tree with no balance information and one rule: after every access — search, insert or delete — the node accessed is rotated up to the root by a specific sequence of double rotations called splaying. Nothing bounds the height; a splay tree can be a chain, and a single access can cost Θ(n). What the rule buys is a guarantee on sequences: any m operations on a tree that never exceeds n nodes cost O(m log n) in total, so the amortised cost per operation is O(log n) — the same bound as AVL and red-black, with zero bytes of overhead per node and with an adaptivity they lack, since recently and frequently accessed keys end up near the top. The rule's details matter: rotating the node up one level at a time ("move to root") does not give the bound; the pairwise steps below do.

splay(x): repeat until x is the root, with p = x's parent and g = p's parentZIG p is the root: one rotation at p, lifting x. The keys 8, 6, 7, 2, 1, 5, 4, 3 were inserted without splaying to manufacture a bad shape: height 5, with 3 at the bottom of a zig-zag-zig path below 4, 5, 2, 6, 8. Splaying 3 takes three steps. The zig-zig with parent 4 and grandparent 5 (both left children) rotates at 5 first, then at 4; the potential is unchanged at 10.714 because the three nodes' subtree sizes were permuted rather than changed. The zig-zag with parent 2 and grandparent 6 drops Φ to 9.807; the final zig at the root 8 drops it to 9.322. Five rotations in all, and the sum of the amortised costs — rotations plus ΔΦ — is 3.61, against the access lemma's bound of 3·(r(root) − r(3)) + 1 = 3·(log₂ 8 − 0) + 1 = 10. The tree after the splay has height 4 rather than 5, and 3 is the root: the shape has been improved as a side effect of being read, which is the whole idea.

20 The splay analysis: the potential Φ = Σ log₂ size, the access lemma, and what the amortised bound does and does not promise

The proof that splaying gives O(log n) amortised is the cleanest example of the potential method in the whole subject, and it is worth carrying through because the same potential — the sum of the logarithms of the subtree sizes — reappears in the analysis of other self-adjusting structures. Define the rank of a node as the log of its subtree size and the potential of the tree as the sum of all ranks. A splay step that lifts x can perform two rotations and still reduce the potential by enough to pay for them; summed over a splay, the telescoping bound is three times the increase in x's rank, plus one, which is at most 3·log₂ n + 1.

definitionssize(x) = number of nodes in x's subtree; rank r(x) = log₂ size(x); potential Φ(T) = ∑ over all nodes r(x) ≥ 0, and Φ(empty) = 0 amortised cost of a step = actual cost (rotations) + Φ(after) − Φ(before). Sum over a sequence: actual total = amortised total + Φ₀ − Φ_final ≤ amortised total + Φ₀. the one inequality everything rests onif a, b > 0 and a + b ≤ c: log₂ a + log₂ b ≤ 2·log₂ c − 2. Cost per access over a sequence: a chain of 128 accessed in sorted order (the sequential-access theorem), or 128 random accesses, or a 4-key working set rotations per access from the real routine; the first sequential access costs 127 — and the total is far below the amortised bound

The default starts from the tree that inserting 1 … 128 in order produces — a left-going chain of height 127 with 128 at the root, since every new maximum is splayed to the top — and then accesses 1, 2, …, 128 in order. The first access, to the bottom of the chain, costs 127 rotations: the Θ(n) single operation. The second costs 64, then 33, 16, 11, 5, 4, 2, …: each splay folds the remaining chain in half, and the total over all 128 accesses is 534 rotations, a mean of 4.17 — O(1) per access as the sequential-access theorem promises, and nowhere near the general bound of 3·log₂ 128 + 1 = 22 per access. Random accesses on the same tree cost a mean of 8.3 rotations with a maximum of 88 (an unlucky deep key early on); the 4-key working set costs 3.81 per access overall — 240 rotations to lift the four keys out of the chain, then 2.0 per access once they sit at the top, because the four keys never leave the top of the tree. The dashed line is the amortised bound; individual bars may exceed it — that is what "amortised" allows — but the running mean never does, and the panel checks that the actual total is within m·(3·log₂ n + 1) + Φ₀.

What "amortised" does not promise. A splay tree cannot be used where a single operation's latency matters — a real-time scheduler, a lock held during the operation, a request with a deadline — because that one operation may be Θ(n). It also cannot be shared by concurrent readers without coordination, because reads write. It is the right structure when the total time of a long sequence is what counts and the access pattern is skewed toward a working set — caches, symbol tables in compilers, the internals of some garbage collectors — and it is the structure of choice when per-node memory must be zero.

21 Skip lists: balance by coin flip — the structure, search, insert and delete

A skip list is a sorted linked list with express lanes: level 0 holds every key in order; level 1 holds a random subset of them, each promoted with probability p; level 2 a random subset of level 1; and so on, with a head node of −∞ that reaches every level. Every key is a tower whose height was decided by coin flips at insertion time and never changes. A search starts at the top of the head and, at each level, moves right as far as it can without passing the key, then drops one level; at level 0 it lands on the key or on its predecessor. The structure is not a tree, but it behaves like one — the level-i list is the set of nodes within i levels of the ROOT of a randomised balanced tree, counting from the top — the higher the level, the sparser the list — and it gets the balanced tree's bounds in expectation over its own coins, with insertions and deletions that are nothing but linked-list splices.

the structurelevels L₀ ⊇ L₁ ⊇ … ⊇ L_{h−1}; L₀ = all keys in sorted order; each node in Lᵢ is also in Lᵢ₋₁; head = −∞ at every level (a +∞ tail is optional: this page uses null). a node with tower height ℓ ≥ 1 has ℓ forward pointers next[0 … ℓ−1]. P(ℓ ≥ i) = p^(i−1): ℓ is geometric — flip until tails, ℓ = number of flips. p = ½ or ¼ in practice. search(k) — the "update vector" it leaves behind is what insert and delete usex ← head; for i = h−1 down to 0: while x.next[i] ≠ null and x.next[i].key < k: x ← x.next[i] (scan right) update[i] ← x (the last node before k at level i) y ← x.next[0]; found iff y ≠ null and y.key = k. The search's rightward moves at level i are steps among siblings; the drop is a step to a child.

22 The probabilistic analysis: expected height, expected search cost, expected space — and what "expected" means here

The skip list's bounds are expectations over the coin flips, and the distinction from the random-input BST of the BST page is the whole reason to use one. There, the randomness was assumed to be in the input, and an adversary who chose the insertion order could defeat it. Here the randomness is generated by the structure, the input order is irrelevant to the distribution of shapes, and no adversary who does not know the coins can force a bad case. A bad run of coins can produce a tall tower or a long scan, so the worst case is O(n) — but its probability shrinks polynomially in n, and the figures below measure both the expectation and the tail.

height (number of levels above L₀), with n keys and promotion probability pP(a given tower reaches level i) = pⁱ. P(level i is nonempty) ≤ n·pⁱ (union bound over the n towers). E[levels above L₀] = ∑_{i≥1} P(level i nonempty) ≤ ∑ min(1, n·pⁱ) ≤ log_{1/p} n + 1/(1 − p). p = ½: ≤ log₂ n + 2; p = ¼: ≤ log₄ n + 4/3. tail: P(height > c·log_{1/p} n) ≤ n · p^(c·log_{1/p} n) = n^(1−c). For p = ½, n = 1024: P(more than 30 levels) ≤ 1/n² ≈ 10⁻⁶. So height is O(log n) WITH HIGH PROBABILITY, not just in expectation. search cost — the backward analysistrace the search path BACKWARDS from the key found: at each node, with probability p its tower goes up (the path came from above: a drop), with probability 1 − p it does not (the path came from the left: a scan step). so the path climbs one level per 1/p steps on average: E[steps] ≤ (levels)/p + O(1/(1−p)) ≤ (log_{1/p} n)/p + O(1). p = ½: 2·log₂ n + O(1). p = ¼: 4·log₄ n = 2·log₂ n + O(1) — THE SAME leading term. Measured (figure): 18.7 vs 17.7 comparisons at n = 1024. spaceE[pointers per node] = E[tower height] = 1/(1 − p): 2 for p = ½, 4/3 for p = ¼. E[total pointers] = n/(1 − p) = O(n). Measured: 1.994 and 1.334 per node. the choice of pp = ¼ costs no more comparisons in the leading term and uses a third fewer pointers per node (better cache behaviour, smaller nodes) — the choice of the in-memory store cited in §33. p = ½ has the simplest analysis and fewer levels of scanning per comparison; both are O(log n). expected vs amortised vs worst — placed side by sideskip list: expected O(log n) per op, every op, any input; worst O(n) with probability → 0. splay: amortised O(log n), worst Θ(n) deterministically. AVL / red-black / B-tree: worst O(log n), always. why skip lists suit concurrencyinsert = splice new pointers in, bottom level first; delete = unlink, top level first. A reader that sees a half-inserted tower still finds every key (the bottom list is always complete). no rotation ever moves an existing node or changes a subtree's root; each update touches O(1) pointers per level, at scattered positions. That is what makes lock-free skip lists implementable, and why the concurrent ordered maps of at least one major runtime and the write buffers of several storage engines are skip lists (§33).
200 skip lists of 1 024 keys each, p = ½ and p = ¼: the distribution of heights against the expectation bound, and the measured search cost and pointers per node every list is built with its own seed, invariant-checked, and searched for all its keys

With p = ½ the 200 lists have a mean of 11.2 levels (so 10.2 above the base) against the bound log₂ 1024 + 2 = 12, with the tallest at 17: the histogram is a peak at 10–11 with a geometric tail, which is exactly the shape the union bound predicts. Searching every key of every list costs 18.7 comparisons and 8.8 pointer follows per search on average, and the lists carry 1.994 pointers per node against the expected 2. Switch to p = ¼: 5.9 levels on average, 4.9 above the base against the bound log₄ 1024 + 4/3 = 6.33, a tallest of 10, 17.7 comparisons and 12.5 follows per search — slightly fewer comparisons — the leading terms are equal; the O(1) term is what favours p = ¼, with more rightward scanning and fewer drops — and 1.334 pointers per node against 4/3. Nothing in either configuration depends on the insertion order, which was a random permutation here but could have been sorted: the same seeds would produce the same towers.
