Graph Representations & Traversal

Every structure before this one imposed a shape on its data: a line (arrays, lists), a hierarchy (trees), a partial order (heaps), or no shape at all (hash tables). A graph imposes none and records all of them: it is a set of things and a set of pairwise relationships, and that is the whole definition. Road networks, dependency graphs, social networks, the web, state spaces, version histories, knowledge bases and neural-network computation graphs are all the same object with different labels on the vertices. This page is about two questions that come before any graph algorithm. How is a graph stored — the adjacency matrix, adjacency lists and maps, the edge list, and the compressed sparse row layout that real systems use — and what does each make cheap? And how is a graph walked — breadth-first, which discovers vertices in order of their distance in edges and proves it, and depth-first, whose discovery and finishing times induce a nesting structure so rigid that cycle detection, topological ordering, strongly connected components and articulation points all fall out of a single pass. Every cost on the page is measured on a graph that was built and traversed, every hand example was traced by a script, and every algorithm's output is cross-checked against a brute-force method on hundreds of random graphs.

01 What a graph is

A graph G = (V, E) is a finite set V of vertices together with a set E of edges, each edge being a pair of vertices. If the pairs are unordered — {u, v} — the graph is undirected and the edge can be crossed either way; if they are ordered — (u, v), from u to v — the graph is directed (a digraph) and the edge is an arrow. That is the entire definition. Everything else on this page — degrees, paths, cycles, connectivity, trees, representations, traversals — is vocabulary for talking about the pairs, and the reason the vocabulary is worth learning is that an enormous range of problems are, once the labels are stripped off, questions about pairs: can I get from here to there, how far is it, what must happen before what, which things are mutually reachable, which single failure would cut the system in two. Write V and E for the sets and, by the usual abuse, for their sizes; the notation n = |V|, m = |E| is also common and the page uses both.

Why the pairs are the point

A vertex is a thing; an edge is a fact about two things. That asymmetry is why graphs are the general-purpose model. A list says "these things come in this order"; a tree says "this thing is part of that thing"; a graph says only "these two are related", and lets the relation be anything: is adjacent to, links to, depends on, follows, is one move away from, is the parent commit of, was cited by. The definition does not care what the vertices are, and neither do the algorithms — which is what makes one BFS routine solve a maze, a word ladder, a network-hop count and a social distance query without modification. The price of that generality is that a graph carries no built-in way to be walked. An array has index i + 1; a tree has "left child"; a graph has only "the neighbours of v", and the traversals of §10–§16 are precisely the disciplines for turning that one primitive into a systematic visit of everything.

Undirected against directed, and the two degrees

The undirected/directed distinction is not cosmetic: it changes the theorems. In an undirected graph reachability is symmetric, so "connected" is one notion; in a directed graph u may reach v without v reaching u, and "connected" splits into strongly and weakly connected. A DFS of an undirected graph produces only two kinds of edge; of a directed graph, four (§17). Cycle detection has to exclude the edge you arrived on in the undirected case but not in the directed one (§18). Every one of the four recurring mistakes on this page is a theorem for one kind of graph quoted for the other, so each section says which kind it is about. The degree bookkeeping differs too: an undirected vertex has one degree, a directed vertex an in-degree and an out-degree, and the handshake lemma reads ∑ deg = 2E for one and ∑ in = ∑ out = E for the other. The figure measures both on a random graph by walking every adjacency list and counting entries — the count is 2E or E because the list representation stores each undirected edge twice and each directed edge once, which is the first thing to know about that representation (§05).

The handshake lemma, measured: Σ deg(v) = 2E undirected; Σ in = Σ out = E directed a random simple graph on 12 vertices with 18 edges; every adjacency list is walked and its entries counted — the total is the sum of the degrees

The default is undirected with V = 12, E = 18: the twelve degrees sum to 36 = 2 · 18, the adjacency lists hold 36 entries between them, and the number of odd-degree vertices is even, as the lemma forces. Switch to directed and the same twelve vertices with eighteen arrows have in-degrees summing to 18 and out-degrees summing to 18, with 18 list entries — each arrow stored once, on its tail's list. The density is also shown: 18 of the 12·11/2 = 66 possible undirected edges is 0.27; of the 132 possible directed ones, 0.14.

Why density decides everything

The single number that governs every choice on this page is the ratio of E to V². Since E ≤ V(V−1)/2, a bound of O(V + E) is never worse than O(V²), and on a sparse graph it is smaller by a factor of about V / (1 + E/V) — a quarter of a million times, for a million-vertex road network with three million edges. The adjacency matrix (§04) costs Θ(V²) regardless; the adjacency list (§05) costs Θ(V + E); and every traversal inherits the cost of the representation it runs on, so BFS and DFS are Θ(V + E) with adjacency lists and Θ(V²) with a matrix, and neither statement is complete without its qualifier. The crossover, measured in §08, sits near a density of a quarter for a byte-per-cell matrix against a pointer-based list — and near 3% for a bit-per-cell matrix — so "sparse" in practice means "well below that", which nearly every real graph is.

The one sentence to carry through the page. A graph is a set of pairs; a representation is a way of answering "what are the neighbours of v" and "is (u, v) an edge"; a traversal is a discipline for asking the first question of every vertex exactly once; and every bound on the page is Θ(V + E) because that is what asking it once for every vertex, with adjacency lists, costs.

Three modelling decisions, and what each commits you to

  • Direction. If the relation is symmetric (friendship, adjacency, "shares a border") use an undirected graph and get the simpler theory: one notion of connectivity, two edge classes, cycle detection by "grey neighbour that is not the arrival edge". If it is asymmetric (follows, depends on, links to, can move to) use a directed graph and accept that reachability is one-way, that "connected" needs a qualifier, and that DFS produces four edge classes. Modelling a symmetric relation as two arrows is legal and doubles E; modelling an asymmetric one as undirected loses information you cannot recover.
  • Weight. If "how far" means "how many edges", the graph is unweighted and BFS answers it exactly (§12). If it means a sum of lengths, times or costs, the graph is weighted, BFS is wrong (it counts hops, not weight), and the algorithms of the hand-off in §28 apply. A weighted graph with all weights equal is an unweighted graph.
  • Explicit or implicit. A road network is stored; a puzzle's state space is not — it is defined by a successor function and generated as the traversal reaches it. Every traversal on this page works on either, because they only ever ask for the neighbours of the current vertex; the representation sections (§04–§08) are about the explicit case, and §09 is about the implicit one.

Two claims about real graphs, stated at the level the evidence supports

Real graphs are sparse. A road junction meets a handful of roads; a person has hundreds of contacts out of billions of people; a source file imports tens of modules out of thousands; a web page links to tens of others out of billions. In every case E is a small multiple of V, not a fraction of V², which is the regime where adjacency lists and CSR win and the matrix is unusable. The exception is a graph that was built from a pairwise computation — a full similarity or correlation matrix — and such graphs are usually thresholded back to sparse before any traversal. Real graphs have short paths. The "six degrees of separation" phrase is folklore that predates any large measurement; what has actually been measured is that on one very large social network in 2016 the average shortest path between accounts was reported at about 4.6 friendship links (the company phrased it as "3.57 degrees", counting the intermediaries). That is one platform, one year and one definition of the edge, not a law about humanity, but it illustrates the general finding that large sparse social graphs have small average distances — which is why BFS layers on such graphs grow very fast and why the frontier, not the depth, is what exhausts memory (§25).

What graph databases store. Systems that call themselves "native" graph stores describe their design as index-free adjacency: each vertex record holds direct references to its incident edges, so following an edge is a pointer dereference rather than an index lookup, and a traversal's cost depends on the part of the graph it touches rather than on the whole graph's size. That is an adjacency-list layout (§05) persisted to disk, and it is a design claim by those systems rather than a theorem — a relational table of (source, target) rows with an index on source is a perfectly good adjacency list too, at the cost of one index probe per hop.

30 Pitfalls

  • "BFS/DFS is Θ(V + E)" without naming the representation. With adjacency lists, yes; with a matrix it is Θ(V²) at any density, because neighbours(v) scans a row. §12's figure shows both at V = 64.
  • "BFS gives shortest paths." Shortest in edge count, from the source, one per vertex. With weights it is wrong (§28); between two non-source vertices the BFS-tree path is not generally shortest (§13).
  • Four edge classes on an undirected graph. Undirected DFS has tree and back edges only (§17). Seeing the arrival edge a second time produces a spurious back edge (the tree edge seen from the child) or a spurious forward edge (a back edge seen from the ancestor's side); a "cross" label means an undirected edge was stored in only one adjacency list — the graph is secretly directed.
  • Cycle detection that excludes the parent vertex. Correct on simple graphs, blind to parallel edges. Exclude the arrival edge (§18's fourth case).
  • "A graph is a DAG iff DFS finds no back edge" applied to an undirected graph. A back edge in an undirected graph means a cycle, but the statement "DAG" is directed; an undirected graph with no cycles is a forest, and the test is E = V − c (§18, §19).
  • Assuming the topological order is unique, or that the two algorithms agree. D has 40 valid orders; DFS gives h a c f b d e g, Kahn a h b c d f e g. Unique iff a Hamiltonian path (§21).
  • Emitting reverse finishing order without checking for a back edge. On a cyclic graph the DFS still finishes, and the "order" it produces violates some edge. Check, or use Kahn's count (§20).
  • The naive stack DFS. "Push all white neighbours" is a traversal, not DFS: no valid finishing times, a vertex pushed up to in(v) times, and none of §16–§24 applies (§15, §26).
  • Recursive DFS on a big graph. Stack overflow on a path, a grid, or most random sparse graphs at V in the hundreds of thousands. Use the explicit-stack version, which reproduces the same timestamps (§26).
  • "BFS uses more memory than DFS." BFS holds a layer, DFS holds a path; the star and the path graph are each other's worst cases, and a random sparse graph is bad for both (§25).
  • Forgetting the outer loop. A single BFS/DFS from s reaches s's component only. Components, topological sort, SCCs and the DFS forest need the loop over all vertices (§10, §19).
  • "Connected" on a digraph. Say weakly or strongly. G2 is weakly connected with three strong components (§22).
  • Following edges into completed components in the low-link SCC algorithm. The onStack test is what stops g→d from merging {f, g, h} with {d, e} (§23).
  • Using > for cut vertices or forgetting the root rule. Cut vertex: low[w] ≥ d[u]; bridge: low[w] > d[u]; root: two or more tree children (§24).
  • Multi-source BFS as several BFS runs. One run with all sources enqueued at distance 0 gives the nearest-source distances at Θ(V + E), not |S| times that (§13).
  • Bipartite check on a directed graph without saying so. It answers for the underlying undirected graph; direction is irrelevant to 2-colourability (§14).
  • Matrix "always worse". Dense graphs, worst-case O(1) edge queries, bit-parallel neighbourhood operations and algebraic methods all favour it; the space crossover is near density 1/4 (byte) or 1/32 (bit) (§04, §08).
  • Treating CSR as a mutable adjacency list. It is static: an insertion is a rebuild. Its benefit is cache behaviour, not asymptotics (§07).
  • The implicit-graph visited set keyed by object identity. Two generations of the same state are different objects; hash the encoded state, or the search loops (§09).
  • Counting edges with the adjacency-list loop. "For each vertex, for each neighbour" counts every undirected edge twice: 2E, the number every scan counter on this page reports (§05, §06).
