10 What a traversal is: the visited set, the frontier, and the two disciplines

A traversal (or search) is a procedure that, starting from a source vertex, visits every vertex reachable from it exactly once and examines every edge leaving a visited vertex, using only the neighbours(v) query. All traversals share the same skeleton: keep a set of visited vertices (so that no vertex is processed twice and the procedure terminates on graphs with cycles) and a collection of discovered-but-not-yet-expanded vertices, the frontier; repeatedly take a vertex from the frontier, mark it, and add its unvisited neighbours to the frontier. The single design decision is which frontier vertex to take next, and two answers give the two traversals of this page: the oldest (a queue) gives breadth-first search, the newest (a stack) gives depth-first search.

the generic traversalvisited ← {s}; frontier ← [s] while frontier ≠ ∅: u ← frontier.take(); for each v in neighbours(u): if v ∉ visited: visited.add(v); parent[v] ← u; frontier.add(v) the invariants every instance keeps(1) a vertex enters the frontier at most once (it is marked visited when it enters) ⇒ at most V takes, and each vertex's list is scanned exactly once ⇒ total work Θ(V + E) with adjacency lists (2) every vertex in visited is reachable from s (induction: it entered via an edge from a visited vertex) (3) at termination every vertex reachable from s is in visited (if not, take a path from s to an unvisited vertex; its first unvisited vertex has a visited predecessor whose list was scanned, so it would have been added) (4) the parent pointers form a TREE rooted at s spanning the visited vertices — the TRAVERSAL TREE (a BFS tree or a DFS tree), with Vr − 1 tree edges for Vr vertices reached the two disciplinesfrontier = QUEUE (FIFO: take the oldest) → BREADTH-FIRST: vertices are taken in non-decreasing distance from s; distances in edges come out exactly (§11–12) frontier = STACK (LIFO: take the newest) → DEPTH-FIRST: follow one edge as far as it goes before backtracking; discovery/finish times nest like parentheses (§15–16) frontier = PRIORITY QUEUE keyed by tentative distance → DIJKSTRA (weighted; §28's hand-off); keyed by distance + heuristic → A*; keyed by edge weight to the tree → PRIM the outer loopfor each s in V: if s ∉ visited: traverse(s) — visits ALL of V, one tree per connected component (undirected) or one tree per "first unvisited vertex" (directed); total still Θ(V + E)

The three colours

It is convenient — and, for DFS's edge classification, necessary — to refine "visited" into three states. A vertex is white before it is discovered, grey from the moment it enters the frontier until it has been expanded (all its neighbours examined), and black afterwards. The grey vertices are exactly the frontier. In BFS the greys are the queue contents, which at any moment span at most two consecutive distances (§12's key lemma); in DFS the greys are the stack contents, which at any moment form a single path from the root down to the current vertex, and the whole classification of edges in §17 comes from asking what colour the far end of an edge is when the edge is examined: white means a tree edge, grey means an edge back to the current path, black means an edge to something already finished. The colouring costs nothing extra — it is the same visited flag with one more value — and every figure on the page draws it: white as the default node fill, grey as amber, black as blue.

What a traversal computes, beyond a visit

  • Reachability. The visited set at the end is exactly the set of vertices reachable from s (invariants 2 and 3). "Can I get from s to t?" is one traversal, stopped early if t is found.
  • A spanning tree of the reached region (invariant 4), via the parent pointers. For BFS it is a shortest-path tree from s (§12); for DFS it is the tree whose ancestor relation the parenthesis theorem describes (§16).
  • A vertex order — the order of takes (the BFS order, the DFS preorder) or of finishes (the DFS postorder). The reverse DFS postorder is a topological order of a DAG (§20).
  • An edge classification in DFS (§17), from which cycles (§18), strongly connected components (§22) and articulation points (§24) follow.
  • Distances in edges in BFS (§12), and hence bipartiteness (§14) and shortest-path reconstruction (§13).

Where the two traversals come from

Both are the tree traversals of the tree page generalised to a graph, with the visited set added because a graph may have more than one path to a vertex. Level-order traversal of a tree, with its queue, is BFS on the tree from the root; preorder traversal, with its recursion or explicit stack, is DFS. On a tree the visited set is redundant (there is one path to everything) and the two searches produce the level order and the preorder respectively; on a graph the visited set is what stops a cycle from being walked forever, and the "already visited" edges it rejects are the non-tree edges whose classification carries all the structural information. The queue and the stack themselves are the linked-structures page's; this page assumes their O(1) operations and does not re-teach them.

Why the choice of frontier is everything. The generic skeleton with a bag (take any element) still visits everything reachable in Θ(V + E). What the bag's discipline adds is order, and order is what the applications use: the queue's order is by distance, the stack's order is by nesting. Every result in §11–§24 is a theorem about one of those two orders.

11 Breadth-first search: the queue, the layers, and a traced example

Breadth-first search from a source s discovers vertices in order of their distance from s in edges: first s itself, then everything one edge away, then everything two edges away, and so on, each layer completely before the next begins. The mechanism is the generic traversal of §10 with a FIFO queue as the frontier; the order it induces is the whole point, and §12 proves that the layer a vertex lands in is exactly its distance δ(s, v). BFS also records, for each discovered vertex, the vertex it was discovered from — the parent — and those pointers form a tree in which the path from s to any vertex is a shortest path.

What the trace shows

  • The layers are the distances, and the queue is the layer boundary. BFS is often described as "process layer k, then layer k + 1"; the queue implements that without ever storing the layer index, because vertices of layer k + 1 are appended behind the remaining vertices of layer k. An explicit two-list version ("current layer, next layer") is equivalent and is what a bit-parallel or parallel BFS uses.
  • A vertex's distance is fixed at discovery. dist[v] is assigned once, when v turns grey, and never changes — there is no relaxation. That is why BFS is right for unit weights and wrong for general ones: with weights, the first discovery is not necessarily the cheapest.
  • Non-tree edges are wasted scans, and there are E − (Vr − 1) of them. G1 has 10 edges, 7 are tree edges, 3 are not; each non-tree edge is scanned twice and rejected twice. On a dense graph almost every scan is a rejection, and the E term dominates the V term.
  • The queue is short on this graph, and that is not typical. The queue peaks at 2 because G1 is nearly a path. On a star it holds V − 1; on a random graph it holds a whole layer, which can be most of the graph (§25). The queue's maximum length is the BFS memory bound, and it is a property of the graph's shape, not of V.

12 BFS computes shortest paths in edges: the proof, the BFS tree, and the Θ(V + E) accounting

The claim to prove is that on termination dist[v] = δ(s, v) for every vertex, where δ(s, v) is the length in edges of a shortest path from s to v (∞ if none). The proof is in three steps: an easy inequality in one direction, a structural lemma about the queue, and a minimal-counterexample argument that closes the gap. It holds for directed and undirected graphs alike, and it is exactly the statement that fails the moment edges carry unequal weights.

lemma 1 — the triangle inequality for δfor any edge (u, v): δ(s, v) ≤ δ(s, u) + 1. Proof: if u is reachable, a shortest path to u followed by the edge is a path to v of that length; if u is not, the right side is ∞. lemma 2 — dist never undershootsat every moment, dist[v] ≥ δ(s, v) for all v. Proof: induction on enqueues. Initially dist[s] = 0 = δ(s, s) and all others ∞. When v is discovered from u, dist[v] = dist[u] + 1 ≥ δ(s, u) + 1 ≥ δ(s, v) by the hypothesis and lemma 1; dist[v] is never changed again. lemma 3 — the queue holds at most two consecutive distances, in orderif Q = ⟨v₁, …, vr⟩ (head first), then dist[vr] ≤ dist[v₁] + 1 and dist[vi] ≤ dist[vi+1] for all i. Proof: induction on queue operations. A dequeue removes v₁; the rest is still sorted and dist[vr] ≤ dist[v₁] + 1 ≤ dist[v₂] + 1. An enqueue of v (discovered while scanning u, already dequeued) appends dist[v] = dist[u] + 1; since u was the head before the current head v₁, dist[u] ≤ dist[v₁], so dist[v] ≤ dist[v₁] + 1; and dist[vr] ≤ dist[u] + 1 = dist[v], so the order is kept. corollary: vertices are enqueued in non-decreasing dist, and hence DEQUEUED in non-decreasing dist. theorem — BFS is correcton termination dist[v] = δ(s, v) for every v, and every vertex reachable from s is discovered. Proof: suppose not; among the vertices with dist[v] ≠ δ(s, v) take one with SMALLEST δ(s, v). Then v ≠ s, v is reachable (else δ = ∞ = dist), and by lemma 2 dist[v] > δ(s, v). Let u be v's predecessor on a shortest path, so δ(s, v) = δ(s, u) + 1; by the minimality of v, dist[u] = δ(s, u). Hence dist[v] > dist[u] + 1. (★) Look at the moment u is dequeued. v is white, grey or black. WHITE: the scan of u's list sets dist[v] = dist[u] + 1, contradicting (★). BLACK: v was dequeued before u, so by the corollary dist[v] ≤ dist[u], contradicting (★). GREY: v was discovered from some w dequeued before u, so dist[v] = dist[w] + 1 ≤ dist[u] + 1 by the corollary, contradicting (★). Every case is impossible, so no such v exists. Reachable vertices are all discovered: an undiscovered reachable v would have dist = ∞ ≠ δ(s, v) < ∞. the BFS treethe parent pointers form a tree T rooted at s on the reached vertices (Vr vertices, Vr − 1 edges, one path from s to each); since dist[v] = dist[parent[v]] + 1 = δ(s, v), the tree path from s to v has length δ(s, v): T is a SHORTEST-PATH TREE — from s, and only from s. for every edge (u, v) of the graph: |dist[u] − dist[v]| ≤ 1 (undirected) or dist[v] ≤ dist[u] + 1 (directed) — lemma 1 again; the non-tree edges of §11's figure all satisfy it the cost, as an aggregate argument (Algorithm Analysis §19)each vertex turns grey at most once ⇒ at most V enqueues and V dequeues, O(1) each with a ring buffer or linked queue; each vertex is dequeued at most once ⇒ each adjacency list is scanned at most once ⇒ ∑ |adj[u]| = 2E (or E) entry examinations total Θ(V + E) with adjacency lists — plus the Θ(V) initialisation, which is why it is V + E and not just E even on a graph with no edges with an adjacency MATRIX the scan of u's neighbours costs V cells ⇒ Θ(V · Vr) = Θ(V²) on a connected graph, at ANY density — measured below space: the queue holds at most one or two layers — up to Θ(V) on a wide graph (§25) — plus the dist/parent/colour arrays, Θ(V)

What the proof used, and what it did not

Every step leaned on one fact: a vertex's distance is assigned as dist[parent] + 1 at discovery and the queue keeps discoveries in non-decreasing order, so by the time u is dequeued, everything closer than u has been dequeued and everything at u's distance is at most one behind it. That is a property of unit edge lengths: with a weight w(u, v) the candidate distance dist[u] + w can be beaten later by a path through a vertex dequeued after u, the "grey" case of the proof breaks, and the fix — take the vertex of smallest tentative distance rather than the oldest, and allow a tentative distance to be lowered — is Dijkstra's algorithm, the first item of §28's hand-off. Nothing in the proof cared whether the graph was directed; the undirected case simply has each edge available in both directions, which is why the same routine and the same theorem serve both. And nothing cared about the representation, which is why the cost line has to say "with adjacency lists" separately: the theorem is about the order, the bound is about neighbours(u).

"BFS gives shortest paths." Incomplete in three ways, each of which the proof makes explicit: shortest in number of edges (unit weights); from the source only (the tree is a shortest-path tree rooted at s, and a tree path between two non-source vertices is generally not a shortest path between them); and one shortest path per vertex, the one through the parent — other shortest paths may exist and BFS does not enumerate them, though the layer structure lets a second pass count them (§13).

Path reconstruction, and the direction of the pointers

The parent pointers point towards the source, so following them from a target yields the path backwards: g → f → d → b → a for G1, reversed to a b d f g, four edges, equal to dist[g]. This is the cheapest possible representation of a whole shortest-path tree — one integer per vertex — and it is why BFS (and Dijkstra after it) never store paths explicitly. The recursion above is the same shape as printing a linked list in reverse; the iterative version needs a reversal or a stack. A subtle point: the path is a shortest path from s to g, and its prefix a b d is a shortest path to d (every prefix of a shortest path is a shortest path — otherwise a shorter prefix would give a shorter whole), and its suffix d f g is likewise a shortest d-to-g path — every subpath of a shortest path is a shortest path between its own endpoints (splice in a shorter one and the whole gets shorter), the optimal substructure that Dijkstra and Bellman–Ford later rest on. What is not guaranteed is the tree path between two vertices on different branches: d to h through the BFS tree climbs to a and back down, five edges, where the graph offers d f e h in three.

Multi-source BFS and the super-source

Seeding the queue with several sources at distance 0 is not a trick but the same algorithm on a slightly different graph: add a virtual vertex σ with an edge to every source, run BFS from σ, and subtract one from every distance. Lemma 3 and the theorem of §12 apply verbatim, so the result is the distance to the nearest source, in one pass. The applications are all "how far is the closest X": the distance transform in image processing (every pixel's distance to the nearest edge pixel), evacuation planning (nearest exit), facility problems (nearest depot), and the partition of a graph into regions by nearest source, which falls out by recording, alongside dist, the source from which each vertex was reached. Running |S| separate searches and taking the minimum gives the same numbers at |S| times the cost.

14 Bipartiteness by 2-colouring: BFS parity, and the odd cycle it produces when it fails

An undirected graph is bipartite if its vertices can be split into two sides with every edge joining the two sides — equivalently, if it can be properly 2-coloured — and a graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no odd cycle. BFS decides this in one pass: colour each vertex by the parity of its distance from the source, and check every edge. The proof of the "if and only if" is short, and it is constructive in the useful direction: when the check fails, the failing edge together with the BFS tree yields an odd cycle as a certificate.

the algorithm (undirected graphs; run from every uncoloured vertex to cover all components)BFS from s, setting colour[v] = dist[v] mod 2 — i.e. colour[v] = 1 − colour[u] when v is discovered from u. When scanning an edge (u, v) with v already coloured: if colour[v] = colour[u], report NOT BIPARTITE with witness (u, v); else continue. If every scan passes, the colouring is proper. cost Θ(V + E): it is BFS with one comparison per scan. (DFS works equally well — the colouring is by tree depth parity — but BFS's witness cycle is easier to read off.) why an odd cycle blocks any 2-colouringwalk around a cycle v₀ v₁ … vk−1 v₀ in a proper 2-colouring: colours alternate, so colour(vi) = colour(v₀) + i mod 2, and closing the cycle needs k ≡ 0 mod 2. An odd k is a contradiction. why a failed check yields an odd cyclethe check fails on an edge (u, v) with dist[u] ≡ dist[v] (mod 2). Let w be the lowest common ancestor of u and v in the BFS tree. The tree path u → w has length dist[u] − dist[w], the tree path w → v has length dist[v] − dist[w]; together with the edge (v, u) they form a closed walk of length (dist[u] − dist[w]) + (dist[v] − dist[w]) + 1 = dist[u] + dist[v] − 2·dist[w] + 1, which is ODD. The two tree paths share only w, so the walk is a simple cycle. hence: no failure ⇒ proper colouring ⇒ bipartite; failure ⇒ odd cycle ⇒ not bipartite. The two directions together are the theorem. the exampleC₆ = a b c d e f a: colours a 0, b 1, c 0, d 1, e 0, f 1 — bipartite ({a, c, e} | {b, d, f}) C₆ + chord a–d: in the hexagon a and d are three apart, so they carry opposite colours (0 and 1), and the chord joins opposite colours: still bipartite — the chord splits the hexagon into two EVEN cycles, a b c d a and a d e f a, of length 4 each C₆ + chord a–c: BFS from a (lists in insertion order a: b f c) discovers b, f, c all at distance 1, colour 1; scanning b's list finds c with colour[c] = colour[b] = 1 → NOT BIPARTITE, witness edge (b, c); lca(b, c) = a, so the odd cycle is b a c of length 3 — the triangle the chord created

Why this is stated for undirected graphs, and what it is used for

Bipartiteness is a property of the undirected structure: an edge either joins the two sides or it does not, and direction plays no role. Run the 2-colouring on a directed graph and it answers the question about the underlying undirected graph, which is usually what is meant but should be said. The theorem's uses are broad. Matching: the bipartite matching and assignment algorithms (the flow hand-off in §28) require the two sides as input, and this check produces them. Consistency: any constraint of the form "these two must differ" over a set of items is an edge, and the items are consistently assignable to two classes iff the graph is bipartite — the odd cycle is the minimal inconsistent subset. Parity structure: in a bipartite graph every cycle is even, every walk between the same two vertices has the same parity, and the adjacency matrix has a symmetric spectrum; several graph algorithms have simpler special cases on bipartite input. And the certificate is the point of the constructive proof: an algorithm that says "not bipartite" is less useful than one that says "not bipartite, and here is the odd cycle", because the cycle can be shown to a user, used to localise a bug in a constraint set, or fed to the next stage.

Odd cycle, not just any cycle. A bipartite graph can have plenty of cycles — every even cycle, every grid, every tree-plus-even-chords. "Contains a cycle" is §18's question and has nothing to do with bipartiteness; "contains an odd cycle" is this section's, and the two checks are different passes with different witnesses.
