25 BFS against DFS: memory, order, what each finds, and when each is the wrong tool

Both searches cost Θ(V + E) time with adjacency lists and both visit exactly the reachable vertices; the differences are in what they hold in memory, in the order they produce, and in what that order proves. The memory difference in particular is the opposite of the folklore "BFS uses more memory": BFS holds a layer, DFS holds a path, and which is larger is a property of the graph's shape. At V = 1 024 the two searches are mirror images on the extreme shapes. On the path, BFS's queue never holds more than one vertex while DFS's stack holds all 1 024; on the star, BFS's queue holds 1 023 leaves at once while DFS's stack holds two. The grid is BFS's friend — a frontier is a diagonal of at most 32 cells — and DFS's enemy, because with neighbours in the fixed right-then-down order the search snakes through every cell before backtracking: depth 1 024. The complete binary tree reverses that: BFS's last layer holds 512 leaves, DFS's stack holds a root-to-leaf path of 11. The random graph with E = 3V is bad for both, at 561 and 739: a random sparse graph has a giant layer and a long path. The practical rules

  • State spaces: BFS for the optimal solution, DFS only with a depth bound. BFS's memory is the width of the layer at the solution depth, exponential in the depth but bounded by it; unbounded DFS may descend the wrong way forever. Iterative deepening — DFS to depth 1, then 2, then 3 … — gives BFS's order with DFS's memory at a constant-factor cost in time, and is the standard compromise.
  • Structure: DFS. Anything that needs the parenthesis theorem needs DFS's timestamps, and BFS cannot supply them. Topological sort has Kahn's BFS-like alternative and components and undirected cycle witnesses work with either search (§14, §19); SCCs and articulation points do not.
  • Distance: BFS, and only for unit weights. The moment edges carry unequal weights, neither search is the tool; the priority-queue frontier of §28 is.
  • Big graphs: mind the frontier you are holding. A web-scale BFS holds a layer that can be most of the graph; a road-network DFS holds a path that can be most of the graph. Both can be Θ(V), and the pointer-heavy version of either is what runs out of memory first. CSR (§07) and a bit-vector visited set are the mitigations.
The one-line comparison. BFS is a search by distance and holds a layer; DFS is a search by nesting and holds a path. Time is the same; memory is the shape's choice; the theorems are the order's.

26 Recursion depth: why recursive DFS overflows on a long path, and the explicit stack that does not

Recursive DFS has one frame on the call stack per grey vertex, so its depth equals the depth of the DFS tree, and §25 just measured that at V on a path and on a grid. Runtimes bound the call stack at somewhere between a few thousand and a few hundred thousand frames — the recursion page gives the figures and what actually happens when they are exceeded — so recursive DFS on a million-vertex path, a large grid, or a long linked structure crashes, not asymptotically but with a stack overflow. The cure is the conversion of §15: keep the per-vertex iterator in an array and the path on a heap-allocated stack.

Random sparse graph: a large fraction of V (739 of 1024 above). a call frame is tens to hundreds of bytes (return address, saved registers, the loop variable, the adjacency iterator); a default stack of 1–8 MB holds on the order of 10⁴–10⁵ frames; V = 10⁶ on a path needs 10⁶ frames — 10 to 100 times too many the explicit version needs V iterator slots (4 bytes each) and a stack of at most V ids (4 bytes each): 8 MB for V = 10⁶ on the HEAP, which has no such limit the explicit-stack DFS, as in §15 — this is the recursion-to-iteration conversion of Recursion, Divide & Conquer §10 applied to VISITit[u] ← 0 for all u; push(s); discover(s) while stack ≠ ∅: u ← top; if it[u] < |adj[u]|: v ← adj[u][it[u]]; it[u] ← it[u] + 1; classify (u, v); if v white: parent[v] ← u; discover(v); push(v) else: finish(u); pop produces the identical d, f, parent and edge classes as the recursion (checked in §15's figure on every run, and on every random graph of the audits) — so every theorem of §16–§24 applies unchanged the shortcut that is NOT equivalent"push all white neighbours, pop one, repeat" visits the same vertices but: a vertex can be pushed up to in(v) times (stack up to E), the visit order and tree differ, there are no finishing times, and the parenthesis structure is lost. Fine for reachability and components; wrong for everything from §16 on. what does not have this problemBFS — its queue is heap-allocated and its loop is flat. Kahn's algorithm — same. The low-link SCC and articulation algorithms — recursive as written, and converted the same way (the postorder update "low[u] ← min(low[u], low[v])" happens at the moment v is popped, when u is again on top).

Where the depth comes from, and why it is not rare

It is tempting to think a million-deep recursion needs a contrived input. It does not. A linked list is a path; a file system with a deep directory chain, a long-running commit history with no branches, a chain of dependent tasks, a road that runs across a country, a polymer, a time series modelled as a graph — all are paths or nearly so. A grid is worse in a subtler way: with any fixed neighbour order, DFS explores a grid as a space-filling snake, and a 1 000 × 1 000 image is a million-deep recursion. Random sparse graphs, as the figure of §25 shows, have DFS trees whose depth is a large constant fraction of V. Only genuinely tree-like, well-balanced inputs keep the depth logarithmic, and a graph algorithm rarely gets to assume its input is balanced. The rule is therefore simple: a DFS that may be run on a large graph is written with an explicit stack, and the recursive version is kept for the proofs and for small inputs.

Tail calls do not help. DFS is not tail-recursive — after the call on v returns, u must continue its loop and then finish — so no tail-call optimisation removes the frame. The recursion page explains which shapes can be eliminated and which cannot; DFS is squarely in the "cannot" class, which is why the conversion has to be done by hand.

27 Union-find, named: the dynamic-connectivity structure that belongs to the MST part

Twice on this page a second algorithm answered a connectivity question by processing edges one at a time — the union-find check in §19's figure, and the "incremental" column of §19's discussion — and both times the structure was used without being built. Union-find (the disjoint-set forest) maintains a partition of V under two operations: find(x), which returns a canonical representative of x's set, and union(x, y), which merges two sets. Feeding it the edges of a graph one by one makes it answer "are u and v connected yet?" between insertions, which a traversal cannot do without re-running. Its construction, the two heuristics and the inverse-Ackermann amortised analysis are the business of the Algorithms series' shortest-paths-and-MST part, where it is Kruskal's inner loop; this section says only what it is, what it costs, and why it is not this page's.

the interfacemakeSet(x): x alone. find(x): the representative of x's set — two vertices are in the same set iff find(u) = find(v). union(x, y): merge the sets of x and y (no effect if already one). the representation, in one lineeach set is a tree of its members stored as a parent array, the root being the representative; find climbs to the root; union links one root under the other. With UNION BY SIZE/RANK (link the smaller under the larger) and PATH COMPRESSION (re-point every node visited by find straight at the root), any m operations on n elements cost O(m · α(n)), α the inverse Ackermann function, ≤ 4 for every n that fits in a computer. what it answers on this page's questionsconnected components of an undirected graph: union over all edges; count = V − successful unions; same result as §19's traversal (checked on 300 random graphs there), at near-linear cost, with the bonus of answering between insertions. cycle detection in an undirected graph, incrementally: an edge {u, v} with find(u) = find(v) closes a cycle — the test Kruskal's algorithm makes for every candidate edge. what it cannot doedge DELETION: a union cannot be undone; "are u and v still connected after removing this edge" needs the offline trick (process deletions backwards as insertions) or a fully dynamic connectivity structure. Directed reachability: union-find is symmetric; it says nothing about strong components. Distances: nothing.

The reason the structure is not built here is that this page's questions are all static — the graph is given and traversed — and for a static graph the traversal of §19 is simpler, exact and linear. Union-find earns its place when the edge set is being discovered, and the canonical place that happens is Kruskal's algorithm: sort the edges by weight, walk them in order, and accept each one whose endpoints are not yet connected. That loop makes E find-queries and V − 1 unions, and the near-constant amortised cost per operation is exactly what makes the algorithm O(E log E) — dominated by the sort — rather than O(E · V). The structure, its heuristics, the potential-function analysis and the reason the bound is not quite constant are on the Algorithm Design & Analysis hub's "Shortest Paths, MST & Flow" card; the amortised vocabulary it uses is Algorithm Analysis §18.

Traversal or union-find? Static graph, one pass: traverse. Edges arriving with queries in between, or an algorithm that selects edges one at a time (Kruskal, percolation, image segmentation by merging): union-find. Edges leaving: neither.

28 Weighted graphs: what changes, and where Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, MST and flow live

Everything on this page treated an edge as a unit step. A weighted graph attaches a number w(u, v) to each edge, and the length of a path becomes the sum of its weights rather than its edge count. That single change invalidates BFS's shortest-path theorem (§12) — the first-discovered path is no longer the shortest — and none of the representations change at all: a weight is one more field per adjacency entry, one more parallel array in CSR, or the cell's value in a matrix. This section is a hand-off: what each of the weighted problems is for, and which frontier discipline of §10 solves it, with the algorithms themselves left to the part that owns them.

what a weight changeslength(path) = ∑ w over its edges; δ(s, v) = min over paths. BFS computes this only when every w is equal (then δ = w · edge count). representation: adj[u] holds (v, w) pairs; CSR gains a weights[] array aligned with targets[]; matrix cells hold w with ∞ for "no edge". Costs unchanged. the weighted analogue of BFS is a frontier ordered by TENTATIVE DISTANCE instead of by arrival — the priority-queue row of §10's table — and a vertex's distance may be LOWERED after discovery (relaxation), which BFS never does the problems, and what each is forSINGLE-SOURCE SHORTEST PATHS, non-negative weights — Dijkstra's algorithm: BFS with a min-priority queue keyed by dist; a vertex is final when extracted. Routing, navigation, "cheapest way from here". Needs w ≥ 0: one negative edge breaks the finality invariant. O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap — what the heap buys it, and why the Fibonacci heap's better bound rarely wins. SINGLE-SOURCE with NEGATIVE weights — Bellman–Ford: V − 1 rounds of relaxing every edge (the edge-list loop of §06); detects a negative cycle if a V-th round still improves something. Arbitrage, constraint systems, the correctness backstop. O(V · E). SHORTEST PATHS IN A DAG — one relaxation pass in topological order (§20), any weights, no cycles to worry about. Θ(V + E): the cheapest shortest-path algorithm there is, and the longest-path / critical-path computation is the same pass with max. ALL PAIRS — Floyd–Warshall on the matrix, Θ(V³), the recurrence dk[i][j] = min(dk−1[i][j], dk−1[i][k] + dk−1[k][j]); its boolean form is the transitive closure this page used to check SCCs (§22). Or V runs of Dijkstra for sparse graphs. SINGLE TARGET with a heuristic — A*: Dijkstra with the key dist + h(v), h an admissible (never over-estimating) estimate of the remaining distance; explores far fewer states on the implicit graphs of §09. Route planning, puzzle solving, motion planning. MINIMUM SPANNING TREE — the cheapest edge set connecting everything: Kruskal (sorted edges + union-find, §27) or Prim (BFS with a priority queue keyed by the edge weight to the tree — Dijkstra's skeleton with a different key). Network design, clustering (cut the MST's heaviest edges), approximation algorithms. MAXIMUM FLOW / MINIMUM CUT — how much can be pushed from s to t through capacitated edges, and the bottleneck that limits it: augmenting paths found by BFS on the residual graph (Edmonds–Karp), or push–relabel. Bipartite matching (§14 supplies the sides), assignment, scheduling, image segmentation, network reliability. where they areall of the above — with proofs, traces and measured costs — belong to the Algorithms series part Shortest Paths, MST & Flow, reached from the hub's map. This page's contribution is the graph they run on and the traversals they are built from.

Why the hand-off is here and not earlier

Every weighted algorithm above is one of this page's traversals with a different frontier. Dijkstra is BFS with the queue replaced by a heap and the assignment dist[v] = dist[u] + 1 replaced by the relaxation dist[v] = min(dist[v], dist[u] + w); Prim is the same loop keyed by a different quantity; DAG shortest paths is a topological sort followed by one relaxation sweep; Bellman–Ford is the edge-list loop that §12 used as a brute-force check, run with real weights; Edmonds–Karp is BFS on a graph derived from the original. Reading them without this page means learning the traversal and the weight logic at once; reading them after it means learning only the weight logic. The single conceptual step is the one the §12 proof isolated — with unit weights the frontier's arrival order is the distance order, and with general weights it is not, so the frontier must be ordered explicitly and distances must be allowed to improve.

Before reaching for any of them. If the weights are all equal, it is BFS. If the graph is a DAG, it is one topological pass. If only one path is needed and a good estimate of remaining distance exists, it is A*. Dijkstra is the general non-negative case, Bellman–Ford the general case with negatives, and each costs more than the one before; the cheapest applicable algorithm is decided by the graph's properties, which this page's tools — unit weights?, acyclic? (§18), connected? (§19) — establish first.

29 Where graphs go in machine learning: message passing, knowledge graphs, proximity graphs, computation graphs

Four of this site's other domains are built on the object this page defines, and each uses a different one of its representations and traversals. Naming the correspondence makes those pages easier to read: a "neighbourhood aggregation" is an adjacency-list scan, a "normalised adjacency matrix" is §04's matrix with weights, a "k-hop neighbourhood" is a BFS layer, and "backpropagation order" is a reverse topological order.

PROXIMITY GRAPHS FOR SEARCH — the HNSW index is a layered graph whose edges join nearby vectors; a query is a GREEDY traversal (always move to the neighbour closest to the query, a best-first search with a bounded frontier), and the layers are BFS-like coarse-to-fine levels. Vector Databases (HNSW). COMPUTATION GRAPHS — a DAG of operations; the forward pass evaluates in topological order, the backward pass in REVERSE topological order (each gradient needs all downstream gradients first — §21); dead-code elimination is reachability from the outputs. Autodiff frameworks record the DAG and sort it. PROBABILISTIC GRAPHICAL MODELS — a DAG (Bayesian network) or an undirected graph (Markov random field) over random variables; d-separation is a reachability question with edge-direction rules; inference on a tree is a two-pass traversal (belief propagation), and on a general graph is hard for exactly the reason cycles are hard. RANKING BY RANDOM WALK — PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random walk on the web digraph: a power iteration with the column-normalised adjacency matrix, one adjacency-list sweep per iteration. What transfers, and what does not

What transfers is the representation and the cost model. A GNN library's "sparse tensor" is CSR (or the COO edge list of §06, converted); its "scatter-add over edges" is the adjacency scan; its memory is Θ(V·d + E·d) per layer and its time the same, so a graph with a few hubs of enormous degree costs what the handshake lemma says it costs. The neighbourhood a k-layer network can see is exactly the set BFS reaches in k layers, and on a sparse random graph with small distances (§02) that set is most of the graph within a handful of layers — the "over-smoothing" problem of deep GNNs is, in this page's terms, the observation that BFS layers on such graphs grow to cover everything, so that every vertex aggregates nearly the same set. What does not transfer is the notion of "shortest path": a message-passing network does not compute distances, and an attention weight on an edge is not a length. The weighted-graph tools of §28 are a different toolbox, and the ML pages that need real path lengths (route prediction, graph-distance features) call them as pre-processing rather than learning them.

The same object under every name. "Sparse tensor", "adjacency structure", "triple store", "proximity index", "computation graph", "factor graph" — each is an adjacency representation from §04–§07 with a specialised access pattern, and each one's cost is a §12-style aggregate over the adjacency lists. Read the representation first and the model second.
