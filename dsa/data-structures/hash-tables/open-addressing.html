13 Open addressing: go and find another slot

The second collision policy refuses the auxiliary structure entirely. Every entry lives in the bucket array itself, one entry per slot. If a key's first slot is taken, the key does not get a list — it gets a probe sequence, an ordered list of alternative slots to try, determined by the key. Insertion walks the sequence until it finds an empty slot; search walks the same sequence until it finds the key or an empty slot. That last clause is the entire design: an empty slot means "not here", and it is the only thing that stops a search. Everything difficult about open addressing, and all of §19, is a consequence of that one sentence.

Why the probe sequence must be a permutation

If the sequence repeats a slot or omits one, a key can be turned away from a table that still has empty slots. That is not merely inefficient, it is a correctness failure of the container: put reports "full" when it is not. Every scheme in the next three sections is therefore judged first on whether it generates a permutation and only then on how fast it is. Linear probing generates a permutation unconditionally, double hashing whenever gcd(h₂(k), m) = 1; plain quadratic probing does not, and §15 is largely about what to do about that. Note the subtlety in the insert loop above: it also stops when it finds the key itself, which is what enforces the map's uniqueness invariant, and it must do that before deciding the key is absent — a probe sequence that found an occupied non-matching slot has learned nothing and must continue.

What open addressing buys

  • No per-entry allocation and no link words. The entire structure is one array. For an 8-byte key and an 8-byte value at α = 0.5, open addressing uses 32 bytes per stored entry; chaining uses 16 bytes of array slot plus a node of key, value, link and allocator overhead — typically 40 or more bytes per entry, plus a second dereference to reach any of it.
  • Locality. A linear-probing lookup reads consecutive slots, which is the best possible access pattern for a cache: a probe sequence of four may be four reads inside one 64-byte cache line, costing one memory transaction rather than four. This is the property that outweighs everything else in practice at low load factors, and it is specific to linear probing — double hashing's probes are scattered and pay a miss each, which is a real and often-forgotten cost of its better probe count.
  • Predictable memory. No allocator on the hot path means no allocation failure mid-insert, no fragmentation, and a footprint you can compute from m alone. That matters in embedded and real-time settings more than any asymptotic argument.

What it costs

  • A hard ceiling at α = 1, and a soft one well below. Cost is hyperbolic in α, so the table must be resized earlier and kept emptier than a chained one. The memory saved per entry is partly given back as unused slots.
  • Deletion is genuinely hard. This is the dominant practical objection, and it is not a small implementation wrinkle — it changes the cost model. Once a table uses deletion markers, the probe-count theorems of §18 no longer apply, because search cost stops depending on α and starts depending on the history of insertions and deletions. A table that has churned can be slow while looking almost empty.
  • Clustering. Occupied slots are not independent: they gang up into runs, and the runs make themselves longer. §14 makes that mechanism visible and §17 compares its severity across the three schemes.
The rule of thumb, with its condition attached. Open addressing wins when entries are small, deletions are rare or absent, and the load factor is kept modest; chaining wins when entries are large, deletions are frequent, or the load factor must be allowed to climb. The two are not ranked — they are suited to different workloads, and the deletion question decides it more often than the speed question does.

14 Linear probing and primary clustering

The simplest probe sequence is "try the next slot": h(k,i) = (h′(k) + i) mod m. It is a permutation for every m and every h′ with no conditions at all, it needs no second hash function, and its memory access pattern is a forward scan, which is the fastest thing a processor can do. It also has the worst clustering behaviour of any scheme here, for a reason that is precise and worth understanding rather than memorising: an empty slot preceded by a run of i occupied slots is (i+1)/m likely to be filled next, so long runs grow faster than short ones and the distribution of run lengths becomes heavy-tailed.

Where the schemes separate unmistakably is the all keys hashing near slot 4 order, which makes every one of the ten keys land on home slot 4. Linear probing fills slots 4 through 2 in order, paying 1+2+3+⋯+10 = 55 probes for ten insertions — a mean of 5.5 in a table of eleven, and the purest possible picture of primary clustering. Double hashing places the same ten keys in 19 probes, because each key's step h₂(k) differs and the ten probe sequences diverge immediately. Quadratic probing does something worse than either: it spends 65 probes and places only six of the ten keys, refusing four of them while five slots are still free. That is not a bug in the figure; it is the defining limitation of quadratic probing, and §15 is about it.

Why the two linear-probing formulas are different from everything else

The formulas quoted above are not the uniform-hashing formulas of §18, and substituting one for the other is the classic error this page is most at risk of. Uniform hashing assumes each key gets an independent random permutation; linear probing gives each key a fixed scan from a random start, so the occupancies of adjacent slots are strongly positively correlated and the independence the uniform-hashing proof uses is simply absent. The consequence is quantitative and large: at α = 0.9 uniform hashing predicts 10 probes for an unsuccessful search and linear probing costs about 50. The successful-search figures are much closer (2.56 against 5.5), because a successful search stops at the key rather than at the end of a run. Both formulas are asymptotic in m, and the approach to them is slow: the figure in §18 measures 42.9 probes at m = 1009 against the predicted 50.5, and 50.1 at m = 8009. The figure in §18 uses tables of several thousand slots for exactly this reason.

What rescues linear probing in practice

Given all of that, linear probing remains extremely widely used, and the reason is the one asymptotics cannot see. Its probes are sequential memory reads. A run of eight 16-byte entries occupies two cache lines, so eight probes can cost two memory transactions where double hashing's eight scattered probes cost eight. If a probe is counted as "a memory access" the comparison favours double hashing; if it is counted as "a cache miss" — which is what actually takes time — linear probing wins at moderate load and keeps winning until the runs get long. The engineering conclusion is a conditional, not a ranking: linear probing below about α = 0.5 is usually the fastest scheme in wall-clock terms, and above about 0.7 it degrades faster than anything else on this page. (That is a statement about time, not probes — the one place this page steps outside §01's probe-count cost model, and it rests on the cache argument above, not on the formulas.) Two further repairs change the picture again — Robin Hood reordering, which evens out the run occupancy without changing the probe pattern, and a strong hash, which is what keeps the starting slots genuinely uniform in the first place.

15 Quadratic probing, secondary clustering, and the half-table condition

Quadratic probing keeps linear probing's single hash function but accelerates away from the home slot: h(k,i) = (h′(k) + c₁·i + c₂·i²) mod m. The acceleration is what defeats primary clustering — two keys with different home slots separate after one step instead of queueing — and it costs two things. Keys with the same home slot still follow an identical sequence, which is secondary clustering; and, in the plain form c₁ = 0, c₂ = 1, the probe sequence is not a permutation of the table. For m an odd prime it reaches exactly ceil(m/2) slots and no more. A prime is the guaranteed case, not the best one: for composite m the reachable count is erratic — a few composites do slightly better (i² mod 6 takes four values, i² mod 10 six) but many do far worse (i² mod 16 takes only four values, not eight; i² mod 12 also four) — which is why plain quadratic probing is quoted with a prime modulus or not at all. Either way the scheme carries a load-factor condition that the other two do not.

What the half-table condition actually says — and what it does not

This is a boundary worth stating carefully, because it is routinely reported as both stronger and weaker than it is. The theorem is a guarantee, not a description of typical behaviour: below α = 1/2, insertion cannot fail; at or above it, insertion may fail, and whether it does depends on which slots happen to be occupied. Those are different claims and both halves matter. The figure of §14 demonstrates the failure concretely: eleven slots, ten keys all with home slot 4, and the plain quadratic sequence reaches only the six slots {4, 5, 8, 2, 9, 7} — that is ceil(11/2) = 6, exactly as the theorem predicts — so the seventh key onward is refused while five slots sit empty. But the same scheme fed random keys fails far less readily, because a failure needs every one of the ceil(m/2) reachable slots to be occupied simultaneously, which random occupancy makes very unlikely until the table is nearly full. The sweep in §17 measures the failure count directly at each load factor, so the gap between "not guaranteed" and "actually fails" is a number rather than an impression.

Secondary clustering, and why it is the milder disease

Secondary clustering is the residue that quadratic probing cannot remove: two keys that land on the same home slot are condemned to the same probe sequence, so they collide at every step, not just the first. Compare the two mechanisms directly. Primary clustering merges keys with different home slots into shared runs, so the number of keys competing for a slot grows with the size of the run, which grows with the table's history. Secondary clustering only affects keys with identical home slots, and the number of those is governed by the hash function and the load factor, not by the table's shape. That is why the damage is bounded in a way primary clustering's is not: under a good hash, only about α keys share any given home slot on average, whereas a primary cluster can be tens of slots long. The measured probe counts in §18 put numbers on "milder": at α = 0.9 an unsuccessful search costs about 50 probes under linear probing and about 11 under quadratic.

The practical verdict

Quadratic probing occupies an awkward middle position and is, in current practice, the least used of the three. It needs only one hash function (cheaper than double hashing), it removes primary clustering (better than linear probing), and it keeps some locality for the first few probes since the offsets 1, 4, 9 are small. Against that: the reachability condition forces a low load factor or a non-obvious variant, the parameters c₁, c₂ and m are coupled and easy to choose wrongly, and its probes scatter after the first few, so it loses linear probing's cache advantage without gaining double hashing's probe-count advantage. If you want the middle ground in practice, the modern answer is usually Robin Hood linear probing rather than a quadratic sequence.

16 Double hashing and the coprimality requirement

Double hashing makes the step depend on the key as well as the start: h(k,i) = (h₁(k) + i·h₂(k)) mod m. Two keys now differ in where they begin and in how far they jump, so two keys that collide on their first probe almost certainly diverge on the second — there is neither primary nor secondary clustering. It generates about m² distinct probe sequences against the other schemes' m, which is why its measured behaviour tracks the idealised uniform-hashing model so closely. The price is a second hash computation per key, scattered memory accesses, and one hard requirement that is easy to violate and fails silently: h₂(k) must be coprime with m.

Note what makes this dangerous: nothing announces it. The table is not full, no assertion fires, and the symptom is an insertion that loops m times and then reports failure — or, in a carelessly written loop that terminates on i < m without checking, an infinite loop or a silently dropped entry. And it is not a rare accident: if m is even and h₂ is roughly uniform, half the keys get an even step and are affected. This is precisely why the two standard recipes exist and why they are stated as recipes — "m prime with h₂ < m" and "m a power of two with h₂ odd" are the two combinations that make d = 1 structurally impossible to violate.

Where double hashing stands

  • It is the closest practical approximation to uniform hashing. With Θ(m²) sequences instead of m, its measured probe counts sit almost exactly on the 1/(1−α) and (1/α)·ln(1/(1−α)) curves, which §18 verifies. Neither of the other two schemes does.
  • It tolerates a higher load factor than the others. Because the curve it follows is the gentlest of the three, it remains usable at loads where linear probing has collapsed.
  • It pays for that in cache behaviour. Each probe is a jump of h₂(k) slots, which at any realistic table size is a different cache line and often a different page. Five probes means five misses. Linear probing's five probes may mean one. Counting probes ranks double hashing first; counting memory transactions often does not.
  • It costs a second hash. Usually cheap — the second hash can be derived from spare bits of the first, provided the derivation is guaranteed to produce a value coprime with m, which forcing the low bit to 1 does for a power-of-two table.

17 Clustering, compared and measured

The three probe schemes are usually ranked by an adjective — linear probing "clusters badly", quadratic "clusters mildly", double hashing "does not cluster". Those are claims about a measurable quantity: the lengths of the maximal runs of consecutive occupied slots. This section measures them on one key set at one load factor, against a baseline that makes the comparison meaningful — what the runs would look like if the occupied slots were simply a uniformly random subset of the table, which is what a scheme with no clustering at all would produce.

What the measurement establishes

  • Double hashing genuinely does not cluster. Its mean run length sits on the random-subset baseline at every load factor tested — 2.00 against 2.00, 3.34 against 3.33, 19.90 against 20.00. That is the quantitative content of "no clustering": the set of occupied slots is statistically indistinguishable from a random subset of the same size, so the run structure carries no information about which scheme produced it.
  • Linear probing clusters, by a factor that grows with α. Its mean run is 27% above baseline at α = 0.5, 36% above at 0.7, 47% at 0.85, 54% at 0.95. The excess is modest at low load and compounds as the table fills, which is exactly the rich-get-richer mechanism of §14 expressed as a number.
  • Quadratic probing sits between, closer to double hashing. Its residual excess is secondary clustering, and the measurement confirms it is the milder disease: 14% above baseline at α = 0.5 against linear probing's 27%, and 5% at 0.95 against 54%.
  • The maximum run separates the schemes far more than the mean does. At α = 0.95 the means are 30.7 / 20.9 / 19.9 — a factor of 1.54 across the three — while the longest runs observed are 764 / 284 / 159, a factor of 4.8. Clustering is a tail phenomenon, and a comparison that only reports means understates it badly. This is the same lesson as the maximum-bucket discussion of §12, arriving from the other direction.

The consequence for cost, stated carefully

It is tempting to convert cluster lengths directly into probe counts, and the conversion is not quite that simple. A search's cost is not the length of the run it lands in; it is the distance from its home slot to the end of the run, which is on average about half the run for a uniformly placed home — and because longer runs are also more likely to be landed in, the expected cost involves the second moment of the run-length distribution, not the mean. That is the technical reason linear probing's unsuccessful-search cost contains 1/(1−α)² where uniform hashing has 1/(1−α): squaring is what a second moment does. It is also why the long tail of the run distribution matters so much more than its mean, and why the three schemes' probe counts diverge faster than their mean run lengths do.

18 The probe-count theorems, each with its own hypothesis

Six formulas are in circulation for open-addressed probe counts — three groups of two, a successful and an unsuccessful figure in each. They look interchangeable and are not: they are proved under different hypotheses about the probe sequence, they apply to different schemes, and substituting one for another produces errors of a factor of five at high load. This section states each with its hypothesis attached, then measures all of them at once so the differences are visible rather than argued about.

Proof of the Group A unsuccessful bound

It is short, and seeing it makes clear exactly which hypothesis is doing the work. Let X be the number of probes in an unsuccessful search. Every probe but the last hits an occupied slot. Under H2, the first probe hits an occupied slot with probability n/m; given that the first j−1 probes hit occupied slots, the j-th lands uniformly among the m − (j−1) slots not yet examined, of which n − (j−1) are occupied. So:

the tail probabilityPr[X ≥ i] = (n/m)·((n−1)/(m−1))·((n−2)/(m−2))⋯((n−i+2)/(m−i+2)) each factor is at most n/m, since (n−j)/(m−j) ≤ n/m whenever n ≤ mPr[X ≥ i] ≤ (n/m)^(i−1) = α^(i−1) and E[X] = ∑ over i ≥ 1 of Pr[X ≥ i]E[X] ≤ ∑ over i ≥ 1 of α^(i−1) = ∑ over i ≥ 0 of α^i = 1/(1 − α) ∎ the intuition the series carries1 + α + α² + α³ + ⋯ — always one probe, a second with probability ≈ α, a third with ≈ α², … boundary checksα = 0 (empty table): 1 probe exactly ✓ α → 1: the bound diverges, correctly ✓ α = 1 (full table): the geometric series does not converge; the real cost is m probes

The successful-search bound follows from the insert bound: a search for a key reproduces the probe sequence its insertion took, and if the key was the (i+1)-th inserted, that insertion faced load i/m and so cost at most m/(m−i) probes. Averaging over the n keys and bounding the resulting harmonic sum by an integral gives (1/α)·ln(1/(1−α)). Notice the second hypothesis that entered silently there: every stored key is equally likely to be the one sought. A workload that looks up recent keys far more often than old ones does better than the bound, because recent keys were inserted at lower load.

Each point is the mean over real searches on real tables; each curve is its formula evaluated independently. The separation is the whole message: at low load all six agree and the choice of scheme is irrelevant, and past about α = 0.7 the linear-probing series peels away from the other two and climbs. Turn the logarithmic axis off to see the scale of it — at α = 0.9 the unsuccessful linear curve is five times the uniform-hashing curve that people often quote for it. The m control exposes a second, quieter point: at the smaller table sizes the measured linear-probing points sit visibly below their curve, because the Group B formulas are asymptotic in m and converge slowly. At α = 0.9 the measured unsuccessful cost is 42.9 at m = 1009, 52.2 at m = 4001 and 50.1 at m = 8009, against a formula value of 50.5 — non-monotone, because at these table sizes the run-to-run spread is itself several probes wide. The convergence is much easier to see at α = 0.95, where the formula value is 200.5 and the measurement climbs towards it from far below: 95.8 at m = 509 (52% low), 102.5 at 1009 (49% low), 122.0 at 2003, 150.8 at 4001, and 222.4 at 8009 — which has finally overshot, by 11%. The magnitude of the gap falls monotonically, 52% → 49% → 39% → 25% → 11%, and its sign turns over on the way; what does not happen at any of these sizes is a clean landing on the curve. A formula matching a measurement is a claim about both, and the table size is part of the claim. The double-hashing points, by contrast, sit on their curve at every table size the control offers — which is the strongest evidence on the page that double hashing really does approximate uniform hashing.

How to hold the three groups apart

  • Match the hypothesis to the scheme, not to the formula you remember. If the scheme is double hashing, Group A is the right family and the measurement confirms it. If the scheme is linear probing, Group A is wrong and Group B is right. If the scheme is quadratic probing, Group C is right and is an approximation rather than a theorem.
  • Unsuccessful is always worse than successful, in every group. An unsuccessful search runs to an empty slot; a successful one stops at its key. Any formula pair in which the unsuccessful figure is the smaller one has been transcribed the wrong way round — the two Group B formulas are the pair most often swapped, because they differ only by a squared denominator.
  • An insertion costs what an unsuccessful search costs. Insertion must first establish that the key is absent, so it walks the same sequence to the same empty slot. This is why a table's build cost is governed by the unsuccessful formula, and why filling a linear-probing table to α = 0.9 is so expensive.
  • All of them assume no deletion markers. Every formula here is derived for a table built by insertions alone. §19 explains why a table that has seen deletions obeys none of them.

19 Deletion, the broken probe chain, and the tombstone

In a chained table, deletion unlinks a node and the structure is exactly as it would have been had the key never arrived. In an open-addressed table, you cannot simply empty the slot. A search stops at the first empty slot it meets, so emptying a slot in the middle of a probe path tells every key beyond it on that path that it is absent. The entry is still there, still occupying memory, and permanently unreachable. This is the single sharpest edge in the whole subject, it is easy to write and impossible to notice in testing until the table is dense enough for probe paths to be long, and the standard repair — a tombstone (the textbook name for the technique is lazy deletion) — fixes correctness at the cost of a cost model.

Why the tombstone changes the cost model, not just the cost

Every probe-count theorem in §18 is derived for a table built by insertions alone, where a slot is empty if and only if no key is stored there. Once tombstones exist, that equivalence breaks, and the quantity governing search cost is α_eff = (occupied + tombstones)/m. Two facts follow and both are unpleasant. First, α_eff is monotone non-decreasing between rebuilds: an insertion can consume a tombstone, but a deletion always creates one, so a workload with equal inserts and deletes drifts upward and never recovers. Second, the load factor you can measure — n/m — stops predicting anything. A cache holding a steady thousand entries in a table of ten thousand slots reports α = 0.1 and, after a few hundred thousand insert/delete cycles, can be probing dozens of slots per lookup because the other nine thousand are tombstones. The symptom is a table that gets slower over time at constant size, which is a mystery until you know to look for it.

The repairs, and what each costs

  • Rehash on a tombstone threshold. Track tombstones and rebuild the table when α_eff exceeds a bound, even if n has not grown — often into a table of the same size. This is the standard fix, it costs a Θ(m) pass, and it amortises exactly as a resize does (§20). The threshold must be on α_eff, not on n/m; a table that only resizes when n grows will never clean itself.
  • Backward-shift deletion, for linear probing only. Instead of a marker, shift subsequent entries in the run backwards into the hole, stopping when a key is reached whose home slot would be passed. This restores the no-tombstone invariant exactly and keeps the table's statistics identical to a never-deleted one — the cleanest solution available, at the cost of a deletion that is O(run length) rather than O(1), and of code that is genuinely fiddly to get right. It does not generalise to quadratic or double probing, because there is no "subsequent entry in the run" when probes jump.
  • Use chaining. The honest option. If a workload deletes heavily, the whole tombstone apparatus — the third slot state, the remembered-tombstone insert rule, the α_eff accounting, the cleanup threshold — is machinery that a chained table does not need. This is the single most common reason to choose chaining.
