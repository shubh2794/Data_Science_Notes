20 Resizing: when, by how much, and why it is free on average

Every cost result on this page is a statement about α = n/m, and n is chosen by the workload. Holding α below a threshold therefore requires growing m, and growing m means rehashing — every entry's slot is a function of m, so a new table size invalidates every stored position at once. There is no in-place repair and no incremental trick that avoids the work; the entire table must be re-placed. The reason this is affordable is the same reason a dynamic array is affordable: grow by a constant factor, and the total work over n insertions is Θ(n), so each insertion pays O(1) amortized even though one insertion in every batch pays Θ(m).

The amortized argument, and where to find it in full

The accounting is identical to the dynamic array's and is derived there rather than repeated here: see Algorithm Analysis §20 for the worked potential-function version, §18 for what "amortized" means as opposed to "average", and §19 for the aggregate, accounting and potential methods side by side. The shape of the argument: charge each insertion a constant number of extra "credits"; when a resize happens, the credits accumulated since the previous resize exactly pay for moving the entries. With g = 2, an insertion that triggers a resize at size m moves n ≈ α_max·m entries, and the previous resize fired when the m/2-slot table itself reached load α_max, at α_max·m/2 entries — so α_max·m/2 insertions have occurred since, and two credits each is exactly the α_max·m entries moved. Everything else follows.

The two additions that are specific to hash tables

  • Tombstones must be counted in the resize trigger. A table that grows only on n/m will never rebuild under a balanced insert/delete workload, and will fill with tombstones until every lookup scans a long path (§19). The trigger must be on α_eff = (n + tombstones)/m, and the "resize" it fires may legitimately keep m the same — a same-size rebuild that drops the tombstones is the correct response when n itself has not grown.
  • The new size is not always 2m. A prime-modulus table must jump to the next prime at least 2m, which is why implementations of that style carry a table of primes. A power-of-two table simply shifts. Either way the factor is what the amortized argument needs; the exact value only affects the constant.
Per-insertion cost through a table's growth, and the running average the spikes are real rehashes, counted; the flat line is their running mean, which is what "amortized O(1)" means

The spikes are the rehashes and their height is the number of entries moved, counted by the routine as it moves them. The running-average curve is the total work divided by the number of insertions so far, and its behaviour carries the point: it sawtooths early, when a single rehash is a large fraction of all work done, and then settles into a narrow band. It does not converge to a single value, and that is worth being precise about — the familiar constant g/(g−1) is the value the mean takes just after a rehash, and between rehashes the numerator is frozen while n keeps growing, so the mean slides down to 1/(g−1) before the next spike lifts it again. At the default g = 2 the band is [1.00, 2.00] moves per insertion, and the figure measures 1.535 inside it. The exact cross-check is stronger than the band: the total number of entries moved is compared against the count the resize schedule alone predicts, computed without touching the table, and the two agree to the unit — 6 141 against 6 141 at the default settings.

Drag g down to 1.25 and the measured moves per insertion rise to 4.96 in a band of [4.00, 5.00], with 27 rehashes instead of 9 — a smaller growth factor means rehashing more often, and the cost is exactly the g/(g−1) the formula predicts. Drag it up to 4 and the moves fall to 1.02 in a band of [0.33, 1.33], at the cost of a table that is far emptier on average and which ended at m = 16 384 for the same 4 000 entries rather than 8 192. Neither end is wrong; the slider is the memory-versus-time dial, and it is one of the few places in this subject where the trade is completely explicit. Note also that the probe cost moves with it in the opposite direction — 2.55 probes per insertion at g = 4 against 5.26 at g = 1.25 — because a larger growth factor keeps the table emptier, so the two effects partly cancel and the total-work curve is flatter than either component.

What amortization does not buy

The tallest spike in the figure is the cost of the last rehash, and it is Θ(m). Amortized O(1) says the total is linear; it says nothing about any individual operation, and the individual operation that triggers a rehash of a million-entry table will take milliseconds. For a batch program that is invisible. For an interactive system, a game loop, an audio callback or anything with a latency budget, it is a stall — and it arrives at the least convenient moment, since the table rehashes precisely when it is busiest. That is the entire motivation for §21. It is also a general lesson worth carrying beyond hash tables: amortized analysis is a statement about throughput, and it is silent about tail latency by construction.

21 Incremental and lazy rehashing

The stop-the-world rehash of §20 is cheap on average and terrible at the tail. The standard repair is to spread the migration across the operations that follow it: allocate the new table, keep the old one alive, and move a bounded number of entries on every subsequent operation until the old table is empty. Nothing is saved — the total work is identical — but the Θ(m) spike becomes m/k operations each doing O(k) extra work, which converts a latency problem into a modest, uniform slowdown.

Why k has a lower bound, and what happens below it

This is the detail most often left out, and it is the one that decides whether the scheme works. Migration must finish before the new table itself needs to grow, or the structure ends up with three live tables and then four. Between two resizes the table absorbs about (1 − 1/g)·α_max·m′ = (g−1)·α_max·m new insertions; migration must move about α_max·m entries in that time, and each insertion moves k. Dividing, the α_max cancels and the requirement is simply k ≥ 1/(g−1) — the load-factor threshold does not enter it at all, which is worth noticing because it is often quoted as though it did. With doubling that is k ≥ 1, and the awkward part is that it is an equality: at k = 1 the migration finishes at exactly the moment the next resize fires, with no margin for the rounding, the deletions and the resizes-of-a-resize that a real implementation has. Count the same budget in slots scanned rather than entries moved — which is what the cursor actually does, since it must walk the empty slots too — and the requirement becomes k ≥ 1/((g−1)·α_max) = 1.33. So moving two per operation is safe and moving one is not, and "one per operation" is exactly the value a first implementation reaches for. The failure it produces is slow and confusing: the table appears to work, memory use creeps, and lookups get gradually slower because they are searching an ever-growing stack of half-migrated tables.

Lazy variants, and where each is used

  • Migrate on touch. Instead of a cursor, move an entry only when an operation happens to find it in the old table. Simple and self-tuning for skewed workloads, but entries that are never touched are never migrated, so the old table can persist indefinitely — and the memory it holds with it. Usually combined with a cursor to guarantee completion.
  • Generational / two-table lookup. Formalise the pair as a permanent design: a small "new" table absorbing writes and a large "old" one being drained. Lookups check both. This is the same idea as the memory-table-plus-file layering in a log-structured store, and it has the same trade — cheap writes, more expensive reads.
  • Chunked rehash on a background thread. Moves the work off the critical path entirely, at the price of needing every operation to be safe against concurrent migration. This is a concurrency problem rather than a data-structure one, and it is where most of the difficulty of concurrent hash tables lives.
  • Don't resize at all. If the maximum entry count is known ahead of time, size the table once, for that count at the target load factor, and the entire section is unnecessary. This is the correct answer far more often than it is used, and it is the only one that removes the Θ(m) rehash stall entirely. It does not, of course, touch the Θ(n) adversarial worst case of §09 — a probe path is bounded by the table's contents, not by its size policy.

The workload is deliberately boring: the table holds a constant number of entries, and each step inserts one new key and deletes one old one, so n never changes — the readout verifies that it did not — and the reported load factor n/m is pinned at 0.30 from the first cycle to the last. Under the default policy the measured cost of an unsuccessful lookup nevertheless climbs from 1.54 probes to 197, a factor of 127, because α_eff rises from 0.30 to 0.996 while α stands still. The second curve shows it doing so. The unsuccessful case is the one to watch and the figure measures it deliberately: a successful lookup stops at its key and is barely harmed by tombstones, so measuring that instead would have hidden the entire effect — and an insertion, which must first establish that the key is absent, pays the unsuccessful cost too.

Switch to rebuild on a threshold and the same run ends at 2.42 probes after six rebuilds, each of which drops every tombstone and returns the cost to its floor; the curve becomes a bounded sawtooth instead of a climb. Switch to chaining and the cost is flat at about 0.3 probes — which is α itself, exactly as §10's unsuccessful-search theorem says — because a chained delete leaves nothing behind to step over. This is the measurement behind §19's claim that a churned open-addressed table can behave like a nearly full one while reporting that it is nearly empty, and it is the single strongest argument for chaining in a delete-heavy workload.

22 Universal hashing: moving the randomness out of the keys

Every bound so far has rested on a hypothesis about the keys — that they scatter like independent uniform draws. §09's pigeonhole argument says that hypothesis can always be falsified deliberately: for any fixed h, an adversary can find n keys that all collide. Universal hashing removes the problem at its root by choosing the hash function at random when the table is created, from a family designed so that the collision probability is bounded whatever the keys are. The randomness now lives in the algorithm rather than in the input, exactly as it does in randomised quicksort, and the guarantee stops being an assumption and becomes a theorem.

Why pairwise is enough

Universality is a strictly weaker property than simple uniform hashing: it constrains only pairs of keys, and says nothing about whether three keys land independently or whether the induced distribution on slots is anywhere near uniform. It would be reasonable to expect that to be too weak to recover the chaining bounds — and it is not, for a specific and instructive reason. Look back at the proof in §10: the only probabilistic fact it ever uses is E[Xᵢⱼ] = Pr[h(kᵢ) = h(kⱼ)] = 1/m for a pair, and then linearity of expectation does all the rest. Linearity of expectation does not require independence. So replacing the equality = 1/m by the inequality ≤ 1/m carries the entire argument through unchanged, with = becoming ≤ in the conclusion. That is why the pairwise definition is the right one: it is exactly as strong as the analysis needs and no stronger, which makes it cheap to satisfy.

One thing the figure deliberately does not claim. The dashed reference line is where fully independent uniform hashing would put the longest bucket — 7.17 here — and the measured mean sits below it, not on it. That is not a bug and it is not a bonus guarantee: universality constrains pairs of keys and says nothing whatever about the longest bucket, so the balls-in-bins result of §12 does not transfer to this setting and quoting it as a prediction would be importing a theorem past its hypothesis. The reason the measurement comes out better than independence is specific to these keys: an arithmetic progression stays an arithmetic progression under (a·k + b) mod p, and a progression reduced modulo m spreads more evenly than random draws do. What the figure does verify is the actual guarantee, and it is worth being careful about how, because this is exactly the place where a measurement can look like a refutation. The bound is on the expectation over the random draw of h; a finite sample of draws fluctuates either side of that expectation and carries no promise at all to land below 1/m. So the figure reports three numbers rather than one. The exact collision probability for this family follows from Step 2 of the proof above — the bijection makes (r, s) uniform over ordered distinct pairs, so the probability is just the fraction of such pairs that agree modulo m, which depends only on p and m and not on which keys are asked about. What universal hashing does and does not promise

  • It does not make the worst case go away. A particular draw can still be unlucky, and a table can still degrade. What changes is who chooses: bad performance now requires the algorithm's own coin flips to come up badly, which happens with small probability and independently of the input, rather than requiring an adversary to have a bad day.
  • It guarantees nothing if the draw is reused or leaked. The parameters must be drawn fresh — per process at least, and per table ideally — and must not be recoverable from observable behaviour. An attacker who can time lookups precisely enough can in principle learn about the hash, and the practical form of this arms race is §25.
  • The stronger relatives, and a distinction worth keeping. A family is k-independent (k-wise independent) if any k distinct keys are mapped independently and uniformly. Strongly 2-universal is the exact-uniform pairwise version, Pr[h(k)=i ∧ h(k′)=j] = 1/m², and is the same thing as 2-independence. Plain universal — sometimes written 2-universal, which is why the two get confused — is strictly weaker: it bounds only the collision probability and says nothing about the marginals. The family above is on the weak side of that line, and precisely so: (a·k + b) mod p alone is 2-independent over {0…p−1} when a ranges over all of {0…p−1} (with a ≠ 0, as the family above is defined, step 2 of the proof shows it is uniform over distinct pairs only — a pair can never land on the same residue), but the second fold mod m destroys it, because m does not divide p and so the m slots do not receive equal numbers of residues. What survives the fold is universality — the ≤ 1/m collision bound proved above — and that is all the chaining analysis needed. Some analyses — notably of linear probing — genuinely need more than pairwise independence: 5-independence is known to suffice for linear probing's expected constant cost, and some 2-independent families provably make it fail (expected cost Θ(log n) on adversarial key sets) — a real caveat on applying this section's guarantee to §14's scheme rather than to chaining.
  • It costs almost nothing. One integer multiplication, one addition and two remainders. The multiply-shift relative — odd a, take the top p bits of a·k mod 2^w — is a multiply and a shift, and is universal up to a factor of two (collision probability ≤ 2/m, "almost-universal") rather than ≤ 1/m. The same machinery is the MAD compression of §04, which is why that construction looked arbitrary there and does not here.

23 Perfect hashing: O(1) worst case, for a static key set

Every bound on this page has been an expectation, and the worst case has been Θ(n) throughout. There is one setting in which that can be fixed completely: when the key set is static — fixed, known in advance, never inserted into again. Reserved words in a compiler, opcodes in an interpreter, country codes, the field names of a wire format, the vertices of a fixed graph. For such a set you can afford to search for a hash function that collides on none of your keys, and then every lookup is one probe, guaranteed, with no hypothesis at all. The construction that makes this affordable in O(n) space is two-level, and it is a small masterpiece.

Why the squares are affordable

The construction looks as though it should explode. Each bucket's secondary table is quadratic in its own occupancy, and §12 established that the largest bucket at α = 1 holds Θ(log n / log log n) keys — so the biggest secondary table has tens of slots. The reason the total stays linear is that ∑nⱼ² counts something concrete: it is n plus twice the number of colliding pairs, and under a universal family the expected number of colliding pairs at m = n is only (n−1)/2. Most buckets hold zero or one key and contribute zero or one to the sum; the rare bucket holding five contributes 25, and there are few enough of those that the total still averages under 2n. Squaring a distribution that is overwhelmingly zeros and ones is cheap; it is only expensive if the distribution has mass in the tail, and Poisson(1) does not.

The two randomised searches, and the Markov argument that bounds them

Both levels use the same trick: compute the expectation, then use Markov's inequality — for a non-negative random variable, Pr[X ≥ t] ≤ E[X]/t — to argue that the realised value exceeds twice its expectation less than half the time, so redrawing succeeds after an expected two attempts. At the second level, the expected number of collisions in a table of size nⱼ² is under 1/2, so a draw is collision-free with probability over 1/2. At the first level, E[∑nⱼ²] < 2n, so Pr[∑nⱼ² ≥ 4n] < 1/2 and a draw gives linear total space with probability over 1/2. Both searches are Las Vegas: the answer is always correct and only the running time is random, which is exactly the property you want when the result is going to be baked into a program and used for years. Construction is therefore O(n) in expectation, and since it happens once, offline, even a much worse construction time would be acceptable.

Where this actually shows up

  • Compiler and interpreter keyword tables. The set of reserved words is fixed at language-design time, and generators that emit a perfect hash function for such a set are a standard build-time tool. The payoff is not only speed but determinism: no probe loop, no load factor, no resize, no adversary.
  • Minimal perfect hashing. A refinement that maps n keys onto exactly n slots with no gaps, giving an ideal-density lookup table. Modern constructions reach a few bits of auxiliary storage per key. This is the machinery behind compact static dictionaries, immutable key-value bundles shipped inside binaries, and index structures over fixed vocabularies.
  • The conceptual payoff. Perfect hashing is the proof that the Θ(n) worst case elsewhere on this page is not intrinsic to hashing — it is the price of supporting insertion. Once the key set stops changing, the price disappears. That framing is worth keeping: the dynamic-versus-static distinction buys more here than almost anywhere else in data structures.
