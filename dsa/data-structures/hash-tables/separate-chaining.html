09 Collisions are guaranteed — pigeonhole and the birthday bound

Two separate results say that collisions must be designed for rather than hoped away, and they say different things. The pigeonhole principle says collisions exist — for any fixed h, some slot receives many keys of the universe, and an adversary who knows h can find them. The birthday bound says collisions arrive early — even with a perfectly random hash and a table far larger than the number of keys, the first collision shows up after about √m insertions, not after m/2. The first result kills the hope of a collision-free fixed hash; the second kills the intuition that a sparse table is a collision-free one.

Pigeonhole: why no fixed hash function is safe

The counting argument is immediate — a function from a big set to a small one must repeat — but the useful version is the quantified one. With a universe of size |U| and m slots, some slot is the image of at least ceil(|U|/m) universe keys. For realistic universes this number is astronomically larger than any n you will ever store, which means that for any fixed hash function there exists a set of n keys that all land in one slot, turning every operation into a linear scan. This is not a hypothetical: it is the mechanism of a hash-flooding denial of service, in which an attacker submits form fields or JSON keys chosen to collide, and a server that expected O(n) total work does Θ(n²) instead (§25). The important structural point is that no analysis of the hash function fixes this, because the adversary gets to see the function and choose afterwards. The only repair is to make the function unknown to the adversary — randomly chosen per table or per process — which is exactly what universal hashing formalises.

The birthday bound: why sparse tables still collide

Assume the ideal — a perfectly random hash, hypothesis H1 in full. Insert keys one at a time. When the (i+1)-th key arrives, i slots are already spoken for, so it avoids all of them with probability (1 − i/m); multiplying over i = 1 … n−1 makes all n keys distinct with probability ∏(1 − i/m), which the bound 1 + x ≤ e^x compresses to e^(−n(n−1)/2m). Setting that to 1/2 gives n ≈ 1.1774·√m. The number to remember is √m, not m: a table of a million slots holding a mere twelve hundred keys — a load factor of 0.0012 — is already more likely than not to contain a collision. The pairs are what matter, and n keys make n(n−1)/2 pairs, so the pair count reaches m when n reaches roughly √(2m). Everything surprising about the birthday paradox is contained in the fact that people count keys and the mathematics counts pairs.

When does the first collision arrive? a Monte Carlo run of the real insertion process, against the exact expectation and the square-root approximation

Three independent numbers are printed and they agree: the simulated mean from running the real insertion loop until a slot repeats; the exact expectation ∑ₖ Pr[first k distinct] summed term by term with no approximation; and the closed form √(πm/2) + 2/3. Agreement of a Monte Carlo with an exact sum confirms the simulation; agreement of the exact sum with the closed form confirms the approximation. The histogram carries the part the mean hides — the distribution is broad and right-skewed, so "about √m" describes a typical run and individual runs vary by a factor of two either way. Push m to its maximum and watch the mean grow like √m, not like m: quadrupling the slots only doubles the wait.

The two results do not say the same thing

It is worth separating them, because they are often blurred into a single "collisions happen" and they have different consequences. Pigeonhole is adversarial and worst-case: it concerns a chosen key set, it applies with probability 1, and it is defeated only by randomising the hash function. The birthday bound is probabilistic and typical-case: it concerns random keys, it says nothing about an adversary, and it is not defeated by anything — randomising the hash does not make random keys collide any less. For table design the birthday bound is reassuring rather than alarming, because a collision in a hash table merely costs a probe; it is the fingerprinting job of §05, where a collision is a wrong answer, that has to size its output by 2^(b/2) and take the bound as a hard constraint.

10 Chaining: put the collisions in a list

The first collision policy is the obvious one. If two keys want slot j, let them both have it: slot j holds not an entry but a bucket — a container of all entries whose key hashes to j. The container is classically a singly linked list, whose node mechanics are exactly those set out in Linked Lists, Stacks & Queues, and which is where the name comes from. The scheme's virtues are that it is short, that it is correct without any special cases, that deletion is genuinely easy, and that n may exceed m without the structure breaking. Its cost is one pointer indirection per bucket and one allocation per entry.

The unsuccessful-search theorem, proved

Search for a key k that is not present. Under H1, k is equally likely to hash to any slot, so the bucket it lands in is a uniformly chosen one, and the search must walk that bucket to its end — an unsuccessful search cannot stop early, because any of the remaining entries could still be the one. The expected number of entries examined is therefore E[n_h(k)] = α, by definition of the mean bucket length. Adding the O(1) to compute the hash and index the array gives Θ(1 + α). The 1 + is not cosmetic: at α = 0 the cost is one hash computation and one array access, not zero, and writing the bound as Θ(α) would wrongly claim an empty table costs nothing.

The successful-search theorem, proved — and a detail worth getting right

Now search for a key that is present, each of the n stored keys equally likely. Suppose entries are prepended, so the entries lying in front of xᵢ in its bucket are exactly those inserted after it that happened to collide with it. Define the indicator Xᵢⱼ = 1 if h(kᵢ) = h(kⱼ); under H1, E[Xᵢⱼ] = 1/m. The number examined when searching for xᵢ is 1 + ∑ over j > i of Xᵢⱼ, so by linearity of expectation:

average over the n keysE = (1/n) · ∑ over i = 1 … n of ( 1 + ∑ over j = i+1 … n of 1/m ) = 1 + (1/nm) · ∑ over i = 1 … n of (n − i) = 1 + (1/nm) · n(n−1)/2 = 1 + (n − 1)/(2m) = 1 + α/2 − α/(2n) boundary check 1 — a single entry, n = 11 + 0/(2m) = 1 exactly. One comparison, no expectation involved. ✓ boundary check 2 — the degenerate table, m = 1 (all keys in one list)1 + (n−1)/2 = (n+1)/2, which is exactly the mean cost of a linear search over n items ✓ boundary check 3 — α = 0successful search is UNDEFINED at n = 0; the formula is stated for n ≥ 1 only

The detail worth getting right is that the prepending assumption does not affect the answer. If entries are appended to the tail instead, then the entries before xᵢ are those inserted before it, giving 1 + (i−1)/m for the i-th key; averaging over i gives 1 + (1/nm)·n(n−1)/2 — the identical sum, read backwards. What changes is the distribution across keys, not the mean: with prepending the most recently inserted keys are cheapest to find, with appending the oldest are. A table with strong recency in its access pattern therefore prefers prepending, and that is a real effect that the mean deliberately hides. The two boundary checks above are worth keeping because they catch the commonest algebra slip: at m = 1 the hash table is a linked list, and any formula that does not reduce to (n+1)/2 there has a mistake in it.

What chaining buys, itemised

  • Deletion is trivial and complete. Unlink the node and it is gone — no marker, no residue, no effect on any other key's search path. This single property is the reason chaining is the default recommendation whenever deletion is a normal part of the workload, and §19 shows in detail what open addressing has to do instead.
  • α > 1 is legal. The table degrades smoothly rather than failing: at α = 4 a search examines about four entries. There is no wall. Open addressing has a hard wall at α = 1 and a soft one well before it.
  • The bucket can be any map. Nothing requires a list. A bucket that grows past a threshold can be replaced by a balanced tree, converting the worst case for that bucket from Θ(nⱼ) to O(log nⱼ) — which is what "treeified buckets" means in §25, and it is a defence against adversarial collisions rather than an optimisation of the common case.
  • Insert is O(1) worst case — if you skip the uniqueness check. Prepending a node is two writes. But a map must not store a duplicate key, so a correct put searches the bucket first and therefore costs what a search costs. Implementations that advertise O(1) worst-case insertion are describing a multimap, or are assuming the caller has already checked.

What it costs, in memory and in cache

Every entry carries at least one link, and every entry is a separate allocation, so chaining pays the node overhead derived in Linked Lists §03 — a link word per entry, allocator grain rounding, and nodes that may land anywhere in the heap. A bucket walk is a pointer chase, so a chain of length 3 can mean three cache misses where an open-addressed table would have made one. This is the real reason open addressing is preferred in performance-critical libraries despite its harder deletion story: at low load factors an open-addressed lookup touches one or two cache lines and a chained one touches two or three, and at modern memory latencies that ratio is the whole performance difference. The asymptotics are identical; the constant is not.

11 The load factor α — what it controls and what it does not

α = n/m is the single number that summarises how hard a table is being pushed, and it appears in every cost formula on this page. It deserves a section of its own because it is routinely over-trusted: α controls the expected cost under a uniformity hypothesis, and it controls nothing at all about the worst case, the longest bucket, the variance, or what happens when the hypothesis fails. Two tables at the same α can differ by a factor of n in their slowest operation.

The two families have qualitatively different relationships with α

For chaining, cost is linear in α: doubling the load doubles the mean bucket walk, and there is no value of α at which anything special happens. For open addressing, cost is hyperbolic in α: the 1/(1−α) term is nearly flat until the table is about two-thirds full and then climbs without bound. The numbers make the asymmetry concrete — under uniform hashing (H2), which is the hypothesis that 1/(1−α) belongs to and which only double hashing comes close to satisfying — an unsuccessful search costs 2 probes at α = 0.5, 10 at 0.9, 100 at 0.99, and at α = 1 it does not terminate until it has scanned all m slots. Linear probing's own theorem is worse at every one of those loads (2.5, 50.5, 5 000.5), and quoting the 1/(1−α) row for it is the error §18 is built around. Chaining at those same loads costs 0.5, 0.9 and 0.99 entries examined. This is why the two families have different conventional thresholds (§25) and why "keep the load factor below 0.75" is meaningless advice until you say which family it is about.

Switch the range control to 3.0 and the difference becomes categorical rather than quantitative: the open-addressing curves simply stop at α = 1, because there is no such thing as an open-addressed table holding more entries than it has slots, while the chaining line continues unbothered at three entries per bucket. Chaining degrades; open addressing has a wall.

Why α says nothing about the longest bucket

This is the single most useful corrective in the section. Set m = n, so α = 1 and the mean bucket length is exactly 1. The maximum bucket length is not 1, and it does not stay bounded: under H1 it is Θ(log n / log log n) with high probability. The asymptotic shape is what that statement pins down; the actual expected maximum, computed from the bucket distribution rather than from the asymptotic form, is about 4.2 at a hundred entries, 5.5 at a thousand, 8.8 at a million and 11.6 at a billion. Slowly growing, but growing, and unbounded — and note that the bare log n / log log n expression evaluates to about 5.3 at a million, so it captures the growth rate and not the value, which is exactly what Θ claims and no more. The consequence for engineering is that a hash table's tail latency is not governed by its load factor. If your service has a p99.9 latency budget, the mean-cost theorems on this page do not speak to it; the maximum-load result does, and it says the tail creeps upward with n no matter how much memory you spend on slots. The figure in §12 measures the maximum alongside the mean so the gap between them is visible rather than theoretical.

Choosing a target

  • Chaining tolerates a high α. Thresholds near 0.75 to 1 are common, and analyses support holding α below about 0.9; past that the linear growth is still gentle but the memory saved is small compared with the node overhead you are already paying.
  • Open addressing needs a low one. Linear probing degrades fastest and is usually held below about 0.5; other probing schemes tolerate somewhat more, and implementations using thresholds around two-thirds are common. §18 measures where each curve turns up so the choice can be made from the graph rather than from a remembered constant.
  • The threshold is a memory-versus-time dial, and it is not linear on either axis. Going from α = 0.9 to 0.45 doubles the memory and, for linear probing, cuts the expected unsuccessful probe count from 50.5 to 2.15. Going from 0.45 to 0.225 doubles the memory again and takes it to 1.33 — a saving of 0.8 probes against the first halving's 48.3. Almost all of the benefit is in the first halving.
  • α must be maintained, not merely chosen. A table that is sized once and then filled has a load factor that rises to whatever the data dictates. Keeping α bounded requires resizing, which is §20.

12 What chaining really costs, measured

The two theorems of §10 predict means. This section runs the real structure and measures four quantities — the mean successful cost, the mean unsuccessful cost, the bucket-length distribution, and the maximum bucket — and compares each against an independently computed prediction. The purpose is not to confirm arithmetic that was already proved; it is to make visible how much of the story the mean leaves out, which is most of the story whenever you care about the slowest operation rather than the average one.

The distribution, not the mean

Throwing n keys independently into m slots makes each bucket's occupancy a Binomial(n, 1/m) variable, which for large m and fixed α is Poisson with parameter α. That single fact explains several things at once that are otherwise folklore. At α = 1 — the setting that sounds like "perfectly packed" — about e⁻¹ ≈ 37% of slots are empty and about 26% hold two or more keys. The array is not neatly one-per-slot and never will be; it is patchy, and the patchiness is intrinsic to randomness rather than a defect in the hash function. This is why a chained table sized m = n wastes about a third of its slot array, and it is a large part of why open addressing, which cannot tolerate that waste in the same way, runs at lower load factors.

A chained table, filled for real: distribution, mean and maximum every number is measured by building the table and running the searches; predictions are computed separately and printed beside them

The histogram is the measured bucket-length distribution and the overlaid curve is the Poisson prediction computed independently; they track each other closely, which is the confirmation that the simulated hash really is behaving like H1. The two mean-cost readouts come from running searches — one pass over every stored key for the successful figure, one pass over an equal number of absent keys for the unsuccessful one — and each is checked against its formula. The number to look at, though, is the gap between the mean and the maximum. At the default α = 1 with m = 1024, the mean successful search examines 1.50 entries — matching 1 + (n−1)/2m = 1.500 to three digits — while the longest bucket averages 5.8, close to the independently computed expectation of 5.5. That is a factor of nearly four between the typical operation and the worst one in a table whose hash function is behaving perfectly. Raise α and both grow, at different rates, and the ratio between them is the tail-latency risk that the Θ(1 + α) bound is silent about. Note also the sanity check the readout performs on the sum of the bucket lengths: it must equal n exactly, and if it ever did not, every other number in the figure would be worthless.

Reading the maximum correctly

The maximum bucket is the quantity most often misread as evidence about the hash function, in both directions. A longest bucket of 9 in a table of a million entries at α = 1 is exactly what a perfect hash function produces — the expected maximum computed from the bucket distribution is 8.79 — and the figure prints the measured and the predicted maximum side by side so they can be compared at whatever n and m you choose. Concluding "the hash is bad" from such an observation is a mistake; so is concluding "the hash is fine" from a mean that looks right while the maximum is 500. The correct test is the comparison the figure makes: measure the maximum, compute what a uniform hash would give at this n and m, and look at the ratio. A factor of two is noise; a factor of fifty is a broken hash function or an adversary, and the two are distinguished only by asking where the keys came from.
