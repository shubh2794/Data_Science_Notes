19 Fibonacci heaps: the structure, the potential Φ, and the lazy operations

A Fibonacci heap is a collection of min-heap-ordered trees of arbitrary shape, linked into a circular doubly linked root list, with a pointer to the root holding the minimum. Each node carries its key, its parent, one child pointer into a circular doubly linked list of its children, its degree (number of children), and a one-bit mark recording whether it has lost a child since it last became somebody's child. The design principle is do as little as possible, as late as possible: insert and merge just splice root lists in O(1) and do no tidying; the tidying is deferred to extractMin, which has to scan the roots anyway. The accounting that makes the deferral honest is the potential method, with a potential that counts exactly the two kinds of untidiness the lazy operations create.

Why inserting lazily is safe

A binomial heap inserts by merging a one-node tree and immediately carrying: O(log n) worst case, O(1) amortized by the binary-counter argument. A Fibonacci heap skips the carrying. After k inserts into an empty heap there are k one-node trees in the root list, which is ugly, and the potential records the ugliness: Φ = k. The next extractMin will have to look at all k roots — its actual cost is Θ(k) — but consolidating them links them into O(log k) trees and the potential drops by nearly k, so the amortized cost of that extractMin is only O(log k). The k inserts each paid one unit in advance, the extractMin spent them. That is the whole logic of the structure, and the figure in §20 shows exactly this sequence: seven lazy inserts, Φ = 7, one extractMin, Φ = 2. Merge is the same story with two root lists instead of one: the splice is O(1), the potential is additive, and whatever mess the two heaps brought with them is paid for by the extractMin that eventually tidies it.

What the structure gives up

The trees have no fixed shape and no array; every node is a separate allocation with four pointers, a degree and a mark, so the constant factors are large and the memory traffic is worse than an index heap's. There is no search: as with every heap, decreaseKey and delete need a handle, and here the handle is the node pointer itself, which the caller must keep. The bounds are amortized: a single extractMin can cost Θ(n) actual time (right after n inserts), which rules the structure out wherever a per-operation worst case matters — real-time schedulers, interactive latency budgets. And the O(1) is for decreaseKey only; increaseKey is delete-then-insert, O(log n) amortized. All of this is why §24 will conclude that the Fibonacci heap is the structure that proves the O(E + V log V) bound for Dijkstra and rarely the structure that runs it. None of this makes the analysis less worth knowing: it is the cleanest worked example of the potential method on the site, and the reasoning transfers to every lazy structure.

20 extractMin and consolidate: paying the deferred work in O(log n) amortized

extractMin is where the laziness is collected. Remove the min root and promote its children to the root list — at most D(n) of them, each an O(1) splice. The root list now has up to t(H) − 1 + D(n) trees, and somewhere among them is the new minimum, so a scan is unavoidable. Consolidate does the scan and, while it is there, restores "at most one root per degree" by repeatedly LINKing equal-degree roots — the same operation the binomial heap uses. It keeps a table A[0 … D(n)] with A[d] = the root of degree d seen so far; when a second root of degree d appears the two are linked into one of degree d+1, which may in turn collide with A[d+1], and so on up. When the scan ends, every root has a distinct degree, so there are at most D(n)+1 roots, and Φ has dropped to at most D(n)+1 + 2m.

Why the trees are binomial until decreaseKey interferes

LINK joins two trees of equal degree by making one root a child of the other — exactly the recursive definition of Bₖ from two copies of Bₖ₋₁. Starting from one-node trees and applying only LINK, every tree that consolidate produces is a binomial tree: a root of degree k with 2ᵏ nodes, and the root list after consolidate mirrors the binary representation of n just as a binomial heap does. The readout of the figure checks this — every tree of degree k has exactly 2ᵏ nodes, and the number of roots equals the number of one-bits in n — and it holds because no key has been decreased yet. The "Fibonacci" in the name only becomes relevant once cuts start removing children from the middle of trees, which is §21, and the degree bound has to be re-proved for the damaged trees, which is §22.

21 decreaseKey in O(1) amortized: cut, don't sift, and cascade

In a binary heap, lowering a key means sifting it up: O(log n) comparisons along a path. A Fibonacci heap refuses to walk the path. If the decreased key is now smaller than its parent, the node is simply cut out of its tree and dropped into the root list, where it is a root and the heap order is trivially satisfied. That is O(1). The catch is that cutting children out of trees makes them thin, and a thin tree of degree k with few nodes would break the D(n) = O(log n) bound that extractMin depends on. The mark is the damage limiter: a node may lose one child and be marked; when it loses a second, it is cut too — a cascading cut that may run up several ancestors — so that no non-root node ever has lost more than one child. The cascade can be long, but every marked node it cuts had paid two units of potential in advance.

Why cutting preserves heap order and why the cascade is bounded

A cut node becomes a root, and a root has no parent to be ordered against, so the only edge the decrease could have broken — the one above x — is removed rather than repaired; every other edge is untouched. The subtree under x moves with it and was already ordered. That is why there is no comparison at all beyond the one against the parent: the structure handles a decrease by forgetting the relationship instead of fixing it. The cascade's length is unbounded in the worst case for a single call (a chain of marked ancestors of any length can be built), and that is exactly what the potential absorbs: each marked node on the chain contributed 2 units when it was marked, the cut consumes them, and the total over any sequence is what the O(1) amortized bound describes. The figure's example is built to hit the bound with equality — c = 3 cascading-cut calls, two of which cut and one of which marks, ΔΦ = +1 = 4 − c — so the inequality in the theorem can be seen to be tight, not slack.

delete, and increaseKey

delete(x) is decreaseKey(x, −∞) followed by extractMin: O(1) + O(log n) amortized. There is no cheaper way, because deleting an interior node must promote its children somewhere, and "somewhere" means the root list, which is the same work extractMin does. increaseKey has no O(1) version: raising a key can violate the order with any number of descendants, and the honest implementation is delete-then-insert at O(log n) amortized. This asymmetry is the reason the structure is sometimes described as good for algorithms whose keys only ever move in one direction — Dijkstra's distances only fall, Prim's edge weights only fall — and not as a general-purpose replacement for a binary heap.

22 The degree bound D(n) ≤ floor(log_φ n), and why the structure is named after Fibonacci

Every extractMin bound above is O(D(n)), and D(n) is only useful if it is O(log n). For binomial trees it is: a root of degree k has 2ᵏ descendants, so k ≤ log₂n. Cascading cuts break that — a node of degree k can lose children — but the marking rule guarantees each child has lost at most one of its own children, and that is enough to show a node of degree k still has at least Fₖ₊₂ descendants, where F is the Fibonacci sequence. Since Fₖ₊₂ ≥ φᵏ with φ = (1 + √5)/2, a node of degree k heads a subtree of at least φᵏ nodes (itself included), hence k ≤ log_φ n ≈ 1.44·log₂n. The trees are allowed to thin out from "binary-exponential" to "Fibonacci-exponential", and no further.

Where the marking rule enters, precisely

Lemma 1 is the only place the marks are used, and it uses them in one clause: "since then, yᵢ has lost at most one child". Without cascading cuts a child could lose all its children and keep its place, degree(yᵢ) could be 0 for every i, and a node of degree k could have exactly k+1 descendants — no exponential bound, D(n) could be n − 1, and extractMin would be O(n) amortized. With a rule that cut a node after losing its j-th child instead of its second, the recurrence would become sₖ ≥ j + ∑ᵢ₌ⱼ..ₖ sᵢ₋ⱼ, still exponential with a smaller base for every fixed j — the "2" is the tightest choice that keeps the constant small, not the only one that works. And the reason the mark is cleared when a node becomes a root (in CUT) or a child (in LINK) is that Lemma 1 counts losses since the node was linked: a fresh link starts a fresh count.

Why "Fibonacci" and not "golden ratio", and what the bound is worth

The structure is named for the lemma, not the constant: the minimum subtree sizes satisfy the Fibonacci recurrence exactly, because a degree-k node's children have degrees at least 0, 0, 1, 2, …, k−2 and the minimum sizes of those add up, by identity (I), to the next Fibonacci number. The constant φ is what falls out when the recurrence is solved. In practical terms the bound says that the trees may be about 1.44× taller in degree than binomial trees of the same size, so consolidate's degree table is 44% longer and extractMin's constant is correspondingly worse — one more entry on the ledger of why §24 finds the structure's practical performance disappointing despite its bounds. The proof itself is the durable thing: it is the template for every "lazy structure with a damage-limiting invariant" analysis, and the same shape of argument — bound the size from below by a recurrence the invariant enforces — reappears in the analysis of pairing heaps and of splay trees.

23 van Emde Boas trees: integer keys, the square-root recursion, and O(log log u) for everything

Every heap above is comparison-based: it learns about keys only by comparing them, and a comparison-based priority queue cannot beat Θ(log n) per extractMin, because n inserts and n extractMins would sort, and comparison sorting costs Ω(n log n). A van Emde Boas tree steps outside that model. Its keys are integers from a fixed universe {0, 1, …, u − 1}, and it uses the bits of a key as an address, the way counting sort does. In return it supports member, insert, delete, min, max, successor and predecessor in O(log log u) worst-case time — about five steps for 32-bit keys, whatever n is. The price is space proportional to the universe, not to the set, and a structure that only makes sense when keys really are small integers.

Where the square root comes from

Build up to it. A bit vector of length u gives O(1) member, insert and delete, but successor must scan: O(u). Put a binary tree of OR-bits over it — each internal bit says "something below me is present" — and successor walks up and down: O(log u). Now make the tree shallower instead: split the u bits into √u clusters of √u bits and keep one summary bit per cluster. A successor query looks inside its own cluster, and if nothing is there, asks the summary for the next non-empty cluster and takes that cluster's minimum. Each of those questions is itself a successor query on a universe of size √u — and if the clusters and the summary are built the same way, recursively, the query costs T(u) = c·T(√u) + O(1). The whole design problem is to make c = 1: at most one recursive call per level. With c = 2 the recurrence solves to Θ(log u), no better than the binary tree.

How c = 1 is achieved: min and max outside the recursion

Two design choices do it. First, every V stores its min and max in its own fields, so "the largest element of cluster h" and "the smallest element of cluster h" are O(1) reads, not recursive calls. Successor then needs at most one recursion: if low(x) is below its own cluster's max, recurse into that cluster; otherwise recurse into the summary to find the next non-empty cluster and read its min. Second — the subtle one — the min is not also stored inside a cluster. That makes inserting into an empty V an O(1) assignment (min = max = x) rather than a recursion, and it is what rescues insert: when x lands in an empty cluster, the structure must record the cluster in the summary and put x in the cluster, which looks like two recursive calls — but the second is into an empty V, so it is O(1), and only the summary insert recurses. Delete mirrors it: the summary is touched only when a cluster becomes empty, and a cluster becomes empty only when its delete was the O(1) single-element case.

The space problem, and the two standard remedies

Θ(u) space is fine for u = 2¹⁶ and hopeless for u = 2⁶⁴, and it is paid even for a set of ten keys. Most of it is empty clusters. Store only the non-empty clusters, in a hash table keyed by cluster number, and each key contributes O(1) cluster entries per level — O(n log log u) space, reducible to O(n) with more care — at the cost of the bounds becoming expected rather than worst case, since they now rest on hashing. The y-fast trie reaches O(n) space with O(log log u) expected amortized operations another way: it hashes one representative per group of about log u consecutive keys into a trie over the bits, and keeps each group in a small balanced search tree, where O(log log u) comparisons suffice because the group has only O(log u) elements. Both keep the doubly-logarithmic query and drop the universe-sized table.

When it beats a binary heap — and when it does not

As a priority queue, a vEB tree gives insert, delete and extractMin in O(log log u), findMin in O(1), and decreaseKey as delete-then-insert. It stores a set, so equal priorities are handled by keeping a list of items per key. The comparison with a binary heap is log log u against log n, which favours the vEB tree when keys are small integers and the queue is large. The textbook case is Dijkstra with integer edge weights in [0, C]: tentative distances are integers below V·C, so u = V·C and the run costs O(E · log log (V·C)) — against the binary heap's O(E log V) and on sparse graphs with modest C it can undercut even the Fibonacci heap's O(E + V log V) of §24. Where it loses: keys that are not integers (or not small ones), queues that are small, and any workload where the Θ(u) table or the hash-table constants cost more cache misses than log n sift steps in one array. For bounded integer priorities, simpler structures often win in practice — a bucket array indexed by key (O(1) insert, a scan to find the next non-empty bucket) or a radix heap exploiting the fact that Dijkstra's extracted keys never decrease.

Two things share the name. The van Emde Boas tree of this section is a dynamic set of integers with O(log log u) operations. The van Emde Boas layout mentioned in §29 is a way of placing the nodes of any balanced tree in memory — cut the tree at half its height, store the top subtree contiguously, then each bottom subtree contiguously, recursively — so that a root-to-leaf path touches O(log_B n) blocks for every block size B at once. The layout involves no integer keys and supports no successor query; what the two share is the recursive split at the square root — of the universe in one, of the tree's height in the other.
