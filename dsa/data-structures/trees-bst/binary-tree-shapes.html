02 Binary trees, and full, complete, perfect, degenerate

A binary tree is an ordered tree in which every node has at most two children, each labelled as the left child or the right child. The labelling is part of the structure, not a drawing convention: a node with one child that is a left child is a different tree from a node whose one child is a right child, and in a search tree the two mean opposite things. The recursive definition adjusts accordingly — a binary tree is empty, or a root with a left subtree and a right subtree, either or both of which may be empty — and the empty subtrees are real: they are where a search ends unsuccessfully, where an insertion attaches, and where the array layout of §05 wastes its space. Four named shapes recur, and because they are routinely confused and have different counting facts, they are separated here with their definitions side by side.

Full trees: leaves = internal + 1, proved

This identity is the one most often misapplied, so its hypothesis deserves emphasis: it holds for full binary trees — every node has zero or two children — and not for binary trees in general (the figure's degenerate chain has 1 leaf and h internal nodes, and its complete tree with an odd-sized last level fails it too whenever some node has exactly one child). The proof is a counting of edges two ways. Let a full tree have n_I internal nodes and n_E leaves, so n = n_I + n_E. Counting edges by their lower end, every node but the root is the child end of one edge: edges = n − 1 = n_I + n_E − 1. Counting edges by their upper end, every internal node is the parent end of exactly two (that is the fullness hypothesis) and leaves of none: edges = 2·n_I. Equating, 2·n_I = n_I + n_E − 1, so n_E = n_I + 1. The same double count with "exactly two" replaced by "at most two" gives only n − 1 ≤ 2·n_I, i.e. n_E ≤ n_I + 1, for a general binary tree — the identity becomes an inequality the moment a single one-child node exists. An equivalent removal argument — repeatedly delete a leaf together with its (internal) parent and reattach its sibling, which removes one of each kind and keeps the tree full, until one node is left — reaches the same conclusion and is the argument to remember if the edge count does not come to mind.

At the default h = 3 the four trees have 15, 11, 7 and 4 nodes respectively — the same height spanning nearly a factor of four in size, which is the first concrete sign that height and size are only loosely coupled. The perfect tree hits 2ʰ⁺¹ − 1 = 15 and 2ʰ = 8 leaves exactly; the complete tree with 11 nodes has per-level counts 1, 2, 4, 4 — the last level half full, from the left — and happens to satisfy leaves = internal + 1 (6 = 5 + 1) because 11 nodes leave no one-child node, whereas at h = 2 its 5-node version does the same but at other sizes a single one-child node breaks it; the full tree has 7 nodes, 4 = 3 + 1, and per-level counts 1, 2, 2, 2 — full but nowhere near perfect; the degenerate chain has 4 nodes, one leaf, and fails the identity at every h. Nothing in those tables is typed in: each tree is built, then walked, and the "yes / NO" flags are the identities re-tested on the result.

A vocabulary warning. Some treatments use "full" for what is called perfect here, "complete" for what is called full here, or "strictly binary" for full. The definitions above are the ones the rest of this page and the heap page use, and every claim that depends on shape — h = floor(log₂ n), leaves = internal + 1, n = 2ʰ⁺¹ − 1 — is stated with the shape it needs. When you meet a shape word elsewhere, look for the definition, not the name.

03 The height bounds: floor(log₂ n) ≤ h ≤ n − 1

Because every search-tree operation in the second half of this page costs O(h), the range of possible heights for an n-node binary tree is the range of possible costs, and both ends of it are attained: h = n − 1 by a chain and h = floor(log₂ n) by any complete tree. The derivation is short and it is worth doing exactly rather than as "about log n", because the exact form is what the heap page's index arithmetic, the expected-height theorem of §23 and the balance criteria of the next part all rest on.

What the two extremes mean for cost

A binary tree of a million nodes can have height 19 (floor(log₂ 10⁶) = 19, since 2¹⁹ = 524 288 ≤ 10⁶ < 2²⁰) or height 999 999, and nothing in the definition of a binary tree prefers one to the other. Every operation whose cost is "walk one root-to-leaf path" — search, insert, delete, min, max in a search tree; sift-up and sift-down in a heap — is therefore either logarithmic or linear depending on a property the operation itself does not control. There are exactly three ways out, and this page and its neighbours take one each. Control the shape by construction: a heap is always complete because insertion and deletion are defined to keep it so, which is why its height is exactly floor(log₂ n) with no hypothesis. Rely on randomness: a search tree built from keys in random order has expected height O(log n) — a theorem with hypotheses, in §23. Rebalance: pay extra work on every update to keep h = O(log n) in the worst case — the AVL, red-black and B-tree families, which are built at Balanced & Multiway Trees and are only pointed at here from §22.

The amber curve is the mean height of twelve search trees built from twelve random permutations of 1 … n; it is the subject of §23, and for now the point is only where it sits: well above the logarithm, far below the line. At the default n = 64 the readout gives the complete height 6 — levels 0 through 5 hold 2⁶ − 1 = 63 nodes and the 64th opens level 6, so h = 6 = floor(log₂ 64), and that is what the walk over the built tree reports — the chain height 63, and a random mean of about 10.5, between 2·ln 64 ≈ 8.3 and 4.311·ln 64 ≈ 17.9; slide to 4096 and the chain is at 4095 while the random mean is still in the twenties.

04 Representations: linked nodes, parent pointers, left-child / right-sibling

A tree in memory is a choice about which of its relations to store explicitly. The parent relation defines the tree, but almost every algorithm walks downward, so the standard representation stores the inverse: each node holds references to its children, and the parent is stored too only if some operation needs to climb. For binary trees that is two fields, left and right, and optionally parent; for general trees, whose nodes have any number of children, there are two honest layouts — a per-node list of child references, or the left-child / right-sibling trick, which encodes an arbitrary tree as a binary one with exactly two links per node and no lists at all.

The parent pointer: what it buys and what it costs

Without a parent link, a node knows nothing about where it sits; the only way to reach its parent is to search for it again from the root, remembering the previous node on the way — exactly the predecessor problem of a singly linked list, in two dimensions. Insertion into a search tree can live with that, because the insertion walk naturally has the parent in hand when it reaches the null it will replace (the trailing pointer of §18). Two operations cannot: the successor of a node with no right subtree is found by climbing (§16), and deletion of an arbitrary node must rewire the parent's link to it. Both can be written without parent pointers by re-searching from the root for the node's key — the cost stays O(h), since the re-search is one more root-to-node path — or by writing them recursively so that the recursion's return path plays the role of the parent chain. The parent pointer's price is a third reference per node (50% more link memory on a two-link node) and a third field to keep consistent on every structural change, which is where a large fraction of tree bugs live: the transplant operation of §20 exists precisely to update parent in exactly one place.

Left-child / right-sibling: an arbitrary tree with two links

A node with k children could store them in a list, but then the tree is a tree of lists — an extra allocation per internal node, a variable-size object, and an iteration protocol for "the children of v". The left-child / right-sibling representation removes all of it. Each node keeps only its first child and its next sibling; the children of v are recovered by taking v.firstChild and then following nextSibling links until null, which touches exactly k nodes for k children — linear in the number of children, never in n. What makes this more than a storage trick is that the result is a binary tree: rename firstChild to left and nextSibling to right and every binary-tree algorithm applies. A preorder traversal of the binary form visits the nodes in the general tree's preorder (root, then the first child's subtree, then the next sibling's — which is the rest of the children), as the figure's readout confirms on the 13-node sample. The costs shift in one visible way: sibling chains become depth in the binary form, so a general tree of height 3 with a node of three children becomes a binary tree of height 6, and the height of the binary form is bounded by the number of nodes on a root-to-leaf path counting siblings, not by the general height. Any algorithm whose cost is "the height of the binary form" is paying for that.

The heights do not — 3 against 6 — and that gap is the sibling chains: the root's three children B, C, D now sit at depths 1, 2, 3 of the binary form instead of all at depth 1. This is worth holding onto when the next part of the series introduces multiway trees (B-trees, tries): a B-tree node with hundreds of children is a node with a list, and its shallowness is real; the same tree in LCRS form would be as deep as the lists are long.

Where the array goes. The representations above are pointer-based and inherit the pointer-chasing cost of Linked Lists §03: each link followed is potentially a cache miss, and the nodes of a tree built over time are scattered wherever the allocator put them. The one representation that avoids all of it — a tree stored in a flat array with the structure in the index arithmetic — is the subject of the next section, and it is excellent for exactly one shape.

05 The array layout: level numbering, and why it is only for complete trees

Number the potential positions of a binary tree level by level, left to right, starting at 0 — the root is 0, its children 1 and 2, their children 3 … 6, and in general the children of position f are 2f + 1 and 2f + 2. Store the node at position f in slot f of an array and the tree needs no links at all: parent, left and right are arithmetic. For a complete tree the numbering is gap-free and the array has exactly n slots — that is the binary heap, whose index arithmetic, height and cache behaviour are derived in full on Heaps §02, §03 and §29 and are not repeated here. This section is about the other case: what the layout costs when the tree is not complete.

The default is the eleven-key search tree that the second half of the page uses throughout. Its deepest node, 9, is at level number 21 — root 15 is 0, its left child 6 is 1, 6's right child 7 is 2·1 + 2 = 4, 7's right child 13 is 2·4 + 2 = 10, and 13's left child 9 is 2·10 + 1 = 21 — so the array needs 22 slots for 11 nodes and half of them are holes. Switch to the complete tree of the same size and the holes vanish: 11 slots, 11 nodes, which is the case the heap page owns. Switch to the right-going chain and drag k: at k = 6 the chain needs 63 slots, at k = 10 1 023, at k = 20 1 048 575 — a million slots for twenty keys, exactly 2²⁰ − 1, because each step down doubles the level number. The left-going chain is half as bad (2¹⁹ = 524 288 at k = 20) and just as hopeless. Every slot count in the readout is the measured largest level number plus one, and the chain counts are checked against the closed forms 2ᵏ − 1 and 2ᵏ⁻¹.

When the array layout is the right choice anyway

  • When the shape is complete by construction — the binary heap — and then it is not merely acceptable but the best representation available: n slots, no links, parent and children by shift-and-add, siblings adjacent in memory. The locality is genuinely good near the root and genuinely poor deep down, and the exact picture (Heaps §29) is that a root-to-leaf walk touches indices 0, 1–2, 3–6, 7–14, …, so the gap between a node and its child doubles at every level and only the top log₂ L levels share a cache line of L keys. "Trees have bad locality" is true of pointer trees and false of this one for its top levels; neither slogan survives without the shape it refers to.
  • When the tree is perfect and static — a tournament tree, a segment tree over a power-of-two range, the comparison tree of a binary search over a sorted array. A sorted array is a particular balanced search tree read in inorder rather than by level number, and the next part of the series makes that identity explicit for implicit search trees laid out in level order (Eytzinger layout), which trade the sorted array's simplicity for the heap's locality.
  • Never for a dynamic search tree, whose shape is dictated by the keys: the space is Θ(2ʰ) and a deletion that promotes a subtree must move every node in it. A dynamic tree is a pointer tree, and its locality problem is solved, when it must be, by making the nodes bigger — the B-tree of the next part — rather than by flattening the tree.

06 Computing depth and height, and the Θ(n²) trap

Depth and height are both defined recursively, and the two recursions run in opposite directions: depth is computed by climbing up parent links from a node to the root, height by descending down from a node to its leaves. Each is a one-line function with an obvious cost. The trap is in combining them. The definition "the height of a tree is the maximum depth of its leaves" is true, and implementing it literally — find every leaf, compute each one's depth by climbing, take the maximum — is correct and Θ(n²) on the wrong shape, while the direct recursion is Θ(n) on every shape. Both are on the figure with their operations counted.

On the default comb the red curve has slope 2 and the green curve slope 1, and the readout makes it numeric: at n = 512 the naive method performs 33 408 operations (512 node visits plus 32 896 parent links climbed across its 256 leaves — the sum 1 + 2 + ⋯ + 256 = 256·257/2 = 32 896, each leaf at depth i climbing i links) against the recursive method's 1 025 = 2·512 + 1 calls, a ratio that has doubled with every doubling of n: about 2.6 at n = 32, about 32.6 at 512. Switch to the chain and the red curve drops onto the green one: a chain has a single leaf, and one climb of length n − 1 is linear. That contrast is the point of the section — "the naive height algorithm is quadratic" is a statement about a shape, and the shape is not the one most people picture. The complete tree sits in between at Θ(n log n), and the random search tree close to it. The green curve is the same on every shape, and it lies on the dashed 2n + 1 line to the pixel, because the count of recursive calls does not depend on shape at all.

Where this reappears. Any algorithm phrased "for every node, compute something about its ancestors" — the depth of every node, the lowest common ancestor of many pairs by climbing, "is u an ancestor of v" tested by walking — has the same quadratic shadow. The fixes are also always the same: compute the quantity in one top-down pass that hands the running value to the children (depth is parent's depth + 1, which the preorder traversal of the next section delivers in Θ(n)), or precompute a structure that answers the per-node question in O(1), which for ancestor queries is the Euler tour of §11.
