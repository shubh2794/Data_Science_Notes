14 What a binary search tree is: the invariant, exactly

A binary search tree is a binary tree with a key in every node such that, for every node x, every key in x's left subtree is less than x.key and every key in x's right subtree is greater. The word that carries the whole definition is subtree. The condition is not "the left child is smaller and the right child is larger" — that is a weaker statement, it is the one most people write down first, and a tree can satisfy it at every node while being useless for search. The correct invariant is what makes a search a single path: at any node, the key you want is either here, or entirely to one side, or absent; nothing anywhere in the other subtree can be it.

Why "subtree" and not "child"

A search compares the target with the root and then commits: if the target is smaller, it goes left and never looks at anything to the right again. That commitment is justified only if nothing in the right subtree could be the target, i.e. if every key there is larger than the root — the subtree condition. The child condition guarantees far less: it says the right child is larger, and says nothing about the right child's left subtree, which might contain a key smaller than the root. The counterexample in the figure is the smallest one: 12 is the right child of 5, correctly larger than 5, and is a descendant of 10 on the wrong side of it. A search for 12 compares with 10, goes right, finds 15, goes left into an empty subtree, and reports "absent" — correctly, in the sense that the algorithm did exactly what a search tree entitles it to do, and wrongly, because the tree lied. The checker that catches this is the interval form: each node receives the bounds its ancestors impose and tests its own key against them, so 12 is checked against (−∞, 10) and fails. A checker that only compares each node with its two children will pass this tree and every tree like it, and it is one of the most common wrong answers to "verify this is a BST".

The classic wrong invariant: a tree that passes the child-only check and fails as a search tree every parent–child pair is ordered; search for 12 and watch it go right at the root

At the default the search for 12 goes 10 → 15 — right at the root, since 12 > 10, then left of 15 into nothing — and misses after 2 comparisons, while 12 sits in plain view two links down the other side. The panel's two checkers disagree: the child-only check passes, the interval check fails at 12 with the bound "must be less than 10". The inorder walk, 2 5 12 10 15, is not sorted, which is the third, one-pass way to see the same defect (§21). Slide the target to 2, 5, 10 or 15 and the search succeeds — the tree works for every key that happens to be on the correct side of all its ancestors, which is exactly why this bug survives testing.

Two invariants that look alike and are not. A heap requires each node to be no larger than its children — a child condition, which by transitivity becomes a subtree condition for free, because "no larger than" is the same relation on both sides. The search tree's condition has opposite relations on the two sides, and transitivity does not carry "left child smaller" past the child to the child's right subtree; that asymmetry is why the BST invariant must be stated over subtrees and why a heap gives no help at all in locating a key (Heaps §30).

15 Search, minimum, maximum: one path each

Every query on a search tree is a walk down a single root-to-node path, and its cost is the length of that path plus one — at most h + 1 nodes examined. Search compares at each node and turns left or right; minimum turns left until it cannot; maximum turns right until it cannot. None of them backtracks, none of them visits a sibling, and that is the entire reason the invariant was worth imposing: on a tree without it, the only way to find a key is to look at every node.

Binary search, made dynamic. Binary search on a sorted array (Sorting & Searching §28) compares with the middle element and discards half; the comparison structure it follows is a perfectly balanced search tree that exists only implicitly, its "nodes" being midpoints. A BST materialises that tree as real nodes so that it can be changed — an insertion into a sorted array costs Θ(n) element moves, into a tree O(h) — at the price of two links per key and of no longer being promised that the tree is the balanced one. Everything in the second half of this page is the working-out of that trade.

16 Successor and predecessor: the two cases, and the amortised walk

The successor of a node x is the node with the next larger key — the one that follows x in inorder — and a search tree finds it without comparing any keys, from the structure alone. There are exactly two cases. If x has a right subtree, its successor is the smallest key there: go right once, then left to the bottom. If it has none, every larger key is above x: climb until the step just taken was from a left child, and the node arrived at is the successor; if the climb reaches the root without ever stepping up from a left child, x was the maximum and has none. Predecessor is the mirror image. Each call is O(h), and yet a full inorder walk by repeated successor calls is Θ(n), not O(n·h) — the two bounds are both true and the distinction is the first amortised argument on this page.

17 Range queries, floor and ceiling, and the tree as a sorted sequence

The operations of §15 and §16 combine into the one that most distinguishes a search tree from a hash table: report every key in an interval [a, b], in sorted order, in time O(h + m) where m is the number of keys reported. The bound has two parts for a reason: O(h) is the cost of finding the interval — the two boundary paths — and O(m) is the cost of walking it, which no structure can avoid since the answer has m entries. The same machinery gives the ceiling of a key (the smallest stored key that is ≥ a) and the floor (the largest ≤ b) — the queries a hash table answers only by scanning everything — and, with the size field of §26, the count of keys in a range without visiting them.

18 Insertion: an unsuccessful search, then one link

To insert a key, search for it. The search fails — it reaches a null link — and that null is the one place in the tree where the new key can go without disturbing the invariant: every ancestor on the path already sent it to the correct side. Replace the null with a new leaf and the tree is a search tree again. Insertion is therefore exactly as expensive as a failed search, O(h), and it never restructures anything: new keys always become leaves, which is why the shape of a tree is a function of its insertion order and why sorted input is a disaster.

19 Lowest common ancestor, and building a balanced tree from sorted input in Θ(n)

Two more things the ordering invariant gives away for free. The lowest common ancestor of two keys — the deepest node that has both below it — is, in a search tree, simply the first node on the way down from the root whose key lies between them, so it costs one partial search, O(h), with no parent pointers and no marking, where a general tree would need either parent links and a climb or a preprocessing pass. And the catastrophe of §18's sorted-input build has an exact inverse: if the keys are already sorted, the perfectly balanced tree can be built directly in Θ(n) by taking the middle key as the root and recursing — the one case where the best tree is also the cheapest to construct.

20 Deletion: the three cases, transplant, and the successor-or-predecessor choice

Deletion is the one operation on a plain search tree that has cases, and the reason is structural: removing a node with two children leaves a hole that must be filled by some key without violating the invariant, and only two keys can fill it — the node's successor or its predecessor, the two keys adjacent to it in sorted order. A node with no children is simply unlinked; a node with one child is replaced by that child; a node with two children is replaced by its successor, which has no left child (if it had one, that would be a smaller key still larger than the deleted one) and so can itself be removed by the one-child rule. A small helper, transplant, does every "replace this subtree with that one" in one place, so that the parent pointers are updated exactly once and correctly.

Deletion on the sample tree, stepped: the leaf, one-child and two-child cases, with transplant red = the node being deleted, green = the successor (or predecessor) that replaces it, amber = the successor's own child being spliced up

The default is the hard case: deleting the root, 15, whose successor 17 is not its right child. The steps are: locate 15; find the successor as the minimum of the right subtree (18 → 17, which has no left child, as it must); because 17's parent is 18 and not 15, splice 17 out by transplanting its (empty) right subtree into its place and then give 17 the whole right subtree of 15, rooted at 18; finally transplant 17 into the root position and hand it 15's left subtree. The footer line shows the inorder sequence after each step — the intermediate states are not valid search trees, and the footer says so while a step is half done — and at the end reads 2 3 4 6 7 9 13 17 18 20, sorted, invariant intact, height 4. Choose the predecessor variant of the same deletion and 13 becomes the root instead, with height 3: both are correct, and they produce different trees, which is the seed of the asymmetry discussed in §24. The leaf and one-child cases are one transplant each and are worth stepping through once to see that transplant with a null second argument is the leaf case.

Deletion is not commutative. Deleting x then y can leave a different tree from deleting y then x, because each deletion's choice of replacement depends on the tree at that moment. This is harmless for correctness — both results are valid search trees on the same keys — and it is the reason the random-tree theorem of §23 has "no deletions" among its hypotheses: after a deletion the tree is no longer distributed like a randomly built one.
