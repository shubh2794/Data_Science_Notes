Hallucination & Factuality

Language models produce fluent text that is sometimes confidently false. Knowing why — and how to define, detect, measure, and reduce it — is what separates a demo from a deployable system.

LLMs & Generative AI Reliability Evaluation

01 What hallucination is

A hallucination is generated content that is unsupported — untrue of the world, or not entailed by the evidence the model was handed — and delivered with exactly the fluency of a correct answer.

The objective has no truth term

  • Pretraining maximises the likelihood of the next token given the previous ones. That objective rewards text that looks like the training distribution; nothing in the loss asks whether a claim is true, or whether it follows from a document sitting in the context window.
  • Decoding always returns some continuation. Uncertainty therefore never surfaces as silence — the gap is filled with the most probable-looking string: a plausible citation, a plausible method name, a plausible date.
what is actually optimisedminθ −∑t log pθ( xt* | x<t* ) ⟵ likelihood of the observed text no term for truth(x) no term for supported(x | evidence)

Why it is structural, not a bug

  • Fluency and content come out of the same machinery, so the cues humans use to gauge a speaker's confidence are decoupled from whether the content is right.
  • This is a property of generative modelling, not of one checkpoint — so every real mitigation adds something external: retrieved evidence, a verifier, or an abstention path.
One sentence: hallucination is a model optimising for plausibility rather than support — and never marking the difference in its output.

02 Taxonomy: factuality vs faithfulness

"Wrong" is not one thing. Two independent axes — what the claim is measured against and how it deviates — decide which fix applies.

Axis 1 — the reference: world vs source

  • Factuality asks: is the claim true of the world? The reference is external knowledge. This is the relevant axis for closed-book question answering, advice, and any answer generated without supplied evidence.
  • Faithfulness (groundedness) asks: is the claim supported by the provided context? The reference is the prompt's own documents. This is the axis for summarisation, RAG, and tool outputs.
  • They come apart both ways: a summary can be faithful to a source that is itself wrong, and an answer can be factually correct yet unfaithful because the cited passage does not actually say it.

Axis 2 — the deviation: intrinsic vs extrinsic

  • Intrinsic hallucination contradicts the source: the document says the launch slipped to Q3, the summary says Q2. It is detectable by comparing output against source, and an entailment model catches it.
  • Extrinsic hallucination adds content the source neither states nor denies. It is harder — not a contradiction, just an unverifiable addition — so detection means judging whether an unsupported statement is fair inference or fabrication.

03 Why it happens

Five distinct mechanisms, each with a different signature — and each responsive to a different lever.

Knowledge gaps and the long tail

  • Facts seen once or twice in pretraining are stored weakly. The model still produces the shape of an answer — a name, a number, an identifier — from the surrounding distribution, so rare entities fail loudly rather than quietly.
  • Anything past the training cutoff is unavailable, and the model has no marker for "beyond my horizon" — it answers from the pre-cutoff world as if nothing changed.

Exposure bias and decoding

  • Training conditions on gold prefixes; generation conditions on the model's own output. Once a wrong token is emitted the model stays consistent with the mistake — errors compound along the sample.
  • Sampling knobs trade diversity for accuracy: higher temperature and wide nucleus sampling flatten the distribution and make fabricated continuations reachable.
exposure biastrain: x<t ~ data generate: x<t ~ pθ ⟹ the two prefixes drift apart temperaturepT(x) ∝ exp( zx / T ) T ↑ ⟹ entropy ↑ ⟹ fabrication ↑

Retrieval failures

  • In a RAG system most "model hallucinations" are really retrieval misses: the answer was never in the retrieved set, and a helpful model answers anyway from parametric memory.
  • Distractors are worse than empty results: a topically similar but irrelevant passage actively pulls generation off course. Long contexts add the "lost in the middle" effect (see Context-Length Extension).

Alignment side-effects

  • Preference training rewards answers humans rate highly, and confident, complete answers outscore hedged ones — so the reward model can quietly teach the policy to bluff (see Policy & Preference Optimization).
  • Sycophancy is the sharp version: challenged on a correct answer, the model reverses to agree. A preference artefact, not a knowledge failure.
Diagnose before you patch. A retrieval miss, a decoding-temperature problem, and a sycophancy problem look identical in the output and need completely different fixes.

04 Detecting hallucinations

No single detector is reliable. Practical systems stack several cheap, independent signals and escalate only the disagreements.

Sampling-based consistency

  • Draw k samples at nonzero temperature and compare. Facts the model knows are stable across samples; fabricated ones vary, because they came from a flat, high-entropy region.
  • String comparison is too strict — "born in 1912" and "born in nineteen twelve" agree. Cluster samples by meaning (bidirectional entailment) and take entropy over clusters, so paraphrase is not counted as disagreement.
token-level uncertaintyH(xt) = −∑v∈V p(v) log p(v) conf = mint∈claim p(xt) semantic entropy over k samplesHsem = −∑c∈clusters p̂(c) log p̂(c) , p̂(c) = |c| / k Hsem ≈ 0 ⟹ one meaning, model is committed Hsem high ⟹ likely fabrication

Uncertainty from the token distribution

  • Log-probabilities are free once you have generated. Minimum or mean token probability over the content-bearing span is a usable signal; averaging over the whole sentence washes it out, since function words are always confident.
  • Caveat: this measures commitment to the text, not truth — a memorised misconception scores as certain.

Entailment-based faithfulness checks

  • Decompose the answer into atomic claims and ask, per claim, whether the evidence entails it — a natural-language-inference problem (see Textual Entailment), cheap enough to run on everything with a small NLI model.
  • The three-way output matters: entailed is fine, contradicted is intrinsic, neutral is extrinsic. Collapsing neutral into "fail" over-flags legitimate paraphrase.
claim-level faithfulnessanswer → { c₁ … cm } score = (1/m) ∑i 1[ evidence ⊨ ci ]

Retrieval-grounded verification and judges

  • Post-hoc retrieval: query the corpus for each claim and check support. This brings in new evidence, so it catches factual errors an entailment check against the original context cannot.
  • Judge models: a strong model grades claims against evidence with a rubric — flexible on messy claims, but it carries its own biases and can hallucinate the verdict, so calibrate against human labels first (see LLM-as-a-Judge).

05 Calibration & abstention

The cheapest way to stop being wrong is to stop answering. Selective prediction turns a fixed model into a tunable one: choose a confidence bar, trade coverage for accuracy.

Attach a confidence c(x) to each answer and a threshold τ: answer when c(x) ≥ τ, otherwise abstain. Sweeping τ traces a risk–coverage curve, a better summary of a factual system than accuracy at one operating point — it shows whether confidence carries real information.

selective predictioncoverage(τ) = (1/N) ∑i 1[ c(xi) ≥ τ ] risk(τ) = ∑i 1[ c ≥ τ ] · err(xi) ⁄ ∑i 1[ c ≥ τ ] τ ↑ ⟹ coverage ↓ , risk ↓
Abstention threshold: coverage vs accuracy Answer only when confidence ≥ threshold; raising it answers fewer questions but more accurately

Calibration is what makes the threshold meaningful

  • A model is calibrated when stated confidence matches empirical accuracy: of the claims asserted at 0.8, about 80% are right. Only then does a threshold cleanly separate answerable from abstain.
  • Expected calibration error bins predictions by confidence and measures the gap. Models are often over-confident after preference training, so re-measure calibration after every alignment stage.
  • Confidence can come from token probabilities, sample agreement, or asking the model directly. Verbalised confidence is readable but is itself generated text, so validate it against outcomes.
expected calibration errorECE = ∑b=1..B (nb / N) · | acc(b) − conf(b) | perfectly calibrated ⟹ ECE = 0
Abstention is only a good deal when someone can act on it. Wire "I don't know" to a fallback — retrieve, escalate to a human, ask a clarifying question — or you have traded wrong answers for dead ends.

06 Mitigation levers

Mitigations live at four layers — the evidence you supply, the way you decode, the way you train, and the checks you run afterwards. They compose; none of them alone is sufficient.

Grounding and citations

  • Retrieval-augmented generation is the highest-leverage single change: it replaces "recall this fact" with "read this passage" — an easier task whose failures are inspectable.
  • Span-level citations do double duty: they pull generation toward the evidence and make each claim checkable. They only help if verified — log an uncited-claim rate and a bad-citation rate.

Decoding and prompt-level constraints

  • Low temperature for factual tasks, sampling for creative ones; constrained decoding into a schema removes a class of format-driven invention.
  • Explicit permission to refuse — answer only from the passages, otherwise say "not stated" — raises abstention on unanswerable questions by removing the pressure to produce something.

Training-time honesty

  • Preference data can mark calibrated hedging and refusal as preferred, so the reward model stops paying for bluffing — the direct counter to sycophancy.
  • Where verifiable ground truth exists, tie reward to it rather than to rater preference, removing the incentive to sound right rather than be right.

System-level verification

  • A second pass — extract claims, check each, revise or drop the unsupported — catches what the generator missed, at the price of latency and another call.
  • Tools beat memory for anything mechanical: a calculator for arithmetic, a query for a lookup, a clock for the date.

07 Measuring it

Aggregate "accuracy" hides hallucination, because a wrong answer and an abstention score the same. Factuality evaluation needs metrics that separate wrong, unsupported, and declined.

Decompose, then score

  • Long-form answers are not one claim. Split the response into atomic facts and score the fraction supported — precision over claims, far more sensitive than a single sentence-level verdict.
  • Precision alone is gamed by saying almost nothing, so pair it with a recall or length term, and always report abstention rate beside accuracy: answering 40% perfectly is a different product from answering everything at 70%.
atomic-fact precisionF = (1/m) ∑i=1..m 1[ ci is supported ] report alongside m (informativeness) answer-level accounting1 = correct + incorrect + abstained honest system: incorrect ↓ faster than correct

Families of benchmark

  • Adversarial misconception sets (TruthfulQA-style) test whether the model repeats common human falsehoods — imitation over truth.
  • Long-form factuality suites (FActScore-style) apply the atomic-fact decomposition to biographies and open-ended answers.
  • Faithfulness / RAG suites score answer-to-context entailment, context relevance, and citation correctness separately, separating retrieval failures from generation failures.
  • Calibration is measured with ECE and risk–coverage curves, not a leaderboard score — the axis most often skipped.
Build a domain eval set. Public benchmarks measure general tendencies; your hallucinations live in your entity names, your document formats, and your users' phrasing.

08 Pitfalls & what to remember

The recurring mistakes are conceptual, not technical — measuring the wrong axis, trusting the wrong signal, and declaring victory on the wrong benchmark.

Pitfalls

  • Confusing the axes. Reporting faithfulness while users care about factuality makes a system look fixed when it is not.
  • Trusting confidence as truth. Token probability measures commitment, not correctness.
  • Unverified citations. Unchecked spans are decoration, and may themselves be fabricated.
  • Blaming the model for retrieval. Measure retrieval recall first — most RAG "hallucinations" are answers whose evidence never arrived.
  • Abstention with no fallback, and judge-marks-own-homework — grading with the same model family that generated inflates scores.

What to remember

  • Plausibility is the objective; truth is not — every mitigation is an external correction bolted onto that fact.
  • Separate factuality from faithfulness, intrinsic from extrinsic; the four combinations have four different fixes.
  • Stack independent detectors: cheap uncertainty to gate, entailment and retrieval to adjudicate.
  • Calibration + abstention turn an unfixable error rate into a tunable coverage decision.
  • Grounding is the biggest lever, bounded by your retriever and your corpus.
