Large Language Models

Take a Transformer decoder, train it to predict the next token on a large fraction of the internet, and scale it up. Out fall in-context learning, instruction following, and reasoning — none of it explicitly programmed.

LLMs & Generative AI Transformers Scaling

01 What an LLM actually is

Strip away the mystique and an LLM is a very large next-token predictor.

  • Architecturally it's a Transformer decoder — a causal stack of self-attention + feed-forward blocks — with billions of parameters.
  • It's trained on a single, dumb-sounding objective: given some text, predict the next token. Repeat over trillions of tokens.
  • The surprise is emergence: at sufficient scale, that one objective produces translation, summarization, coding, arithmetic, and step-by-step reasoning as side effects — capabilities nobody hand-coded.
One sentence: an LLM compresses the statistical structure of language (and the knowledge encoded in it) into weights, then re-expresses it one token at a time.

02 The next-token objective

Everything an LLM does is sampling from P(next token | everything so far).

The model factorizes the probability of a sequence autoregressively and is trained to maximize the likelihood of real text — equivalently, to minimize cross-entropy / perplexity:

P(x₁…x_n) = Πᵢ P(xᵢ | x₁ … xᵢ₋₁) loss = − (1/N) Σᵢ log P(xᵢ | x_<ᵢ) (cross-entropy) perplexity = exp(loss)
  • Self-supervised: the labels are just the next tokens in the corpus — no human annotation needed, which is what lets it scale to trillions of tokens.
  • Causal mask: position i may only attend to positions ≤ i, so prediction can't peek at the answer.
  • To generate, you feed the model's own output back in and predict the next token again — autoregression.

03 Pre-training

The expensive phase: one enormous self-supervised run that builds the base model.

  • Data: a curated, deduplicated, filtered mix of web text, books, code, and more — quality and diversity matter as much as quantity.
  • Tokenization: text is split into sub-word tokens (BPE / SentencePiece) so any string is representable with no out-of-vocabulary gaps. See Tokenization.
  • Compute: thousands of GPUs/TPUs for weeks, trained with Adam-style optimizers, learning-rate warmup + decay, and heavy parallelism (data / tensor / pipeline).
  • The result is a base model — fluent at continuation, but not yet helpful or safe as an assistant. That's what post-training fixes.

04 Scaling laws & emergence

Loss falls predictably with scale — and new abilities appear at thresholds.

Scaling laws

Test loss follows a smooth power law in model size N, dataset size D, and compute C:

L(N) ≈ (N_c / N)^α_N (loss vs parameters, power law)

The Chinchilla finding: for a fixed compute budget, most large models were under-trained — you should scale parameters and tokens together (roughly ~20 tokens per parameter), not just grow the model.

Emergent abilities

Some capabilities (multi-step arithmetic, instruction following, chain-of-thought reasoning) are near-absent in small models and appear relatively suddenly past a scale threshold — though how "sharp" emergence really is depends on the metric.

Practical upshot: predictable loss curves make scaling an investment decision — you can forecast the payoff of more compute before spending it.

05 In-context learning

The behaviour that made LLMs a general-purpose tool: learning a task from the prompt, with no weight updates.

  • Zero-shot: just describe the task. Few-shot: include a handful of input→output examples in the prompt and the model infers the pattern.
  • Nothing is trained — the model conditions on the examples in its context window and adapts on the fly. This is why context engineering and RAG are so powerful: changing the context changes the behaviour.
  • Chain-of-thought: prompting the model to "think step by step" elicits intermediate reasoning and sharply improves multi-step tasks.

06 From base model to assistant

Pre-training makes it fluent; post-training makes it useful and aligned.

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): train on curated instruction→response demonstrations so the model learns the assistant format and follows instructions.
  2. Preference alignment: optimize against human (or AI) preferences so outputs are more helpful, honest, and harmless — RLHF (PPO) or direct methods like DPO. See Policy / Preference Optimization.

For cheap adaptation to a domain without retraining everything, use parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, adapters).

07 Decoding: turning probabilities into text

The model outputs a distribution; a decoding strategy chooses the actual token. Drag the controls to reshape it.

Next-token distribution · temperature & nucleus (top-p) Temperature sharpens/flattens the softmax; top-p keeps the smallest set of tokens covering probability p
  • Greedy — always take the argmax. Deterministic, often repetitive/bland.
  • Temperature — divide logits by T before softmax. T<1 sharpens (more confident), T>1 flattens (more random/creative), T→0 ≈ greedy.
  • Top-k — sample only from the k highest-probability tokens.
  • Top-p (nucleus) — sample from the smallest set whose cumulative probability ≥ p; adapts the cutoff to how peaked the distribution is.
  • Beam search — keep several high-probability sequences; better for closed-ended tasks (translation), worse for open-ended generation.
P(token) = softmax(logits / T) T = temperature

08 Capabilities & limits

Knowing the failure shapes is as important as the strengths.

StrengthLimitation
Fluent, broad knowledge across domainsHallucination — confidently fabricates when unsure
In-context learning & instruction followingKnowledge cutoff — frozen at training time (mitigate with RAG)
Step-by-step reasoning (with CoT)Brittle arithmetic/logic; sensitive to prompt phrasing
Long-context understandingFinite context window; "lost in the middle" of long inputs
Code, translation, summarizationQuadratic attention cost; expensive inference (see KV cache, MoE)

09 The model landscape

Most frontier LLMs share the same recipe; they differ in scale, data, and post-training.

  • Decoder-only is dominant — a single causal Transformer stack trained on next-token prediction (GPT, Llama, Mistral, Gemini, Claude families).
  • Efficiency levers: Mixture-of-Experts for sparse scaling, grouped-query attention + KV cache for fast inference, RoPE for long context.
  • Open vs closed weights, and a steady march toward longer context, multimodality (see VLMs), and agentic use (see Agentic AI).

10 Takeaways

  • An LLM is a scaled Transformer decoder trained to predict the next token — one self-supervised objective, trillions of tokens.
  • Capability emerges from scale; scaling laws make the loss (and the investment) predictable, and Chinchilla says scale data with parameters.
  • In-context learning — adapt from the prompt with no weight updates — is what makes it general-purpose.
  • Base → assistant via SFT + preference alignment; adapt cheaply with PEFT.
  • Decoding matters — temperature and top-p trade determinism for diversity over the model's next-token distribution.
  • Mind the limits — hallucination, knowledge cutoff, finite context, inference cost — which is exactly what RAG, longer context, MoE, and agentic scaffolding address.
