LLM-as-a-Judge

Human evaluation is the gold standard but slow and expensive; string-overlap metrics are cheap but blind to meaning. Putting a strong model in the rater's seat — an autorater — buys scale, if you control for the ways it is systematically wrong.

LLMs & Generative AI Evaluation Autoraters

01 Why judge with an LLM?

Open-ended generation broke the old evaluation loop. There is no single correct answer to compare against, so the reference-matching metrics that carried machine translation and summarisation for two decades stopped measuring anything useful.

The gap the judge fills

  • Overlap metrics (see Evaluation Metrics) score n-gram agreement with a reference. A correct answer phrased differently scores low; a fluent wrong answer that reuses the reference's words scores high.
  • Human raters judge what actually matters — correctness, helpfulness, tone, instruction-following — but a careful pairwise rating costs minutes and money, which rules out running the eval on every commit.
  • A capable model given an explicit rubric sits in between: it reads meaning like a human and runs like a metric, cheap enough to gate a deploy and fast enough to sit inside a training loop.
what a judge computesJ : ( instruction , response [ , reference ] , rubric ) → score or preference score ∈ {1…5} preference ∈ { A ≻ B , B ≻ A , tie }
One sentence: a judge is a rubric-following stand-in for a human rater — powerful, cheap, and biased in known, correctable ways.

02 Modes of judging

Three axes: how many responses the judge sees at once, whether it has a gold answer, and whether it emits a number or a choice. The combination determines both reliability and what you can do with the output.

ModeInputOutputStrengthWeakness
Pointwise scoringOne response + rubricScore on a scaleAbsolute, aggregatable, dashboard-friendlyScale drifts between runs and models
Pairwise preferenceTwo responsesWinner or tieMost reliable; feeds reward modelsO(n²) comparisons; needs order control

Why pairwise usually wins

  • Relative judgements are easier than calibrated absolute ones — for models as for people. "Is B better than A?" needs no shared notion of what a 4 means, which is exactly the notion that drifts across prompts, sessions, and model versions.
  • Pairwise verdicts also convert cleanly into a scalar ranking. Fit a latent strength s per system under a Bradley–Terry model and you get an Elo-style leaderboard from win/loss records alone — the same functional form a reward model uses.
pairwise → latent scores (Bradley–Terry)P( A ≻ B ) = σ( sA − sB ) = 1 / ( 1 + e−(sA−sB) ) fit s by maximising ∑(w,l) log σ( sw − sl ) ⟵ identical to the reward-model loss
Use checklists when the criteria are objective, pairwise when comparing systems, and pointwise only when you need an absolute number you will re-anchor regularly.

03 Designing the judge prompt

A judge is a prompt, and most judge failures are prompt failures. Four design choices do nearly all the work.

Decompose the rubric

  • "Rate the quality 1–5" collapses several independent dimensions into one number the judge resolves arbitrarily. Split into named criteria — factual correctness, instruction-following, completeness, safety, format — and score each, then aggregate with weights you choose rather than weights the model invented.
  • Define every scale point in words. A 3 that means "mostly correct with one minor omission" is reproducible; a 3 that means "okay" is not.

Reason first, then commit

  • Ask for a short analysis before the verdict. If the verdict comes first, the reasoning becomes a post-hoc rationalisation of an already-sampled token, and quality drops.
  • The written rationale is also the audit trail: when the judge disagrees with a human, the rationale tells you whether the rubric or the judge was at fault.

Anchor and structure

  • Few-shot anchors — two or three graded examples per scale point pin the scale far more effectively than adjectives, and are the cheapest fix for score drift.
  • Structured output — force a schema ({criterion: score, rationale, verdict}) so results parse deterministically and a missing field is an error rather than a silent zero.
  • Keep temperature low and, where the stakes justify it, sample the judge k times and take the majority verdict.
Treat the judge prompt as versioned code. Change it and every historical score becomes incomparable — so pin it, and re-run the calibration set whenever it moves.

04 The biases (and a live one)

Judges are not noisy-but-unbiased raters. Their errors are systematic, which is worse: averaging over more examples does not make them go away.

Position bias can flip the verdict Two answers of similar true quality; the judge adds a bias toward the first-shown one

The catalogue

  • Position bias — a systematic preference for whichever candidate appears first (sometimes last). It is the easiest to measure: present both orders and count how often the verdict survives the swap.
  • Verbosity bias — longer answers are rated better at equal content, because length correlates with thoroughness in the judge's training data. This one is dangerous in a training loop: optimising against a verbose-biased judge produces a verbose model.
  • Self-preference — a judge scores text from its own model family higher, plausibly because that text is more probable under it. Generating and grading with the same model inflates results.
  • Style over substance — confident tone, tidy markdown, headings, and plausible-looking citations lift scores independently of correctness. A judge is a good reader of form and a mediocre checker of facts.
  • Compression at the ends — pointwise judges cluster on 3s and 4s and rarely use the extremes, so real differences get squeezed out of the scale.
measuring position biasconsistency = (1/N) ∑i 1[ verdict(A,B)i = verdict(B,A)i ] consistency ≈ 1 ⟹ order-invariant consistency ≈ 0.5 ⟹ the judge is reading position, not content
Every bias here is a direction, not noise. Report swap-consistency and mean response length alongside win rate, or you will not see them.

05 Controlling the biases

Each bias has a specific, cheap countermeasure. Applied together they turn an unreliable rater into a usable instrument.

Randomise and average

  • Swap-and-average: judge both orderings and average the two verdicts. Cases that disagree are recorded as ties rather than coin-flips, which removes position bias by construction and simultaneously surfaces the genuinely ambiguous pairs.
  • Self-consistency: sample the judge several times and take the majority. This cuts variance but not bias — do it in addition to swapping, never instead of it.

Constrain and anchor

  • Length controls: either normalise for length in the analysis, or add an explicit rubric line stating that concise complete answers are preferred to padded ones. Length-controlled win rates are now standard on serious leaderboards.
  • Reference answers pull the verdict toward content and away from style, and checklists convert a vague preference into a set of yes/no facts that are hard to charm.
  • Juries: average several judges from different model families. This is the only real fix for self-preference, and it also smooths any one model's idiosyncratic rubric reading.
order-debiased pairwise scorewin(A) = ½ · [ 1[ J(A,B) = A ] + 1[ J(B,A) = A ] ] ∈ { 0 , ½ , 1 } jury of m judgesscore = (1/m) ∑j=1..m scorej ⟵ different families, not m samples of one
Swap-and-average plus a jury of two or three different families removes most of the measurable bias for roughly 4–6× the judging cost — still orders of magnitude below human raters.

06 Agreement with humans

A judge is only worth what it correlates with. Before trusting one at scale, measure it against human labels on a held-out set — and measure the humans against each other too.

Which statistic to use

  • For categorical verdicts (A / B / tie), raw agreement overstates quality because chance agreement is high with three classes. Cohen's κ subtracts the agreement expected from the marginals.
  • For ordinal scores, use Spearman's ρ or Kendall's τ on ranks rather than Pearson correlation — judges compress the scale, so rank order survives even when the absolute numbers do not.
  • Correlate at the level you will act on. System-level correlation (does the judge rank models the way humans do?) is usually high; example-level correlation is much lower, so a judge that reliably picks the better model may still be unreliable on any single response.
Cohen's kappa — agreement above chanceκ = ( po − pe ) / ( 1 − pe ) po = observed agreement pe = ∑c p1(c) · p2(c) Spearman rank correlationρ = 1 − 6 ∑i di² / ( n ( n² − 1 ) ) di = rank difference on item i

The ceiling

  • Human raters disagree with each other constantly on open-ended quality. That inter-annotator agreement is the ceiling: a judge matching humans about as often as two humans match each other is doing as well as the task allows.
  • So report the human–human baseline next to the judge–human number. A κ that looks mediocre in the abstract may be at parity with the humans it is replacing.
Validate on a stratified sample that includes the hard cases. A judge calibrated only on obvious wins looks excellent and tells you nothing about the pairs you actually care about.

07 Where judges are used

Judging shows up in four distinct roles, and the reliability bar rises sharply as you move down the list.

Measurement

  • Regression tests in CI: a fixed prompt set plus a pinned judge catches quality drops from a prompt edit, a model upgrade, or a retrieval change before users do.
  • Leaderboards and A/B tests: arena-style pairwise judging converts head-to-head battles into rankings via the Bradley–Terry fit from §02.

Training signal

  • Preference data: judge-generated preferences substitute for human labels when training reward models or running direct preference methods — see Policy & Preference Optimization. This is the highest-stakes use, because the policy will optimise against every bias the judge has.
  • Rejection sampling and data filtering: generate many candidates, keep the ones a judge scores highest, and fine-tune on those. Cheaper and safer than full preference training, and it fails gracefully.

Runtime

  • Guardrails: a judge screens outputs against policy before they ship, or checks a claim against retrieved evidence — see Hallucination & Factuality. Here latency is on the critical path, so a small distilled judge usually beats a frontier one.
  • Agent self-critique: a judge step inside a plan-act-observe loop decides whether to accept, retry, or escalate — cheap when it is a verifier, dangerous when it is the same model grading its own plan.
Offline measurement tolerates a noisy judge. Training signal does not: a systematic judge bias becomes a systematic model behaviour.

08 Judges vs metrics vs humans

A judge is not a replacement for the other two — it is the middle rung of a ladder you climb as the stakes rise.

Cost, latency, and drift

  • A judge call costs roughly one extra generation — and reason-then-verdict prompts are long, so budget for the rationale tokens. Swap-and-average doubles it; a three-model jury with swapping is six calls per item.
  • Distil where volume is high: fine-tune a small model on a strong judge's verdicts and run it for a fraction of the cost. Accept a small agreement drop for a large throughput gain.
  • Pin the judge model and version. A silently upgraded judge shifts every score, and you will read it as a change in your system rather than in your instrument.
Cheap metrics for what they can measure, judges for everything open-ended, humans for the calibration set that keeps the judges honest. See also Model Evaluation.

09 Pitfalls & what to remember

Autoraters fail quietly. The output is always a confident number, so a broken judge looks exactly like a working one until you check it against people.

Pitfalls

  • Never validated against humans. Without an agreement number the score is a vibe with decimal places.
  • Grading with the generator. Same model or family on both sides inflates results through self-preference.
  • Optimising against the judge. Used as a training reward, a judge is a proxy that will be gamed — verbosity and formatting go up, substance does not.
  • Unpinned prompt or model. Scores across time become incomparable the moment either changes.
  • One order only. Reporting pairwise win rates without swapping bakes position bias straight into the headline number.
  • Judging what a checker could verify. If code can be run, tests can be executed, or an answer can be string-matched, do that instead — a deterministic verifier beats a judge every time.

What to remember

  • Pairwise beats pointwise; reason-then-verdict beats verdict-only; explicit rubrics beat adjectives.
  • Biases are systematic — position, verbosity, self-preference, style — so correct them structurally, not by averaging.
  • Measure agreement with κ and ρ, and compare it against the human–human ceiling, not against 1.0.
  • Raise the bar with the stakes: noisy is fine for dashboards, not for reward signal.
  • Feeds preference optimization and factuality checks; complements Evaluation Metrics and Model Evaluation.
The instrument rule: a judge is a measuring device. Calibrate it, pin it, and never let the thing being measured be optimised directly against it without a second, independent check.
