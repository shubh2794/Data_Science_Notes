Model Merging

Take several models fine-tuned from the same base and combine them in weight space — average them, add their deltas, or interpolate between them — to get one model with the skills of all of them, no training data and no gradient steps required. It works far more often than it has any right to, and the reasons why also mark exactly where it breaks.

01 What merging is, and why it is attractive

A merge is a function from a handful of checkpoints to a new checkpoint — computed with arithmetic on tensors, in minutes, on a CPU.

  • The setup: one pretrained model θ_pre, and several fine-tunes of it — one for code, one for maths, one for a language, one for chat style. Each lives in its own checkpoint, and serving all of them means hosting all of them.
  • The merge: produce one parameter vector θ_merged = f(θ_pre, θ₁, …, θ_K), using element-wise operations: averages, weighted sums, interpolation, masking. Same architecture, same size, same inference cost as any single input.
  • Why it is attractive:
    • No training data. The original fine-tuning sets may be private, lost, or licensed separately; a merge needs only the weights.
    • No GPU-days. Merging a 7B model is a few minutes of tensor arithmetic. Multi-task fine-tuning on the union of the datasets is a full training run.
    • Composable skills. Fine-tunes trained by different people at different times can be combined after the fact — and recombined with different weights for different products.
    • Often a free quality bump. Averaging several fine-tunes of the same task routinely beats the best single one, and is more robust under distribution shift.
  • The catch: it only works when the models are close in weight space — in practice, when they were fine-tuned from the same pretrained checkpoint. Section 02 is about why.
One sentence: fine-tuning moves a model a short distance from a shared starting point; merging adds those short moves together, betting that they don't get in each other's way.

02 Why it works: loss-landscape geometry

Averaging two neural networks' weights is usually nonsense. It stops being nonsense when both networks sit in the same low-loss basin — and a shared pretrained initialisation is what puts them there.

Linear mode connectivity

Two solutions θ_A and θ_B are linearly mode connected if the loss stays low along the straight line between them. The quantity to watch is the barrier: how far the loss along the path rises above the straight-line mix of the endpoint losses.

interpolation pathθ(t) = (1 − t)·θA + t·θB, t ∈ [0, 1] loss barrierbarrier = maxt [ L(θ(t)) − ((1 − t)·L(θA) + t·L(θB)) ] connectedbarrier ≈ 0 → every point on the segment is (roughly) as good as the ends
  • Two networks trained from different random initialisations almost always show a large barrier: the midpoint of their weights is about as good as a random network.
  • Two networks that share the first part of training — in the limit, a whole pretraining run — and only diverge afterwards are typically linearly connected. Fine-tuning from a large pretrained model is the extreme case: every fine-tune starts from the same point and moves only a little.
  • Pretraining seems to place the model in a wide, flat basin; fine-tuning on different tasks moves to different points in that basin, and the region between them stays low-loss for all of the tasks often enough to be useful.
Interpolating between two fine-tunes on an illustrative loss surface Brighter = lower loss. The origin is the all-zeros weight vector; the arc keeps the norm, the chord cuts inside

The shared-initialisation requirement: permutation symmetry

Why do independently initialised networks fail? Because a network's weights are only defined up to a relabelling of its hidden units. Swap two neurons in a hidden layer — permute the rows of the incoming weights and the columns of the outgoing ones — and the function is identical, but the weight vector is different.

a two-layer block and a permutation matrix Pf(x) = W2 · σ(W1x + b1) same function, different weightsW1 → P·W1, b1 → P·b1, W2 → W2·Pᵀ (σ is element-wise, so σ(Pz) = P·σ(z), and PᵀP = I) symmetries per hidden layer of width nn!
  • Two independently trained networks have, in effect, assigned their features to hidden units in unrelated orders. Averaging them averages unit 17 of one with unit 17 of the other — two unrelated features — and destroys both.
  • Re-basin (weight matching / activation matching): before averaging, solve for permutations that line up network B's units with network A's (a linear-assignment problem per layer, on weight or activation similarity). For sufficiently wide networks, the aligned pair is often close to linearly connected. This is the "independent, then permutation-aligned" scenario in the figure.
  • Re-basin is mostly demonstrated on small vision models; for LLMs nobody bothers, because fine-tunes of one base already share the same unit ordering — the pretrained init fixes it, and short fine-tuning never reshuffles it.
Takeaway: merging is not "averaging networks", it is "averaging small perturbations of one network". Without a shared base (or an explicit alignment) the premise is false.

03 Task vectors and task arithmetic

Subtract the base from a fine-tune and you get a direction in weight space that "means" the task. Directions can be added, negated and scaled.

task vector for task iτi = θift − θpre addition (multi-task)θ = θpre + ∑i λi · τi negation (forgetting)θ = θpre − λ · τforget analogy a : b :: c : dτd ≈ τc + (τb − τa) scaling coefficients λtypically 0.3 – 1.0 each, tuned on held-out data
  • Addition: adding the code vector and the maths vector gives a model that is decent at both — usually a little worse at each than the specialist, much better than the base.
  • Negation: fine-tune on the behaviour you want less of (e.g. toxic text), then subtract that task vector. Performance on control tasks is largely preserved. This is a cheap "unlearning" heuristic, not a guarantee — the behaviour is suppressed, not provably removed.
  • Analogies: with tasks related as a : b :: c : d (e.g. sentiment on domain A : language modelling on domain A :: sentiment on B : language modelling on B), combining three vectors can approximate the fourth without any data for it.
  • λ matters. Summing K vectors at λ = 1 moves K times further than any fine-tune did — often off the basin. Using λ = 1/K recovers plain averaging (section 04). Values in between are the usual sweet spot.
  • Task arithmetic works because, near the pretrained point, the network behaves approximately linearly in its weights, and task vectors from unrelated tasks are close to orthogonal in a space with billions of dimensions — so adding them interferes surprisingly little.
Takeaway: a task vector is a learned edit. Merging is editing with several of them at once — and the scale of each edit is a hyperparameter you must not forget to tune.

04 Weight averaging and model soups

The simplest merge: average the weights. When the inputs are fine-tunes of the same task with different hyperparameters, the average is often better than any of them.

uniform soup of K fine-tunesθsoup = (1/K) · ∑k=1..K θk = θpre + (1/K) · ∑k τk greedy soupsort ingredients by held-out accuracy; start with the best; add the next one only if the average with it improves held-out accuracy ema of one run (β ≈ 0.999)θ̄t = β · θ̄t−1 + (1 − β) · θt
  • Model soups: run a hyperparameter sweep of fine-tunes from one base; instead of keeping only the winner, average them. Inference cost is one model, unlike an output ensemble of K models.
  • Uniform vs greedy: the uniform soup is hurt by bad ingredients (a run with too-high learning rate that left the basin). The greedy soup filters them out using a held-out set, and is the more reliable recipe. Note that the greedy loop is itself a small optimisation on the held-out set — keep a separate test set.
  • Same idea, one trajectory: stochastic weight averaging (SWA) averages checkpoints from late in a single training run (often with a cyclic or constant learning rate), and an exponential moving average (EMA) of weights does it continuously. Both find flatter, better-generalising points near the centre of a basin; soups do the same across runs.
  • Interpolating with the base — θ = (1 − α)·θ_pre + α·θ_ft — is the two-ingredient soup, and it is the standard fix for robustness lost during fine-tuning (see Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning).
Takeaway: averaging is the base case of every merge method. Most of what follows is about the case where averaging fails: different tasks whose deltas collide.

05 SLERP: interpolating on the sphere

Spherical linear interpolation walks along the arc between two weight vectors instead of the chord, so the interpolated weights keep the size of the originals.

angle between the two models (per tensor)cos Ω = (θ0 · θ1) / (‖θ0‖ ‖θ1‖) slerpslerp(θ0, θ1; t) = [sin((1 − t)·Ω) / sin Ω] · θ0 + [sin(t·Ω) / sin Ω] · θ1 checkst = 0 → θ0, t = 1 → θ1, Ω → 0 → (1 − t)·θ0 + t·θ1 (fall back to lerp) norm loss of lerp, equal norms‖½θ0 + ½θ1‖ = ‖θ0‖ · cos(Ω/2) slerp keeps ‖θ(t)‖ = ‖θ0‖ for all t
  • Why norm preservation matters: the linear midpoint of two vectors that point in different directions is shorter than either. In weight space that shrinks the scale of the weights, which changes activation magnitudes layer after layer. Normalisation layers absorb some of this, but not all; SLERP sidesteps it. Switch the figure in section 02 to SLERP and watch the path follow the low-loss ring instead of cutting inside it.
  • In practice it is applied tensor by tensor (each weight matrix flattened to one vector, with its own Ω), often with different t for different layer types — e.g. a ramp that favours one parent in early layers and the other in late layers.
  • For fine-tunes of one base, Ω between full weight tensors is tiny (the deltas are small relative to the weights), so SLERP and lerp barely differ. SLERP's advantage shows up when the two parents have drifted far apart — longer fine-tunes, continued pretraining.
  • Limitation: exactly two models. SLERP is defined for a pair. Merging more means chaining pairwise merges (order-dependent) or switching to a task-vector method.
Takeaway: SLERP is the "blend two parents smoothly" tool. Its whole point is to stay on the sphere where the weights live; its limit is that it only ever has two parents.

06 Interference: TIES-merging and DARE

When you add several task vectors, two things go wrong: many entries are tiny noise that piles up, and on the important entries the tasks pull in opposite directions. TIES and DARE attack each in turn.

TIES-merging: trim, elect sign, disjoint merge

1 · trim (per task, keep top-k by magnitude)τ̂i = keep the largest k% of |τi| entries, zero the rest (k ≈ 20 is common) 2 · elect a sign per parameter pγp = sgn( ∑i τ̂i,p ) (the side with more total mass wins) 3 · disjoint mean over agreeing tasksAp = { i : τ̂i,p ≠ 0 and sgn(τ̂i,p) = γp } τm,p = (1 / |Ap|) · ∑i ∈ Ap τ̂i,p 4 · applyθ = θpre + λ · τm
  • Trim removes redundant small updates — fine-tuning changes many weights a little for no particular reason, and summed across tasks that noise becomes signal-sized.
  • Elect resolves sign conflicts: if two tasks push parameter p up and one pushes it down hard, a plain average lands near zero and serves nobody. TIES picks a direction and discards the dissenters at that parameter.
  • Disjoint mean averages only over tasks that actually updated the parameter in the elected direction, so a delta that only one task cares about is kept at full size instead of being divided by K.

DARE: drop and rescale

random mask, drop rate pmj ~ Bernoulli(1 − p) rescale the survivorsτ̃ = (m ⊙ τ) / (1 − p) expectation preservedE[τ̃j] = (1 − p) · τj / (1 − p) = τj but variance growsVar[τ̃j] = τj² · p / (1 − p)
  • Empirically, the delta of a supervised fine-tune is extremely redundant: you can randomly drop 90% of its entries and rescale the rest with little loss on the task. Each sparsified vector then overlaps far less with the others, so adding them interferes less.
  • DARE is a pre-processing step on each task vector, combined with task arithmetic ("DARE-linear") or with TIES' sign election ("DARE-TIES"). It only works on deltas: dropping 90% of the raw weights would destroy the model.
  • It degrades when deltas are large and dense — long fine-tunes or continued pretraining — because the variance term τ² · p/(1 − p) is no longer negligible. One random draw is one sample; results can vary with the seed.
TIES / DARE explorer: three task vectors over 24 parameters Thin bars = per-task deltas after DARE and trimming (faded = removed). Wide outline = merged delta. Red dot = surviving sign conflict
Takeaway: TIES is deterministic surgery on conflicts; DARE is random sparsification that is unbiased on average. They solve different problems and are routinely stacked.

07 Merging LoRA adapters vs full weights

A LoRA adapter stores its update as two thin factors. The update is their product — and products do not average like sums.

a lora update (see peft)ΔWi = Bi · Ai Bi ∈ ℝd×r, Ai ∈ ℝr×k right: merge the productsΔW = ½ (B1A1 + B2A2) wrong: average the factors½(B1 + B2) · ½(A1 + A2) = ¼ (B1A1 + B2A2 + B1A2 + B2A1) exact sum as a bigger adapter[B1 B2] · [A1 ; A2] = B1A1 + B2A2 rank ≤ r1 + r2
  • Factor averaging is wrong because of the cross terms B₁A₂ + B₂A₁: they pair the output basis of one adapter with the input basis of the other, which never trained together. They are not small, just meaningless. (LoRA factors also have a gauge freedom — BA = (BM)(M⁻¹A) for any invertible M — so two adapters' factors are not even in comparable coordinates.)
  • Safe recipes: compute each ΔWᵢ = BᵢAᵢ, merge those as task vectors (any method above, including TIES/DARE), then add to W. Or concatenate the factors into one rank-r₁+r₂ adapter, which reproduces the weighted sum exactly while staying detachable. If the result must be low rank again, refactorise with a truncated SVD (lossy).
  • Adapters vs full fine-tunes: LoRA deltas are low-rank and already small, so they tend to merge cleanly with each other; but a LoRA delta and a full fine-tune of the same base can also be merged, because both are just ΔWs relative to the same W. Adapters trained on different bases (or quantised bases) cannot be mixed.
  • Keep them separate instead? Serving many adapters on one base and switching per request (see PEFT serving) avoids interference entirely — at the price of needing to know which adapter each request wants. Learned routing over adapters is the bridge to section 09.
Takeaway: always merge ΔW = BA, never A and B separately. Concatenation is the lossless way to keep the merged result in adapter form. Background: LoRA.

08 Frankenmerging: passthrough layer stacking

Instead of blending weights, copy whole layers from one or more models and stack them into a deeper network. Odd, crude, and sometimes surprisingly good.

depth up-scaling of an L-layer model, overlap mcopy 1: layers 1 … L − m copy 2: layers m + 1 … L stacked depth2·(L − m) e.g. L = 32, m = 8 → 48 layers, ≈ 1.5× the parameters
  • Passthrough merges take layer ranges verbatim — no averaging — and concatenate them, so the result is larger than its parents. Sources can be one model (self-stacking) or several fine-tunes of a shared base.
  • Why it sometimes works: in a pre-norm transformer each block adds a residual update to a shared residual stream, and adjacent middle layers compute similar, incremental refinements. Repeating a stretch of middle layers is close to "iterating the refinement a few more times", which the stream tolerates. The seams are worst at the very first and last layers, which is why the overlap drops those.
  • Why it needs healing: immediately after stacking, the seam is a distribution shift — layer m+1 of copy 2 receives a residual stream that has already passed through L − m layers, not m. Performance typically drops at first; a round of continued pretraining or fine-tuning recovers it, and then the extra depth can pay off. Without that healing step, gains are anecdotal and fragile.
  • This is an architecture change, so everything downstream (inference cost, memory, quantisation) grows with it. It is a way to initialise a bigger model cheaply, not a free lunch.
Takeaway: passthrough stacking is weight-reuse for scaling up, not skill-combination. Treat it as a warm start that must be trained, and evaluate it as a new model.

09 Relatives: mixture-of-experts and distillation

Merging is one of three ways to turn several specialists into one system. The other two keep the specialists apart at run time, or retrain a student on their outputs.

  • Mixture-of-experts. An MoE keeps experts separate and routes each token to a few of them, so there is no interference — but memory holds every expert, and the router must be trained. Merging is the static, zero-routing end of the same spectrum.
  • Merging into an MoE. Dense fine-tunes of one base can be stitched together as the expert FFNs of a new MoE layer, with the attention and embedding weights (which they mostly share) averaged; a router is then trained briefly. Similarly, a mixture of adapters keeps several LoRA modules on one base and learns per-input weights over them — merging coefficients that depend on the input.
  • Distillation. Knowledge distillation combines several teachers by training one student to match their outputs. It needs data and compute, but it has a superpower merging lacks: it does not care about weight space at all, so teachers can differ in architecture, size and tokenizer.

10 Choosing a method, and when merging beats multi-task fine-tuning

Each method answers a different failure of plain averaging. Pick by how many models, how far apart they are, and whether they conflict.

When merging wins

  • The inputs are fine-tunes of one base, with modest deltas (short SFT, LoRA), on reasonably different but compatible skills — a language plus a domain plus a style.
  • The training data is unavailable, or retraining on the union is too expensive, or you need many different mixtures cheaply (a merge per customer).
  • Same-task soups: the merge is close to free and frequently beats the best single run.

When it doesn't

  • Interference. Merged multi-task models generally land below the individual specialists on each task, and below a model trained jointly on all the data. The gap grows with the number of tasks and with how much they compete for the same parameters.
  • Distant tasks or large deltas. Long fine-tunes and continued pretraining move far from the base; the linear approximation fails and barriers appear. Two models fine-tuned from different versions of a base count as distant.
  • Impossible cases: different architectures, different hidden sizes, or different tokenizers / vocabularies. There is no element-wise correspondence to merge — the embedding row for token 5000 means different things. Use distillation instead.
  • If you do have the data and the compute, multi-task fine-tuning remains the stronger baseline; merging is the cheaper approximation to it, not a replacement.

11 Evaluation pitfalls

Merging makes it trivially cheap to produce thousands of candidate models — which turns every benchmark into a target to search against.

  • Contamination inherits. A merge contains whatever its parents learned. If any ancestor was fine-tuned on benchmark test items (deliberately or not), every descendant carries it, and the lineage of community merges is often several generations deep and poorly documented. Public leaderboards were flooded with merges of merges in 2024 for exactly this reason. Prefer fresh, held-out or private evaluations.
  • Overfitting the coefficients. λ values, trim percentages, layer-wise SLERP ramps and greedy-soup selection are all tuned on some evaluation set. Search over enough recipes — automated evolutionary searches over merge configurations make this easy — and you are fitting that set. Tune on a validation split and report on an untouched test split.
  • Regressions on what you didn't test. A merge evaluated on the two skills you combined may have silently lost a third (long-context recall, multilingual quality, tool-call formatting). Run a broad regression suite, not just the target tasks.
  • Safety alignment erodes. Safety behaviour is itself a delta — the refusal and harmlessness training layered on by preference optimisation. Merging an aligned chat model with an unaligned domain fine-tune dilutes that delta (with λ = ½, halves it), and TIES can trim or out-vote it outright. Re-run red-teaming and jailbreak evaluations after every merge (see LLM Security); adding back a "safety vector" is a mitigation, not a proof.
  • Seed variance. DARE masks are random; two draws of the same recipe can differ by more than the gap you are trying to measure. Report the spread across seeds.
Takeaway: a merge is a new model. It needs the full evaluation — capabilities, regressions, safety — that any new model needs, on data its recipe was not tuned on.

12 Pitfalls & what to remember

  • Shared base or nothing. Merging works because fine-tunes of one pretrained model sit in one linearly connected basin; independent inits need permutation alignment first, and different architectures or tokenizers cannot be merged.
  • Think in task vectors τ = θ_ft − θ_pre: add them for skills, subtract for forgetting, scale them with λ — and tune λ.
  • Averaging is the baseline; soups of same-task runs are almost free wins. SWA and EMA are the single-run versions.
  • SLERP preserves weight norms along the arc but merges only two models.
  • TIES trims noise and resolves sign conflicts; DARE drops and rescales deltas, unbiased but noisy. They stack.
  • LoRA: merge ΔW = BA, or concatenate factors — never average A and B separately.
  • Passthrough stacking builds a bigger model that needs healing training.
  • Evaluate the merge as a new model, including safety, on data the recipe never saw.
