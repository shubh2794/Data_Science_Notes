Policy / Preference Optimization

Pre-training teaches a model to imitate text; preference optimization teaches it which outputs people actually prefer. This is the alignment step that turns a capable base model into a helpful, honest, harmless assistant — via RLHF or its direct successor, DPO.

LLMs & Generative AI Alignment RLHF · DPO

01 Why preferences, not just imitation

Supervised fine-tuning copies demonstrations; preference optimization optimizes for what's better.

  • SFT teaches the format and basic instruction-following by imitating good responses — but "good" is hard to demonstrate exhaustively, and the model never learns what to avoid. Cross-entropy treats one reference answer as absolutely correct and gives no gradient at all about the space of bad outputs.
  • Demonstrations are also capped by the demonstrator: where a model already beats the average annotator, imitating annotators drags it down.
  • It's far easier to compare two responses than to author the perfect one, and annotators agree with each other far more on a comparison than on a rewrite. The unit of supervision becomes a triple (x, y_w, y_l) — prompt, chosen response, rejected response — usually with both responses sampled from the model itself.
the two objectives, side by sideSFT: L = − ∑t log πθ( yt | x, y<t ) ⟵ copy one reference answer preference: L = f( log πθ(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x) ) ⟵ widen a margin between two answers
One sentence: learn a policy that produces outputs humans prefer, using pairwise comparisons rather than gold answers. Everything below is a different way of turning that margin into a gradient.

02 The RLHF pipeline

The classic three stages from base model to aligned assistant. Each stage produces the input the next one needs.

  1. SFT: fine-tune the base model on instruction→response demonstrations to get a starting policy π_SFT. This teaches the chat format and moves the output distribution somewhere worth collecting preferences over — preferences on gibberish are useless.
  2. Reward model: sample k responses per prompt, have annotators rank pairs, and train r_φ(x, y) — normally the SFT model with its unembedding swapped for a scalar head — to score how preferred a response is. One session over k samples yields k(k−1)/2 pairs, so comparison data is cheap per label.
  3. RL optimization: use reinforcement learning (PPO) to push the policy up the reward model's score while a KL penalty keeps it near π_SFT. The loop is on-policy: the policy generates, the RM scores, the gradient updates, and the next batch comes from the improved policy.

In practice the whole cycle repeats — new policy, new samples, new preference batch, retrained reward model. Alignment is iterative, not a one-shot pass.

Takeaway: SFT sets the distribution, the reward model compresses human judgement into a scalar, and RL climbs that scalar without letting the policy escape the region the reward model understands.

03 The preference model

Comparisons become probabilities via Bradley-Terry: the bigger the reward margin, the more confidently one response is preferred. Drag the margin — or hit "train step."

Bradley-Terry: comparisons to a scalar

Bradley-Terry is the classic model for paired comparisons — give every item a latent strength and make the win probability a logistic function of the difference. Here the latent strength is the reward, so preference probability is a sigmoid of the reward margin.

bradley-terry preference likelihoodP( yw ≻ yl | x ) = exp r(x,yw) / [ exp r(x,yw) + exp r(x,yl) ] = σ( r(x,yw) − r(x,yl) ) reward-model loss over the preference setLRM(φ) = − 𝔼(x, yw, yl) ~ D [ log σ( rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl) ) ]
Reward margin → preference probability Optimization widens the margin between chosen and rejected responses

What the reward is — and isn't

  • The loss depends only on the difference, so the reward is identified up to an additive constant per prompt: r and r + c(x) fit equally well. Absolute values are meaningless; only within-prompt comparisons are.
  • It is a proxy, fit on finitely many pairs from one policy's outputs, inheriting every bias and disagreement in the annotation pool.
  • Headline metric: held-out pairwise accuracy, typically 65–80%. Humans themselves only agree 70–80% of the time, so an RM claiming 95% is usually fitting an artefact such as length.
  • Ensembles, or an uncertainty head, let you penalize disagreement and blunt over-optimization (§07).
Takeaway: the reward model squeezes a crowd of noisy comparisons into one differentiable number. Everything downstream optimizes that number — so its errors become the policy's behaviour.

04 RL with a KL leash (PPO)

Maximize reward — but don't drift so far that the model breaks.

the rlhf objectivemaxθ 𝔼x ~ D, y ~ πθ(·|x) [ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x) ) as a per-token shaped rewardRt = rφ(x, y) · 𝟙[ t = |y| ] − β · log ( πθ(yt|x, y<t) / πref(yt|x, y<t) )
  • The KL penalty keeps the new policy close to the SFT reference — without it the policy chases the reward model into gibberish that scores high but reads badly (reward hacking). β is the leash: small β buys more reward and more drift.
  • The KL term is folded in per token, so the sequence-level RM score lands on the final token while every token carries a shaping term — dense credit assignment instead of one scalar per rollout.

The clipped surrogate

Generation is a token-level MDP: state = prompt plus tokens so far, action = next token. PPO improves the policy from a batch of rollouts while forbidding large per-step changes, clipping the probability ratio ρ_t into a trust region (typically ε ≈ 0.2).

importance ratio and clipped objectiveρt(θ) = πθ(at|st) / πθold(at|st) LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t [ min( ρt·Ât , clip( ρt, 1−ε, 1+ε )·Ât ) ] ⟵ never reward a move outside the trust region
  • Â_t is the advantage — how much better this token was than the value model expected — usually estimated with GAE.
  • It works, but is complex and finicky: four models in play (policy, frozen reference, reward, value), a generation loop inside the training loop, and coupled knobs (β, ε, advantage normalization, temperature). See Reinforcement Learning for the foundations.
Takeaway: RLHF is "climb the reward, on a leash." The clip keeps each step small; the KL term keeps the destination near a model that already speaks English.

05 DPO: skip the reward model

Direct Preference Optimization shows you can optimize preferences with a simple supervised loss — no separate reward model, no RL loop.

Derivation sketch

The KL-regularized objective in §04 has a closed-form optimum. Invert it for the reward, substitute into Bradley-Terry, and the intractable per-prompt normalizer log Z(x) cancels — because the likelihood only ever sees a difference of rewards at the same prompt.

1 · optimal kl-regularized policyπ*(y|x) = (1 / Z(x)) · πref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β ) 2 · invert for the implicit rewardr(x,y) = β · log ( π*(y|x) / πref(y|x) ) + β · log Z(x) 3 · substitute into bradley-terry — log Z(x) cancelsLDPO(θ) = − 𝔼 [ log σ( β·log (πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x)) − β·log (πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x)) ) ]

Reading the loss

  • It raises the likelihood of y_w and lowers y_l relative to a frozen reference — the same "widen the margin" picture as the viz above. The language model is its own reward model, read off as β·log(π_θ/π_ref).
  • The gradient is weighted by σ(−margin), so pairs the model already ranks correctly contribute almost nothing and learning concentrates on currently mis-ranked pairs.
  • Much simpler and more stable than PPO: one trainable model plus a frozen reference, a standard loss, no rollouts, no value network.
  • Known wrinkle — likelihood displacement: only the difference is constrained, so log π_θ(y_w|x) often falls too, just slower than y_l. Mixing in an SFT term on y_w is the usual patch.
  • DPO is offline: the pairs came from some other policy, so the further training drifts the more off-policy the data is. Iterative DPO — regenerate pairs from the current policy, relabel, repeat — closes most of the measured gap.
Takeaway: DPO doesn't replace the RLHF objective — it solves it in closed form and turns the remainder into logistic regression on log-probability ratios.

Once you see the pattern — a margin, a link function, an anchor — the variants form a small design space: what signal you need, what you anchor against, how you shape the loss.

The pairing that dominates reasoning post-training is GRPO + RLVR: sample a group of solutions, score each by whether it passes the checker, use the group statistics as the baseline. See Reasoning in LLMs for it in action.

Takeaway: the axis that matters is where the reward comes from. A learned reward model can be gamed; a verifier cannot — which is why verifiable domains improved first and fastest.

07 Reward hacking & pitfalls

One theme underlies every failure here: the reward is a proxy, and optimizing a proxy hard enough eventually optimizes the gap between the proxy and what you meant.

Over-optimization

  • Reward hacking: the policy exploits flaws in an imperfect reward model — proxy score climbs, true quality falls. Plot gold reward against KL distance and the signature appears: it rises, peaks, then turns over while the proxy keeps rising.
  • That peak is what the KL penalty, early stopping, RM ensembles and periodic relabelling buy you. More data and bigger reward models push it further out; nothing removes it.

Learned biases

  • Length bias: raters mildly prefer longer, thorough-looking answers, so the RM learns "longer = better" and the policy pads. Length is the single most reliable confound in preference data — hence length-controlled evaluation, a subtracted length term r − α·|y|, or decorrelating length in the RM training set.
  • Sycophancy: agreeable, confident answers get preferred even when wrong, so the model mirrors the user's stated view instead of correcting it.
  • Format gaming: bullets, headers, hedges and confident closings all score well independent of content.
  • Diversity collapse: sharpening toward high-reward modes shrinks output entropy — fine for assistants, bad for creative sampling.

Distribution shift

  • The RM is accurate only near the responses it was trained on. As the policy improves it walks off that support and RM scores become extrapolations — exactly why the on-policy relabelling loop in §02 exists.
  • Alignment can also tax capabilities; mixing pre-training or SFT data back into the objective is the standard mitigation.
Takeaway: treat a rising reward curve with suspicion, not satisfaction. The useful question is "how high per unit of KL, and does an independent evaluator agree?"

08 Evaluating an aligned model

The training reward cannot grade itself. Alignment evaluation needs a signal the optimizer never saw.

  • Reward-model accuracy: held-out pairwise agreement with human labels. Cheap, and the first thing to check — a weak RM caps everything downstream. Break it out by category and by response length to expose confounds.
  • Win rate: the policy head-to-head against a fixed baseline on held-out prompts, judged by humans (authoritative, expensive) or a strong model (scalable — see LLM-as-a-Judge). Always report a length-controlled win rate, since both kinds of judge reward verbosity.
  • Capability and safety suites: reasoning, coding, factual recall, refusal behaviour, jailbreak resistance — the things preference tuning can silently break. Alignment gains are only real if these hold flat.
  • Watch for judge–optimizer collusion: tuning against the same model that grades you is reward hacking with extra steps. Keep one evaluator entirely out of the loop.
what to plot — the alignment frontierquality( πθ ) vs KL( πθ ‖ πref ) ⟵ compare methods along the curve, never at one point
Takeaway: any method can trade KL for reward with one knob, so a single number means nothing. Report win rate at matched KL, length-controlled, from a judge the optimizer never touched.

RLHF (PPO)DPO
Reward modelSeparate, trained firstNone — implicit in β·log(π_θ/π_ref)

Data sourceFresh on-policy samples every stepA fixed, previously collected pair set
StabilityFinicky — many coupled hyperparametersMore stable; mainly sensitive to β
ComputeHigh — generation inside the training loopLow — a few forward passes per pair
FlexibilityOnline; extends to verifiable rewardsOffline; iterative variants recover much of the gap
Use whenYou need online RL, a reusable RM, or a verifierDefault for straightforward preference tuning

Most teams start with DPO because it is one script and a fixed dataset, then move on-policy when they hit its ceiling — typically when the preference data has gone stale relative to the policy, or when a verifier exists and outcome rewards beat learned ones. The two compose: DPO as a cheap first pass, then GRPO/RLVR on the domains you can check.

Takeaway: the real dividing line is offline vs on-policy, not "RL vs supervised." DPO's limitation is stale data; PPO's cost is the price of freshness.

10 Pitfalls & what to remember

  • Alignment optimizes preferences, not imitation — comparisons are easier to collect and richer than gold answers.
  • RLHF = SFT → reward model (Bradley-Terry) → PPO's clipped objective with a KL leash to the SFT model.
  • DPO solves that same objective in closed form and reduces it to a supervised loss on log-probability ratios.
  • The shared picture: widen the margin between chosen and rejected, anchored to a frozen reference.
  • Where the reward comes from is the design decision. Learned reward models can be hacked; verifiers can't, but only exist for checkable tasks.
  • Watch for reward hacking, sycophancy, length bias and diversity collapse — never read a reward curve without its KL axis.
  • Evaluate off-policy: length-controlled win rate at matched KL, from a judge the optimizer never saw.
