Reasoning in LLMs

A base LLM predicts the next token in one shot. Reasoning techniques get it to think first — spend intermediate tokens (and sometimes extra samples or training) to work through hard, multi-step problems before answering.

LLMs & Generative AI Inference-time compute Prompting

01 Why reasoning needs eliciting

The capability is latent in the weights; the trick is getting the model to use it instead of blurting an answer.

  • Asked for just the answer, an LLM commits its entire computation to one forward pass — fine for recall, brittle for multi-step logic and arithmetic ("System 1").
  • Let it generate intermediate steps and each step conditions the next, effectively giving the model more serial computation and a scratchpad ("System 2").
  • So reasoning is largely about spending more inference-time compute — more tokens, more samples, or longer trained "thinking" — in exchange for accuracy.
One sentence: reasoning = trading extra tokens/samples/training at inference time for correctness on problems a single forward pass can't solve.

02 Chain-of-thought

The foundational move: make the model write its working before the answer.

answer ~ P(answer | question, rationale), rationale ~ P(rationale | question)
  • Prompt with "let's think step by step" (zero-shot CoT) or show worked examples (few-shot CoT), and accuracy on math/logic jumps sharply.
  • Each generated step becomes part of the context for the next, so the model can decompose, carry intermediate results, and self-correct mid-stream.
  • It's an emergent behaviour — CoT mostly helps once models are large enough to produce coherent steps.

03 Self-consistency

Don't trust one chain — sample several and take the majority vote. Add paths and watch the answer stabilize.

Self-consistency: sample many chains, vote Each path reasons independently and may land on a different answer; the majority is usually right

Because individual chains make independent mistakes but tend to agree on the correct answer, voting over many sampled chains (at temperature > 0) reliably beats a single greedy chain — a pure inference-time accuracy boost.

04 Structured reasoning

Beyond a single linear chain — search and decomposition.

  • Tree-of-Thought: branch into multiple candidate steps, evaluate them, and search (BFS/DFS) — backtracking from dead ends instead of committing to one line.
  • Graph-of-Thought: let thoughts merge and reference each other, not just branch.
  • Least-to-most / decomposition: break a hard problem into ordered sub-problems and solve them in sequence, each feeding the next.
  • Self-reflection / self-critique: have the model review and revise its own answer before finalizing.

05 Tool-augmented reasoning

Some steps are better delegated than generated — don't make the model do arithmetic in its head.

  • ReAct: interleave reasoning ("thought") with actions (tool calls) and observations — the basis of agents.
  • Program-aided (PAL / PoT): have the model emit code, then execute it for the exact answer — offloading computation to an interpreter.
  • Retrieval in the loop: fetch facts mid-reasoning (see RAG) so steps are grounded, not guessed.

06 Trained reasoners

Instead of only prompting for reasoning, train the model to reason.

  • Recent "reasoning models" (o1 / R1-style) are post-trained — often with reinforcement learning on verifiable outcomes (RLVR) — to produce long internal chains before answering.
  • They learn to think longer when a problem is hard, allocating more reasoning tokens automatically.
  • Reward comes from whether the final answer is correct (math, code, proofs), so the model is optimized for outcomes, not just imitating human rationales.

07 Scaling test-time compute

A new axis of scaling: spend more compute at inference, not just at training.

  • Accuracy climbs as you spend more inference compute — longer chains, more sampled paths, or search over thoughts.
  • Best-of-N / verifier-guided: sample many candidates and pick the best with a reward model or verifier, rather than majority vote.
  • This trades latency and cost for quality on demand — cheap questions get one pass, hard ones get a search.
The shift: capability now scales with inference compute too, not only model size and training tokens.

08 Limits & caveats

  • Unfaithful CoT: the written steps don't always reflect the true computation — a plausible rationale can accompany a wrong answer (or vice-versa).
  • Hallucinated steps: a confident but invalid intermediate step can derail the whole chain.
  • Overthinking & cost: long reasoning is slow and expensive, and can hurt on easy questions.
  • Evaluation: judging reasoning quality (not just the final answer) is hard — see LLM-as-a-Judge.

09 Takeaways

  • Reasoning = spending inference compute to turn a one-shot prediction into a multi-step process.
  • Chain-of-thought writes the working; self-consistency votes over many chains for reliability.
  • Structured (tree/graph/decomposition) and tool-augmented (ReAct, code execution) reasoning push further on hard problems.
  • Trained reasoners learn (via RL on correct outcomes) to think longer automatically.
  • Test-time compute is a scaling axis — but mind faithfulness, hallucinated steps, and cost.
  • Builds on Large Language Models; pairs with Context Engineering and Preference Optimization.
