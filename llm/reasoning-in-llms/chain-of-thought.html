Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Put worked intermediate steps into the few-shot exemplars and a large enough model will write its own steps before answering — no fine-tuning, one frozen checkpoint. This page goes past "think step by step": exactly what was in the prompt, how the gain depends on model scale and on problem difficulty, the three ablations that rule out the cheap explanations, how robust the effect was to who wrote the exemplars, what the wrong chains looked like, the commonsense and symbolic results including length generalisation, and what the original study explicitly does not claim.

01 What chain-of-thought prompting is

A chain of thought is a series of intermediate natural-language reasoning steps that lead to the final output. Chain-of-thought prompting is ordinary few-shot prompting in which every exemplar is a triple ⟨input, chain of thought, output⟩ instead of a pair ⟨input, output⟩. Nothing is trained; the model sees the triples in context and imitates the format on the test question.

standard few-shot prompt vs chain-of-thought promptstandard: P = [x₁, y₁] [x₂, y₂] … [x_k, y_k] x model continues with y CoT: P = [x₁, c₁, y₁] … [x_k, c_k, y_k] x model continues with c, y x = question c = chain of thought (natural language + inline arithmetic) y = answer k = 8 hand-written exemplars for the math word problems (AQuA: 4, see §02) what the model is effectively sampling (greedy decoding in the study)p(c, y | P, x) = p(c | P, x) · p(y | c, P, x) the answer token(s) are conditioned on the chain already written, so the chain can change the answer — the point the "reasoning after answer" ablation tests (§06)

The study's own illustration (its Figure 1) is a single exemplar followed by a test question. With the plain prompt the model answers directly and gets it wrong; with the chain in the exemplar it writes a chain and gets it right:

standard prompting
Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?
A: The answer is 11.
Q: The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought 6 more, how many apples do they have?
model: A: The answer is 27. ✗
chain-of-thought prompting
Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?
A: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.
Q: The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought 6 more, how many apples do they have?
model: A: The cafeteria had 23 apples originally. They used 20 to make lunch. So they had 23 - 20 = 3. They bought 6 more apples, so they have 3 + 6 = 9. The answer is 9. ✓

Highlighted text is the chain of thought. The figure is illustrative; the prompt actually used for evaluation (§02) has eight exemplars.

The four properties the study argues for

1 · decomposition
More computation for harder problems
A multi-step problem is split into intermediate steps, so problems that need more steps get more generated tokens — "in principle".
2 · interpretability
A window into the behaviour
The chain suggests how an answer was reached and where it went wrong — with the explicit caveat that fully characterising the computation behind an answer remains open (§11).
3 · generality
Any task solvable via language
Math word problems, commonsense, symbolic manipulation — and, at least in principle, any task humans solve through language.
4 · ease
Off-the-shelf models, a few exemplars
Elicited from a sufficiently large frozen model just by writing chains into the few-shot exemplars. No model was fine-tuned for the study.
Why "chain of thought" and not "solution". The study notes the chain often reads like a worked solution, but keeps the name to stress that it mimics a step-by-step thought process leading to the answer — whereas explanations in earlier rationale work typically come after the prediction. That ordering is exactly what §06 tests. The in-context learning that makes any of this possible is covered on large language models; how few-shot exemplars are chosen and ordered in general is on prompt engineering.

02 The setup: eight exemplars, five math benchmarks, five model families

Most of the math datasets have only an evaluation split, so the exemplars were written by hand once and the same set of eight was reused for every free-response benchmark — no per-dataset prompt engineering.

The prompt

  • Math word problems: one set of eight hand-written ⟨question, chain, answer⟩ exemplars, used for GSM8K, SVAMP, ASDiv and MAWPS. The study states these exemplars "did not undergo prompt engineering". AQuA is multiple choice, so it got four exemplars with solutions taken from its training set instead.
  • The number eight was set by the context budget: the main experiments used a fixed eight exemplars "as constrained by the input length of 1024 tokens".
  • Standard prompting (the baseline) uses the same questions with the chains removed: question → "The answer is …".

One of the eight math exemplars exactly as it appears in the full prompt (the Annotator A set; the study calls its author "Annotator A"):

exemplar 6 of 8 — math word problem promptQ: There were nine computers in the server room. Five more computers were installed each day, from monday to thursday. How many computers are now in the server room? A: There were originally 9 computers. For each of 4 days, 5 more computers were added. So 5 * 4 = 20 computers were added. 9 + 20 is 29. The answer is 29.

Note the fixed suffix The answer is N. on every exemplar: it is what makes the final answer parseable from free text, and the model copies it.

03 Headline results: a prompt beats a fine-tuned, verifier-reranked model on GSM8K

PaLM 540B goes from 17.9% with standard prompting to 56.9% with chain-of-thought prompting on GSM8K, past the prior best of 55% — a fine-tuned GPT-3 175B with a verifier. The fine-tuned GPT-3 alone scored 33%.

04 Emergence with scale: it hurts small models and helps only near 100B

Chain-of-thought prompting does not positively impact performance for small models and only yields gains with models of ~100B parameters. Below that it is usually worse than standard prompting — the study says it hurts "for most models smaller than 10B parameters". Small models write fluent but illogical chains.

Every point below is a cell of the study's appendix tables (arithmetic, MAWPS subsets, commonsense, symbolic). Standard prompting is dashed, chain of thought solid; the shaded band between them is the CoT gain (teal) or loss (red) at each size. The x-axis is logarithmic in parameters. Codex is in the tables but has no stated size, so it cannot be placed on this axis.

Solve rate vs model size, standard vs chain-of-thought prompting — the study's reported numbers pick GSM8K and PaLM: the curves cross between 8B and 62B; pick MAWPS SingleOp and they barely separate at any size
  • Where the crossover sits (GSM8K, from the tables): LaMDA turns positive at 68B (5.7 → 8.2), PaLM between 8B (4.9 → 4.1, negative) and 62B (9.6 → 29.9), GPT-3 only at 175B (every smaller GPT-3 model loses accuracy with CoT). The "~100B" in the study's summary is a description of these three families, not a sharp threshold.
  • Standard prompting is nearly flat on GSM8K across these sizes, so the chain-of-thought curve is not "the same scaling, shifted" — it is a different curve. The study's framing: for tasks where standard prompting has a flat scaling curve, CoT can produce a steeply increasing one, which makes standard prompting "only a lower bound" on what a model can do.
  • Why small models fail, per the study's FAQ: they fail even easy symbol mapping (§10 shows small models cannot copy a fixed chain structure onto new names); they have weaker arithmetic; and they often never produce a parseable final answer, through repetition or chains that wander. It calls the scale effect a mix of several emergent abilities (semantic understanding, symbol mapping, staying on topic, arithmetic, faithfulness) and notes that scale is confounded with other factors such as training compute.

05 Harder problems gain more; one-step problems gain nothing

The gain tracks how many steps a problem needs. GSM8K (lowest baseline) more than doubled for the largest models; on MAWPS's one-step SingleOp subset the change was "either negative or very small".

condition 1

A challenging, multi-step reasoning problem — GSM8K, MultiArith — not a one-step lookup or operation.
condition 2
The model is large
§04: in these families, ~100B parameters.
condition 3
Standard prompting's scaling curve is flat
If bigger models already solve it with plain prompting (SingleOp at 90%+), there is little headroom for a chain to add.

06 The ablations: three cheaper explanations, each ruled out

If chain of thought helps, maybe something simpler is doing the work. The study builds a prompt variant that isolates each candidate cause and checks whether it alone recovers the gain. None does on GSM8K.

what goes into each exemplar, per variantstandard prompting: ⟨ x , y ⟩ chain of thought: ⟨ x , c , y ⟩ equation only: ⟨ x , e , y ⟩ e = the math equation, no words variable compute only: ⟨ x , "…" , y ⟩ a run of dots, as many as the characters in e reasoning after answer: ⟨ x , y , c ⟩ the same chain, but written after the answer
varianthypothesis it isolatesresultwhat that rules out
equation onlyCoT helps because it makes the model write down the equation to evaluatedoesn't help much on GSM8K; does help on one- and two-step sets (SVAMP, ASDiv, MAWPS)"it's just the equation" — on hard problems the question cannot be translated into an equation directly; the natural-language steps do the translation
variable compute onlyCoT helps because more tokens = more computation, more for harder problemsabout the same as standard prompting"it's just extra compute" — the tokens must express intermediate steps in language to help
reasoning after answerCoT helps because the chain activates relevant pretrained knowledgeabout the same as standard prompting"it's just priming" — the answer must actually depend on the chain, i.e. the chain must come first

The study reports these ablations on GSM8K for LaMDA 137B and PaLM 540B in a bar chart; the numeric tables give them for LaMDA 137B on four math datasets and, without the equation-only variant, on five commonsense and symbolic tasks (SayCan was run with PaLM). Why equation-only fails on GSM8K: the study's example

LaMDA 137B, one GSM8K question, two promptsQ: Mike plays ping pong for 40 minutes. In the first 20 minutes, he scores 4 points. In the second 20 minutes, he scores 25% more points. How many total points did he score? equation only (wrong): (4 + 20 * 0.25) = 6. The answer is 6. chain of thought (right): … So he scored 25% more in the second 20 minutes. 4 x 1.25 = 5. So he scored 5 points in the second 20 minutes. So he scored 9 points in total. The answer is 9.

Writing one equation forces the whole semantic parse into a single step; the chain lets each clause be handled separately. On SVAMP, ASDiv and MAWPS, where one or two steps suffice, the equation is easy to read off the question and equation-only prompting recovers much of the gain — the explorer's readout shows how much.

What the ablations do and do not show. They rule out three specific, cheaper explanations for the size of the gain in these models. They do not show what computation the network performs when it writes a chain — the study is explicit that whether the model is "reasoning" stays open (§11).

07 Robustness: other annotators, other styles, other exemplars

Few-shot prompting is notoriously sensitive — the study cites exemplar permutations moving GPT-3 on SST-2 from 54.3% to 93.4%. So it re-ran CoT with chains written by different people and with entirely different exemplars. Every variant beat standard prompting by a large margin, with visible variance between them.

  • Annotators B and C (two other co-authors) independently wrote chains for the same eight questions, with no instructions beyond "write the step-by-step reasoning". Their chains differ in style — C, for example, splits "2 toys each from mom and dad" into two additions — and both match or beat A on GSM8K.
  • Concise style: A rewrote the chains tersely in the style of the GSM8K reference solutions, e.g. "5 * 4 = 20 new computers were added. So there are 9 + 20 = 29 new computers in the server room now". It was the weakest variant on GSM8K (11.1) but still well above 6.5.
  • Exemplars from an independent source: three sets of eight sampled from the GSM8K training set, whose reference solutions were written by crowd workers — restricted to ≤ 60 tokens and ≤ 2 steps for a fair comparison. They performed comparably to the hand-written ones on all four datasets, even though each set was used on datasets it was not drawn from.
  • Exemplar order: standard deviations across orders are small almost everywhere — except coin flip, a binary classification task, where several same-label exemplars in a row bias the output (Annotator B ± 7.9, C ± 11.1).
  • Number of exemplars: on five LaMDA 137B tasks with flat standard-prompting curves (GSM8K, MultiArith, Sports, Coin Flip, Last-letter concat), the CoT advantage held across exemplar counts from 1 up to 8 (1 to 4 for concat). In preliminary runs, going from 8 to 16 standard exemplars did not let standard prompting catch up.
  • Across models: the same prompts improved all three main families on every dataset except CSQA and StrategyQA for GPT-3 (§09).
"Robust" is not "prompt engineering doesn't matter." The study is explicit: on coin flip, accuracy ranged from 99.6% (Annotator A) to 71.4% (Annotator C), both above the 50.0% baseline. And in a preliminary experiment on reversing a list of five items, two co-authors could not write a chain that solved it while a third wrote one that solved it perfectly. The study suggests generating chains automatically with a large model as future work.

08 Reading the chains: what was right, what was wrong, what scale fixed

The study hand-read LaMDA 137B's GSM8K chains: 50 random examples it got right and 50 it got wrong. Right answers almost always came with right chains; about half of the wrong answers were "almost right".

50 correct answers

  • The detailed appendix count: 49 of 50 had correct logic and math; 1 reached the right answer through wrong reasoning ("correct by chance"). Five of the 49 had minor imperfections but stayed coherent — an underspecified statement, an unrelated but true statement, two equations with omitted steps, and one with correct math but inverted semantics.
  • The main text's summary says "except two that coincidentally arrived at the correct answer", and the appendix table caption counts 7 imperfect and 43 human-equivalent chains. The three counts are not fully consistent; the qualitative point — correct free-response answers rarely come from broken chains — holds under any of them.
  • The study's own caveat: free-response arithmetic is hard to hit by accident. On multiple-choice or binary tasks (all the commonsense sets) a wrong chain can much more easily land on the right answer, so this result should not be extrapolated to them.

What scale fixed: PaLM 62B → 540B

A coarser follow-up read 45 errors made by PaLM 62B: semantic understanding (20), one step missing (18) and other (7 — hallucinations, repetitive outputs, symbol mapping). Scaling to PaLM 540B fixed "a substantial portion" of errors in all three categories; the main text singles out a large portion of the one-step-missing and semantic-understanding errors. (The per-category fixed counts appear only in a chart and are not reproduced here.) The study reads this as consistent with semantic understanding and logical skills being acquired with scale — with the caveat that scale is confounded with training compute.

Broken chains are a special case of unfaithful or ungrounded generation; the general problem and its mitigations are on hallucination & factuality.

10 Symbolic reasoning and length generalisation

Two synthetic tasks with known structure let the study test something the math sets cannot: do chains generalise to inputs longer than any exemplar? Exemplars always have 2 items; the out-of-domain (OOD) test sets have 3 or 4.

Last-letter concatenation

  • In domain, PaLM 540B with CoT is near 100%. Standard prompting already solves the 2-flip coin task at that size (98.1), but not for LaMDA 137B (49.0, chance).
  • These in-domain tasks are "toy tasks": the exemplar chain already contains the full solution structure, and the model only has to repeat it with new symbols. Small models still fail — the ability to do even that only appears around 100B parameters in these families.
  • Out of domain, standard prompting fails on both tasks (concat ≈ 0; coin ≈ chance), while CoT gives upward-sloping scaling curves, though below in-domain accuracy. That is the study's evidence that chains enable length generalisation "for language models of sufficient scale".

11 Limitations the original study states

interpretation
Not evidence the network "reasons"
Chain of thought emulates human reasoners' thought processes; whether the network is actually reasoning is left as an open question.
correctness
No guarantee of correct reasoning paths
Wrong chains lead to wrong answers and, especially on multiple-choice or binary tasks, to accidentally right ones. Improving factuality of generations is named as future work.
cost
Only emerges at large scale
Which makes it costly to serve; inducing reasoning in smaller models is named as a direction.
annotation
Cheap for prompting, costly for fine-tuning
Writing eight chains is trivial; writing enough for fine-tuning could be prohibitive — possibly addressed by synthetic data or zero-shot generalisation.
transfer
Gains don't transfer perfectly across models
The same prompts hurt GPT-3 on CSQA and StrategyQA (§09); how pretraining data and architecture affect the gain is left open.
prompting
Prompt choice still matters
Large annotator variance on some tasks, and at least one task (list reversal) that only one annotator's chain could solve (§07).

Also worth stating plainly: most of the large-model results are single greedy runs with one exemplar order, and the ablation and robustness tables are LaMDA 137B only (plus SayCan on PaLM), because further GPT-3 and PaLM queries were described as limited and expensive.

The faithfulness question

The study already hedged that a chain is "an interpretable window" but not a full account of the computation (§01, §11). Later work sharpened this: a written chain need not be the cause of the answer — models can be swayed by features of the prompt that the chain never mentions, and editing or truncating a chain does not always change the answer. Treat a chain as useful for accuracy and debugging, not as an audit trail. The hub covers this under limits & caveats; grading chains rather than answers is discussed on LLM-as-a-judge.

13 Common misconceptions

"Chain-of-thought prompting is the 'Let's think step by step' trick." Not in the original study. It is few-shot: eight exemplars each containing a hand-written chain. The zero-shot cue came from a separate, later study (§12).
"Showing your working helps any model." In the reported results it hurt most models under ~10B parameters. Small models produced fluent but illogical chains and scored below standard prompting; the gain appeared only around 100B in these families (§04).
"It works because the model gets more tokens to think with." The variable-compute ablation says no. Replacing the chain with the same number of dots performed about like standard prompting (§06). The tokens have to carry natural-language intermediate steps.
"It's just making the model write the equation." Only on easy sets. Equation-only prompting recovered much of the gain on one- and two-step datasets but none on GSM8K, where translating the question into an equation is the hard part (§06).
"The chain is an explanation of how the model got its answer." The study explicitly does not claim this. A correct chain usually accompanied a correct free-response answer, but there is no guarantee of correct reasoning paths, and accidentally right answers are more likely on multiple-choice tasks (§08, §11).
"CoT helps on every reasoning benchmark." Gains were near zero on MAWPS SingleOp and CSQA, and negative for GPT-3 on CSQA and StrategyQA. The study's own guidance: multi-step task, large model, flat standard-prompting curve (§05).

14 Takeaways

  • Method: few-shot exemplars as ⟨input, chain of thought, output⟩; one frozen model; greedy decoding; answer parsed from "The answer is …".
  • Headline: PaLM 540B on GSM8K, 17.9% → 56.9%, past a 55% prior best from a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier.
  • Scale: the gain is emergent — negative for most models under ~10B, positive around 100B in the families tested.
  • Difficulty: large gains on multi-step problems (GSM8K, MultiArith), little or none on one-step ones (SingleOp).
  • Ablations: equation-only, dots-for-compute and chain-after-answer do not reproduce the GSM8K gain — the natural-language steps, written before the answer, are what matter.
  • Robustness: every annotator, style and exemplar set beat standard prompting by a wide margin, but with real variance — prompt choice still matters.
  • Beyond math: commonsense gains were smaller and less uniform; on symbolic tasks chains enabled length generalisation at sufficient scale.
  • Limits: no claim the network "reasons", no guarantee of correct chains, expensive scale. Later work — self-consistency, program-aided reasoning, ReAct, reasoning-trained models — builds directly on these gaps. Back to the Reasoning in LLMs hub.
