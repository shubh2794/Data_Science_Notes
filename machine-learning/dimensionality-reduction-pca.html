Dimensionality Reduction (PCA)

Unsupervised Learning Preprocessing Rotate the coordinate system until the first axis carries as much variance as possible, then throw away the tail. This page is the practitioner's view — scaling, choosing k, reconstruction, whitening, and what to use instead. The eigenvector derivation lives on Quadratic Forms, Covariance & PCA. The last third of the page covers the two classical linear methods that sit beside PCA and are constantly confused with it — linear discriminant analysis, which is supervised and maximises class separation rather than variance, and factor analysis, which is an actual latent-variable model rather than a rotation.

01 Overview: why fewer dimensions

Real feature spaces are high-dimensional but rarely full-rank in spirit: pixels, sensors and survey items are heavily correlated, so the data lives near a much smaller subspace than the ambient one.

High dimension hurts in specific, mechanical ways. Distances concentrate — the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbour tends to 1, which quietly destroys kNN, k-means and RBF kernels. Sample requirements grow exponentially with d if you want to cover the space. Covariance estimates become unstable once d approaches n, so anything that inverts a covariance matrix (LDA, Gaussian models, Mahalanobis distance) breaks. And you cannot look at the data.

PCA answers all four with one move: find the orthogonal directions along which the data varies most, keep the top k, and represent every point by its k coordinates in that rotated frame. The result is the best possible linear k-dimensional approximation in the squared-error sense — no other rank-k linear reconstruction has lower error.

Takeaway: PCA is a rotation followed by a truncation. It never invents features; it re-expresses the ones you have in an order that puts the discardable directions last.

02 Centering, scaling, and the covariance matrix

PCA operates on the second moment of the data, so the two preprocessing decisions — where the origin sits and what one unit means — completely determine the answer.

centered data (n samples × d features), then the sample covariance X̃ = X − 1·μᵀ Σ = (1/(n − 1))·X̃ᵀX̃ ∈ ℝ^(d×d) diagonal = variances, off-diagonal = covariancesΣⱼⱼ = Var(xⱼ) Σⱼₖ = Cov(xⱼ, xₖ) the components are the eigenvectors, the variances along them the eigenvaluesΣ vᵢ = λᵢ vᵢ λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λ_d ≥ 0 vᵢᵀvⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j

Centering is not optional

Skip the mean subtraction and XᵀX measures distance from the origin rather than spread, so the first "component" mostly points at the data cloud's mean vector and encodes nothing about structure. Every derivation on this page assumes μ = 0.

Scaling changes the answer entirely

Variance has units, so a feature measured in millimetres has 10⁶ times the variance of the same feature in metres — and PCA will hand it the first component. Concretely, take a two-feature dataset with Σ = [[4, 2], [2, 3]]. Its eigenvalues are λ₁ = 5.562, λ₂ = 1.438, so PC1 explains 79.5% of the variance and sits at about 38° from the first axis — a genuine blend of both features. Now re-measure feature 1 in units ten times smaller, which multiplies its variance by 100 and the covariance by 10.

the same data, one unit change Σ = [[4, 2], [2, 3]] ⟹ λ = (5.562, 1.438) EVR₁ = 79.5% v₁ ≈ [0.788, 0.615]ᵀ (38°) Σ′ = [[400, 20], [20, 3]] ⟹ λ = (401.0, 1.995) EVR₁ = 99.5% v₁ ≈ [0.9987, 0.050]ᵀ (2.9°)

Nothing about the underlying phenomenon changed, yet PC1 went from a meaningful mixture to "feature 1, essentially". The standard fix is to standardize each feature to zero mean and unit variance before the decomposition, which is exactly equivalent to eigendecomposing the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix. Use covariance-PCA only when all features already share a meaningful unit (pixel intensities, spectra, repeated measurements of one quantity) and relative magnitude is itself signal.

Takeaway: "PCA on the correlation matrix" and "standardize, then PCA on the covariance matrix" are the same procedure. Choosing between correlation and covariance is a modelling decision about whether variance differences between features are real information.

03 The recipe, and the one rule that breaks pipelines

  1. Center each feature: xᵢⱼ ← xᵢⱼ − μⱼ. Scale by σⱼ unless the units are already comparable.
  2. Decompose. Conceptually: eigendecompose Σ. Practically: run an SVD on X̃ directly (see §07).
  3. Sort eigenvalues descending and take the top k eigenvectors as columns of P ∈ ℝ^(d×k). Because Σ is symmetric positive semi-definite, the eigenvalues are real and non-negative and the eigenvectors are orthogonal — P has orthonormal columns.
  4. Project: Z = X̃P ∈ ℝ^(n×k). Row i of Z holds the scores of sample i; column j of P holds the loadings that say how each original feature contributes to component j.
forward and back z = Pᵀ(x − μ) x̂ = μ + P z = μ + P Pᵀ(x − μ) PᵀP = I_k but PPᵀ ≠ I_d for k < d ← PPᵀ is the projection matrix onto the retained subspace

Fit on train only

The mean μ, the scales σⱼ and the basis P are all learned parameters. Fitting them on the full dataset before splitting leaks test-set structure into training and produces an optimistic score — the same failure as fitting a scaler globally. Inside cross-validation, PCA belongs in the pipeline object that gets re-fitted on every fold.

Reading the loadings

Components are only identified up to sign: v and −v span the same direction, so two library versions can return mirrored plots from identical data. When eigenvalues are near-equal (λᵢ ≈ λᵢ₊₁) the individual components inside that block are essentially arbitrary — only the subspace they span is stable — which is why "PC3 means customer loyalty" style interpretations are fragile unless the gap is large.

Takeaway: μ, σ and P are model parameters. Learn them from training data, apply them to everything else, and never re-fit on the evaluation set.

05 Choosing k: scree, cumulative variance, reconstruction error

Each eigenvalue λᵢ is the variance of the data along component i, and the eigenvalues sum to the total variance (the trace of Σ). That gives an exact accounting of what each component buys.

explained variance EVRᵢ = λᵢ / ∑ⱼ₌₁..d λⱼ CumEVR(k) = ∑ᵢ₌₁..k λᵢ / ∑ⱼ₌₁..d λⱼ and what you throw away — mean squared reconstruction error(1/n)·∑ᵢ ‖xᵢ − x̂ᵢ‖² = ∑ⱼ₌ₖ₊₁..d λⱼ = (1 − CumEVR(k))·∑ⱼ λⱼ

The two are the same statement: explained variance up, reconstruction error down, and they sum to a constant. For the Σ = [[4,2],[2,3]] example above, keeping k = 1 retains 79.5% of the variance and leaves a mean squared reconstruction error of exactly λ₂ = 1.438 out of a total variance of 7.

Four ways to pick k

  • Cumulative variance threshold. Smallest k with CumEVR(k) ≥ 0.95 (or 0.90, or 0.99). Simple, defensible, and the usual default for a preprocessing step.
  • Scree plot elbow. Plot λᵢ against i and look for the bend where the curve flattens into a noise floor. Real structure decays fast; noise decays slowly and linearly.
  • Kaiser's rule. On standardized data every feature has variance 1, so a component with λᵢ < 1 explains less than one original feature and is arguably not worth keeping. Crude and known to over-retain, but a fast sanity check.
  • Downstream cross-validation. The only criterion that matters if PCA feeds a classifier: treat k as a hyperparameter and tune it against the end metric. The variance-optimal k and the accuracy-optimal k are frequently different.

For visualisation, k is simply 2 or 3 and the question becomes how much you are not seeing — always print CumEVR(2) on the scatter plot. If it reads 18%, the plot is a shadow of a shadow and clusters that look separated may not be.

Takeaway: report CumEVR(k) with every PCA. A 2-D scatter with 92% cumulative variance and one with 18% are different objects and deserve different levels of trust.

06 Reconstruction, decorrelation, whitening

Reconstruction and compression

Because P has orthonormal columns, going back is a transpose rather than an inverse: x̂ = μ + Pz. Storage drops from n·d numbers to n·k + d·k + d (scores, basis, mean), which pays off whenever k ≪ d and n is large. The per-sample error ‖x − x̂‖² is also a useful anomaly score: fit PCA on normal data and points that reconstruct badly are the ones that do not live on the learned subspace.

Denoising

If the signal is low-rank and the noise is isotropic, the noise spreads its variance evenly across all d directions while the signal concentrates in a few. Truncating at k therefore removes roughly (d − k)/d of the noise energy and only the smallest slice of the signal. This is why PCA denoising works on images and spectra — and why it fails when the noise is structured (a coherent artefact has high variance and lands in the top components, where truncation preserves it perfectly).

Decorrelation and whitening

The covariance of the scores is diagonal by construction — that is what orthogonal eigenvectors buy you. Dividing each score by √λᵢ goes one step further and makes it the identity.

scores are uncorrelated; whitened scores are isotropic Cov(Z) = PᵀΣP = diag(λ₁, …, λ_k) z̃ᵢ = zᵢ / √(λᵢ + ε) ⟹ Cov(Z̃) = I_k

Whitening helps anything that assumes spherical geometry — Euclidean-distance methods, k-means, isotropic Gaussian priors — and it is what makes a Mahalanobis distance equal to a plain Euclidean distance in the transformed space. The cost: dividing by a tiny λᵢ amplifies the noisiest directions, so whitening without truncation (or without the ε) is a reliable way to make a model worse. Whiten only the components you kept.

Takeaway: truncation and whitening pull in opposite directions — one suppresses low-variance directions, the other boosts them. Truncate first, whiten second, and never whiten the tail.

07 How it is actually computed: SVD and scale

Forming Σ = X̃ᵀX̃/(n−1) explicitly squares the condition number of the data matrix, so small singular values are lost to rounding. Every serious implementation therefore decomposes X̃ directly.

thin SVD of the centered data matrix X̃ = U S Vᵀ UᵀU = I VᵀV = I S = diag(s₁ ≥ s₂ ≥ … ≥ 0) V holds the principal directions; the singular values give the eigenvaluesΣ = X̃ᵀX̃/(n−1) = V (S²/(n−1)) Vᵀ ⟹ λᵢ = sᵢ² / (n − 1) the scores fall out for freeZ = X̃ V_k = U_k S_k

Sizes: V ∈ ℝ^(d×d) gives the loadings, U ∈ ℝ^(n×n) (or n×d in the thin form) the normalised scores. Because Σ is symmetric PSD, its eigenvalues and the squared singular values agree up to the 1/(n−1) factor — no negative or complex values can appear.

Cost, and what to do when it hurts

A full decomposition costs O(n d min(n,d)), which becomes intolerable at large d. Three standard escapes: truncated / randomized SVD computes only the top k directions in roughly O(ndk) by projecting onto a random sketch and refining — usually accurate enough when the spectrum decays; incremental PCA processes mini-batches and updates the basis, so the data never has to fit in memory; and the Gram trick, which decomposes the n×n matrix X̃X̃ᵀ instead when n ≪ d (the two share non-zero eigenvalues), the same identity that makes kernel PCA possible. For sparse data, note that centering destroys sparsity — either use a sparse-aware implementation or accept the memory cost.

Takeaway: never build the covariance matrix by hand when a truncated SVD is available. It is faster, more accurate, and gives the scores without a second multiplication.

08 What PCA is actually used for — and when it hurts

  • Visualisation. Project to 2–3 components for a first look. Fast, deterministic, and — unlike neighbour-embedding methods — the axes and the distances mean something.
  • Compression. Store k scores instead of d features, with a known, tunable error budget.
  • Speed. Cutting d from 5,000 to 100 shrinks training time and memory for every downstream model, often with no accuracy loss.
  • Denoising. Drop the low-variance tail where isotropic noise lives.
  • Decorrelation. Removes multicollinearity outright, which stabilises linear and logistic regression coefficients (principal-component regression) and repairs the singular covariance matrices that break LDA and Gaussian models.
  • Rescuing distance-based methods. kNN, k-means and RBF kernels degrade as d grows; a PCA step before them is often the cheapest available improvement.

The failure mode nobody expects

PCA is unsupervised: it optimises variance, and variance is not relevance. If the discriminative signal happens to live in a low-variance direction — a subtle but consistent offset between two classes, drowned out by a large but label-irrelevant source of variation such as lighting or scanner calibration — truncation deletes exactly the information you needed. The symptom is a model that gets worse as you add PCA and does not recover as you increase k up to a point where k is no longer a reduction. Two guards: tune k against the downstream metric rather than against explained variance, and consider a supervised alternative (LDA, or simply a regularized model on the raw features). §10 builds the pathological case explicitly: two classes whose separating direction is exactly the direction PCA ranks last.

PCA is also linear. Data on a curved manifold — a spiral, a Swiss roll, a torus of poses — cannot be flattened by a rotation, and PCA will need almost as many components as the ambient dimension to represent it. It is sensitive to outliers, because squared error rewards chasing the extreme point; and it inherits any pathology from unscaled features. Finally, components are dense mixtures of every original feature, so PCA destroys interpretability of the sort a feature-importance plot gives you. If you need to keep actual named features, use feature selection, not PCA.

Takeaway: PCA maximises variance, not separability. When the label matters, validate k against the label — never assume that 95% of the variance contains 95% of the signal.

09 Linear discriminant analysis: separation instead of variance

PCA asks "along which direction do the data spread out the most?" and never looks at the labels. Linear discriminant analysis asks a different question — "along which direction do the classes pull apart the most, relative to how much they smear out internally?" — and the two answers can be at right angles to each other.

Setup and the two scatter matrices

Take n labelled samples in ℝ^d, falling into C classes, with n_c samples in class c. Write m_c for the mean of class c and m for the grand mean. LDA decomposes the total spread into the part that lives inside the classes and the part that lives between them.

within-class scatter — how fat each blob is, summed over blobs S_W = ∑_c ∑_{i ∈ c} (xᵢ − m_c)(xᵢ − m_c)ᵀ Σ̂_W = S_W / (n − C) ← the pooled covariance estimate between-class scatter — how far the blob centres sit from the grand meanS_B = ∑_c n_c (m_c − m)(m_c − m)ᵀ and they add up exactly to the total scatterS_T = ∑ᵢ (xᵢ − m)(xᵢ − m)ᵀ = S_W + S_B ← PCA eigendecomposes S_T; LDA plays S_B against S_W

That last identity is the whole story in one line. PCA works with S_T alone, so it cannot distinguish variance that comes from within-class scatter (usually noise, or at least label-irrelevant variation) from variance that comes from between-class separation (exactly the signal). LDA refuses to pool them.

Fisher's criterion

Project every point onto a direction w, giving scalars t = wᵀx. The projected between-class spread is wᵀS_Bw and the projected within-class spread is wᵀS_Ww. Fisher's criterion is their ratio — a signal-to-noise ratio for the projection.

Fisher's criterion — a Rayleigh quotient in disguise J(w) = (wᵀ S_B w) / (wᵀ S_W w) w* = argmax_w J(w) for two classes, with Δ = m₁ − m₂, the numerator collapsesS_B ∝ ΔΔᵀ ⟹ wᵀS_Bw ∝ (wᵀΔ)² = (separation of the projected means)²

Notice that J(αw) = J(w) for any α ≠ 0: the criterion depends only on the direction, not the length. That scale-freedom is what makes the maximisation well posed — you fix the length by convention (usually wᵀS_Ww = 1), and the optimum is a ray, not a point.

The solution is a generalized eigenvalue problem

Differentiate the quotient and set it to zero. Writing a = wᵀS_Bw and b = wᵀS_Ww, the quotient rule gives ∇J = (2S_Bw·b − 2S_Ww·a)/b², and ∇J = 0 forces

stationarity condition — the generalized (pencil) eigenproblem S_B w = λ S_W w with λ = a/b = J(w) ← the eigenvalue IS the criterion value if S_W is invertible, an ordinary eigenproblem for a non-symmetric matrixS_W⁻¹ S_B w = λ w two classes: S_Bw = Δ(Δᵀw) is always parallel to Δ, so the eigenvector is available in closed formw* ∝ S_W⁻¹ Δ = Σ̂_W⁻¹ (m₁ − m₂) J(w*) = Δᵀ S_W⁻¹ Δ ← the squared Mahalanobis distance between the class means

So for two classes there is no eigensolver in sight: solve one linear system and you are done. The maximum achievable Fisher criterion equals the Mahalanobis separation of the two centroids — a satisfying result, because Mahalanobis distance is precisely "distance measured in units of the local scatter".

Why it is a generalized problem: simultaneous diagonalization

S_W⁻¹S_B is a product of two symmetric matrices and is generally not symmetric, so you should not hand it to a symmetric eigensolver. The standard repair is to change coordinates so that the within-class scatter becomes the identity — a whitening step — after which the problem is symmetric and everything from the eigendecomposition page applies unchanged.

whiten by the within-class scatter, substitute u = S_W^(1/2) w J(w) = (uᵀ S̃_B u) / (uᵀu) where S̃_B = S_W^(−1/2) S_B S_W^(−1/2) (symmetric PSD) so: ordinary PCA of the between-class scatter, done in whitened coordinatesS̃_B uᵢ = λᵢ uᵢ wᵢ = S_W^(−1/2) uᵢ the pair (S_B, S_W) is simultaneously diagonalized by W = [w₁ … w_k]Wᵀ S_W W = I Wᵀ S_B W = diag(λ₁, …, λ_k)

This is the linear-algebra fact underneath LDA: given one symmetric positive definite matrix and one symmetric matrix, there is a single (non-orthogonal) change of basis that turns the first into the identity and the second into a diagonal. Positive definiteness of S_W is what buys you real eigenvalues and a full set of directions — and it is exactly the condition that fails when n is small relative to d. In practice implementations use a Cholesky factor S_W = LLᵀ and eigendecompose L⁻¹S_BL⁻ᵀ, or take the SVD route on class-centred data, rather than forming a matrix square root. See Eigendecomposition & SVD for the symmetric machinery this leans on.

The whitening picture is also the best intuition for LDA: stretch space until every class blob is a sphere, then just point at the difference of the centroids. Nearest-centroid classification is the special case S_W = I. Everything LDA adds over "point at the difference of means" is the correction S_W⁻¹ that discounts directions along which the classes are already smeared.

How many discriminant directions you get

S_B is a sum of C rank-one terms, but the weighted centred means satisfy ∑_c n_c(m_c − m) = 0 — one linear constraint — so at most C − 1 of them are independent.

hard ceiling on the output dimension rank(S_B) ≤ C − 1 ⟹ k ≤ min(C − 1, d)

Consequences worth internalising. Two classes give exactly one direction — LDA can never produce a 2-D scatter plot of a binary problem. Ten classes give at most nine. And the ceiling has nothing to do with how much data you have: it is a rank fact about the label structure. PCA has no such limit; it will hand you all d components. This is the main reason LDA is a poor general-purpose compressor and an excellent class-aware one.

For C > 2 the multi-class objective generalises the scalar ratio to a matrix ratio, maximised by the top k generalized eigenvectors:

multi-class criterion over a projection matrix W ∈ ℝ^(d×k) J(W) = tr[ (Wᵀ S_W W)⁻¹ (Wᵀ S_B W) ] = ∑ᵢ₌₁..k λᵢ ← "proportion of trace" reports λᵢ / ∑λ

Worked example: the direction PCA throws away

Two classes in ℝ², equal sizes, with a shared within-class covariance that is strongly elongated along the (1, 1) diagonal, and class means offset along the other diagonal.

the setup m₁ = (−1, 1)ᵀ m₂ = (1, −1)ᵀ Δ = m₁ − m₂ = (−2, 2)ᵀ Σ̂_W = [[4, 3], [3, 4]] total covariance = within + between (equal class sizes ⟹ between = ¼ΔΔᵀ)Σ̂_T = [[4,3],[3,4]] + [[1,−1],[−1,1]] = [[5, 2], [2, 5]] PCA sees only Σ̂_Tλ = (7, 3) v₁ = (1, 1)ᵀ/√2 (45°) EVR₁ = 70% LDA sees the labelsΣ̂_W⁻¹ = (1/7)[[4, −3], [−3, 4]] w* ∝ Σ̂_W⁻¹Δ = (−2, 2)ᵀ ∝ (1, −1)ᵀ/√2 (135°)

The two answers are exactly orthogonal. Now score each direction with Fisher's criterion:

along PC1, w = (1,1)ᵀ/√2 — the highest-variance direction in the data wᵀΔ = 0 wᵀΣ̂_Ww = 7 ⟹ J = 0 ← the projected class means coincide; the classes are perfectly superimposed along the LDA direction, w = (1,−1)ᵀ/√2 — the lowest-variance direction in the datawᵀΔ = −2√2 wᵀΣ̂_Ww = 1 ⟹ J = 8 = ΔᵀΣ̂_W⁻¹Δ ← and 8 is the largest generalized eigenvalue, as it must be Mahalanobis separation and the resulting error floor (equal priors, Gaussian classes)D = √(ΔᵀΣ̂_W⁻¹Δ) = √8 ≈ 2.828 Bayes error = Φ(−D/2) = Φ(−1.414) ≈ 7.9%

Read that carefully. Keeping k = 1 principal component preserves 70% of the variance and 100% of the class overlap: the reduced data is worthless for classification. Keeping the single LDA direction preserves 30% of the variance and all of the separability — an error rate of about 8% from one number per sample. This is not a contrived edge case; it is the generic situation whenever some large, label-irrelevant source of variation (illumination, scanner calibration, speaker identity, market level) dominates the covariance. It is the concrete form of the warning in §08.

Takeaway: variance and discriminability are unrelated quantities. PCA's rank-1 answer here is orthogonal to LDA's, and it is the one that destroys the labels. Whenever labels exist, at minimum check J along your retained components.

LDA as a projection versus LDA as a classifier

These are two different products of the same computation, and conflating them causes confusion.

  • LDA as a projection (Fisher's version). Output: a d × k matrix W, k ≤ C − 1. You reduce to k supervised dimensions and then run whatever you like — kNN, a tree, logistic regression, a plot. No distributional assumption is needed to define this: J(w) is a ratio of two sample scatters, and maximising it is a well-posed linear-algebra problem for any data at all.
  • LDA as a classifier. Output: a decision rule. This one does assume a generative model — Gaussian classes with a common covariance — and it is that assumption that supplies the threshold, the priors and the optimality claim.

The generative derivation: assume p(x | c) = N(x; μ_c, Σ) with a shared Σ, and priors π_c. Take logs of π_c·p(x | c), expand the quadratic form, and discard −½xᵀΣ⁻¹x, which is identical for every class and so cannot affect the argmax:

linear discriminant score — linear in x because the quadratic term cancelled δ_c(x) = xᵀΣ⁻¹μ_c − ½ μ_cᵀΣ⁻¹μ_c + ln π_c ĉ(x) = argmax_c δ_c(x) two classes: the boundary is a hyperplane through a prior-shifted midpointδ₁(x) − δ₂(x) = Δᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − (μ₁+μ₂)/2) + ln(π₁/π₂) Δ = μ₁ − μ₂ the normal of that hyperplane is exactly Fisher's directionw = Σ⁻¹Δ ← same w as the criterion maximiser; equal priors put the threshold at the midpoint

In the worked example above, equal priors put the cut at x₁ − x₂ = 0 — the decision boundary is the (1,1) line, which is the PCA axis. The direction PCA keeps is the direction along which the LDA rule is completely indifferent.

If you drop the shared-covariance assumption and let each class keep its own Σ_c, the quadratic term no longer cancels and you get QDA, whose boundaries are conics. QDA is more flexible and much hungrier: it estimates C·d(d+1)/2 covariance parameters instead of d(d+1)/2. Regularized discriminant analysis interpolates between them with a single knob.

QDA score — the |Σ_c| term survives, and the boundary is quadratic δ_c(x) = −½ ln|Σ_c| − ½ (x − μ_c)ᵀ Σ_c⁻¹ (x − μ_c) + ln π_c shrinkage toward a common covariance (α) and toward a sphere (γ)Σ̂_c(α) = α Σ̂_c + (1 − α) Σ̂_pooled Σ̂(γ) = (1 − γ) Σ̂ + γ · (tr Σ̂ / d) · I

LDA and logistic regression produce the same functional form — a linear score fed through a decision threshold — from opposite directions. LDA models p(x | c) and derives p(c | x) (generative); logistic regression models p(c | x) directly and never describes the features (discriminative). When the Gaussian equal-covariance assumption genuinely holds, LDA is more statistically efficient — it uses more of the information in the data, so it needs fewer samples for the same variance in the estimate. When the assumption fails, or when there are heavy-tailed features and gross outliers, logistic regression is the safer bet because it never claimed anything about the feature distribution. The same generative-versus-discriminative axis separates naive Bayes from logistic regression; LDA is the version that keeps the off-diagonal covariance terms that naive Bayes throws away.

Assumptions, and precisely how each one fails

  • Equal (shared) class covariances. Pooling S_W only makes sense if every class has the same shape. If one class is a tight ball and another a long ribbon, the pooled matrix describes neither, and the linear boundary sits in the wrong place. Symptom: strongly unequal per-class error rates. Fix: QDA, RDA, or per-class models.
  • Roughly Gaussian, unimodal classes. Fisher's projection survives non-normality, but the classifier's threshold does not. Worse, a bimodal class has a centroid sitting in the empty space between its two modes, so m_c is a meaningless summary and S_W is enormous. Symptom: a class whose projected histogram is two humps. Fix: cluster first and treat sub-clusters as classes (a mixture discriminant), or use a nonparametric method.
  • Separation lives in the means. S_B is built entirely from centroids. If two classes share a centroid and differ only in spread or orientation — concentric rings, one class wrapped around another, XOR structure — then S_B ≈ 0, J ≈ 0 in every direction, and LDA is blind to a perfectly separable problem. Fix: QDA sees variance differences; kernel FDA or a nonlinear model sees the rest.
  • Invertible S_W. The pooled scatter has rank at most n − C, so if d ≥ n − C it is singular and S_W⁻¹ does not exist. This is the small-sample problem and it is the normal condition in genomics, spectroscopy and image classification. Fix (in rough order of preference): shrinkage LDA with the γ above, PCA to k < n − C dimensions first, diagonal LDA (drop the off-diagonals — this is Gaussian naive Bayes with a shared covariance), or a pseudo-inverse.
  • Outliers. Both means and scatter matrices are least-squares objects, so a handful of extreme points can rotate w substantially. Fix: robust location/scatter estimates, or trim.
  • Wildly unequal class sizes. S_B weights each class by n_c, so a rare class contributes almost nothing to the direction it most needs. Fix: reweight, or set priors explicitly at the decision step rather than accepting the empirical ones.

PCA and LDA together

The standard pipeline for high-dimensional labelled data is PCA then LDA: reduce d to something comfortably below n − C with an unsupervised step so that S_W becomes invertible, then run LDA in that subspace. It works, and it is worth knowing why it is also slightly dangerous — the PCA step is exactly the operation that can delete the discriminative direction before LDA ever sees it. Two guards: keep k generous in the PCA stage (you are only trying to clear the invertibility hurdle, not to compress), and prefer shrinkage LDA when you can afford it, because shrinkage regularises S_W without ever discarding a direction.

Takeaway: LDA is "whiten by the within-class scatter, then point at the difference of the centroids". It gives at most C − 1 directions, needs an invertible S_W, is blind to class differences that are not differences of means, and is a projection and a classifier at the same time — be clear about which one you are using.

Things worth doing with it:

  • Start at the default. The clouds are elongated at 45°, the centroids are offset at 135°. PC1 locks onto the elongation direction (near 45° — the class offset tugs it a few degrees off, since PCA sees Σ_T, not Σ_W) and its histogram is one wide, single-humped, hopelessly mixed distribution — the reported 1-D error rate lands close to chance (roughly 40–50%, drifting with the sample). The LDA histogram is narrow but visibly two-humped.
  • Drag the tilt to 135° (or press Align). Now the within-class elongation and the class offset point the same way, the two axes coincide, and PCA happens to be right. PCA is not wrong in general — it is indifferent, and sometimes indifference lands on the answer.
  • Set elongation to 1. The clouds become spheres, S_W ∝ I, and the LDA direction degenerates to the plain difference of the centroids — nearest-centroid classification. Everything S_W⁻¹ does is a correction for anisotropy.
  • Set separation to 0. Δ → 0, so S_B → 0 and J → 0: the LDA direction becomes noise, spinning under resampling. There is nothing to find, and the criterion says so honestly.
  • Watch the variance percentage on the LDA axis. At the default it is small — LDA routinely selects a direction PCA would rank last. That number is the whole argument for not choosing k by explained variance when a label exists.
Takeaway: the histograms are the point. A projection can be maximally wide and completely useless, or narrow and nearly perfect. Width is wᵀΣ_Tw; usefulness is (wᵀΔ)²/(wᵀΣ_Ww). Optimising one says nothing about the other.

11 Factor analysis: a latent-variable model, not a rotation

PCA is an algebraic re-description of the data you measured: components are weighted sums of the observed variables, and nothing is claimed to exist that you did not record. Factor analysis reverses the arrows. It asserts that a small number of unobserved causes generated your measurements, and asks you to estimate them. That is a statistical model with parameters, residuals, degrees of freedom and a goodness-of-fit test — none of which PCA has.

The model

the common-factor model, for one observation x ∈ ℝ^d with m < d factors x = μ + Λ f + ε Λ ∈ ℝ^(d×m) — the loadings f ∈ ℝ^m — the common factors ε ∈ ℝ^d — the specific factors assumptions that give the model its contentE[f] = 0 Cov(f) = I_m E[ε] = 0 Cov(ε) = Ψ = diag(ψ₁, …, ψ_d) Cov(f, ε) = 0 and therefore the implied covariance structure — the only thing FA is ever fitted toΣ = Λ Λᵀ + Ψ

Everything hangs on Ψ being diagonal. That is the substantive hypothesis: once you condition on the m common factors, the observed variables are uncorrelated with one another. All the correlation among the xⱼ is claimed to flow through the factors. Each variable is then allowed its own private, uncorrelated variance ψⱼ that no factor explains — measurement error plus whatever is genuinely idiosyncratic to that variable.

This is exactly where PCA differs, and the difference is not cosmetic. PCA has no Ψ. It treats all variance as common variance to be explained, so its rank-k approximation Σ ≈ V_kΛ_kV_kᵀ has to account for the diagonal of Σ as well as the off-diagonal. Factor analysis is free to route the diagonal into Ψ and spend all m factors on the off-diagonal — on the correlations, which are the only evidence that something shared exists at all. PCA models a closed system, FA an open one.

Communalities and uniquenesses

each variable's variance splits in two Var(xⱼ) = σⱼⱼ = ∑ₖ₌₁..m λ²ⱼₖ + ψⱼ = hⱼ² + ψⱼ communality: the share of variable j explained by the common factorshⱼ² = ∑ₖ λ²ⱼₖ uniqueness (specific variance): everything the factors do not reachψⱼ = σⱼⱼ − hⱼ² on standardized data (correlation scale) this reads as a partition of 1hⱼ² + ψⱼ = 1 0 ≤ hⱼ² ≤ 1

A variable with communality 0.85 is almost entirely a manifestation of the common structure; one with communality 0.10 is essentially noise with respect to that structure and is a candidate for deletion. There is no analogue of this diagnostic in PCA — a low-communality variable still gets loadings, and PCA will happily spend a component on it if its variance is large enough.

Worked example: three variables, one factor

Suppose three standardized measurements are all driven by one latent factor with loadings 0.9, 0.8, 0.7. The model's implied correlation matrix follows immediately from Σ = ΛΛᵀ + Ψ.

forward: model ⟹ correlations Λ = (0.9, 0.8, 0.7)ᵀ Ψ = diag(0.19, 0.36, 0.51) h² = (0.81, 0.64, 0.49) r₁₂ = 0.9·0.8 = 0.72 r₁₃ = 0.9·0.7 = 0.63 r₂₃ = 0.8·0.7 = 0.56 R = [[1, .72, .63], [.72, 1, .56], [.63, .56, 1]] backward: correlations ⟹ model, in closed form for m = 1, d = 3λ₁² = r₁₂·r₁₃ / r₂₃ = (0.72 × 0.63)/0.56 = 0.81 ⟹ λ₁ = 0.9 then λ₂ = r₁₂/λ₁ = 0.8, λ₃ = r₁₃/λ₁ = 0.7

Note what was used: only the three off-diagonal entries. The diagonal was never touched — it is absorbed by Ψ by construction. Now run PCA on the same R and keep one component:

PCA of the same correlation matrix, k = 1 eigenvalues of R = (2.2756, 0.4545, 0.2699) EVR₁ = 75.9% v₁ = (0.5997, 0.5804, 0.5510)ᵀ PC loadings = √λ₁ · v₁ = (0.905, 0.876, 0.831) versus the factor loadingsΛ = (0.900, 0.800, 0.700) ← PCA inflates every loading, and inflates the weakest variable most (0.831 vs 0.700) reproduced correlations from the rank-1 PCA fit, and their residualsr̂₁₂ = 0.792 (residual −0.072) r̂₁₃ = 0.752 (−0.122) r̂₂₃ = 0.728 (−0.168) FA residuals: 0, 0, 0 — exact by construction

This one example contains the entire conceptual difference. Because PCA is also trying to reproduce the 1s on the diagonal with a single component, it over-fits the diagonal and systematically overstates the correlations — and it distorts most for the variable with the largest unique variance. Factor analysis, having a Ψ to absorb the diagonal, reproduces the off-diagonal exactly. The gap shrinks as communalities rise toward 1 and as d grows (the diagonal is d of the d(d+1)/2 entries, so its influence fades), which is why with many highly correlated variables PCA and FA loadings look nearly identical — and why with few variables or low communalities they do not.

The d = 3, m = 1 case is also the cleanest illustration of identification. Six distinct entries in R; six free parameters (three loadings, three uniquenesses); zero degrees of freedom. The model fits perfectly no matter what data you feed it, so its perfect fit is not evidence of anything. Fit is only informative once the model is over-identified.

counting: distinct entries of Σ, minus free parameters, plus the rotational constraint m(m−1)/2 df = d(d+1)/2 − [ d·m + d − m(m−1)/2 ] = ½[ (d − m)² − (d + m) ] so m is capped by df ≥ 0d = 3, m = 1 ⟹ df = 0 d = 8, m = 2 ⟹ df = 13 d = 10, m = 5 ⟹ df = 5 d = 10, m = 6 ⟹ df = −0.5 < 0, inadmissible

Rotational indeterminacy — the useful, slippery part

Let G be any m × m orthogonal matrix. Then (ΛG)(ΛG)ᵀ = ΛGGᵀΛᵀ = ΛΛᵀ, so Λ and ΛG imply identical covariance, identical likelihood, identical communalities and identical fit.

the model cannot distinguish these two loading matrices — ever Σ = ΛΛᵀ + Ψ = (ΛG)(ΛG)ᵀ + Ψ for every G with GGᵀ = GᵀG = I_m what is identified is the column space of Λ, not Λ itselfcol(Λ) and hⱼ² = ∑ₖ λ²ⱼₖ ← both rotation-invariant

For m ≥ 2 the loadings are therefore not estimable; only the subspace they span is. Estimation routines break the tie with an arbitrary convention (typically ΛᵀΨ⁻¹Λ diagonal), which produces a mathematically convenient but usually uninterpretable first solution in which the first factor loads on nearly everything.

Why rotation is useful. Since every G fits equally well, you may as well pick the one that is easiest to read. The target is simple structure: each variable loads heavily on one factor and near zero on the rest. Varimax is the standard orthogonal criterion — it maximises the variance of the squared loadings within each column, which pushes loadings toward 0 or ±1:

varimax objective (orthogonal rotation; quartimax is the row-wise analogue) max_G ∑ₖ₌₁..m [ (1/d)∑ⱼ λ⁴ⱼₖ − ( (1/d)∑ⱼ λ²ⱼₖ )² ] ← per-column variance of squared loadings oblique rotations (promax, oblimin) drop orthogonality: G need only be invertibleΣ = (ΛG)Φ(ΛG)ᵀ + Ψ with Φ = Cov(f) no longer I — the factors are allowed to correlate

Why rotation is slippery. Rotation changes nothing about the fit and everything about the story. It cannot be validated by the data, because every rotation is an exact tie. So a rotated solution is a choice of description, and the names you then attach to factors ("verbal ability", "credit risk appetite", "level and slope") are hypotheses you brought with you, not findings the model produced. Three specific hazards: a different rotation criterion yields different loadings and a different narrative; oblique rotations let factors correlate, which is often more realistic but makes "the variance explained by factor 2" ill-defined, since contributions now overlap; and reporting only the rotated solution hides the fact that infinitely many equally good ones exist. State the rotation you used, and treat factor names as labels, not results.

Estimation, in outline

  • Principal-factor (principal axis) extraction. Guess the communalities — a common start is the squared multiple correlation of each variable on the others — form the reduced correlation matrix R − Ψ̂ (i.e. R with communalities replacing the 1s on the diagonal), take its top m eigenpairs to get Λ̂ = V̂_m Λ̂_m^(1/2), recompute ψ̂ⱼ = 1 − ∑ₖ λ̂²ⱼₖ, and iterate to convergence. Cheap, distribution-free, no fit test. The one-line summary of FA versus PCA: FA eigendecomposes the correlation matrix with the diagonal replaced by communalities; PCA leaves the diagonal alone.
  • Maximum likelihood. Assume multivariate normality and minimise the Gaussian discrepancy between the sample covariance S and the model Σ(Λ, Ψ) = ΛΛᵀ + Ψ. This is what a modern factor-analysis routine defaults to, and it is the version that comes with inference.
ML discrepancy function and its large-sample χ² test of "m factors suffice" F(Λ, Ψ) = tr(Σ⁻¹S) − ln|Σ⁻¹S| − d (≥ 0, and = 0 exactly when Σ = S) X² = [ n − 1 − (2d + 5)/6 − 2m/3 ] · F_min ~ χ²(df) df = ½[(d − m)² − (d + m)]

Three practical notes on ML factor analysis. First, it is scale-equivariant: rescale a variable and the loadings and uniqueness rescale with it, so the fitted model is substantively the same whether you work from the covariance or the correlation matrix. PCA has no such property (see §02) — this is a genuine advantage of the model-based approach, not a matter of taste. Second, the optimisation can drive some ψ̂ⱼ to zero or below, a Heywood case, which signals too many factors, too few variables per factor, or a variable that is nearly a pure copy of a factor; a boundary solution should be reported, not silently clipped. Third, the χ² test rejects almost any model at large n, because with tens of thousands of observations even a trivially wrong Σ is detectable — a p-value of 0 on a very large sample tells you the sample is large, not that the factor structure is useless. Use it comparatively (does m + 1 improve on m?) and alongside parallel analysis — compare observed eigenvalues to those from resampled or randomly permuted data and keep the ones that beat chance — or an information criterion.

Factor scores are estimated, not computed

A PCA score is a definition: z = Pᵀ(x − μ), exact, deterministic, invertible on the retained subspace. A factor score is an inference about an unobserved quantity, and it cannot be recovered exactly even if Λ and Ψ were known perfectly — there are m unknowns per observation on top of the d unknown residuals, so the system is underdetermined. This is factor score indeterminacy, and it is a property of the model, not of the estimator.

two standard estimators of f given x regression (Thomson): f̂ = Λᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) ← minimum MSE; biased, shrunk toward 0, correlated across factors weighted least squares (Bartlett): f̂ = (ΛᵀΨ⁻¹Λ)⁻¹ ΛᵀΨ⁻¹ (x − μ) ← unbiased, higher variance

Practical consequence: if what you actually need is a set of numbers to feed a downstream model, PCA scores are exact and free, while factor scores carry estimation error that your downstream model will not know about. If what you need is an interpretable claim about latent structure, the reverse holds.

The bridge: probabilistic PCA

Constrain the uniquenesses to be equal, Ψ = σ²I, and the factor model becomes probabilistic PCA. Its maximum-likelihood solution is available in closed form and spans exactly the principal subspace; letting σ² → 0 recovers classical PCA. So PCA is the corner of the factor-analysis family in which every variable is assumed to have the same amount of private noise.

PPCA: the isotropic-noise special case Σ = ΛΛᵀ + σ²I σ̂² = (1/(d − m))·∑ⱼ₌ₘ₊₁..d λⱼ ← the average discarded eigenvalue Λ̂ = V_m (Λ_m − σ̂²I)^(1/2) R R arbitrary orthogonal σ² → 0 ⟹ classical PCA

This is worth carrying around because it makes the choice concrete. Are the unique variances plausibly all equal (pixels of one sensor, repeated measurements of one instrument)? Then PCA is the right member of the family and is far cheaper. Do they obviously differ (a survey mixing a precise behavioural measure with a noisy self-report; a panel of assets with wildly different idiosyncratic risk)? Then forcing them equal is a modelling error, and it will show up as distorted loadings.

When a practitioner should actually reach for factor analysis

  • You believe in the latent construct. Psychometrics, survey research, questionnaire scale construction, quality-of-service and brand perception studies: the variables are noisy indicators of something you cannot measure directly, and that "something" is the object of interest.
  • Unique variances plainly differ across variables. Mixed instruments, mixed reliability, some items known to be noisy. Forcing Ψ = σ²I would be wrong on the face of it.
  • The correlations are the evidence. You care about which variables hang together and how strongly, not about compressing rows. FA fits the off-diagonal and reports what is left over as residual correlations — a diagnostic PCA cannot provide.
  • You want to test a structure. Does a two-factor model suffice? Is a proposed grouping of items consistent with the data? FA has degrees of freedom and a fit statistic; PCA has neither. (Pushed further, this becomes confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modelling, where Λ's zero pattern is fixed in advance and tested.)
  • You need results comparable across rescalings. ML factor analysis is scale-equivariant; PCA's answer changes with units.

And when to stay with PCA: compression and speed; decorrelation and whitening; a quick 2-D look; any pipeline where the transform must be exact, cheap and applicable to new rows with one matrix multiply; and every situation where you have no theory about latent causes and would only be inventing one. Note also that FA is a model of variables, so it does not compete with methods that model observations — if your goal is to group rows rather than columns, see Clustering.

Takeaway: the arrows point in opposite directions. In PCA the components are consequences of the observed variables (z = Pᵀx); in FA the observed variables are consequences of the factors (x = Λf + ε). PCA is guaranteed to exist and explains variance; FA can be wrong, which is exactly what makes it informative when it fits.

A decision procedure

  1. Do you have labels, and is the downstream task classification? If yes, LDA is a candidate — but check d against n first. If d ≥ n − C, either shrink S_W or run a generous PCA step first, then LDA.
  2. Do you want to reduce features, or to explain correlations? Reduce features ⟹ PCA. Explain correlations with a named latent structure ⟹ factor analysis.
  3. Are the unique variances plausibly equal? If yes, PCA is the right (and cheaper) member of the family — it is factor analysis with Ψ = σ²I and σ² → 0. If no, the equal-noise assumption is a modelling error.
  4. Do you need exact, cheap, reproducible scores for new rows? PCA or LDA. Factor scores are estimates and are not unique.
  5. Do you need a claim you can be wrong about? Only factor analysis offers one. PCA always "works", which is exactly why a good PCA fit is not evidence of anything.

Two combinations are worth knowing as standard practice. PCA → LDA repairs the small-sample problem, at the cost of possibly discarding the discriminative direction in the unsupervised step — keep the PCA stage generous. PCA → clustering (see Clustering) makes distance-based methods behave in high dimension, and pairs naturally with whitening from §06.

Takeaway: unsupervised max-variance (PCA), supervised max-separation (LDA), latent-cause model (FA). Only the second uses labels; only the third can be rejected by the data; only the first is guaranteed to be optimal at the thing it optimises, which is not necessarily the thing you want.

13 Kernel PCA and the alternatives

Kernel PCA is the nonlinear extension. Rather than mapping the data through some φ(x) and decomposing the covariance in that (possibly infinite-dimensional) space, it uses the Gram trick from §07: everything can be written in terms of inner products, so replace them with a kernel and eigendecompose the n×n kernel matrix instead.

The table below places PCA among the nonlinear and neighbour-embedding alternatives; for the head-to-head with the two classical linear siblings — LDA and factor analysis — see §12.

double-center the kernel matrix (the substitute for subtracting the mean in feature space) K̃ = K − 1ₙK − K1ₙ + 1ₙK1ₙ where (1ₙ)ᵢⱼ = 1/n then eigendecompose and project a new pointK̃ αⱼ = n λⱼ αⱼ zⱼ(x) = ∑ᵢ₌₁..n αⱼᵢ · k(xᵢ, x)

The price is real: cost is O(n²) memory and up to O(n³) time in the number of samples rather than features, projecting a new point requires the whole training set, and there is no exact pre-image — reconstructing x̂ from the scores is itself an optimisation problem. Kernel PCA is a good tool at a few thousand samples and a bad one at a million.

14 Pitfalls & what to remember

  • Not scaling mixed-unit features. The largest-variance feature becomes PC1 by arithmetic, not by importance. Standardize unless the units are genuinely comparable.
  • Fitting on the full dataset. μ, σ and P are learned; fit them on the training fold only.
  • Choosing k by variance when a label exists. Cross-validate k against the downstream metric instead.
  • Interpreting the sign or the identity of a component. Signs are arbitrary; near-tied eigenvalues make individual components arbitrary too. Only the subspace is stable.
  • Whitening everything. Dividing by a near-zero √λ amplifies pure noise. Truncate first.
  • Expecting PCA to unfold a manifold. It is a rotation. Curved structure needs kernel PCA, UMAP or an autoencoder.
  • Ignoring outliers. A single extreme point can dominate a squared-error criterion; inspect or use a robust variant.
  • Applying it to one-hot or categorical data. Variance on an indicator column is a function of frequency, not meaning; look at correspondence analysis or a learned embedding instead.
  • Using PCA when you have labels and a classifier downstream. Variance is not separability; check Fisher's criterion along the components you kept, or use LDA.
  • Calling a PCA component a "factor". PCA is a rotation of the observed variables, not a latent-variable model. If you mean unobserved causes, per-variable unique variances and a testable fit, you want factor analysis.
  • Reporting a 2-D plot without the explained variance. Two components out of 300 may be showing you almost nothing.
The one idea: PCA finds the rotation whose first k axes capture the most variance, which is identically the rank-k linear subspace with the smallest reconstruction error. Everything on this page — scaling, choosing k, whitening, kernelizing — is bookkeeping around that single fact. For the derivation itself, see Quadratic Forms, Covariance & PCA.
