EM Algorithm & Mixture Models

UnsupervisedProbabilisticLatent Variables When some variables are hidden, you cannot maximize the likelihood directly. Expectation-Maximization (EM) alternates between guessing the hidden structure (E-step) and fitting the parameters to that guess (M-step), monotonically climbing the likelihood surface until it reaches a local maximum. Gaussian Mixture Models are the flagship application; k-Means is a hard-assignment special case.

01 Overview

Many probabilistic models posit latent (hidden) variables that make the joint distribution elegant but make the marginal likelihood intractable.

  • Observed data X = {x₁, …, xₙ}, latent variables Z = {z₁, …, zₙ}, parameters θ.
  • Goal: maximize the marginal (incomplete-data) log-likelihood:
    marginal log-likelihoodlog p(X | θ) = Σᵢ log p(xᵢ | θ) = Σᵢ log [ Σ_z p(xᵢ, z | θ) ]
    The log of a sum has no closed-form optimum in general.
  • EM sidesteps this by working with the complete-data log-likelihood log p(X, Z | θ), which does decompose cleanly, weighted by the posterior over Z.
  • Applications: Gaussian mixture models, missing-data imputation, hidden Markov model training (Baum-Welch), topic models, factor analysis, mixture of experts.

02 The EM algorithm

Starting from an initial guess θ⁽⁰⁾, repeat until convergence:

  1. E-step — compute the posterior distribution of the latent variables given the current parameters:
    E-step: posterior over latent variablesq(Z) = p(Z | X, θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
  2. M-step — maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood with respect to θ:
    M-step: maximize the Q functionQ(θ | θ⁽ᵗ⁾) = E_{Z|X,θ⁽ᵗ⁾}[ log p(X, Z | θ) ] θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = argmax_θ Q(θ | θ⁽ᵗ⁾)

Intuitively: "if I knew the hidden assignments, the MLE would be easy" — EM replaces the unknown assignments with their expected values, solves the easy problem, and iterates.

Monotonic guarantee. Every EM iteration satisfies log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾) ≥ log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁾). The log-likelihood never decreases. Proof: the ELBO section below shows EM maximizes a lower bound that is tight at each E-step.

03 Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

A GMM models the data as a weighted sum of K Gaussian components:

mixture density (K components)p(x | θ) = Σₖ₌₁ᴷ πₖ · N(x | μₖ, Σₖ) mixing weights sum to 1πₖ ≥ 0, Σₖ πₖ = 1
  • Parameters: θ = {πₖ, μₖ, Σₖ}ₖ₌₁ᴷ — mixing weights, means, and covariance matrices.
  • The latent variable zᵢ ∈ {1, …, K} indicates which component generated point xᵢ.
  • GMMs can model elliptical, overlapping clusters of different sizes and orientations — far more flexible than the spherical assumption of k-Means.

E-step: responsibilities

Compute the posterior probability that point xᵢ belongs to component k:

responsibility of component k for point iγ(zᵢₖ) = πₖ · N(xᵢ | μₖ, Σₖ) / Σⱼ₌₁ᴷ πⱼ · N(xᵢ | μⱼ, Σⱼ)

M-step: update parameters

Let Nₖ = Σᵢ γ(zᵢₖ) be the effective number of points assigned to component k.

updated means, covariances, and weightsμₖ_new = (1/Nₖ) Σᵢ γ(zᵢₖ) · xᵢ Σₖ_new = (1/Nₖ) Σᵢ γ(zᵢₖ) · (xᵢ − μₖ_new)(xᵢ − μₖ_new)ᵀ πₖ_new = Nₖ / N
Worked example (1D, K=2). Data: {1, 2, 5, 6, 7}. Init: μ₁=1.5, μ₂=6.0, σ₁=σ₂=1.5, π₁=π₂=0.5. E-step: N(1|1.5,1.5)≈0.246, N(1|6.0,1.5)≈0.0009 ⇒ γ(z₁₁)≈0.996, γ(z₁₂)≈0.004. Point 1 is almost entirely assigned to component 1. Points 5,6,7 go almost entirely to component 2. Point 2 is mixed but mostly component 1. M-step updates μ₁ toward ~1.5, μ₂ toward ~6.0, with refined variances and weights.

05 k-Means as a hard-assignment limit of EM

k-Means is what you get from EM for a GMM when you make three simplifications:

  1. Equal, isotropic covariances: all components share Σₖ = σ²I.
  2. Equal mixing weights: πₖ = 1/K.
  3. Hard assignment: take the limit σ² → 0, so each responsibility becomes 0 or 1 — every point belongs entirely to its nearest centroid.

In practice, k-Means is often used to initialize EM — run k-Means first to get rough centroids, then refine with EM to capture the full covariance structure. See the full k-Means treatment on the Clustering page.

06 ELBO / free energy view

The deepest way to understand EM is through a lower bound on the log-likelihood.

For any distribution q(Z) over the latent variables:

Jensen's inequality gives the evidence lower bound (ELBO)log p(X | θ) ≥ E_q[ log p(X, Z | θ) ] − E_q[ log q(Z) ] = E_q[ log p(X, Z | θ) ] + H[q] =: L(q, θ) ← the ELBO

Equivalently, the gap between the log-likelihood and the ELBO is the KL divergence:

the gap is a KL divergencelog p(X | θ) = L(q, θ) + KL( q(Z) || p(Z | X, θ) ) KL ≥ 0, with equality iff q = p(Z | X, θ)
  • E-step = maximize L w.r.t. q ⇒ set q(Z) = p(Z | X, θ⁽ᵗ⁾), which makes KL = 0 and the bound tight.
  • M-step = maximize L w.r.t. θ ⇒ increase the log-likelihood (since KL ≥ 0).
  • EM is coordinate ascent on the ELBO: alternate between optimizing q and θ. This is the bridge to variational inference, where the E-step uses an approximate q from a restricted family because the exact posterior is intractable.
Why monotonic. At the E-step, L = log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁾) (bound is tight). At the M-step, θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ maximizes L, so L(θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾) ≥ L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾) = log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁾). Since log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾) ≥ L(θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾), we get log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾) ≥ log p(X | θ⁽ᵗ⁾).

07 Convergence properties

  • Monotonic increase: the log-likelihood never decreases across iterations (proved above).
  • Convergence to a stationary point: under mild regularity conditions, EM converges to a local maximum (or saddle point) of log p(X | θ). It does not guarantee the global maximum.
  • Rate: EM converges linearly, with the rate depending on the "fraction of missing information." When latent variables are highly informative (responsibilities are sharp), convergence is fast. When assignments are ambiguous, convergence can be very slow.
  • Stopping criteria: (a) change in log-likelihood |Δℓ| < ε, (b) change in parameters ||θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ − θ⁽ᵗ⁾|| < ε, or (c) maximum iterations.

08 Practical issues

Initialization sensitivity

  • EM inherits the local-optima problem. Poor initialization can lead to bad solutions.
  • Strategies: (a) run k-Means first; (b) random restarts — run EM many times and keep the highest log-likelihood; (c) use k-means++ seeding for the initial means.

Singularities in GMMs

  • If a Gaussian component collapses onto a single data point, its variance goes to zero and the likelihood goes to infinity. This is an artifact of maximum likelihood with mixtures, not a useful solution.
  • Fixes: (a) add a small regularization to the covariance: Σₖ + εI; (b) set a minimum variance floor; (c) use a Bayesian prior (MAP estimation with an inverse-Wishart prior on Σ); (d) detect and re-initialize collapsed components.

Model selection: how many components?

  • The log-likelihood always increases with K (more components = more capacity), so raw likelihood cannot choose K.
  • BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion): BIC = −2 log p(X | θ̂) + p · log(n), where p = number of free parameters. Penalizes complexity; pick the K that minimizes BIC.
  • AIC (Akaike): AIC = −2 log p(X | θ̂) + 2p. Lighter penalty than BIC; tends to pick larger models.
  • For a d-dimensional GMM with K full-covariance components: p = K·d + K·d(d+1)/2 + (K−1) free parameters (means + covariances + weights).

10 Applications beyond clustering

  • Missing data imputation: treat missing values as latent variables. E-step fills them in with their expected values; M-step re-estimates the model. This is the original motivation for EM.
  • Hidden Markov Models: the Baum-Welch algorithm for training HMMs is EM, where the latent variables are the hidden state sequences. The E-step uses the forward-backward algorithm. See Sequence Models.
  • Topic models (LDA): the per-document topic proportions and per-word topic assignments are latent. Variational EM is the standard inference method. See Topic Modeling.
  • Factor analysis: a linear latent-variable model where EM estimates the factor loadings and noise. Closely related to probabilistic PCA. See Factor Analysis.
  • Semi-supervised learning: EM can leverage unlabeled data by treating class labels as latent for the unlabeled portion, using a generative classifier (e.g., Naive Bayes + EM).
  • Image segmentation: model pixel intensities as a mixture; responsibilities give a soft segmentation map.
Pattern: whenever a model has a clean joint p(X, Z | θ) but an intractable marginal, EM is the go-to algorithm. The E-step computes whatever posterior is needed; the M-step solves the resulting weighted MLE.
