Linear & Logistic Regression

The two workhorses of supervised learning: a straight line for continuous targets, and that same line squashed through a sigmoid for class probabilities. This is the overview — what they share, how they differ, and when to reach for them. The full notes live on two sub-pages.

Supervised Learning Regression Classification 2 sub-topics

Overview

Both methods are supervised — they learn from labeled input→output pairs — and both are linear in their parameters, which is exactly what makes them simple, fast, and highly interpretable. They're also the conceptual foundation for much of modern ML.

  • Linear regression predicts a continuous target (price, temperature, demand).
  • Logistic regression, despite the name, is a classification method: it predicts the probability that an observation belongs to a class.
  • Simple regression uses one predictor; multiple regression uses several.
Where this page sits. The linear-algebra view of least squares owns the geometry: projecting the target onto the column space of the design matrix, and the pseudoinverse that falls out of it. This page owns the ML angle — why the loss is what it is, how the coefficients are actually found, what the assumptions buy you, the logistic extension, and how you evaluate the result.

map Two models, one linear score

Both compute wᵀx + b. Linear regression reports that score; logistic regression pushes it through a sigmoid and reads it as a probability. Everything else — the loss, the fitting, the diagnostics — follows from that one difference.

Both are generalized linear models: a linear score, a link function, and a loss that is the negative log-likelihood of the matching noise distribution. Swap the link and the distribution and you get Poisson regression for counts, gamma regression for positive skewed targets, and so on — the fitting machinery never changes.

Does feature scaling matter?

Three different answers, depending on what you're doing. Getting this wrong is one of the most common silent bugs in a regression pipeline.

  • Closed-form OLS: irrelevant. Rescale xⱼ by a factor c and β̂ⱼ simply becomes β̂ⱼ/c. Predictions, residuals, MSE and R² are all bit-for-bit identical. The only reason to scale here is numerical conditioning when the columns differ by many orders of magnitude.
  • Gradient descent: critical. Wildly different feature ranges make the loss contours long thin ellipses. The gradient points across the valley rather than down it, so the path zig-zags and the largest-curvature direction caps the safe learning rate for every parameter. Standardizing makes the contours near-circular and can cut iteration counts by orders of magnitude. Details on the optimization page.
  • Regularization: mandatory. The L1/L2 penalty λ‖β‖ is not scale-invariant. A feature measured in metres gets a coefficient 1000× smaller than the same feature in millimetres, so it is penalized 10⁶× less under L2 — the penalty silently decides which features matter based on their units. Always standardize before fitting Ridge, Lasso, or a regularized logistic regression. (Leave the intercept unpenalized; it is not a feature.)
  • Logistic regression: effectively always scale. Because a typical implementation regularizes by default, the point above applies whether or not you asked for it, and the iterative solver benefits regardless.
Fit the scaler on training data only. Compute the mean and standard deviation on the training split and apply those same numbers to validation and test. Fitting the scaler on everything leaks test-set statistics into training. Standardization vs. min-max normalization, and how to wire it into a pipeline, are covered under feature scaling.

Regularization, extensions & pitfalls

Regularization (prevent overfitting)

Add a penalty on coefficient size to the loss. For linear regression this also cures a singular XᵀX: Ridge solves (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, and XᵀX + λI is invertible for any λ > 0 even when p > n.

penalized objectivesRidge (L2): MSE + λ · Σⱼ βⱼ² Lasso (L1): MSE + λ · Σⱼ |βⱼ| Elastic Net: MSE + λ · [ ρ·Σⱼ |βⱼ| + (1 − ρ)·Σⱼ βⱼ² ]
  • Ridge (L2) — shrinks coefficients toward (never exactly to) zero; keeps every feature; handles correlated predictors gracefully by splitting the credit between them.
  • Lasso (L1) — drives some coefficients exactly to zero, performing automatic feature selection. Among correlated features it tends to pick one arbitrarily and zero the rest.
  • Elastic Net — a blend that keeps L1's sparsity while letting correlated groups enter together.
  • Same idea for logistic regression, applied to the log-loss instead of the MSE — and there it doubles as the fix for perfect separation.

The geometry of why L1 produces corners and L2 doesn't, how to choose λ, and the connection to Bayesian priors are all developed on the regularization page; the generalization story behind it is the bias-variance tradeoff.

Extensions

  • Polynomial regression — add x², x³, … to capture curvature while staying linear-in-parameters. Degree is a variance dial: raise it and training error always falls while test error eventually explodes.
  • Interaction terms — x₁·x₂ lets one feature's effect depend on another's, which a plain additive model cannot express.
  • Basis expansions and splines — piecewise polynomials with continuity constraints; local flexibility without a globally wild high-degree polynomial.
  • Weighted least squares — per-observation weights, the standard fix for known heteroscedasticity.
  • Other GLMs — Poisson for counts, gamma for positive skewed targets, ordinal logistic for ranked categories.

Multicollinearity

When two or more predictors are highly correlated they carry redundant information, which destabilizes the fit:

  • Unstable coefficients with inflated standard errors — variables can look insignificant despite real effects, and signs can flip on a small change to the sample.
  • Hard interpretation — credit for the effect can't be cleanly assigned, and "holding the others fixed" describes a scenario the data never contains.
  • Predictions are usually fine. Multicollinearity is an inference problem, not a prediction problem — if you only need ŷ, it may not matter at all.
  • Detect with the Variance Inflation Factor, VIFⱼ = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ) where R²ⱼ comes from regressing xⱼ on all the other features (VIF > 5–10 is a red flag), or a correlation matrix (values near ±1). Note VIF catches pairwise and multi-way collinearity; a correlation matrix only catches pairs.
  • Remedies: drop one of the correlated variables, combine them into a single index, apply PCA to decorrelate, or use Ridge — which is the standard answer, since the λI term is exactly what makes the problem well-conditioned again.

When is this the right tool?

Reach for it when …

  • You need to explain the model. Every prediction decomposes into a sum of per-feature contributions with signs, units and confidence intervals. Nothing else is this transparent.
  • You need coefficient-level inference — "does this variable matter, and by how much, with what uncertainty?" Trees and neural nets give you importance scores, not estimates with standard errors.
  • You need calibrated probabilities for a downstream cost calculation or an expected-value decision.
  • The signal is roughly linear, or can be made so with a handful of transforms and interactions you understand.
  • n is small to medium, or n is not much larger than p. A low-variance model wins when there isn't enough data to estimate a complicated one.
  • You need a fast, strong baseline. It fits in milliseconds, has almost no hyperparameters, and is astonishingly hard to beat by much on tabular data with modest sample sizes. Always run it first — a fancy model that can't beat it isn't worth deploying.
  • The domain is regulated (credit, insurance, clinical risk) and you must justify individual decisions.
  • Inference must be cheap — a dot product, on any hardware, in microseconds.

Reach for something else when …

  • The relationship is strongly nonlinear or interaction-heavy and you can't hand-engineer your way there. Gradient-boosted trees find interactions automatically.
  • Features are raw images, audio, or text. Pixels and characters have no linear relationship to the label; you need learned representations.
  • You have very high-cardinality categoricals. One-hot encoding explodes the dimension and produces a sparse, ill-conditioned design; trees or target/embedding encodings handle this far better.
  • The data has heavy outliers and you're using OLS — either switch loss (Huber, quantile regression) or switch model.
  • p ≫ n with no regularization — the fit is exactly determined or undetermined, XᵀX is singular, and training error hits zero with no generalization. Regularize or reduce dimension first.
  • The decision boundary is genuinely non-monotone in a feature (risk high at both extremes, low in the middle). A single linear term cannot express that at all.
  • Errors are strongly dependent — panel data, repeated measures, spatial or time-series structure. Use mixed models, GLS, or a time-series method rather than pretending the observations are independent.
The honest framing. Linear and logistic regression are the reference point of supervised learning, not a rung you graduate off. Their bias is high and their variance is low, so they degrade gracefully and fail in ways you can see. Start here, measure, and only add capacity where the residuals prove you need it.
