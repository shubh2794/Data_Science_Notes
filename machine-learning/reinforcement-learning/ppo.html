PPO: Proximal Policy Optimization

Collect a batch with the current policy, then squeeze several epochs of minibatch gradient ascent out of it — without the policy running away from the data that justified the update. The hub page derives the policy gradient, baselines and actor-critic (§08 there); this page starts where that leaves off: why reusing a batch breaks the plain estimator, what TRPO's constraint was buying, how the clipped probability ratio replaces it with a first-order objective, which samples actually carry gradient, the adaptive-KL alternative, the joint policy–value–entropy loss, truncated GAE, the data-reuse arithmetic of the algorithm, the surrogate ablation, and what clipping does not guarantee.

01 The problem: one batch, many gradient steps

On-policy data is expensive, so you want to take many gradient steps per batch. The plain policy-gradient loss does not survive that. PPO is the answer to a narrow question: what objective can you safely optimise for several epochs on data that a slightly older policy collected?

The standard estimator (derived on the hub, §08) is the gradient of a surrogate loss that automatic differentiation can handle:

the policy-gradient estimator and the loss it differentiatesĝ = Êₜ[ ∇θ log π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) · Âₜ ] L^PG(θ) = Êₜ[ log π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) · Âₜ ] ∇θ L^PG = ĝ Êₜ[…] = the empirical average over a finite batch of timesteps Âₜ = an advantage estimate, computed once, under the policy that collected the data

ĝ is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient only at the parameters that collected the batch. After the first step, the samples come from a different policy than the one being differentiated, and L^PG has no term that notices. Worse, it has no maximum: with a mean baseline some Âₜ are negative, and pushing log π_θ of those actions towards −∞ increases L^PG without bound. The original study's verdict is that taking multiple optimisation steps on L^PG with the same trajectory is not well-justified and empirically produces destructively large policy updates.

02 TRPO in one paragraph: a constraint, and why a penalty is hard

TRPO's move was to change the objective and forbid large changes in the policy, measured as a KL divergence between old and new action distributions — not as a distance in parameter space.

TRPO: constrained surrogatemaximise over θ Êₜ[ π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) / π_θold(aₜ|sₜ) · Âₜ ] subject to Êₜ[ KL[ π_θold(·|sₜ), π_θ(·|sₜ) ] ] ≤ δ solved approximately with conjugate gradient, after a LINEAR approximation of the objective and a QUADRATIC approximation of the constraint the penalty form the theory actually suggestsmaximise over θ Êₜ[ rₜ(θ)·Âₜ − β · KL[ π_θold(·|sₜ), π_θ(·|sₜ) ] ] (a surrogate using the MAX KL over states, not the mean, lower-bounds the performance of the new policy — so penalising is the principled form)

So why does TRPO use a hard constraint instead of the simpler penalty that first-order SGD could optimise? Because no single fixed β works: the right coefficient differs between problems, and even within one problem the characteristics change as learning proceeds. The study's experiments confirm that simply choosing a fixed β and optimising the penalised objective with SGD is not sufficient (§11's ablation shows the fixed-β rows). PPO keeps the idea — stay close to π_θold — but encodes it directly in the objective's shape. KL divergence itself, and why it is non-negative, is on the concentration & inequalities page.

03 The probability ratio and the conservative-policy-iteration surrogate

Rewrite the objective in terms of the ratio of new to old action probabilities. It is the importance weight that turns data from π_θold into an estimate about π_θ, it equals 1 at the start of every update, and it makes "how far did the policy move on this sample" a single number.

the ratio and the unclipped surrogaterₜ(θ) = π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) / π_θold(aₜ|sₜ) rₜ(θold) = 1 L^CPI(θ) = Êₜ[ π_θ(aₜ|sₜ)/π_θold(aₜ|sₜ) · Âₜ ] = Êₜ[ rₜ(θ) · Âₜ ] CPI = conservative policy iteration, where this surrogate was introduced why it is an importance-sampling estimate (per state)𝔼_(a ~ π_θ)[ A(s,a) ] = ∑ₐ π_θ(a|s)·A(s,a) = ∑ₐ π_θold(a|s) · (π_θ(a|s)/π_θold(a|s)) · A(s,a) = 𝔼_(a ~ π_θold)[ r(θ) · A(s,a) ] at θ = θold it has the same gradient as L^PG∇θ rₜ(θ) = ∇θ π_θ / π_θold = rₜ(θ) · ∇θ log π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) ∇θ L^CPI = Êₜ[ rₜ(θ) · ∇θ log π_θ(aₜ|sₜ) · Âₜ ] → at θ = θold (r = 1): ĝ
  • Same first step, different afterwards. The importance weight rₜ is what L^PG was missing: as the policy moves, each sample's contribution is reweighted by how much more or less likely its action has become. The two objectives agree to first order at θold, and only L^CPI remains a meaningful estimate once θ moves.
  • But it is still unconstrained. Without a constraint, maximising L^CPI would lead to an excessively large policy update: it is linear in the new probabilities, so for a positive-advantage action it pays to push rₜ as high as it goes. Importance weights also become high-variance far from 1 — the variance story is on the Monte Carlo variance-reduction page.
  • A second, hidden approximation (general knowledge, not spelled out in the 2017 study): the ratio corrects for which actions are taken, but the states sₜ still come from π_θold's state distribution. That mismatch is small only while the policies are close — another reason the surrogate is trustworthy only near θold.

04 The clipped surrogate, case by case

Clip the ratio into [1 − ε, 1 + ε], then take the minimum of the clipped and unclipped terms. The clip removes the incentive to move rₜ outside the interval; the min makes sure the clip can only ever make the objective smaller, never hide a move that made things worse.

05 Why it is a pessimistic lower bound — and a hinge penalty in disguise

Because min(x, y) ≤ x, every term of L^CLIP is at most the corresponding term of L^CPI. The change in ratio is ignored when it would improve the objective, and included when it makes it worse.

three facts, each from the definition(1) L^CLIP(θ) ≤ L^CPI(θ) for every θ (min ≤ its first argument) (2) L^CLIP(θ) = L^CPI(θ) while every rₜ ∈ [1 − ε, 1 + ε] (3) ∇L^CLIP(θold) = ∇L^CPI(θold) = ĝ at θold every rₜ = 1, strictly inside rearranged: the clip subtracts a per-sample hinge (this page's rearrangement)L^CLIP = L^CPI − Êₜ[ penaltyₜ ] penaltyₜ = (rₜ − (1 + ε)) · Âₜ if Âₜ > 0 and rₜ > 1 + ε ≥ 0 = (rₜ − (1 − ε)) · Âₜ if Âₜ < 0 and rₜ < 1 − ε ≥ 0 (both factors negative) = 0 otherwise
  • Compare with the KL penalty of §02. The theory said: subtract β·KL. Clipping subtracts something with the same job — a penalty for moving too far — but it is zero inside the trust region, grows linearly outside it, applies only on the side that would have helped, and needs no coefficient. The study describes L^CLIP exactly this way: a lower bound on L^CPI with a penalty for having too large a policy update.
  • Measured on a real update. Interpolating the parameters along one PPO update direction on the Hopper task, the study plots several surrogates against the interpolation factor. The updated policy ends at a KL of about 0.02 from the initial one, and that is where L^CLIP attains its maximum — while L^CPI keeps rising past it. The clip turned an objective that recommends going further into one whose optimum sits at a modest step.
  • Pessimism is a property of the surrogate, not of performance. "Lower bound" means lower than the unclipped surrogate. It is not a certified lower bound on the true return of the new policy — that is what the max-KL theory of §02 would give, at the price of a very conservative step.

06 The alternative: a KL penalty with an adaptive coefficient

If a fixed β fails because no single value fits, adapt it: after each policy update, compare the KL actually achieved with a target, and halve or double β. The study presents this as an alternative (or addition) to clipping; it performed worse than clipping in their experiments, but is an important baseline.

per policy update1. optimise L^KLPEN(θ) = Êₜ[ rₜ(θ)·Âₜ − β · KL[ π_θold(·|sₜ), π_θ(·|sₜ) ] ] with several epochs of minibatch SGD 2. compute d = Êₜ[ KL[ π_θold(·|sₜ), π_θ(·|sₜ) ] ] if d < d_targ / 1.5 → β ← β / 2 (moved too little: loosen) if d > d_targ × 1.5 → β ← β × 2 (moved too far: tighten) the updated β is used for the NEXT policy update
  • A thermostat, not a constraint. Within one update the KL can still overshoot; the controller only corrects the next update. Occasionally a policy update ends with a KL far from d_targ, but β adjusts quickly.
  • Robust to its own knobs. The 1.5 and 2 were chosen heuristically and the algorithm is not very sensitive to them; the initial β is unimportant because it adapts (it was initialised at 1 in the ablation).
  • The idea survives. The Roboschool runs of §12 adjust the Adam step size based on a target KL — the same feedback loop pointed at a different knob.

07 A live toy: what K epochs on one batch do to each surrogate

A four-armed bandit with a softmax policy is the smallest setting where the failure of §01 is visible. Everything below is computed in your browser; it is an illustration of the mechanism, not a reproduction of any reported result.

Reusing one noisy batch for K epochs: unclipped surrogates commit, the clipped one stops raise the epochs or the step size; switch to "many iterations" to see what commitment costs over a whole run
  • Start with K = 1. All three methods then produce exactly the same policy: every ratio is 1 at the first step, so the three gradients coincide (§05, fact 3). Every difference you see at larger K comes from reusing the batch.
  • Why the unclipped curves run away. On a fixed batch, L^CPI is linear in the new probabilities — ∑ₐ π_θ(a) · (sum of Âᵢ on arm a) / (n·π_θold(a)) — so its maximum is a deterministic policy on whichever arm has the largest importance-weighted batch advantage. L^PG = ∑ₐ (sum of Âᵢ on arm a) · log π_θ(a) has no maximum at all: any arm with a negative total is pushed towards probability zero forever. Both "fit the batch", including its noise.
  • Why that is destructive, not just noisy. Once an arm's probability is near zero it is almost never sampled again, so no future batch can correct the mistake. The many-iterations view shows this as seeds stuck on the wrong arm. Clipping makes each iteration a small step, so a lucky or unlucky batch only nudges the policy and the next batch can disagree.
  • Clipping is per sample, not per arm. With noise, one arm has both positive- and negative-advantage pulls. Once that arm's ratio passes 1 + ε, its positive samples go silent while its negative samples stay active, so the ratio is held near the boundary rather than frozen — watch the teal bars hover around the band edge. A large η can still jump past the band in one step; §14 returns to that.

08 The joint objective: policy, value and entropy in one network

The advantages need a learned value function V(s). If policy and value share parameters — one network, two heads — the loss must combine the policy surrogate with a value-function error term, and the study adds an entropy bonus to keep exploring. This is the compatibility with parameter sharing that TRPO lacked.

maximised each iteration (approximately)L^(CLIP+VF+S)ₜ(θ) = Êₜ[ L^CLIPₜ(θ) − c₁ · L^VFₜ(θ) + c₂ · S[π_θ](sₜ) ] L^VFₜ = ( V_θ(sₜ) − Vₜ^targ )² squared-error value loss (subtracted: we maximise) S = entropy bonus of the action distribution at sₜ c₁, c₂ = coefficients entropy, for reference (general knowledge)discrete: S[π](s) = −∑ₐ π(a|s) · log π(a|s) largest when uniform Gaussian, per action dimension with std σ: S = ½ · log(2πe·σ²) grows with log σ
  • When the terms are present. In the MuJoCo surrogate ablation (§11) policy and value had separate networks and there was no entropy bonus, so c₁ was irrelevant and c₂ = 0. In the Atari runs (§12) the network was shared, with c₁ = 1 and c₂ = 0.01.
  • Why the entropy term is there. Policy-gradient updates sharpen the policy; nothing in L^CLIP resists premature collapse to a near-deterministic policy. The entropy bonus is the standard counterweight, covered with the other exploration mechanisms on the hub (§09).
  • The value target Vₜ^targ is left general in the study's formula. A common choice in implementations (general knowledge) is the bootstrapped return Âₜ + V_θold(sₜ), computed once with the same advantages.
  • Scale matters with sharing. c₁ balances two losses on very different scales flowing into the same trunk; a value loss that dominates can drown the policy gradient (general knowledge).

09 Advantages from fixed-length segments: truncated GAE

PPO collects fixed-length segments of T timesteps — much shorter than an episode — so the advantage estimator may not look past step T. The study uses a truncated form of generalized advantage estimation, with λ as the bias–variance dial.

T-step bootstrapped advantageÂₜ = −V(sₜ) + rₜ + γ·rₜ₊₁ + … + γ^(T−t−1)·r_(T−1) + γ^(T−t)·V(s_T) truncated GAEδₜ = rₜ + γ·V(sₜ₊₁) − V(sₜ) (the one-step TD error) Âₜ = δₜ + (γλ)·δₜ₊₁ + … + (γλ)^(T−t−1)·δ_(T−1) computed backwards: Â_(T−1) = δ_(T−1), Âₜ = δₜ + γλ·Âₜ₊₁ λ = 1: the sum telescopes to the T-step advantage above λ = 0: Âₜ = δₜ, the one-step actor-critic advantage of the hub's §08

  • The dial. λ = 0 trusts the critic after one step: low variance, biased if V is wrong. λ = 1 uses the actual rewards out to the segment end: unbiased up to the bootstrap at s_T, but the variance of a sum of many random rewards. Every experiment in the study used λ = 0.95. The general principle — trade a little bias for a lot of variance with a control variate — is the subject of the variance-reduction page; the baseline argument is on the hub (§08).
  • Episode boundaries inside a segment (general knowledge): if sₜ₊₁ is terminal, the bootstrap γ·V(sₜ₊₁) and the γλ carry are zeroed at that step.

10 The algorithm, annotated — and how often each sample is reused

The whole method is an actor-critic loop with one unusual feature: the optimisation phase makes K passes over the batch in minibatches, which is safe only because of the surrogate.

  • That is the data efficiency claim in one line. Vanilla policy gradient takes one step per batch; on MuJoCo, PPO takes 320. The MuJoCo hyperparameter table lists no actor count, so N·T = 2048 above assumes the horizon is the whole batch.
  • Adam is the default optimiser — the study notes it usually works better than plain SGD here. Its mechanics are on the optimizers page.
  • "A few lines of code change." Starting from a vanilla policy-gradient implementation, the changes are: replace L^PG with L^CLIP, and run multiple epochs of minibatch updates instead of one step. That simplicity is the study's main selling point against TRPO.
  • Still on-policy. Every batch is generated by the current θold and thrown away after K epochs. The ratio corrects only for the drift within one iteration; contrast the replay buffer of DQN, which reuses transitions from many past policies.

13 Results: continuous control, the humanoid, and 49 Atari games

Continuous control against the existing methods

On the same seven MuJoCo tasks at one million timesteps, PPO with clipping at ε = 0.2 was compared with a tuned TRPO, the cross-entropy method (CEM), vanilla policy gradient with an adaptive step size, A2C, and A2C with a trust region. A2C is the synchronous version of A3C, which the study found to perform the same as or better than the asynchronous one. PPO outperforms the previous methods on almost all the continuous-control environments.

14 What clipping does not guarantee

This section is general knowledge and later analysis, not claims of the original study. The clip removes an incentive; it is not a constraint, and nothing in the method bounds the KL divergence of the update.

no hard bound
Ratios do leave the band
Parameters are shared across all states and actions, so gradients from active samples move the ratios of clipped samples too. Several epochs of minibatch steps compound this, and a single large step can jump far outside [1−ε, 1+ε].
no restoring force
Flat means flat
A positive-advantage sample with r > 1+ε has zero gradient: if it was pushed there, nothing in its own term pulls it back. Only samples on the other side of the min (§04) resist.
tuning still matters
Step size and epochs set the real trust region
The effective step depends on the learning rate, K and M as much as on ε. Many implementations therefore also monitor the approximate KL and stop the epoch loop early when it exceeds a target.
implementation details
The clip is not the whole story
Later careful re-implementations found that code-level choices — advantage normalisation, value-loss clipping, observation and reward normalisation, initialisation, learning-rate annealing — account for a large share of PPO's performance relative to TRPO.

The bandit in §07 shows the first point live. Even at the default settings the clipped policy's largest ratio ends near 1.3, outside the [0.8, 1.2] band. Set η = 8 and K = 1: the first step is unclipped by construction, and it already moves one arm's ratio to about 2.6. In the many-iterations view with the initial seeds, η = 8 leaves 5 of 40 clipped runs stuck on a wrong arm (none at η = 2). The clip limits how far an incentive reaches, not how far a step lands. The adaptive-KL idea of §06 and the KL-targeted step size of the humanoid runs (§12) are the ways to put a number on the drift directly.

15 PPO beyond control: language models and agents

This is later work beyond the original study, which evaluated only simulated robotics and Atari.

  • Preference fine-tuning of language models. Generation is treated as a token-level MDP, a learned reward model scores finished responses, and PPO's clipped surrogate updates the policy while a separate KL term to a frozen reference model keeps it from drifting into reward-model exploits. The two leashes do different jobs: the clip limits each update, the reference KL limits the destination. The full pipeline, and the methods that later replaced PPO there, are on Policy / Preference Optimization.
  • Multi-turn agents. Training tool-using agents on long trajectories with sparse, verifiable outcomes reuses the same machinery — ratio, clip, advantages — with new problems in credit assignment over many turns. See Agentic RL.

16 Common misconceptions

"PPO clips the gradient." No — it clips the probability ratio inside the objective. The gradient of each term is either the full importance-weighted policy gradient Â·r·∇log π or exactly zero (§04). Gradient-norm clipping is a separate, optional trick.
"The clip keeps the new policy within ε of the old one." No — it removes the incentive to go further, per sample. Ratios routinely leave the band through shared parameters and repeated minibatch steps, and the KL of an update is not bounded (§14).
"The min is redundant; clip(r)·Â alone would do." Without the min, harmful moves would be clipped too. A bad action made more likely (Â < 0, r > 1+ε) would sit on a flat region with zero gradient and could never be undone. The min keeps the unclipped, worse value precisely there (§05).
"Reusing the batch for K epochs makes PPO off-policy." It is on-policy. Each batch comes from the current θold and is discarded after one iteration; the ratio only corrects for the small drift within that iteration (§10).
"PPO means the KL-penalty version." The study's main method is the clipped surrogate. The adaptive KL penalty is presented as an alternative and a baseline, and scored lower in the ablation (0.74 at best vs 0.82, §11).
"ε = 0.2 is the universal setting." It won the MuJoCo ablation. The Atari runs used ε = 0.1, annealed to 0 over training (§12).

17 Takeaways

  • The problem: multiple gradient steps on L^PG with one batch produce destructively large updates; TRPO fixes it with a KL constraint and second-order machinery, and a fixed KL penalty fails because no single β fits.
  • The ratio rₜ = π_θ/π_θold makes L^CPI = Êₜ[rₜÂₜ], which matches the policy gradient at θold but rewards unlimited movement.
  • The clip: L^CLIP = Êₜ[min(rÂ, clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)Â)]. For Â > 0 the term is Â·min(r, 1+ε); for Â < 0, Â·max(r, 1−ε). Gradient is zero only where the policy already moved far enough in the helpful direction.
  • A pessimistic bound: L^CLIP ≤ L^CPI, equal near θold; the difference is a coefficient-free hinge penalty on moving too far.
  • The loop: N actors × T steps, truncated GAE with λ = 0.95, then K epochs of Adam on minibatches — 320 steps per batch on MuJoCo — with a joint value loss and entropy bonus when the network is shared.
  • The evidence: no constraint scored −0.39, clipping at 0.2 scored 0.82, the best adaptive KL 0.74; on Atari PPO learned faster than A2C and ACER and matched ACER at the end, with much less machinery.
  • The caveat: clipping is an incentive, not a guarantee — learning rate, epochs, minibatch size and implementation details set the real trust region.
