Splines & GAMs

Nonlinear Models Regression Flexibility Interpretability

Moving beyond straight lines without losing control. Splines fit smooth, piecewise-polynomial curves with local flexibility; Generalized Additive Models stack those curves to handle multiple predictors while keeping each effect interpretable.

01 Overview

Linear models assume a constant relationship between x and y across the entire range. The real world is rarely that uniform.

A linear regression commits to one slope per feature, applied everywhere. When the true relationship curves, bends, or plateaus, that rigidity becomes bias no amount of data can cure. The natural impulse is to reach for polynomials (x, x², x³, …), but high-degree polynomials are global — a wiggly region at one end forces wild oscillations everywhere else.

Splines solve this by thinking locally: fit a low-degree polynomial in each of several intervals and stitch them together smoothly at the boundaries (“knots”). The result is a curve that adapts to local curvature without polluting distant regions. Add a roughness penalty and you get smoothing splines — a form of regularization that automatically controls complexity.

Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) extend the idea to multiple predictors: fit one smooth function per feature and add them up. Each component can be plotted separately, giving the interpretability of a linear model with the flexibility of a nonlinear one.

Key insight: every method in this page turns a nonlinear fitting problem into a linear one — by transforming the input with basis functions. That means ordinary least squares, confidence intervals, and the entire linear-regression toolkit still apply.

02 Polynomial Regression

The simplest nonlinear extension: replace x with x, x², x³, … and fit by ordinary least squares.

degree-d polynomial regression y = β₀ + β₁x + β₂x² + … + β_d x^d + ε
  • Still a linear model in the expanded features (1, x, x², …, x^d). The normal equation or gradient descent solves it without modification.
  • d controls flexibility. d = 1 is a line; d = 2 allows one bend; d = 3 two bends; and so on. In practice, d > 4 is rarely useful — the variance cost outweighs the bias reduction.
  • Global structure: every coefficient affects the entire curve. Fitting a bump on the left shifts the right-hand tail too.
  • Boundary blow-up: polynomials oscillate wildly near the edges of the data range, especially at high degree (the Runge phenomenon). Confidence bands fan out dramatically at the extremes.
  • Numerical instability: powers like x¹&sup0; create huge entries in the design matrix, degrading floating-point precision. Centering and scaling help but do not eliminate the problem.
Takeaway: polynomials are a reasonable first step for gentle curvature (d ≤ 3), but their global nature and boundary behaviour make them a poor choice when flexibility or extrapolation matters. Splines were invented precisely to fix these weaknesses.

03 Basis Functions

The unifying abstraction: pick any set of transformations of x, then fit a linear model in the transformed space.

general basis expansion y = ∑_{m=0}^{M} β_m b_m(x) + ε

Once the basis functions b_m(x) are chosen, the model is linear in β. Everything from closed-form OLS to confidence intervals carries over unchanged. The nonlinearity lives entirely in the choice of basis.

Common basis families

BasisFunctionsCharacter

  • Step functions partition x into bins and fit a constant in each. Simple but discontinuous — fine for a quick look, poor for smooth predictions.
  • Piecewise-linear basis: {1, x, (x − ξ_1)_+, …, (x − ξ_K)_+} gives continuous kink-at-each-knot fits. Better than steps, but the kinks are visible.
  • Splines raise the polynomial degree and enforce smoothness at the knots, eliminating visible kinks.

04 Regression Splines

Piecewise polynomials of degree d, joined smoothly at K knots.

Continuity constraints at a knot ξ

  • Order 0 — f is continuous: no jumps.
  • Order 1 — f′ is continuous: no kinks.
  • Order 2 — f′′ is continuous: smooth curvature transition.

A cubic spline (d = 3) enforces all three: the human eye cannot detect the joins.

Counting degrees of freedom

cubic spline with K knots (K + 1) regions × 4 coefficients − 3K continuity constraints = K + 4 df

Truncated power basis

A convenient explicit basis that spans the cubic-spline space:

basis functions (d = 3) {1, x, x², x³, (x − ξ_1)_+³, (x − ξ_2)_+³, …, (x − ξ_K)_+³}

where the truncated power function is

(x − ξ)_+ = max(0, x − ξ)

Each “hinge” (x − ξ_j)_+³ is zero to the left of its knot and cubic to the right, adding exactly one degree of freedom. With K + 4 basis functions total, an ordinary least-squares fit gives the unique cubic spline that minimizes RSS.

Choosing knot number and placement

  • Too few knots → bias (underfitting); too many → variance (overfitting). See Bias-Variance Tradeoff.
  • Place knots at quantiles of the x distribution — more knots where data is denser.
  • Or skip the choice entirely: smoothing splines (Section 07) place a knot at every data point and control smoothness with a penalty.

Worked example (K = 1 knot at ξ = 5)

  • Basis: {1, x, x², x³, (x − 5)_+³} — five basis functions, 5 df.
  • At x = 3: (3 − 5)_+ = 0, so only the global polynomial terms are active.
  • At x = 7: (7 − 5)_+³ = 8 is added, allowing the curve to deviate from the global cubic on the right of the knot.
  • At x = 5: (5 − 5)_+³ = 0 and all three derivatives of the hinge are also zero at the knot, ensuring the smooth join.

05 Natural Splines & B-Splines

Two refinements that fix the boundary problem and the numerical problem, respectively.

Natural cubic splines — taming the boundaries

A cubic regression spline is an unconstrained cubic beyond the outermost knots. With sparse data at the edges, that cubic can extrapolate wildly. A natural cubic spline adds the constraint that f is linear beyond the boundary knots:

f′′(x) = 0 for x < ξ_1 and x > ξ_K
  • These four constraints (two at each boundary: f′′ = 0 and f′′′ = 0) reduce the degrees of freedom from K + 4 to K.
  • The variance reduction at the boundaries is dramatic, with only a small increase in bias — the bias increase is concentrated at the extreme tails, where data is scarce anyway.
  • The natural spline basis uses K basis functions: N_1(x) = 1, N_2(x) = x, and K − 2 additional functions built from differences of truncated cubics (see the ESL derivation in Section 5.2.1).

B-spline basis — numerical stability

The truncated power basis is conceptually clean but numerically poor: the high powers create large, correlated columns. The B-spline basis spans the same function space with far better conditioning.

  • Compact support: each B-spline of degree d is nonzero over at most d + 2 consecutive knot intervals. This makes the design matrix banded, speeding up both fitting and evaluation.
  • De Boor recursion:
    B_{i,0}(x) = 1 if t_i ≤ x < t_{i+1}, else 0 B_{i,k}(x) = ((x − t_i) / (t_{i+k} − t_i)) B_{i,k−1}(x) + ((t_{i+k+1} − x) / (t_{i+k+1} − t_{i+1})) B_{i+1,k−1}(x)
  • Most software (R's bs(), scikit-learn's SplineTransformer) uses B-splines internally.
  • B-splines for natural splines: apply the boundary linearity constraints to the B-spline basis, yielding the ns() function in R.

07 Smoothing Splines

Instead of choosing K knots, put a knot at every data point and control smoothness with a penalty.

smoothing spline objective minimize ∑_{i=1}^{n} (y_i − f(x_i))² + λ ∫ f′′(x)² dx
  • The first term is the usual RSS — fit the data.
  • The second term is a roughness penalty — large second derivatives (wiggly curves) are penalized.
  • λ = 0: no penalty — the spline interpolates every data point (passes through each one). Maximum flexibility, maximum variance.
  • λ → ∞: f′′ must be zero everywhere, so f is a straight line — ordinary linear least squares. Maximum smoothness, maximum bias.
  • In between, λ trades off fidelity to the data against smoothness of the curve.

A remarkable result

The function that minimizes the objective above, over all twice-differentiable functions, is a natural cubic spline with knots at the n unique x_i values. Despite searching an infinite-dimensional function space, the solution lies in a finite-dimensional subspace of dimension n.

Connection to regularization

The penalty λ∫f′′(x)²dx plays exactly the same role as the ridge penalty λ‖w‖² in ridge regression. Both shrink the effective model complexity. The fitted values can be written as:

ŷ = S_λ y

where S_λ is an n × n smoother matrix (analogous to the hat matrix H = X(X’X)⁻¹X’ in OLS).

Effective degrees of freedom

effective df df(λ) = trace(S_λ) = ∑_{i=1}^{n} {S_λ}_{ii}
  • λ = 0: df = n (interpolation — each point is its own parameter).
  • λ → ∞: df = 2 (intercept + slope — a straight line).
  • Specifying df instead of λ directly is often more intuitive: “I want a 6-df smooth” is easier to interpret than “λ = 0.0032.”

Choosing λ

  • Leave-one-out CV — efficiently computed without refitting n times:
    LOOCV = (1/n) ∑_{i=1}^{n} [ (y_i − f̂_λ(x_i)) / (1 − {S_λ}_{ii}) ]²
    This leverages the linear smoother structure. See Model Evaluation for the general CV framework.
  • GCV (generalized cross-validation): replaces the individual diagonal entries with their average, trace(S_λ)/n, for a smoother criterion.

08 Local Regression (LOESS / LOWESS)

A different philosophy: no global model at all — just fit a weighted least squares in a sliding window around each prediction point.

Algorithm

  1. For each target point x_0, find the nearest α · n data points (the span α ∈ (0, 1]).
  2. Assign weights using a kernel that down-weights distant points:
    tri-cube kernel K(u) = (1 − |u|³)³ for |u| ≤ 1, else 0
    where u = (x_i − x_0) / h and h is the distance to the farthest point in the window.
  3. Fit a local polynomial (usually linear or quadratic) by weighted least squares.
  4. Record f̂(x_0) and move to the next target point.
  • Span α controls smoothness: small α → flexible (few points per window), large α → smooth.
  • Robust LOESS: iteratively re-weights to downweight outliers (bisquare function on residuals).
  • Advantages: very flexible, no need to choose knots or basis; excellent for visualization and exploratory analysis.
  • Disadvantages: slow for large n (must refit at every prediction point); no compact model — must store all training data; extends poorly beyond one or two predictors (curse of dimensionality).

09 Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)

Replace each linear term β_j x_j with a smooth function f_j(x_j), keeping the additive structure that makes each effect interpretable.

GAM for regression y = β_0 + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + … + f_p(x_p) + ε

Each f_j can be any univariate smoother: a cubic spline, a smoothing spline, LOESS, etc. Because the model is additive, you can plot each f_j(x_j) separately to see the marginal effect of that predictor — this is the interpretability advantage over black-box models.

Backfitting algorithm

  1. Initialize: β̂_0 = (1/n)∑y_i, and f̂_j = 0 for all j.
  2. Cycle through j = 1, 2, …, p repeatedly:
    partial residual for feature j r_j = y − β̂_0 − ∑_{k ≠ j} f̂_k(x_k)
    update f̂_j ← Smooth(r_j ~ x_j)
    center for identifiability f̂_j ← f̂_j − mean(f̂_j)
  3. Repeat until the functions stop changing (converges when smoothers are linear operators).

Backfitting is elegant: each step is a simple one-dimensional smooth of residuals. No multivariate optimization is needed.

GAMs for classification (logistic GAM)

logistic GAM log(p / (1 − p)) = β_0 + f_1(x_1) + … + f_p(x_p)

Fit by local scoring: an outer IRLS loop (iteratively reweighted least squares) with backfitting as the inner solver. Each f_j now gives the log-odds contribution of that predictor, and you can still plot each one to understand the model.

Strengths and limitations

  • Interpretability: plot each f_j — immediate visual read on how each predictor drives the outcome.
  • Automatic nonlinearity: no need to guess whether a predictor enters linearly, quadratically, or otherwise — the smoother discovers the shape.
  • Additive assumption: GAMs assume the effects of different features combine by addition. If interactions exist (the effect of x_1 depends on x_2), you must add interaction terms like f_{12}(x_1, x_2) explicitly — a 2D smooth surface.
  • Concurvity: the additive analogue of multicollinearity — if two predictors are curvilinearly related, the individual f_j become hard to identify.

10 Comparison & Practical Guidance

When to reach for splines, GAMs, or something else entirely.

Rules of thumb

  • Start simple. Try a linear model first. If residual plots show curvature, add spline terms for those features. See Linear Regression for the baseline.
  • Use GAMs when you need to explain. In domains like healthcare, finance, or policy, being able to show “here is how age affects the outcome” as a curve is worth more than a marginal accuracy gain from a black box.
  • Natural > cubic at boundaries. Unless you have strong data coverage at the extremes, prefer natural splines.
  • Smoothing splines or penalized B-splines over manual knot selection. Let the penalty do the work, and choose df or λ via cross-validation. See Hyperparameter Tuning.
  • Tree ensembles beat GAMs when interactions dominate. Random forests and gradient boosting capture interactions automatically; GAMs require you to specify them.
  • LOESS is for looking, not for modeling. Excellent in scatterplot smoothers; poor in production pipelines (no compact representation, slow prediction).
Bottom line: splines and GAMs occupy a sweet spot — more flexible than linear models, more interpretable than ensembles or neural networks, and mathematically grounded enough to deliver confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.
