Variational Inference

Bayesian InferenceApproximateOptimization-based Turn an intractable posterior into an optimization problem: find the simplest distribution that is closest to the true posterior by maximizing a tractable lower bound on the evidence.

01 Overview

Bayesian inference asks for the posterior p(Z|X). Variational inference (VI) answers with an optimization problem instead of a sampling procedure.

In a Bayesian model, observed data X is generated through latent variables Z. The posterior p(Z|X) tells us everything about the unknowns given what we observed. Computing it requires the evidence (marginal likelihood):

evidence (marginal likelihood)p(X) = ∫ p(X, Z) dZ

This integral is intractable for most interesting models — it sums or integrates over every possible configuration of the latent variables, whose dimension may be enormous.

  • MCMC (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, HMC) draws correlated samples from the exact posterior. Given enough time, it converges. In practice, "enough time" can be prohibitive.
  • Variational inference posits a tractable family of distributions Q and finds the member q*(Z) closest to the true posterior. It trades exactness for speed: the answer is biased but fast.
Core trade-off. MCMC is asymptotically exact but slow; VI is fast but introduces approximation error. When the dataset is large or the model must be refitted often, VI is usually the pragmatic choice.

02 KL divergence and the ELBO

The derivation that makes VI an optimization problem.

Measuring closeness: KL divergence

We want q(Z) close to the true posterior p(Z|X). The KL divergence from q to p quantifies the mismatch:

KL divergence (always ≥ 0, zero iff q = p a.e.)KL(q ‖ p(Z|X)) = E_q[ log q(Z) − log p(Z|X) ]

We cannot evaluate this directly because it contains log p(Z|X) = log p(X,Z) − log p(X), and log p(X) is the very quantity we cannot compute.

Deriving the ELBO

Expand the KL and rearrange. The key step: log p(X) does not depend on Z, so it passes through E_q:

expand KLKL(q ‖ p(Z|X)) = E_q[log q(Z)] − E_q[log p(X,Z)] + log p(X)

Rearranging for log p(X):

the fundamental identitylog p(X) = ELBO(q) + KL(q ‖ p(Z|X))

where the Evidence Lower BOund is:

ELBOELBO(q) = E_q[log p(X, Z)] − E_q[log q(Z)]

Since KL ≥ 0, we have log p(X) ≥ ELBO(q) for every q. Maximizing the ELBO over q is therefore equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence.

Two useful decompositions

reconstruction + complexityELBO = E_q[log p(X|Z)] − KL(q(Z) ‖ p(Z))
energy + entropyELBO = E_q[log p(X, Z)] + H(q)
  • The first form splits the ELBO into a reconstruction term (how well the latent variables explain the data under the model) and a complexity penalty (how far q strays from the prior). This is the form used in VAEs.
  • The second form is the energy minus entropy view: maximize the expected log-joint ("energy") and the entropy of q (prefer spread-out approximations).

Worked example: single Gaussian

Let the model be p(x,z) = N(z; 0, 1) · N(x; z, 1). The true posterior is p(z|x) = N(z; x/2, 1/2). Approximate with q(z) = N(z; m, 1) (fixed variance, free mean m).

ELBO as a function of mELBO(m) = E_q[log p(x,z)] + H(q) = E_q[ −z²/2 − (x − z)²/2 ] + const = −m²/2 − (x − m)²/2 − 1/2 + const = −m² + mx − x²/2 − 1 + const

Differentiate and set to zero: d(ELBO)/dm = −2m + x = 0, so m* = x/2 — exactly the true posterior mean. The approximation is inexact only in variance (we fixed it at 1 instead of 1/2).

03 Mean-field approximation

The most common structural assumption: the latent variables are independent under q.

Partition the latent variables into groups Z = {Z₁, Z₂, …, Z_M} and assume the variational distribution factorizes:

mean-field factorizationq(Z) = ∏ⱼ qⱼ(Zⱼ)

This throws away all correlations between groups. In exchange, each factor can be optimized independently — a huge computational win.

The optimal factor

Hold all factors except qⱼ fixed and maximize the ELBO with respect to qⱼ. Calculus of variations gives a closed-form solution:

optimal factor (up to normalization)log q*ⱼ(Zⱼ) = E_{q₋ⱼ}[log p(X, Z)] + const

where q₋ⱼ denotes all factors except the j-th.

  • Take the expectation of the log-joint with respect to every latent group except Zⱼ.
  • Whatever functional form results, that is the optimal qⱼ. For conjugate-exponential models, it always falls in the same exponential family as the complete conditional.
  • The constant ensures normalization: exponentiate and divide by ∫ exp(…) dZⱼ.

Why mean-field underestimates variance

Minimizing KL(q ‖ p) is mode-seeking: q avoids placing mass where p is small (infinite penalty from log(q/p) when p ≈ 0). For a multimodal posterior, mean-field will collapse onto one mode and understate the variance. This is a fundamental limitation of the "forward KL" direction.

Contrast. Expectation propagation minimizes KL(p ‖ q) (the reverse direction), which is moment-matching / mass-covering: q spreads to cover all of p, even at the cost of placing mass where p is negligible. Neither direction is universally better.

04 Coordinate Ascent VI (CAVI)

The workhorse algorithm for mean-field VI: update one factor at a time, cycling until convergence.

  1. Initialize all factors q₁, …, q_M (e.g. randomly or from the prior).
  2. For each factor j = 1, …, M:
    Set log qⱼ(Zⱼ) ← E_{q₋ⱼ}[log p(X, Z)] + const, normalizing to get a valid distribution.
  3. Compute ELBO. Check convergence (ELBO change < threshold).
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until the ELBO stabilizes.
CAVI guaranteeELBO(q^(t+1)) ≥ ELBO(q^(t))

Each update is guaranteed not to decrease the ELBO (it is a coordinate ascent on a concave objective within the mean-field family). Convergence is to a local optimum — the ELBO landscape can have multiple stationary points.

Worked example: Gaussian mixture

Model: K mixture components with means μₖ and assignments cₙ for each data point xₙ. Mean-field: q(μ, c) = ∏ₖ q(μₖ) · ∏ₙ q(cₙ).

  • Update assignments: q(cₙ = k) ∝ exp( E_q[log πₖ] + E_q[log N(xₙ; μₖ, σ²)] ). These are the "responsibilities" — soft cluster assignments, exactly like the E-step in EM.
  • Update means: q(μₖ) = N(mₖ, sₖ²) where mₖ is a weighted average of data points assigned to cluster k.
  • The two updates alternate, each increasing the ELBO, until the assignments and means stabilize. EM is the special case where q(μₖ) collapses to a point mass.

06 Stochastic Variational Inference (SVI)

Scale VI to massive datasets by replacing full-data expectations with mini-batch estimates.

Classical CAVI requires a full pass over the data at each step. For models with N data points, this is O(N) per iteration — impractical when N is in the millions.

The natural-gradient idea

For exponential-family variational factors, the ELBO gradient in natural parameters has a particularly clean form. SVI:

  1. Sample a mini-batch of data points uniformly at random.
  2. Compute noisy but unbiased natural-parameter gradients from the mini-batch.
  3. Take a step in natural-parameter space with a Robbins-Monro learning rate schedule (ρₜ ∝ t^{-κ}, κ ∈ (0.5, 1]).
SVI update (natural parameters)ηₜ₊₁ = (1 − ρₜ) ηₜ + ρₜ η̃ₜ

where η̃ₜ is the natural parameter that would be optimal if the mini-batch were the entire dataset (scaled appropriately by N / |batch|).

  • SVI inherits the convergence guarantees of stochastic optimization: the ELBO converges to a local optimum as long as the step sizes satisfy ∑ ρₜ = ∞ and ∑ ρₜ² < ∞.
  • Local (per-data-point) latent variables are updated exactly as in CAVI for the current mini-batch; only global parameters use the stochastic step.

07 Amortized inference and the VAE connection

Instead of optimizing per-data-point variational parameters, learn a single neural network that maps any observation to its approximate posterior.

In classical VI, every data point xₙ gets its own variational parameters (e.g. a mean and variance). That is O(N) parameters to store and optimize. Amortized inference replaces them with a shared inference network (encoder):

amortized variational familyq_φ(z | x) = N(z; μ_φ(x), diag(σ²_φ(x)))

where μ_φ and σ²_φ are outputs of a neural network with parameters φ.

  • At test time, a single forward pass through the encoder produces the approximate posterior — no iterative optimization needed.
  • The Variational Autoencoder (VAE) pairs this encoder with a generative decoder p_θ(x|z) and trains both by maximizing the ELBO with the reparameterization trick: z = μ + σ ⊙ ε, ε ~ N(0, I).
  • The amortization gap: a shared network may not reach the per-point optimum that classical VI would. Semi-amortized methods run a few optimization steps on top of the encoder output to close this gap.
See also: Autoencoders & VAEs for the full generative-model story, the reparameterization trick, posterior collapse, and VQ-VAE. This page treats VI as a general inference framework; that page covers the deep-generative-model application.

08 VI vs. MCMC: when to use which

NatureOptimizationSampling
OutputA distribution q*(Z)A set of correlated samples
BiasBiased (approximation error from the family Q)Asymptotically unbiased (exact in the limit)
SpeedFast; scales to large N via SVISlow; each sample requires a full model evaluation
Convergence checkELBO monitored; local optima possibleMixing diagnostics (R-hat, ESS); hard to verify
Posterior shapeSmooth, unimodal (mean-field); may miss modesCan explore multimodal posteriors (if mixing is good)

Practical rule of thumb. Use VI for exploration and model development (fast feedback), then switch to MCMC for the final fit if you need trustworthy uncertainty intervals.

09 Expectation propagation (EP)

An alternative that minimizes the reverse KL — matching moments instead of avoiding empty mass.

EP approximates each factor fₙ(Z) in the joint p(X,Z) = ∏ₙ fₙ(Z) with a simpler site approximation f̃ₙ(Z), keeping the global approximation q(Z) ∝ ∏ₙ f̃ₙ(Z) in a tractable family (typically Gaussian).

  1. Remove site n: form the "cavity" q₋ₙ(Z) ∝ q(Z) / f̃ₙ(Z).
  2. Include the true factor: compute q₋ₙ(Z) · fₙ(Z) (a tilted distribution).
  3. Project back: set q^new(Z) to the member of the exponential family that matches the moments (mean and covariance) of the tilted distribution. This minimizes KL(tilted ‖ q).
  4. Recover the new site: f̃ₙ^new(Z) ∝ q^new(Z) / q₋ₙ(Z).
  • EP does not minimize a single global objective, so convergence is not guaranteed (it can oscillate). In practice it usually converges for log-concave likelihoods.
  • Because it minimizes the reverse KL KL(p ‖ q) locally, EP tends to over-estimate variance (mass-covering), complementing mean-field VI which under-estimates it.
  • EP is the basis for approximate inference in Gaussian processes for classification and in Bayesian logistic regression with Gaussian priors.

10 Applications and cross-links

VI is the inference engine inside many models you encounter across this knowledge base:

  • Gaussian mixture models. CAVI for mixtures is essentially a "soft EM" with full posterior uncertainty over the means and weights. Clustering (k-Means) covers the hard-assignment special case.
  • Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA). The original motivation for SVI: topic models over millions of documents with per-document topic proportions and per-word topic assignments.
  • Variational Autoencoders. Amortized VI with neural encoders/decoders. See Autoencoders & VAEs.
  • Bayesian neural networks. Replace point-estimated weights with variational distributions q(w). The KL term in the ELBO acts as a principled regularizer.
  • Hidden Markov Models. Structured mean-field or EP for the transition and emission parameters. See Hidden Markov Models & CRFs.
  • Probabilistic foundations. The KL divergence, expectations, and exponential families that underpin VI are covered in Probability.
  • Optimization. CAVI is coordinate ascent; SVI is stochastic gradient ascent. The convergence machinery lives in Optimization.
