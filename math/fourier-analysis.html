Fourier Analysis

Every reasonable function is a sum of sinusoids, and the list of their amplitudes and phases is often a far better object to work with than the function itself. This page builds that idea as mathematics: functions as vectors in a space with an inner product, Fourier coefficients as projections onto an orthogonal basis, the Fourier series and exactly when and in what sense it converges (including the Gibbs overshoot that never goes away), term-by-term calculus, Parseval's theorem as Pythagoras in function space, the passage from series to transform, and the convolution theorem proved. It then turns to the side of the subject that statistics and machine learning actually use: characteristic functions as the Fourier transform of a distribution, the spectral density and periodogram of a time series, and the places Fourier ideas sit inside modern models — positional encodings, random Fourier features, the spectral bias of neural networks.

01 What Fourier analysis is

Fourier analysis is the claim — and the machinery that makes it precise — that a function can be written as a sum of sinusoids, each with its own frequency, amplitude and phase, and that the resulting list of amplitudes and phases (the spectrum) determines the function completely. For a function that repeats with period 2π the sum runs over whole-number frequencies n = 0, 1, 2, …; for a function that never repeats it becomes an integral over a continuum of frequencies. Either way, the function has been rewritten in a new coordinate system, and the whole subject is about what that coordinate system makes easy.

the claim, in its two shapesperiodic, period 2π: f(x) = a₀/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ (aₙ·cos nx + bₙ·sin nx) = Σₙ₌₋∞^∞ cₙ·e^(inx) not periodic: f(x) = (1/2π)·∫ F(ω)·e^(iωx) dω, F(ω) = ∫ f(x)·e^(−iωx) dx

Why anyone changes coordinates

A change of basis is worth making when some operation you care about becomes simple in the new basis. Sinusoids are the basis in which three of the most common operations in applied mathematics become simple at once, and that coincidence is not luck: all three are consequences of one fact about how sinusoids behave under shifting.

  • Derivatives become multiplications. d/dx e^(inx) = in·e^(inx): the complex exponential is an eigenfunction of differentiation, with eigenvalue in. Differentiate a Fourier series and every coefficient is multiplied by in; integrate and every coefficient is divided by in. A linear differential equation with constant coefficients turns into one algebraic equation per frequency (§06).
  • Convolutions become products. Smoothing, blurring, moving averages, the density of a sum of independent random variables — each is a convolution, an operation in which every output value depends on a whole neighbourhood of input values. In the Fourier basis it becomes a frequency-by-frequency multiplication: (f ∗ g)ₙ = fₙ·gₙ (§09). This is the single most load-bearing fact on the page.
  • The right basis diagonalises shift. Shifting e^(inx) by s gives e^(in(x − s)) = e^(−ins)·e^(inx): the same function, multiplied by a constant. So every operator that commutes with shifting — every filter whose behaviour does not depend on where in time or space it is applied — maps each e^(inx) to a multiple of itself. In the Fourier basis such an operator is a diagonal matrix. That is the deep reason the basis is sinusoids and not, say, polynomials; the matrix version of the argument, with circulant matrices, is in Frequency Domain · why this basis.
  • Stationarity is shift-invariance in statistics. A stationary time series is one whose covariance between times s and t depends only on the lag t − s. That is a shift-invariance, so the Fourier basis diagonalises the covariance too: the sinusoidal components at different frequencies are (asymptotically) uncorrelated, and the variance of the series splits into a sum of contributions, one per frequency — the spectral density of §11.
  • Distributions have spectra too. The characteristic function φ(t) = E e^(itX) of a random variable is the Fourier transform of its distribution. Adding independent random variables convolves their distributions, so it multiplies their characteristic functions — which is how the central limit theorem is proved (§10).

Four settings, one idea

Whether the frequencies form a discrete list or a continuum depends on whether the function repeats; whether the frequencies themselves repeat depends on whether the function is sampled. The rule is symmetric: periodic in one domain ⇔ discrete in the other.

02 Sinusoids and the complex exponential

A sinusoid has three numbers: how big it is, how fast it oscillates, and where in its cycle it starts. Real-valued bookkeeping keeps those three numbers in an awkward form, scattered across a cosine and a sine. Complex exponentials collect amplitude and phase into a single complex number and turn every trigonometric identity into the law of exponents. Nothing about the mathematics changes; the algebra just becomes short enough to do in your head.

Amplitude, frequency, phase

a sinusoids(t) = A·cos(ωt + φ) A ≥ 0 amplitude, ω angular frequency (radians per unit time), φ phase (radians) frequency and periodω = 2π·f = 2π/T f cycles per unit time, T = 1/f period
  • Amplitude A is the peak height; the sinusoid swings between −A and A. Its mean square over a period is A²/2, the number that will reappear in Parseval's theorem (§07) as the "energy" of one frequency.
  • Angular frequency ω counts radians per unit time. A Fourier series on [−π, π] uses ω = n, a whole number, because only whole-number frequencies complete an exact number of cycles in a window of length 2π; on a window of length T the allowed frequencies are ωₙ = 2πn/T.
  • Phase φ slides the wave sideways: cos(ωt + φ) = cos(ω(t + φ/ω)), a time shift of −φ/ω. Phase is a time shift measured in fractions of a cycle, which is why a time shift of a whole signal shifts each component's phase by an amount proportional to its frequency.

Amplitude–phase form versus cosine–sine form

Expand with the angle-addition formula, cos(ωt + φ) = cos ωt·cos φ − sin ωt·sin φ, and a single shifted cosine becomes an unshifted cosine plus an unshifted sine. That is why a Fourier series needs both cos nx and sin nx at every frequency: two real numbers per frequency encode an amplitude and a phase.

conversionA·cos(ωt + φ) = a·cos ωt + b·sin ωt a = A·cos φ, b = −A·sin φ A = √(a² + b²), φ = atan2(−b, a) workedcos t + √3·sin t: A = √(1 + 3) = 2, φ = atan2(−√3, 1) = −π/3 so cos t + √3·sin t = 2·cos(t − π/3)

Check at t = 0: the left side is 1, the right side is 2·cos(−π/3) = 2·½ = 1. At t = π/3 the right side reaches its peak 2, and the left side is ½ + √3·(√3/2) = ½ + 3/2 = 2.

Euler's formula

Define e^(iθ) by the exponential power series with iθ substituted, and collect real and imaginary parts using i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1:

Euler's formula, from the seriese^(iθ) = Σₖ (iθ)ᵏ/k! = (1 − θ²/2! + θ⁴/4! − …) + i·(θ − θ³/3! + θ⁵/5! − …) = cos θ + i·sin θ and backcos θ = (e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ))/2 sin θ = (e^(iθ) − e^(−iθ))/(2i)

A second derivation that explains why it is true rather than merely that it is: the function z(θ) = cos θ + i·sin θ satisfies z′ = −sin θ + i·cos θ = i·z with z(0) = 1, and the only solution of that initial-value problem is e^(iθ). Geometrically, z′ = iz says the velocity is always the position rotated by 90°: a point moving at unit speed perpendicular to its radius, i.e. going round the unit circle. So e^(iθ) is the point at angle θ on the unit circle, and e^(iωt) is a point going round it ω radians per unit time. The real and imaginary parts are the shadows of that rotation on the two axes, which is the picture the figure below animates. 03 Functions as vectors: the inner product

Everything in a Fourier series — the coefficient formulas, why they never change when you add more terms, why truncating the series gives the best possible approximation, Parseval's theorem — is the linear algebra of an orthogonal basis, carried over to a space whose "vectors" are functions. Once the inner product is in place, the coefficient formulas are not something to memorise: they are the projection formula you already know.

The inner product of two functions

Sample a function at m equally spaced points xⱼ in [−π, π] and you have a vector in ℝᵐ. The dot product of two such vectors, scaled by the spacing Δx = 2π/m, is a Riemann sum, and as m → ∞ it becomes an integral. That integral is the inner product of the two functions. For complex-valued functions the second factor is conjugated, so that ⟨f, f⟩ is a real, non-negative "squared length".

from dot product to integralΔx·Σⱼ f(xⱼ)·g(xⱼ) → ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₋π^π f(x)·ḡ(x) dx ‖f‖ = √⟨f, f⟩ = ( ∫₋π^π |f(x)|² dx )^(1/2) the rules it obeys⟨f, g⟩ = conj⟨g, f⟩, ⟨αf + βh, g⟩ = α⟨f, g⟩ + β⟨h, g⟩, ⟨f, f⟩ ≥ 0, |⟨f, g⟩| ≤ ‖f‖·‖g‖ (Cauchy–Schwarz)
  • The space. The functions with ∫|f|² < ∞ form the space L²(−π, π). It is a vector space (sums and multiples of square-integrable functions are square-integrable, by Cauchy–Schwarz), and with this inner product it is the infinite-dimensional analogue of ℝⁿ with the dot product: lengths, angles, orthogonality and projections all make sense. Two functions that differ only on a set of zero length (for instance at a single jump point) have ‖f − g‖ = 0 and are treated as the same element — the first hint that L² convergence (§05) cannot see what happens at individual points.
  • Orthogonal means "share no frequency content". ⟨f, g⟩ = 0 is exactly the statement that the product f·ḡ has as much area above the axis as below. For two sinusoids of different whole-number frequency, the product oscillates evenly and the areas cancel, which is the next subsection made visual.
  • The vector analogy, made precise. The facts about vectors and norms used here — ‖v‖² = vᵀv, unit vectors, the geometric meaning of the dot product — are in Linear Algebra · vectors; the abstract vector-space and basis language is in vector spaces.

Orthogonality, proved by the integrals

The claim is that the functions 1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, … are pairwise orthogonal on [−π, π]. Every case reduces to one fact: for a whole number k ≠ 0, ∫₋π^π cos kx dx = [sin kx / k]₋π^π = 0 and ∫₋π^π sin kx dx = 0 (an odd function over a symmetric interval). The product-to-sum identities turn every product into such terms.

product-to-sumcos mx·cos nx = ½[cos(m − n)x + cos(m + n)x] sin mx·sin nx = ½[cos(m − n)x − cos(m + n)x] sin mx·cos nx = ½[sin(m + n)x + sin(m − n)x] integrate over [−π, π], m, n ≥ 0 whole numbers∫ cos mx·cos nx dx = 0 if m ≠ n, π if m = n ≥ 1, 2π if m = n = 0 ∫ sin mx·sin nx dx = 0 if m ≠ n, π if m = n ≥ 1 ∫ sin mx·cos nx dx = 0 for all m, n

Reading the cases: when m ≠ n, both m − n and m + n are nonzero whole numbers and each cosine integrates to 0. When m = n ≥ 1, cos(m − n)x = 1 integrates to 2π, which the factor ½ halves to π, and the cos 2nx term contributes 0. The sine–cosine products are odd functions, so they vanish without any case analysis.

The complex form needs one integral instead of three. For whole numbers m, n:

complex exponentials are orthogonal⟨e^(imx), e^(inx)⟩ = ∫₋π^π e^(imx)·e^(−inx) dx = ∫₋π^π e^(i(m − n)x) dx m = n: ∫ 1 dx = 2π m ≠ n: [e^(i(m−n)x) / (i(m − n))]₋π^π = 2·sin((m − n)π)/(m − n) = 0

So {e^(inx)/√(2π)} is an orthonormal set, and {1/√(2π), cos nx/√π, sin nx/√π} is the orthonormal version of the real system. The squared lengths ‖1‖² = 2π, ‖cos nx‖² = ‖sin nx‖² = π, ‖e^(inx)‖² = 2π are exactly the constants that appear in the coefficient formulas, and the odd-looking a₀/2 in a real Fourier series is nothing more than the constant function having twice the squared length of the others.

Whole-number frequencies are essential. cos x and cos 1.5x are not orthogonal on [−π, π]: using ∫₋π^π cos ax dx = 2·sin(aπ)/a, ∫ cos x·cos 1.5x dx = ½∫[cos 0.5x + cos 2.5x] dx = ½·[2·sin(0.5π)/0.5 + 2·sin(2.5π)/2.5] = ½·(4 + 0.8) = 2.4, not 0. Orthogonality on a window of length 2π requires every frequency to complete a whole number of cycles in it — the same requirement that makes the DFT frequencies k/N and that, when violated, produces the leakage discussed in Frequency Domain · windowing.

Fourier coefficients are projections

In ℝⁿ, if q₁, …, qₙ are mutually orthogonal and nonzero, any vector in their span is v = Σⱼ cⱼqⱼ, and taking the inner product of both sides with qₖ kills every term but one: ⟨v, qₖ⟩ = cₖ⟨qₖ, qₖ⟩. So cₖ = ⟨v, qₖ⟩/⟨qₖ, qₖ⟩, the length of the shadow of v on qₖ measured in units of qₖ. No system of equations has to be solved: orthogonality decouples them. This is the projection formula of Linear Algebra · projections, and the Fourier coefficient formulas are that formula with the function inner product.

coefficient = projectionaₙ = ⟨f, cos nx⟩ / ‖cos nx‖² = (1/π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·cos nx dx n = 0, 1, 2, … bₙ = ⟨f, sin nx⟩ / ‖sin nx‖² = (1/π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·sin nx dx n = 1, 2, … cₙ = ⟨f, e^(inx)⟩ / ‖e^(inx)‖² = (1/2π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·e^(−inx) dx n = …, −1, 0, 1, …

The formula for a₀ gives (1/π)∫f, twice the mean of f; the projection onto the constant function is ⟨f, 1⟩/‖1‖² = (1/2π)∫f, which is a₀/2. Writing the series with a₀/2 lets one formula cover every n. The derivation also shows when the formulas are forced: if a trigonometric series converges uniformly to f, it may be integrated term by term after multiplying by cos nx, and orthogonality leaves exactly π·aₙ. A uniformly convergent trigonometric series is therefore always the Fourier series of its own sum.

The truncated series is the best approximation of its degree

Call t(x) = α₀/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N (αₖ·cos kx + βₖ·sin kx), with arbitrary real coefficients, a trigonometric polynomial of degree N, and let S_N be the partial sum of the Fourier series of f up to degree N. Among all trigonometric polynomials of degree N, S_N is the unique one closest to f in mean square.

best approximation‖f − t‖² = ‖f − S_N‖² + ‖S_N − t‖² ≥ ‖f − S_N‖² with equality only when t = S_N the error left over‖f − S_N‖² = ‖f‖² − π·[ a₀²/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N (aₖ² + bₖ²) ]
  • Proof. Let r = f − S_N, the residual. For every k ≤ N, ⟨r, cos kx⟩ = ⟨f, cos kx⟩ − ⟨S_N, cos kx⟩ = π·aₖ − π·aₖ = 0, because by orthogonality the only term of S_N that survives the inner product with cos kx is aₖ·cos kx. The same holds for sin kx and for the constant. So r is orthogonal to the whole subspace of degree-N trigonometric polynomials. Now u = t − S_N lies in that subspace, so ⟨r, u⟩ = 0, and ‖f − t‖² = ‖r − u‖² = ‖r‖² − 2Re⟨r, u⟩ + ‖u‖² = ‖r‖² + ‖u‖². This is Pythagoras: f − t is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the residual and the gap t − S_N inside the subspace.
  • The error formula. Expand ‖f − S_N‖² = ‖f‖² − 2⟨f, S_N⟩ + ‖S_N‖². By orthogonality, ⟨f, S_N⟩ = π[a₀²/2 + Σ(aₖ² + bₖ²)] and ‖S_N‖² is the same number, so one copy cancels.
  • Bessel's inequality. The error is a squared norm, so it is non-negative for every N: a₀²/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N(aₖ² + bₖ²) ≤ (1/π)∫ f². The right side does not depend on N, so the series Σ(aₖ² + bₖ²) converges, and its terms must tend to zero: the Fourier coefficients of any square-integrable function tend to 0. That is the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma in its simplest form, and §07 shows the inequality is in fact an equality.
  • It is least squares with an orthogonal design. Fitting f by t to minimise ∫(f − t)² is a regression of f on the regressors 1, cos x, sin x, …. Because the regressors are orthogonal, the normal equations are diagonal and each coefficient is estimated on its own — which is why adding a higher harmonic never changes the coefficients already found. With non-orthogonal regressors (say powers 1, x, x², …) every coefficient would change each time a new term was added. The general least-squares picture is in Linear Algebra · least squares.

Worked. For the square wave f(x) = sign(x) on (−π, π) (computed in §04), ‖f‖² = 2π and the only nonzero coefficients are bₙ = 4/(nπ) for odd n. The best constant approximation is the mean, 0, with error 2π. The best degree-1 approximation is (4/π)·sin x, with error 2π − π·(4/π)² = 2π − 16/π = 6.2832 − 5.0930 = 1.1902 — it already removes 16/π ÷ 2π = 8/π² = 81.06% of the squared length. Adding cos x or sin 2x with any coefficient other than 0 makes the fit worse.

04 Fourier series

The Fourier series of a function is the expansion in the orthogonal basis of §03, with every coefficient computed by projection. This section fixes the formulas in both the real and the complex form, shows how to move between them and how to change the period, uses symmetry to halve the work, and then computes two series completely — the square wave and the sawtooth — because nearly every phenomenon on the rest of the page can be seen in one of them.

The definitions

Let f be integrable on [−π, π]. Its Fourier series is the trigonometric series whose coefficients are the projections of §03. The symbol ~ instead of = is deliberate: the series is defined for every integrable f, but whether it converges, and to what, is a separate question with a subtle answer (§05).

real formf(x) ~ a₀/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ (aₙ·cos nx + bₙ·sin nx) aₙ = (1/π)∫₋π^π f(x)·cos nx dx, bₙ = (1/π)∫₋π^π f(x)·sin nx dx complex formf(x) ~ Σₙ₌₋∞^∞ cₙ·e^(inx) cₙ = (1/2π)∫₋π^π f(x)·e^(−inx) dx amplitude–phase formf(x) ~ a₀/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ Aₙ·cos(nx + φₙ) Aₙ = √(aₙ² + bₙ²) = 2|cₙ|, φₙ = atan2(−bₙ, aₙ) = arg cₙ

Converting. Substitute e^(−inx) = cos nx − i·sin nx into the formula for cₙ, and the real and imaginary parts are the real coefficients. Going back uses cos nx = (e^(inx) + e^(−inx))/2 and sin nx = (e^(inx) − e^(−inx))/(2i) and collects the coefficients of e^(inx) and e^(−inx).

complex ↔ realc₀ = a₀/2, cₙ = (aₙ − i·bₙ)/2, c₋ₙ = (aₙ + i·bₙ)/2 (n ≥ 1) aₙ = cₙ + c₋ₙ, bₙ = i·(cₙ − c₋ₙ) for real f: c₋ₙ = c̄ₙ, aₙ = 2·Re cₙ, bₙ = −2·Im cₙ

Other periods. If f has period T (or lives on an interval of length T), substitute x = 2πs/T. The frequencies become ωₙ = 2πn/T, the base frequency 2π/T is the fundamental, and its multiples are the harmonics. Because every integrand has period T, the integral may be taken over any interval of length T — [0, T] and [−T/2, T/2] give the same numbers.

period Tf(s) ~ a₀/2 + Σ (aₙ·cos(2πns/T) + bₙ·sin(2πns/T)) aₙ = (2/T)∫_T f(s)·cos(2πns/T) ds, cₙ = (1/T)∫_T f(s)·e^(−2πins/T) ds

The even and odd rules hold because the product of an even and an odd function is odd and integrates to zero over [−π, π], while the product of two even (or two odd) functions is even and its integral is twice the integral over [0, π]. The half-wave rule: substituting x → x + π multiplies e^(−inx) by (−1)ⁿ and f by −1, so cₙ = −(−1)ⁿcₙ, which forces cₙ = 0 for even n.

Half-range expansions. A function given only on [0, π] can be extended to [−π, π] either evenly or oddly before expanding, giving a pure cosine series or a pure sine series. The choice matters for convergence: the even extension never creates a jump at x = 0 or at ±π, the odd one does whenever f(0) ≠ 0 or f(π) ≠ 0, and §05 shows that a jump costs a whole order of coefficient decay. This is precisely the reason block-transform compression uses cosines; see Frequency Domain · the DCT.

Worked: the square wave

Let f(x) = −1 on (−π, 0) and f(x) = 1 on (0, π), repeated with period 2π. It is odd, so aₙ = 0 for every n, and

Worked: the sawtooth

  • The sawtooth is smooth inside, but its periodic extension is not. Every non-periodic function you expand on a window acquires such a jump unless its two ends happen to match.

What the coefficients mean physically

  • a₀/2 is the mean of f over one period — the "DC" level that the oscillating terms average around.
  • Aₙ = √(aₙ² + bₙ²) is how much of frequency n the function contains; Aₙ²/2 is that component's mean-square contribution. §07 shows these contributions add up exactly to the mean square of f.
  • The phase φₙ records where each component's peaks sit. Two functions with the same amplitudes and different phases can look completely different (a square wave and a smeared, asymmetric blob), which is the one-dimensional version of the demonstration in Frequency Domain · magnitude and phase.
  • The pattern of harmonics is the "timbre". A sustained musical note of fundamental frequency f₀ is close to periodic, so it is a Fourier series in f₀, 2f₀, 3f₀, …. Two instruments playing the same pitch differ in their harmonic amplitudes: a square-like waveform has only odd harmonics and sounds hollow, a sawtooth-like one has all harmonics and sounds bright.
  • How fast the amplitudes fall is how smooth the function is. Jumps produce 1/n, corners 1/n², and smoother functions faster still. This is the subject of the second half of §05.

05 Convergence, honestly

"Every function is a sum of sinusoids" is true in one precise sense, false in another, and true with an asterisk in a third. The Fourier series of a square-integrable function always converges to it in mean square; it converges at a point under mild local conditions, and to the midpoint at a jump; it converges uniformly only when the function has no jumps at all, and near a jump its overshoot tends to about 9% of the jump, never to zero, however many terms are taken. This section states each result with its conditions, proves what can be proved in a few lines, and is explicit about what is merely stated.

The square wave converges in mean square and pointwise, and not uniformly — the Gibbs phenomenon below is exactly the failure of uniformity.

A warning first: continuity is not enough

It would be natural to guess that the Fourier series of a continuous function converges to it everywhere. It does not. There exist continuous periodic functions whose Fourier series diverges at a point (an explicit construction dates from 1873), and even at a dense set of points. The obstacle is visible in the formula for the partial sum. Substituting the coefficient integrals into S_N and summing the geometric series gives a convolution with the Dirichlet kernel:

the partial sum as an integralS_N(x) = Σ_(|n|≤N) cₙ·e^(inx) = (1/2π)∫₋π^π f(x + u)·D_N(u) du, D_N(u) = Σ_(|n|≤N) e^(inu) = sin((N + ½)u) / sin(u/2) its two key properties(1/2π)∫₋π^π D_N(u) du = 1 for every N L_N = (1/2π)∫₋π^π |D_N(u)| du ≈ (4/π²)·ln N + 1.27 → ∞

The kernel integrates to 1 (only the n = 0 term survives), but it takes negative values and its absolute integral — the Lebesgue constant L_N, which equals 1.436, 2.223 and 3.139 at N = 1, 10, 100 — grows without bound. Since |S_N(x)| ≤ L_N · max|f| is the best bound available from continuity alone, and the bound is approached arbitrarily closely by suitable continuous functions (the exact extremiser, the sign of D_N, is discontinuous), nothing prevents the partial sums of some continuous function from growing like ln N at a point. Convergence needs a little more than continuity: some control on how f varies near the point.

  • How bad it can get, and how good. There is an integrable function (in L¹) whose Fourier series diverges at every point. In the other direction, the Fourier series of every square-integrable function converges at almost every point — a deep theorem from 1966, far beyond this page. For anything you are likely to meet in practice (piecewise smooth functions), the elementary theorem below already gives convergence everywhere.

Pointwise convergence: Dirichlet's theorem

Dirichlet's theoremLet f be 2π-periodic and integrable on [−π, π]. At a point x, suppose the one-sided limits f(x−), f(x+) exist and the one-sided derivatives lim_(h→0⁺) [f(x + h) − f(x+)]/h and lim_(h→0⁺) [f(x−) − f(x − h)]/h exist. Then S_N(x) → ½·[ f(x−) + f(x+) ] = f(x) wherever f is continuous the usual sufficient conditionf piecewise continuous with piecewise continuous f′ on [−π, π] (finitely many jumps and corners) ⇒ the conclusion holds at every x

The classical "Dirichlet conditions" — f bounded with finitely many maxima, minima and discontinuities per period — give the same conclusion by a different route (they make f of bounded variation). Outline of the proof (the full argument is a page of careful estimates, not reproduced here): (1) Write S_N(x) − ½[f(x−) + f(x+)] using the Dirichlet kernel, split the integral at u = 0, and use (1/2π)∫₀^π D_N = ½ on each half. (2) What remains is ∫₀^π g(u)·sin((N + ½)u) du with g(u) = [f(x + u) − f(x+)]/u · u/(2 sin(u/2)), plus the mirror-image term. The existence of the one-sided derivative is precisely what makes g bounded near u = 0, hence integrable. (3) The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma — the integral of an integrable function against sin(Mu) tends to 0 as M → ∞, because the rapid oscillation makes positive and negative lobes cancel — sends both terms to 0. The point of the outline is step (2): the hypothesis is a local smoothness condition at the single point x, which is why convergence can hold at one point and fail at another.

  • The midpoint rule at a jump is not a convention but a theorem: the series of the square wave gives 0 at x = 0, that of the sawtooth gives 0 at x = π. Changing the value of f at the jump point changes no integral, hence no coefficient, hence not the series — so the series cannot possibly "know" which value you assigned there, and it picks the only symmetric answer.
  • Localisation. Whether the series converges at x, and to what, depends only on f in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of x (Riemann's localisation principle), even though every coefficient depends on all of f. Two functions that agree near x have series whose difference converges to 0 at x.

  • No contradiction with mean-square convergence. The overshoot region has width proportional to 1/N and bounded height, so its contribution to ∫|f − S_N|² goes to 0.
  • Where it matters. Any truncated Fourier representation of a signal with an edge rings: the bright and dark halos beside edges in over-compressed images, ripples next to a step in a low-pass-filtered time series, oscillations in spectral solutions of equations with shocks. Uniform convergence for continuous, piecewise smooth functions

theoremf continuous, 2π-periodic (so f(−π) = f(π)), f′ piecewise continuous ⇒ Σ (|aₙ| + |bₙ|) < ∞ and S_N → f absolutely and uniformly
  • Proof. Integrate by parts in the coefficients of f′: the boundary terms cancel because f(π) = f(−π), giving αₙ = n·bₙ and βₙ = −n·aₙ, where αₙ, βₙ are the coefficients of f′. Then |aₙ| + |bₙ| ≤ √2·√(aₙ² + bₙ²) = √2·√(αₙ² + βₙ²)/n, and by Cauchy–Schwarz on the sum, Σ (|aₙ| + |bₙ|) ≤ √2·[Σ(αₙ² + βₙ²)]^(1/2)·[Σ 1/n²]^(1/2). Bessel's inequality (§03) applied to f′ bounds the first factor by [(1/π)∫(f′)²]^(1/2), and Σ 1/n² < ∞. So the series of absolute coefficients converges, and since |aₙ cos nx + bₙ sin nx| ≤ |aₙ| + |bₙ|, the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence. The limit is f because Dirichlet's theorem already identified the pointwise limit.
  • Why periodicity of f is in the hypothesis. f(x) = x is continuous with a continuous derivative on [−π, π], but f(−π) ≠ f(π); its periodic extension jumps, the boundary terms do not cancel, and the series converges non-uniformly with a Gibbs overshoot at ±π. Continuity of the periodic extension is what counts.

Mean-square convergence for every square-integrable function

theorem∫₋π^π |f|² < ∞ ⇒ ‖f − S_N‖ → 0 (the trigonometric system is complete in L²)

Proof for continuous periodic f. Given ε > 0, Fejér's theorem (or the Weierstrass approximation theorem for trigonometric polynomials) provides a trigonometric polynomial t of some degree M with |f(x) − t(x)| < ε for every x. For N ≥ M, t is a trigonometric polynomial of degree N, so the best-approximation property of §03 gives ‖f − S_N‖² ≤ ‖f − t‖² < 2π·ε². Since ε was arbitrary, ‖f − S_N‖ → 0. For a general square-integrable f, approximate it in L² by a continuous periodic function first (always possible), and use the triangle inequality. The proof never mentions individual points, which is exactly why L² convergence holds for every f and pointwise convergence does not.

Smoothness controls the decay of the coefficients

Integration by parts converts a derivative on f into a factor of 1/(in) on its coefficient. If the periodic extension of f is continuous and piecewise smooth, then cₙ(f′) = in·cₙ(f). Repeating:

06 Differentiation, integration, and why engineers love this basis

In the Fourier basis, calculus is arithmetic: differentiation multiplies the n-th coefficient by in, integration divides by it. That turns linear differential equations with constant coefficients into one division per frequency — the reason the basis was invented. Integration is always safe

If f is piecewise continuous with Fourier series a₀/2 + Σ(aₙ cos nx + bₙ sin nx) — whether or not that series converges — then integrating it term by term gives the Fourier series of the integral, and that series converges uniformly:

term-by-term integration∫₋π^x f(t) dt = a₀(π + x)/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ [ (aₙ/n)·sin nx − (bₙ/n)·(cos nx − cos nπ) ] uniformly for x ∈ [−π, π]

The reason: g(x) = ∫₋π^x f − a₀x/2 is continuous, periodic (the a₀x/2 removes the drift) and has the piecewise continuous derivative f − a₀/2, so the uniform-convergence theorem of §05 applies to g. Integration divides each coefficient by n and so can only improve convergence. Check on the square wave: ∫₀^x sign(t) dt = |x|, and integrating (4/π)Σ sin nx/n term by term from 0 gives (4/π)Σ_odd (1 − cos nx)/n². Comparing with the triangle series of §04, |x| = π/2 − (4/π)Σ_odd cos nx/n², the constants must agree: (4/π)·Σ_odd 1/n² = π/2, i.e. 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + … = π²/8 = 1.2337.

Differentiation needs the function to be continuous after periodic extension

If f is continuous on [−π, π] with f(−π) = f(π) and f′ is piecewise continuous, then at every point where f″ exists, f′(x) = Σ(n·bₙ·cos nx − n·aₙ·sin nx) — the term-by-term derivative. Drop the matching-ends condition and it fails badly. The sawtooth x ~ 2Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ sin nx/n differentiates term by term to 2Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ cos nx, a series whose terms do not even tend to zero, so it converges nowhere — while f′ = 1 on (−π, π), whose Fourier series is just 1. The divergent series is not nonsense: it is the Fourier series of the derivative of the periodic extension including the jumps, 1 − 2π·Σₖ δ(x − (2k + 1)π), in the sense of generalised functions. Differentiation multiplies coefficients by n; a jump leaves coefficients of order 1/n, and n · 1/n does not decay.

The diagonalisation payoff: differential equations become division

A linear differential operator with constant coefficients, L = p₀ + p₁·d/dx + p₂·d²/dx² + …, sends e^(inx) to p(in)·e^(inx), where p(z) = p₀ + p₁z + p₂z² + …. So in the Fourier basis L is the diagonal matrix with entries p(in), and the periodic equation Lu = f is solved one frequency at a time.

periodic solution of Lu = fcₙ(u) = cₙ(f) / p(in) provided p(in) ≠ 0 whenever cₙ(f) ≠ 0 (if p(in) = 0 and cₙ(f) ≠ 0: resonance, no periodic solution) worked: u″ + 2u = square wavep(in) = 2 − n² ⇒ u(x) = (4/π)·Σ_odd sin nx / (n·(2 − n²)) = 1.2732·sin x − 0.0606·sin 3x − 0.0111·sin 5x − 0.0039·sin 7x − …

Since 2 − n² ≠ 0 for every whole n, there is no resonance. The solution's coefficients fall like 1/n³ — two orders faster than the forcing — so u is two derivatives smoother than the square wave that drives it: exactly what solving a second-order equation should do. Had the equation been u″ + 9u = f, the forcing's sin 3x component would hit p(3i) = 0 and no periodic solution would exist.

The heat equation, in one paragraph

Heat on a ring of circumference 2π obeys ∂u/∂t = κ·∂²u/∂x², starting from a temperature profile u(x, 0) = f(x). In the Fourier basis the second derivative is multiplication by (in)² = −n², so each coefficient obeys its own ordinary differential equation dcₙ/dt = −κn²·cₙ, solved by cₙ(t) = cₙ(0)·e^(−κn²t). The solution is u(x, t) = Σ cₙ·e^(−κn²t)·e^(inx): every frequency decays independently, the high ones exponentially faster. Start from the square wave with κ = 1. At t = 0.1 the harmonic amplitudes are 1.1521, 0.1726, 0.0209 for n = 1, 3, 5 (from 1.2732, 0.4244, 0.2546) — the corners have already rounded off, though u(π/2, 0.1) = 0.9991 is still almost the original plateau. At t = 1 they are 0.4684, 5.2×10⁻⁵ and 3.5×10⁻¹²: the profile is, to four decimals, the single sinusoid 0.4684·sin x. For every t > 0 the coefficients decay like e^(−κn²t), faster than geometrically, so heat flow makes any initial profile analytic instantly. 07 Parseval's theorem: Pythagoras in function space

In ℝⁿ, the squared length of a vector is the sum of the squares of its coordinates in any orthonormal basis. Parseval's theorem says the same is true for a function and its Fourier coefficients: the energy of the function equals the sum of the energies of its harmonics, with nothing lost and nothing double-counted. It is Bessel's inequality of §03 promoted to an equality, and it is the reason "the fraction of energy captured" is a meaningful, additive measure of how good a truncation is.

Parseval's theorem, for square-integrable f(1/π)·∫₋π^π f(x)² dx = a₀²/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ (aₙ² + bₙ²) (1/2π)·∫₋π^π |f(x)|² dx = Σₙ₌₋∞^∞ |cₙ|² the polarised form, for two functions(1/2π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·ḡ(x) dx = Σₙ cₙ(f)·c̄ₙ(g)
  • Proof. The error formula of §03 is an identity for every N: ‖f − S_N‖² = ‖f‖² − π[a₀²/2 + Σ_(n≤N)(aₙ² + bₙ²)]. §05 proved that ‖f − S_N‖ → 0 for every square-integrable f. Let N → ∞: the left side goes to 0, so the bracket converges to ‖f‖²/π. That is the whole proof — but it leans on completeness, the fact that the trigonometric system has no "missing directions". Bessel's inequality holds for any orthogonal set; Parseval's equality holds exactly when the set is complete.
  • As Pythagoras. With the orthonormal basis eₙ = e^(inx)/√(2π), the coordinates of f are ⟨f, eₙ⟩ = √(2π)·cₙ, and the theorem reads ‖f‖² = Σ |⟨f, eₙ⟩|²: squared length is the sum of squared coordinates, exactly as for ‖v‖² = Σ vᵢ² in Linear Algebra · vectors. The polarised form is the inner product computed in coordinates, ⟨f, g⟩ = Σ ⟨f, eₙ⟩·conj⟨g, eₙ⟩.
  • The converse, and what it means. Every square-summable sequence (cₙ) — every sequence with Σ|cₙ|² < ∞, the space ℓ² — is the coefficient sequence of some square-integrable function (the Riesz–Fischer theorem). Together with Parseval, this says the map f ↦ (cₙ) is an isometric isomorphism from L²(−π, π) onto ℓ²: the space of functions and the space of coefficient sequences are the same space, measured the same way, in two coordinate systems.
  • The physical reading. The mean square of Aₙ cos(nx + φₙ) over a period is Aₙ²/2 = (aₙ² + bₙ²)/2. Dividing Parseval by 2: the mean square of f equals the mean square of its DC part, (a₀/2)², plus the mean squares of its harmonics. Power is additive across frequencies because the harmonics are orthogonal — cross terms average to zero.

08 From series to transform

A Fourier series needs a period. Most functions — a pulse, a probability density, a decaying signal — do not have one. The way out is to treat a non-periodic function as periodic with an enormous period and let the period grow without bound: the allowed frequencies crowd together, the sum over them becomes an integral, and the coefficients, suitably rescaled, become a function of a continuous frequency — the Fourier transform. This section does that limit carefully, says when the transform exists, collects the symmetries between the two domains, states the uncertainty principle, and then hands off to the page that develops the transform's machinery.

The period-to-infinity limit

Let f be defined on the whole line and restrict it to [−L/2, L/2], extended with period L. Its Fourier series uses the frequencies ωₙ = 2πn/L, spaced Δω = 2π/L apart. Define the truncated transform F_L(ω) = ∫_(−L/2)^(L/2) f(x)·e^(−iωx) dx; then the coefficient is just a sample of it, scaled:

a coefficient is a sample of the transformcₙ = (1/L)·∫_(−L/2)^(L/2) f(x)·e^(−iωₙx) dx = F_L(ωₙ)/L = F_L(ωₙ)·Δω/(2π) so the series is a Riemann sumf(x) = Σₙ cₙ·e^(iωₙx) = (1/2π)·Σₙ F_L(ωₙ)·e^(iωₙx)·Δω on (−L/2, L/2) let L → ∞F_L → F(ω) = ∫₋∞^∞ f(x)·e^(−iωx) dx f(x) = (1/2π)·∫₋∞^∞ F(ω)·e^(iωx) dω

The limit as written is a heuristic: two limits (the truncation of F_L and the Riemann sum) are taken at once. The honest statement is the Fourier integral theorem. If f is absolutely integrable on the line (∫|f| < ∞) and piecewise continuous on every bounded interval, then at every point where both one-sided derivatives exist,

Fourier integral theoremlim_(Λ→∞) (1/2π)·∫_(−Λ)^(Λ) F(ω)·e^(iωx) dω = ½·[ f(x−) + f(x+) ] equivalent real form½·[ f(x−) + f(x+) ] = (1/π)·∫₀^∞ [ ∫₋∞^∞ f(t)·cos λ(t − x) dt ] dλ

It is the exact analogue of Dirichlet's theorem in §05 — same hypotheses (local one-sided derivatives), same midpoint at a jump — and its proof uses the same two ingredients: the integral ∫₀^∞ (sin u/u) du = π/2 plays the role of the Dirichlet kernel's unit mass, and a Riemann–Lebesgue lemma kills the rest. The symmetric limit ∫_(−Λ)^(Λ) matters: for the box function below, F(ω) = 2 sin ω/ω is not absolutely integrable, so the inverse integral exists only as that symmetric limit.

When the transform exists

  • Absolutely integrable f. Then F(ω) exists for every ω, |F(ω)| ≤ ∫|f|, F is continuous, and F(ω) → 0 as |ω| → ∞ (the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma again). A transform that does not tend to 0, or that jumps, cannot come from an integrable function.
  • Square-integrable f. The integral may fail to converge (for f(x) = sin x/x it converges only conditionally), but the transform can be defined as an L² limit of transforms of truncations, and Plancherel's theorem ∫|f|² = (1/2π)∫|F|² holds. This is the natural home of the transform for energy arguments.
  • Neither. Constants, sinusoids and impulses have transforms only as generalised functions (F{1} = 2π·δ(ω)). That extension, and why it is a theorem rather than an abuse, is discussed in Frequency Domain · the continuous transform.
  • Conventions. This page and Frequency Domain put no constant on the forward transform and 1/2π on the inverse; some texts put 1/2π on the forward transform (then the convolution theorem acquires a factor 2π), some split it as 1/√(2π) on each side, and some use ordinary frequency e^(−2πiνx) with no constants at all. Probabilists flip the sign of the exponent (§10). The mathematics is identical; only the bookkeeping constants move.

The uncertainty principle, honestly

The scaling rule is the whole intuition. If g(x) = f(x/s) — f stretched by a factor s — then substituting u = x/s in the integral gives G(ω) = s·F(sω): the transform is compressed by the same factor and taller by it. Stretch in one domain, squeeze in the other; the product of the widths does not change. The Gaussian is the fixed point of this seesaw: e^(−x²/(2σ²)) has transform σ√(2π)·e^(−σ²ω²/2), a Gaussian of width 1/σ, and with σ = 1 the function is its own transform up to the constant √(2π).

The formal statement measures widths as standard deviations of the normalised energy densities |f(x)|²/‖f‖² and |F(ω)|²/‖F‖²: then σ_x·σ_ω ≥ ½ for every square-integrable f (for which both are finite), with equality exactly for Gaussians (shifted and modulated). For the Gaussian e^(−x²/2), |f|² = e^(−x²) is a normal density with variance ½, and so is |F|², giving σ_x = σ_ω = 1/√2 and product exactly ½. The proof is one integration by parts and one Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and it is given in full, with the constants and its consequences for localised filters, in Frequency Domain · the uncertainty principle. Statistically: the more concentrated a distribution, the more slowly its characteristic function decays — a point mass has |φ(t)| = 1 for every t.

09 Convolution, and the theorem that carries the subject

Convolution is the operation in which every output value is a weighted sum of input values over a whole neighbourhood: a moving average, a blur, a filter, the density of a sum of independent random variables. In the original domain it is expensive and awkward. In the Fourier domain it is a pointwise product. That single fact is why the Fourier transform is used in signal processing, in probability, in statistics and in numerical computing alike, and this section proves it with the conditions under which it is true.

Definition and three readings

convolution on the line(f ∗ g)(x) = ∫₋∞^∞ f(x − y)·g(y) dy = ∫₋∞^∞ f(y)·g(x − y) dy on the circle (period 2π)(f ⊛ g)(x) = (1/2π)·∫₋π^π f(x − y)·g(y) dy for sequences: (p ∗ q)ₙ = Σₖ pₖ·qₙ₋ₖ
  • A weighted moving average. With g a non-negative kernel of total mass 1, (f ∗ g)(x) averages f near x with weights g. A narrow kernel barely changes f; a wide one smooths it heavily.
  • A superposition of shifted copies. f ∗ g = ∫ g(y)·f(· − y) dy: one copy of f placed at every y, weighted by g(y). A linear shift-invariant system's output is its input convolved with its impulse response, for exactly this reason.
  • The density of a sum. If X and Y are independent with densities f and g, then P(X + Y ≤ s) = ∫ g(y)·P(X ≤ s − y) dy; differentiate in s and the density of X + Y is f ∗ g. For integer-valued variables, the pmf of the sum is the discrete convolution — derived in full in Joint Distributions · sums and convolution.
  • Polynomial multiplication. (Σ pₖ zᵏ)(Σ qⱼ zʲ) = Σₙ (p ∗ q)ₙ zⁿ: multiplying polynomials convolves their coefficient lists. Two fair dice have generating function ((z + z² + … + z⁶)/6)², and its coefficients 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 (over 36) are the pmf of the total. Putting z = e^(it) turns a generating function into a characteristic function, which is §10 in one substitution.

Convolution is commutative, associative and distributes over addition; the "identity" is the impulse δ. It also smooths: (f ∗ g)′ = f ∗ g′, so the convolution is at least as smooth as the smoother factor. Convolving a step with a box gives a ramp; convolving a box with itself gives a triangle; convolving n boxes gives a piecewise polynomial of degree n − 1 that rapidly looks Gaussian — the central limit theorem seen as repeated smoothing.

The convolution theorem, proved

theoremf, g absolutely integrable on ℝ ⇒ f ∗ g is absolutely integrable, ‖f ∗ g‖₁ ≤ ‖f‖₁·‖g‖₁, and F{f ∗ g}(ω) = F(ω)·G(ω) for every ω circle and sequence versionscₙ(f ⊛ g) = cₙ(f)·cₙ(g) Σₙ (p ∗ q)ₙ·e^(−inθ) = [Σₖ pₖ·e^(−ikθ)]·[Σⱼ qⱼ·e^(−ijθ)]
  • Step 1: the convolution is integrable. The function (x, y) ↦ |f(x − y)|·|g(y)| is non-negative, so by Tonelli's theorem its double integral may be computed in either order: integrating over x first gives ∫|g(y)|·[∫|f(x − y)| dx] dy = ‖f‖₁·‖g‖₁ < ∞. Hence ∫|(f ∗ g)(x)| dx ≤ ∫∫|f(x − y)||g(y)| dy dx = ‖f‖₁‖g‖₁; in particular (f ∗ g)(x) is finite for almost every x.
  • Step 2: swap the integrals. Write out the transform: F{f ∗ g}(ω) = ∫ [∫ f(x − y)·g(y) dy]·e^(−iωx) dx. The integrand f(x − y)·g(y)·e^(−iωx) has absolute value |f(x − y)||g(y)|, which step 1 showed is integrable over the plane. That is exactly the hypothesis of Fubini's theorem, which licenses exchanging the order: = ∫ g(y)·[∫ f(x − y)·e^(−iωx) dx] dy.
  • Step 3: substitute. In the inner integral put u = x − y and split e^(−iωx) = e^(−iωu)·e^(−iωy): ∫ f(u)·e^(−iωu) du · e^(−iωy) = F(ω)·e^(−iωy). The outer integral becomes F(ω)·∫ g(y)·e^(−iωy) dy = F(ω)·G(ω). ∎
  • Where the hypothesis was used. Only in step 2. Without absolute integrability the two iterated integrals can differ, and the "theorem" can fail. For square-integrable functions the product F·G is integrable and the identity holds in the sense f ∗ g = F⁻¹{F·G} pointwise; for generalised functions (impulses, sinusoids) it holds in the distributional sense. The circle version has the same proof with ∫₋π^π and periodicity in place of the substitution on the line; the sequence version is the polynomial multiplication above.
  • The dual. Running the argument on the inverse transform: F{f·g} = (1/2π)·(F ∗ G). Multiplying signals convolves spectra; for series, cₙ(fg) = Σₖ cₖ(f)·cₙ₋ₖ(g). Truncating a signal to a window multiplies it by a box and so convolves its spectrum with a sinc — the origin of leakage.

The derivation as it applies to filtering images, with the circular-versus-linear wraparound that bites when the theorem is used through the DFT, is in Frequency Domain · the convolution theorem and circular versus linear.

Worked: three convolutions done by multiplication

  • Box ∗ box = triangle. The uniform density on [−1, 1], ½·1{|x| ≤ 1}, has transform sin ω/ω. Its convolution with itself is the triangle (2 − |x|)/4 on [−2, 2] — the density of the sum of two independent uniforms — and its transform is (sin ω/ω)². The square decays like 1/ω², one order faster than the box's 1/ω: convolution added one degree of smoothness, exactly as the ladder of §05 predicts (the triangle has corners, not jumps).
  • Gaussian ∗ Gaussian = Gaussian. The N(0, σ₁²) and N(0, σ₂²) densities have transforms e^(−σ₁²ω²/2) and e^(−σ₂²ω²/2). Their product is e^(−(σ₁² + σ₂²)ω²/2), the transform of N(0, σ₁² + σ₂²). In the original domain this is a page of completing the square; here it is the law of exponents. Variances of independent summands add.
  • Smoothing a data set. A kernel density estimate f̂(x) = (1/n)Σᵢ K_h(x − Xᵢ) is the empirical distribution (point masses at the data) convolved with the kernel K_h. Its transform is (1/n)Σ e^(−iωXᵢ) — the empirical characteristic function of §10 evaluated at −ω — times the kernel's transform; for a Gaussian kernel of bandwidth h, that is a factor e^(−h²ω²/2), which suppresses frequencies above about 1/h. Choosing a bandwidth is choosing a low-pass filter, and the bias–variance trade of the bandwidth is the trade between keeping real high-frequency structure and suppressing the sampling noise that lives there. Kernel density estimates in practice are in Descriptive Statistics · shape.

10 The probabilist's Fourier transform: characteristic functions

The characteristic function of a random variable is the Fourier transform of its distribution, written as an expectation. It exists for every distribution without exception, it determines the distribution uniquely, it turns sums of independent variables into products, and its convergence is equivalent to convergence in distribution. Those four facts make it the main tool for proving limit theorems, and the reason the central limit theorem holds under nothing more than a finite variance.

definitionφ_X(t) = E e^(itX) = E cos tX + i·E sin tX, t ∈ ℝ for a density f, and for a pmf on points xⱼφ_X(t) = ∫ e^(itx)·f(x) dx = F(−t) in the convention of §08 φ_X(t) = Σⱼ pⱼ·e^(itxⱼ)

The sign of the exponent is the probabilist's convention (+itx, where §08 used −iωx); for a real density this only conjugates the transform, φ_X(t) = F(−t) = F̄(t). Every statement about Fourier transforms carries over with that one sign change.

It exists for every distribution — unlike the moment generating function

Because |e^(itX)| = 1, the expectation E e^(itX) is the expectation of a bounded quantity and is always finite: |φ_X(t)| ≤ E|e^(itX)| = 1. The moment generating function M(t) = E e^(tX) (developed in Expectation · moment generating functions) replaces the bounded e^(itX) by the unbounded e^(tX), and for heavy-tailed distributions it blows up: the Cauchy's is infinite for every t ≠ 0, the Pareto's for every t > 0, and the lognormal has every moment yet M(t) = ∞ for all t > 0 — none of them has an mgf on an interval around 0, which is what every mgf argument needs. When the mgf is finite on an interval around 0, the two are related by φ(t) = M(it), so every mgf computation transfers. The characteristic function is the mgf on the imaginary axis, where it can never blow up.

Uniqueness and inversion

Two distributions with the same characteristic function are equal. Constructively:

The last line closes a loop with §04. For an integer-valued variable, φ(t) = Σₖ P(X = k)·e^(itk) is a 2π-periodic function whose Fourier coefficients are the probabilities. Worked: the Cauchy, and why averaging it never helps

  • The Laplace transform pair. The Laplace density ½e^(−|x|) is even, so φ(t) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x)cos tx dx; two integrations by parts give 1/(1 + t²).
  • Invert it. 1/(1 + t²) is integrable, so by the first inversion formula, ½e^(−|x|) = (1/2π)∫ e^(−itx)/(1 + t²) dt. Multiply by 2 and rename the variables (x ↔ t, using evenness): ∫ e^(itx)·[1/(π(1 + x²))] dx = e^(−|t|). The left side is the characteristic function of the standard Cauchy. Numerically at t = 1: the integral is 0.36788, and e^(−1) = 0.36788.
  • Averages of Cauchys. For X₁, …, Xₙ independent standard Cauchy, the product rule and the affine rule give φ_(X̄ₙ)(t) = [φ(t/n)]ⁿ = [e^(−|t|/n)]ⁿ = e^(−|t|). The average of n Cauchys has exactly the distribution of one. P(|X̄ₙ| > 1) = 1 − (2/π)·arctan 1 = ½ whether n is 1 or a million. The law of large numbers fails because its hypothesis E|X| < ∞ fails; see Limit Theorems · the strong law and the stable laws in the central limit theorem.

Lévy's continuity theorem, and the central limit theorem

Lévy continuity theoremXₙ ⇝ X ⇔ φ_(Xₙ)(t) → φ_X(t) for every t stronger: if φ_(Xₙ)(t) → g(t) for every t and g is continuous at 0, then g = φ_X for some X, and Xₙ ⇝ X

The continuity-at-0 condition is not decoration. If Xₙ ~ N(0, n), then φₙ(t) = e^(−nt²/2) tends to 1 at t = 0 and to 0 everywhere else; the limit is discontinuous at 0, and indeed Xₙ has no limit distribution — its mass escapes to infinity. Continuity of g at 0 is what rules out escaping mass (it is equivalent to tightness).

The CLT in four lines. Let X₁, X₂, … be iid with mean 0 and variance 1, and Zₙ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/√n. (1) Two finite moments give φ(s) = 1 − s²/2 + o(s²) as s → 0. (2) Independence and the affine rule give φ_(Zₙ)(t) = [φ(t/√n)]ⁿ = [1 − t²/(2n) + o(1/n)]ⁿ. (3) Since (1 + aₙ/n)ⁿ → e^a whenever aₙ → a (valid for complex aₙ), φ_(Zₙ)(t) → e^(−t²/2) for every t. (4) That limit is the characteristic function of N(0, 1), continuous at 0, so Lévy's theorem gives Zₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). The only distributional assumption used was a finite variance — no mgf, no third moment. The mgf version of the proof, its limitations, and the rate of convergence (Berry–Esseen) are in Limit Theorems · the central limit theorem; the characteristic function is the engine that makes the general version work.

  • Stable laws fall out of the same algebra. Ask which characteristic functions satisfy φ(t)ⁿ = φ(cₙt) — which distributions are, up to scale, unchanged by summing independent copies. The symmetric solutions are φ(t) = e^(−γ|t|^α) with 0 < α ≤ 2: α = 2 is the normal, α = 1 the Cauchy. Most stable densities have no closed form, but their characteristic functions do, which is why they are fitted and simulated through φ.
  • Vectors. For a random vector, φ_X(t) = E e^(i·tᵀX), t ∈ ℝᵈ. Evaluating it along a ray t = s·u gives the characteristic function of the projection uᵀX, so the law of a vector is determined by the laws of all its one-dimensional projections (the Cramér–Wold device). The multivariate normal has φ(t) = exp(i·tᵀμ − tᵀΣt/2). Independence of two vectors is equivalent to their joint characteristic function factorising, the basis of characteristic-function tests of independence such as distance covariance.
  • The empirical characteristic function. φ̂(t) = (1/n)Σⱼ e^(itXⱼ) is an unbiased estimate of φ(t) for each t, bounded by 1 whatever the data, and uniformly consistent on bounded sets of t. It underlies goodness-of-fit and normality tests, estimation for stable laws, and deconvolution: if you observe X + noise with known noise distribution, φ_X = φ_observed/φ_noise — a division that, as in §06, is unstable where φ_noise is small.

11 Spectral analysis of time series

A stationary time series has a covariance that depends only on the time lag, and that shift-invariance is exactly what the Fourier basis diagonalises. The Fourier transform of the autocovariance is the spectral density: a decomposition of the series' variance into contributions from each frequency. Its sample counterpart, the periodogram, is the squared magnitude of the data's discrete Fourier transform. It is the natural tool for finding cycles in data — and it has a famous defect: it never converges. This section states the theory, explains the defect honestly, and shows the fix.

Stationarity and the spectral density

A time series X₀, X₁, X₂, … is (weakly) stationary if its mean is constant and Cov(Xₜ, Xₜ₊ₕ) = γ(h) depends only on the lag h. The autocovariance function γ is even (γ(−h) = γ(h)) and positive definite, and γ(0) = Var Xₜ. When Σ|γ(h)| < ∞, its Fourier transform exists:

the Fourier pair of a stationary series (f in cycles per time step)S(f) = Σₕ₌₋∞^∞ γ(h)·e^(−2πifh) = γ(0) + 2·Σₕ₌₁^∞ γ(h)·cos 2πfh, −½ ≤ f ≤ ½ γ(h) = ∫_(−½)^(½) S(f)·e^(2πifh) df in particular Var Xₜ = γ(0) = ∫_(−½)^(½) S(f) df
  • Variance decomposed over frequencies. The last identity is the heart of it: S(f) df is the part of the variance contributed by fluctuations with frequency between f and f + df. It is the continuous analogue of Parseval's harmonic-by-harmonic energy split (§07), and S(f) ≥ 0 for every f, as a variance contribution must be — a consequence of γ being positive definite.
  • Why it is periodic. The series is observed at whole-number times, so frequencies f and f + 1 are indistinguishable: e^(2πi(f+1)t) = e^(2πift) for integer t. Discrete in time, periodic in frequency; the highest resolvable frequency is ½ cycle per step, the Nyquist frequency (see Frequency Domain · aliasing).
  • The spectral representation (statement). A stationary series can be written as a random Fourier integral, Xₜ = μ + ∫ e^(2πift) dZ(f), where the random amplitudes dZ(f) at different frequencies are uncorrelated with E|dZ(f)|² = S(f) df. Stationarity is precisely "sinusoidal components with uncorrelated random amplitudes" — the Fourier basis diagonalises the covariance, as promised in §01.
  • Zero frequency is the long-run variance. S(0) = Σₕ γ(h), and for a stationary series n·Var X̄ₙ → S(0). Dividing by γ(0) gives the integrated autocorrelation time τ = 1 + 2Σρₕ that sets the effective sample size of correlated Monte Carlo output in Stochastic Processes · ergodic averages, and the variance inflation that the block bootstrap of Resampling · variants has to capture. Estimating a standard error from autocorrelated data is estimating a spectral density at frequency 0.

The periodogram

Given data x₀, …, x_(n−1), compute at the Fourier frequencies fₖ = k/n, k = 1, …, ⌊n/2⌋:

the periodogramI(fₖ) = (1/n)·| Σₜ₌₀^(n−1) (xₜ − x̄)·e^(−2πifₖt) |² — the squared magnitude of the DFT of the centred data, divided by n as regressionaₖ = (2/n)·Σₜ xₜ·cos 2πfₖt, bₖ = (2/n)·Σₜ xₜ·sin 2πfₖt I(fₖ) = (n/4)·(aₖ² + bₖ²) as the transform of the sample autocovarianceI(fₖ) = Σ_(|h|<n) γ̂(h)·e^(−2πifₖh), γ̂(h) = (1/n)·Σₜ (xₜ − x̄)(xₜ₊|h| − x̄) as analysis of variance (n odd)Σₜ (xₜ − x̄)² = 2·Σₖ₌₁^((n−1)/2) I(fₖ)
  • Four readings of one number. The first is computational: one FFT of the data gives every ordinate in O(n log n) (the DFT mechanics are in Frequency Domain · DFT). The second is statistical: aₖ, bₖ are the least-squares coefficients of a regression of the data on cos 2πfₖt and sin 2πfₖt — orthogonal regressors at the Fourier frequencies, so each is fitted on its own, as in §03. The third makes the periodogram the sample version of S(f) = Σγ(h)e^(−2πifh). The fourth is Parseval for the DFT: the total sum of squares splits into one term per frequency, an analysis of variance in which each frequency carries two degrees of freedom (a cosine and a sine). The descriptive tools that precede any of this — plotting the series, its spread and its outliers — are in Descriptive Statistics.
  • A cycle shows up as a spike that grows with n. A sinusoid A·cos(2πf₀t + θ) with f₀ a Fourier frequency contributes I(f₀) = nA²/4, which grows linearly with the record length, while noise ordinates stay of order S(f). Doubling the data doubles the height of a genuine peak and leaves the noise floor where it was.
  • Seasonal data. For monthly data over 20 years (n = 240), an annual cycle has period 12, frequency 1/12, and lands on k = 20. A seasonal pattern that is not a pure sinusoid also puts energy at its harmonics, k = 40, 60, … (periods 6, 4, … months) — the Fourier series of one year's shape, repeated. A 40-month business cycle would sit at k = 6.

Why the raw periodogram never settles down

Under Gaussian white noise with variance σ², the coefficients aₖ, bₖ are linear combinations of independent normals, and orthogonality of the sinusoids makes them independent with variance 2σ²/n each. So (n/(2σ²))(aₖ² + bₖ²) = 2I(fₖ)/σ² is chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom, independently for 0 < k < n/2. More generally, for a broad class of stationary series,

the periodogram is inconsistent2·I(fₖ)/S(fₖ) ⇝ χ²₂ (independent across distinct Fourier frequencies); for linear processes the moments converge too: E I(f) → S(f), Var I(f) → S(f)²

The mean is right; the variance does not shrink. χ²₂/2 is the Exp(1) distribution, whose standard deviation equals its mean, so each raw ordinate is off by about 100% of its target, at n = 100 and at n = 100,000 alike. The reason is that more data buys more frequencies (n/2 of them, packed more densely) rather than more information per frequency: each ordinate is always estimated from one cosine coefficient and one sine coefficient, two degrees of freedom. Plotting a raw periodogram of pure noise produces a forest of spikes; the largest of M independent Exp(1) ordinates has its most likely value near ln M and its mean at 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/M times the mean ordinate (for M = 117: mode 4.76, mean 5.34), which is how spurious "cycles" get reported.

  • The fix is smoothing — trading resolution for variance. Average the periodogram over 2m + 1 neighbouring Fourier frequencies (a Daniell smoother). Because neighbouring ordinates are nearly independent and S is nearly constant over a narrow band, the average has variance about S²/(2m + 1) and is approximately S·χ²_(2(2m+1))/(2(2m + 1)). For consistency, let m → ∞ while the bandwidth (2m + 1)/n → 0: enough ordinates to average, a narrow enough band to stay unbiased. It is a bias–variance trade of exactly the kind a kernel density estimate makes.
  • Other routes to the same end. Split the series into segments, compute a (tapered) periodogram of each and average them (Welch's method); or down-weight the noisy long-lag autocovariances γ̂(h) before transforming (lag-window estimators). Tapering the segments trades a little resolution for much lower leakage — the windowing problem of Frequency Domain · windowing. Fitting a parametric model (an AR(p)) and using its spectrum is the other classical option.
  • Worked: is that peak real? White noise with σ² = 1 plus a sinusoid of amplitude A = 0.3 at a Fourier frequency. At n = 240 its ordinate has expected height nA²/4 + 1 = 5.4 + 1 = 6.4, while each noise ordinate exceeds 6.4 with probability e^(−6.4) = 0.0017; across the 118 noise ordinates the chance that at least one reaches the signal's typical height is 1 − (1 − 0.0017)¹¹⁸ = 0.18 — the cycle is genuinely ambiguous. At n = 960 the signal's expected ordinate is 21.6 + 1 = 22.6, and the chance that any of 478 noise ordinates exceeds 22.6 is 7.3×10⁻⁸. Four times the data, unambiguous detection. (Formal tests of a periodogram peak, such as Fisher's test based on the largest ordinate's share of the total, make this calculation precise.)
  • Checking residuals. A well-specified time-series or regression model should leave white-noise residuals, whose spectrum is flat. A smoothed periodogram of residuals with a bump at a seasonal frequency, or a rise towards frequency 0, says structure remains in the errors.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Fourier ideas appear in modern machine learning in two roles. Sometimes the Fourier basis is used deliberately — to encode positions, to approximate kernels, to compute long convolutions, to build audio and image features. Sometimes it appears uninvited, as a description of what gradient descent does to a network. Each of the uses below is one of the earlier sections in disguise, and the section is named.

Sinusoidal positional encodings and rotary embeddings (§02, §06)

A Transformer's attention is permutation-invariant, so position has to be injected. The sinusoidal encoding gives position p a vector of sines and cosines at a geometric ladder of frequencies:

sinusoidal positional encoding, model width dPE(p)[2i] = sin(p·ωᵢ), PE(p)[2i + 1] = cos(p·ωᵢ), ωᵢ = 10000^(−2i/d), i = 0, …, d/2 − 1 shift by k positions = rotate each pair( sin((p + k)ωᵢ), cos((p + k)ωᵢ) ) = R(kωᵢ)·( sin pωᵢ, cos pωᵢ ) R(θ) a 2 × 2 rotation, independent of p inner products depend only on the offsetPE(p)·PE(q) = Σᵢ cos((p − q)·ωᵢ)
  • Each pair is a phasor. Reordered as (cos pωᵢ, sin pωᵢ), the pair is the point e^(ipωᵢ) of §02 written as two real numbers. Moving k positions multiplies it by e^(ikωᵢ) — the shift rule of §06 — which is a rotation that does not depend on where you started (in the (sin, cos) ordering of the table, the same rotation reads as a rotation by −kωᵢ). A fixed linear map can therefore implement "look k tokens back" at every position at once.
  • A geometric ladder of frequencies. With d = 512, the wavelengths 2π/ωᵢ run from 2π ≈ 6.3 tokens (i = 0) to about 60,600 tokens (i = 255). The fast pairs resolve neighbours; the slow pairs distinguish distant positions without wrapping — the same multi-resolution logic as the digits of a binary counter, made smooth.
  • The inner product is a shift-invariant kernel. PE(p)·PE(q) = Σ cos((p − q)ωᵢ) depends only on p − q: the encoding is a deterministic Fourier feature map, and its kernel is the "characteristic function" of a discrete set of frequencies — exactly the construction random Fourier features use below, with fixed instead of random frequencies.
  • Rotary embeddings apply the same rotation directly to query and key pairs: q ↦ R(pθᵢ)q, k ↦ R(p′θᵢ)k. Since rotations are orthogonal, ⟨R(pθ)q, R(p′θ)k⟩ = ⟨q, R((p′ − p)θ)k⟩: the attention score depends on position only through the offset p′ − p. Attention becomes shift-invariant in position, which is §01's "the right basis diagonalises shift" built into the architecture. Derivations, measurements and the frequency-band behaviour are in Transformers · sinusoidal encodings, rotary embeddings and the RoPE spectrum.
  • Fourier features for coordinates. Networks that take a coordinate as input (a pixel position, a point in space, a time) are often fed [sin(2πBx), cos(2πBx)] for a set of frequencies B instead of x itself. The reason is the spectral bias below: without them, the network struggles to represent fine detail.
  • Fourier terms for seasonality. In forecasting regressions, a seasonal pattern of period m is commonly modelled by the regressors sin(2πjt/m), cos(2πjt/m) for j = 1, …, J — a truncated Fourier series (§04) whose degree J controls how sharp the seasonal shape may be, chosen by an information criterion. Orthogonality (§03) keeps the fitted harmonics stable as J changes.

Random Fourier features: kernels as characteristic functions (§10)

Kernel methods (support vector machines, kernel ridge regression, Gaussian processes) work with a similarity k(x, y) and cost O(n²) memory and up to O(n³) time in the number of data points n. For the common shift-invariant kernels, k(x, y) = k(x − y), one theorem turns the kernel into an expectation that can be sampled.

Spectral bias: networks learn low frequencies first (§05, §06)

Train a neural network by gradient descent to fit a target that contains both a slow and a fast oscillation, and the slow one is fitted first; the fast one is fitted late, slowly, or not at all within a fixed budget. This spectral bias (or frequency principle) is observed very widely, and in an idealised setting it can be derived. When a wide network trains in its linearised ("kernel") regime, the residual on the training data evolves like a linear system, dr/dt = −η·K·r, with K the network's tangent kernel matrix. Expand the residual in the eigenvectors of K: each component decays independently as e^(−ηλₖt), exactly like the heat equation of §06. For standard architectures and well-spread inputs the eigenvectors are close to Fourier modes and the eigenvalues λₖ fall with frequency, so low-frequency components of the error die quickly and high-frequency ones linger.

  • A built-in low-pass filter. Early stopping keeps only the components that have had time to converge: it acts as a low-pass filter on the fitted function, an implicit regulariser favouring smooth fits — one explanation of why heavily over-parameterised networks still generalise.
  • And a limitation. Fine detail — sharp edges in an image represented as a function of pixel coordinates, high-frequency texture in a learned 3-D scene — is learned very slowly. Feeding the network Fourier features of its input reshapes the effective kernel so that higher frequencies have larger eigenvalues, which is why coordinate networks almost always use them.
  • Honest scope. The clean derivation holds in the kernel regime and for particular input distributions; real networks leave that regime, and the eigenfunctions are Fourier modes only approximately. The ordering — coarse structure first, detail later — is the robust empirical regularity; the precise rates are model-specific.

FFT convolution, and convolution in the frequency domain (§09)

  • When it wins. A direct 2-D convolution of an N × N map with a K × K kernel costs about N²K² multiply–adds per channel pair; via the convolution theorem it costs O(N² log N) for the transforms (shared across channels) plus N² for the pointwise products, independent of K. It therefore wins for large kernels and loses badly for the 3 × 3 kernels that dominate modern CNNs, where direct methods and minimal-filtering (Winograd) algorithms are faster; see Convolutional Networks · convolution as one matmul. Two practical details: a CNN "convolution" is actually a cross-correlation (no kernel flip), which conjugates one transform; and the DFT computes circular convolution, so inputs must be zero-padded to length N + K − 1 to avoid wraparound (Frequency Domain · circular versus linear).
  • Long convolutions in sequence models. Some sequence architectures use convolution kernels as long as the sequence itself; computed directly that is O(L²) in sequence length, through the FFT O(L log L) — the same economics as attention versus its sub-quadratic alternatives.
  • Learning in the frequency domain. Operator-learning networks for physical simulation parameterise a layer as "transform, multiply the lowest few modes by learned weights, transform back": a learned global convolution kept cheap and smooth by truncating to low frequencies (§05). It is the diagonalisation idea of §06 with the diagonal learned.

Spectrograms, cepstra and JPEG (§07, §09)

  • Audio features. A spectrogram is a sequence of windowed Fourier transforms of short overlapping frames (the short-time transform of Frequency Domain · STFT). Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients take a further step that is pure §09: a voiced sound is approximately a source (the glottal pulse train) convolved with a filter (the vocal tract), so its spectrum is a product, its log-spectrum a sum, and one more cosine transform of the log-spectrum separates the slowly varying filter from the rapidly varying source. How these features feed speech recognition and synthesis is in Speech Processing · features.
  • Images. Block-transform compression keeps the large low-frequency cosine coefficients of each block and quantises or discards the rest: energy compaction (§07) on signals that sit high on the smoothness ladder (§05), with the cosine basis chosen to avoid the artificial jumps of periodic extension (§04). The details are in Frequency Domain · the DCT.

Pitfalls

  • "The Fourier series of a continuous function converges to it." Not necessarily pointwise; always in mean square; uniformly once the periodic extension is continuous and piecewise smooth (§05).
  • Forgetting the periodic extension. A function smooth on the window but with unequal endpoint values has a jump after extension: 1/n coefficients and Gibbs ringing at the window edges. Detrend, or use a cosine (even) extension (§04, §05).
  • Differentiating a series term by term without checking. Valid only when the periodic extension is continuous (§06); otherwise the result may diverge everywhere.
  • Mixing transform conventions. Where the 2π sits, and the sign of the exponent (analysts' −iωx versus probabilists' +itx), change constants in Parseval, the convolution theorem and inversion. Fix one convention per calculation (§08, §10).
  • Reading peaks in a raw periodogram. Each ordinate has a standard deviation equal to its mean, and more data does not shrink it. Smooth, or test the peak against the distribution of the maximum (§11).
  • Treating the characteristic function as an mgf. Moments come from derivatives at 0 only when they exist; a corner at 0 (Cauchy) means no mean. And convergence of characteristic functions to a limit discontinuous at 0 means mass escaping, not a limit law (§10).

The five things to remember. (1) Fourier coefficients are projections onto an orthogonal basis, so truncated series are least-squares optimal and Parseval is Pythagoras. (2) Convergence is mean-square always, pointwise under local smoothness, uniform only without jumps, and the Gibbs overshoot is a fixed 9% of every jump. (3) Smoothness is decay: every derivative buys a power of 1/n. (4) Convolution is multiplication in the Fourier domain — for filters, for differential equations, and for sums of independent random variables, where it becomes the characteristic-function product rule behind the CLT. (5) For a stationary series the spectral density decomposes the variance by frequency, and its raw estimate, the periodogram, must be smoothed to be consistent.
