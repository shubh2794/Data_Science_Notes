02 Sinusoids and the complex exponential

A sinusoid has three numbers: how big it is, how fast it oscillates, and where in its cycle it starts. Real-valued bookkeeping keeps those three numbers in an awkward form, scattered across a cosine and a sine. Complex exponentials collect amplitude and phase into a single complex number and turn every trigonometric identity into the law of exponents. Nothing about the mathematics changes; the algebra just becomes short enough to do in your head.

Amplitude, frequency, phase

a sinusoids(t) = A·cos(ωt + φ) A ≥ 0 amplitude, ω angular frequency (radians per unit time), φ phase (radians) frequency and periodω = 2π·f = 2π/T f cycles per unit time, T = 1/f period
  • Amplitude A is the peak height; the sinusoid swings between −A and A. Its mean square over a period is A²/2, the number that will reappear in Parseval's theorem (§07) as the "energy" of one frequency.
  • Angular frequency ω counts radians per unit time. A Fourier series on [−π, π] uses ω = n, a whole number, because only whole-number frequencies complete an exact number of cycles in a window of length 2π; on a window of length T the allowed frequencies are ωₙ = 2πn/T.
  • Phase φ slides the wave sideways: cos(ωt + φ) = cos(ω(t + φ/ω)), a time shift of −φ/ω. Phase is a time shift measured in fractions of a cycle, which is why a time shift of a whole signal shifts each component's phase by an amount proportional to its frequency.

Amplitude–phase form versus cosine–sine form

Expand with the angle-addition formula, cos(ωt + φ) = cos ωt·cos φ − sin ωt·sin φ, and a single shifted cosine becomes an unshifted cosine plus an unshifted sine. That is why a Fourier series needs both cos nx and sin nx at every frequency: two real numbers per frequency encode an amplitude and a phase.

conversionA·cos(ωt + φ) = a·cos ωt + b·sin ωt a = A·cos φ, b = −A·sin φ A = √(a² + b²), φ = atan2(−b, a) workedcos t + √3·sin t: A = √(1 + 3) = 2, φ = atan2(−√3, 1) = −π/3 so cos t + √3·sin t = 2·cos(t − π/3)

Check at t = 0: the left side is 1, the right side is 2·cos(−π/3) = 2·½ = 1. At t = π/3 the right side reaches its peak 2, and the left side is ½ + √3·(√3/2) = ½ + 3/2 = 2.

Euler's formula

Define e^(iθ) by the exponential power series with iθ substituted, and collect real and imaginary parts using i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1:

Euler's formula, from the seriese^(iθ) = Σₖ (iθ)ᵏ/k! = (1 − θ²/2! + θ⁴/4! − …) + i·(θ − θ³/3! + θ⁵/5! − …) = cos θ + i·sin θ and backcos θ = (e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ))/2 sin θ = (e^(iθ) − e^(−iθ))/(2i)

A second derivation that explains why it is true rather than merely that it is: the function z(θ) = cos θ + i·sin θ satisfies z′ = −sin θ + i·cos θ = i·z with z(0) = 1, and the only solution of that initial-value problem is e^(iθ). Geometrically, z′ = iz says the velocity is always the position rotated by 90°: a point moving at unit speed perpendicular to its radius, i.e. going round the unit circle. So e^(iθ) is the point at angle θ on the unit circle, and e^(iωt) is a point going round it ω radians per unit time. The real and imaginary parts are the shadows of that rotation on the two axes, which is the picture the figure below animates. The complex-number background — modulus, argument, conjugate, and why a rotation matrix has eigenvalues e^(±iθ) — is in Linear Algebra · complex numbers.

  • Negative frequencies are bookkeeping, not physics. A real cosine is the sum of two counter-rotating phasors, cos ωt = ½e^(iωt) + ½e^(−iωt). In the complex form of a Fourier series, a real function therefore has coefficients at both +n and −n, and they are complex conjugates of each other: c₋ₙ = c̄ₙ. Half of the complex coefficients of a real function are redundant — which is also why the DFT of a real signal is conjugate-symmetric.
  • A sum of sinusoids is a chain of rotating arrows. Write each term Aₙ·sin(nt + φₙ) as the vertical shadow of an arrow of length Aₙ turning n times as fast as the first. 03 Functions as vectors: the inner product

Everything in a Fourier series — the coefficient formulas, why they never change when you add more terms, why truncating the series gives the best possible approximation, Parseval's theorem — is the linear algebra of an orthogonal basis, carried over to a space whose "vectors" are functions. Once the inner product is in place, the coefficient formulas are not something to memorise: they are the projection formula you already know.

The inner product of two functions

Sample a function at m equally spaced points xⱼ in [−π, π] and you have a vector in ℝᵐ. The dot product of two such vectors, scaled by the spacing Δx = 2π/m, is a Riemann sum, and as m → ∞ it becomes an integral. That integral is the inner product of the two functions. For complex-valued functions the second factor is conjugated, so that ⟨f, f⟩ is a real, non-negative "squared length".

from dot product to integralΔx·Σⱼ f(xⱼ)·g(xⱼ) → ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₋π^π f(x)·ḡ(x) dx ‖f‖ = √⟨f, f⟩ = ( ∫₋π^π |f(x)|² dx )^(1/2) the rules it obeys⟨f, g⟩ = conj⟨g, f⟩, ⟨αf + βh, g⟩ = α⟨f, g⟩ + β⟨h, g⟩, ⟨f, f⟩ ≥ 0, |⟨f, g⟩| ≤ ‖f‖·‖g‖ (Cauchy–Schwarz)
  • The space. The functions with ∫|f|² < ∞ form the space L²(−π, π). It is a vector space (sums and multiples of square-integrable functions are square-integrable, by Cauchy–Schwarz), and with this inner product it is the infinite-dimensional analogue of ℝⁿ with the dot product: lengths, angles, orthogonality and projections all make sense. Two functions that differ only on a set of zero length (for instance at a single jump point) have ‖f − g‖ = 0 and are treated as the same element — the first hint that L² convergence (§05) cannot see what happens at individual points.
  • Orthogonal means "share no frequency content". ⟨f, g⟩ = 0 is exactly the statement that the product f·ḡ has as much area above the axis as below. For two sinusoids of different whole-number frequency, the product oscillates evenly and the areas cancel, which is the next subsection made visual.
  • The vector analogy, made precise. The facts about vectors and norms used here — ‖v‖² = vᵀv, unit vectors, the geometric meaning of the dot product — are in Linear Algebra · vectors; the abstract vector-space and basis language is in vector spaces.

Orthogonality, proved by the integrals

The claim is that the functions 1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, … are pairwise orthogonal on [−π, π]. Every case reduces to one fact: for a whole number k ≠ 0, ∫₋π^π cos kx dx = [sin kx / k]₋π^π = 0 and ∫₋π^π sin kx dx = 0 (an odd function over a symmetric interval). The product-to-sum identities turn every product into such terms.

product-to-sumcos mx·cos nx = ½[cos(m − n)x + cos(m + n)x] sin mx·sin nx = ½[cos(m − n)x − cos(m + n)x] sin mx·cos nx = ½[sin(m + n)x + sin(m − n)x] integrate over [−π, π], m, n ≥ 0 whole numbers∫ cos mx·cos nx dx = 0 if m ≠ n, π if m = n ≥ 1, 2π if m = n = 0 ∫ sin mx·sin nx dx = 0 if m ≠ n, π if m = n ≥ 1 ∫ sin mx·cos nx dx = 0 for all m, n

Reading the cases: when m ≠ n, both m − n and m + n are nonzero whole numbers and each cosine integrates to 0. When m = n ≥ 1, cos(m − n)x = 1 integrates to 2π, which the factor ½ halves to π, and the cos 2nx term contributes 0. The sine–cosine products are odd functions, so they vanish without any case analysis.

The complex form needs one integral instead of three. For whole numbers m, n:

complex exponentials are orthogonal⟨e^(imx), e^(inx)⟩ = ∫₋π^π e^(imx)·e^(−inx) dx = ∫₋π^π e^(i(m − n)x) dx m = n: ∫ 1 dx = 2π m ≠ n: [e^(i(m−n)x) / (i(m − n))]₋π^π = 2·sin((m − n)π)/(m − n) = 0

So {e^(inx)/√(2π)} is an orthonormal set, and {1/√(2π), cos nx/√π, sin nx/√π} is the orthonormal version of the real system. The squared lengths ‖1‖² = 2π, ‖cos nx‖² = ‖sin nx‖² = π, ‖e^(inx)‖² = 2π are exactly the constants that appear in the coefficient formulas, and the odd-looking a₀/2 in a real Fourier series is nothing more than the constant function having twice the squared length of the others.

Whole-number frequencies are essential. cos x and cos 1.5x are not orthogonal on [−π, π]: using ∫₋π^π cos ax dx = 2·sin(aπ)/a, ∫ cos x·cos 1.5x dx = ½∫[cos 0.5x + cos 2.5x] dx = ½·[2·sin(0.5π)/0.5 + 2·sin(2.5π)/2.5] = ½·(4 + 0.8) = 2.4, not 0. Orthogonality on a window of length 2π requires every frequency to complete a whole number of cycles in it — the same requirement that makes the DFT frequencies k/N and that, when violated, produces the leakage discussed in Frequency Domain · windowing.

Fourier coefficients are projections

In ℝⁿ, if q₁, …, qₙ are mutually orthogonal and nonzero, any vector in their span is v = Σⱼ cⱼqⱼ, and taking the inner product of both sides with qₖ kills every term but one: ⟨v, qₖ⟩ = cₖ⟨qₖ, qₖ⟩. So cₖ = ⟨v, qₖ⟩/⟨qₖ, qₖ⟩, the length of the shadow of v on qₖ measured in units of qₖ. No system of equations has to be solved: orthogonality decouples them. This is the projection formula of Linear Algebra · projections, and the Fourier coefficient formulas are that formula with the function inner product.

coefficient = projectionaₙ = ⟨f, cos nx⟩ / ‖cos nx‖² = (1/π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·cos nx dx n = 0, 1, 2, … bₙ = ⟨f, sin nx⟩ / ‖sin nx‖² = (1/π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·sin nx dx n = 1, 2, … cₙ = ⟨f, e^(inx)⟩ / ‖e^(inx)‖² = (1/2π)·∫₋π^π f(x)·e^(−inx) dx n = …, −1, 0, 1, …

The formula for a₀ gives (1/π)∫f, twice the mean of f; the projection onto the constant function is ⟨f, 1⟩/‖1‖² = (1/2π)∫f, which is a₀/2. Writing the series with a₀/2 lets one formula cover every n. The derivation also shows when the formulas are forced: if a trigonometric series converges uniformly to f, it may be integrated term by term after multiplying by cos nx, and orthogonality leaves exactly π·aₙ. A uniformly convergent trigonometric series is therefore always the Fourier series of its own sum.

The truncated series is the best approximation of its degree

Call t(x) = α₀/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N (αₖ·cos kx + βₖ·sin kx), with arbitrary real coefficients, a trigonometric polynomial of degree N, and let S_N be the partial sum of the Fourier series of f up to degree N. Among all trigonometric polynomials of degree N, S_N is the unique one closest to f in mean square.

best approximation‖f − t‖² = ‖f − S_N‖² + ‖S_N − t‖² ≥ ‖f − S_N‖² with equality only when t = S_N the error left over‖f − S_N‖² = ‖f‖² − π·[ a₀²/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N (aₖ² + bₖ²) ]
  • Proof. Let r = f − S_N, the residual. For every k ≤ N, ⟨r, cos kx⟩ = ⟨f, cos kx⟩ − ⟨S_N, cos kx⟩ = π·aₖ − π·aₖ = 0, because by orthogonality the only term of S_N that survives the inner product with cos kx is aₖ·cos kx. The same holds for sin kx and for the constant. So r is orthogonal to the whole subspace of degree-N trigonometric polynomials. Now u = t − S_N lies in that subspace, so ⟨r, u⟩ = 0, and ‖f − t‖² = ‖r − u‖² = ‖r‖² − 2Re⟨r, u⟩ + ‖u‖² = ‖r‖² + ‖u‖². This is Pythagoras: f − t is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the residual and the gap t − S_N inside the subspace.
  • The error formula. Expand ‖f − S_N‖² = ‖f‖² − 2⟨f, S_N⟩ + ‖S_N‖². By orthogonality, ⟨f, S_N⟩ = π[a₀²/2 + Σ(aₖ² + bₖ²)] and ‖S_N‖² is the same number, so one copy cancels.
  • Bessel's inequality. The error is a squared norm, so it is non-negative for every N: a₀²/2 + Σₖ₌₁^N(aₖ² + bₖ²) ≤ (1/π)∫ f². The right side does not depend on N, so the series Σ(aₖ² + bₖ²) converges, and its terms must tend to zero: the Fourier coefficients of any square-integrable function tend to 0. That is the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma in its simplest form, and §07 shows the inequality is in fact an equality.
  • It is least squares with an orthogonal design. Fitting f by t to minimise ∫(f − t)² is a regression of f on the regressors 1, cos x, sin x, …. Because the regressors are orthogonal, the normal equations are diagonal and each coefficient is estimated on its own — which is why adding a higher harmonic never changes the coefficients already found. With non-orthogonal regressors (say powers 1, x, x², …) every coefficient would change each time a new term was added. The general least-squares picture is in Linear Algebra · least squares.

Worked. For the square wave f(x) = sign(x) on (−π, π) (computed in §04), ‖f‖² = 2π and the only nonzero coefficients are bₙ = 4/(nπ) for odd n. The best constant approximation is the mean, 0, with error 2π. The best degree-1 approximation is (4/π)·sin x, with error 2π − π·(4/π)² = 2π − 16/π = 6.2832 − 5.0930 = 1.1902 — it already removes 16/π ÷ 2π = 8/π² = 81.06% of the squared length. Adding cos x or sin 2x with any coefficient other than 0 makes the fit worse.

04 Fourier series

The Fourier series of a function is the expansion in the orthogonal basis of §03, with every coefficient computed by projection. This section fixes the formulas in both the real and the complex form, shows how to move between them and how to change the period, uses symmetry to halve the work, and then computes two series completely — the square wave and the sawtooth — because nearly every phenomenon on the rest of the page can be seen in one of them.

The definitions

Let f be integrable on [−π, π]. Its Fourier series is the trigonometric series whose coefficients are the projections of §03. The symbol ~ instead of = is deliberate: the series is defined for every integrable f, but whether it converges, and to what, is a separate question with a subtle answer (§05).

real formf(x) ~ a₀/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ (aₙ·cos nx + bₙ·sin nx) aₙ = (1/π)∫₋π^π f(x)·cos nx dx, bₙ = (1/π)∫₋π^π f(x)·sin nx dx complex formf(x) ~ Σₙ₌₋∞^∞ cₙ·e^(inx) cₙ = (1/2π)∫₋π^π f(x)·e^(−inx) dx amplitude–phase formf(x) ~ a₀/2 + Σₙ₌₁^∞ Aₙ·cos(nx + φₙ) Aₙ = √(aₙ² + bₙ²) = 2|cₙ|, φₙ = atan2(−bₙ, aₙ) = arg cₙ

Converting. Substitute e^(−inx) = cos nx − i·sin nx into the formula for cₙ, and the real and imaginary parts are the real coefficients. Going back uses cos nx = (e^(inx) + e^(−inx))/2 and sin nx = (e^(inx) − e^(−inx))/(2i) and collects the coefficients of e^(inx) and e^(−inx).

complex ↔ realc₀ = a₀/2, cₙ = (aₙ − i·bₙ)/2, c₋ₙ = (aₙ + i·bₙ)/2 (n ≥ 1) aₙ = cₙ + c₋ₙ, bₙ = i·(cₙ − c₋ₙ) for real f: c₋ₙ = c̄ₙ, aₙ = 2·Re cₙ, bₙ = −2·Im cₙ

Other periods. If f has period T (or lives on an interval of length T), substitute x = 2πs/T. The frequencies become ωₙ = 2πn/T, the base frequency 2π/T is the fundamental, and its multiples are the harmonics. Because every integrand has period T, the integral may be taken over any interval of length T — [0, T] and [−T/2, T/2] give the same numbers.

period Tf(s) ~ a₀/2 + Σ (aₙ·cos(2πns/T) + bₙ·sin(2πns/T)) aₙ = (2/T)∫_T f(s)·cos(2πns/T) ds, cₙ = (1/T)∫_T f(s)·e^(−2πins/T) ds

Symmetry halves the work

If f is…defining propertythenand the coefficients are
evenf(−x) = f(x)all bₙ = 0 (cosine series)aₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π f(x)·cos nx dx
oddf(−x) = −f(x)all aₙ = 0 (sine series)bₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π f(x)·sin nx dx

The even and odd rules hold because the product of an even and an odd function is odd and integrates to zero over [−π, π], while the product of two even (or two odd) functions is even and its integral is twice the integral over [0, π]. The half-wave rule: substituting x → x + π multiplies e^(−inx) by (−1)ⁿ and f by −1, so cₙ = −(−1)ⁿcₙ, which forces cₙ = 0 for even n.

Half-range expansions. A function given only on [0, π] can be extended to [−π, π] either evenly or oddly before expanding, giving a pure cosine series or a pure sine series. The choice matters for convergence: the even extension never creates a jump at x = 0 or at ±π, the odd one does whenever f(0) ≠ 0 or f(π) ≠ 0, and §05 shows that a jump costs a whole order of coefficient decay. This is precisely the reason block-transform compression uses cosines; see Frequency Domain · the DCT.

Worked: the square wave

Let f(x) = −1 on (−π, 0) and f(x) = 1 on (0, π), repeated with period 2π. It is odd, so aₙ = 0 for every n, and

square wave coefficientsbₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π sin nx dx = (2/π)·[−cos nx / n]₀^π = (2/(nπ))·(1 − cos nπ) = (2/(nπ))·(1 − (−1)ⁿ) bₙ = 4/(nπ) for odd n, 0 for even n f(x) ~ (4/π)·( sin x + sin 3x/3 + sin 5x/5 + sin 7x/7 + … ) complex formcₙ = −i·bₙ/2 = −2i/(nπ) for odd n (both signs), 0 otherwise
  • Only odd harmonics, as the half-wave rule predicted: f(x + π) = −f(x). Amplitudes fall like 1/n: 1.2732, 0.4244, 0.2546, 0.1819, 0.1415 for n = 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.
  • Partial sums at x = π/2, where f = 1 and sin(nπ/2) = ±1 alternates over the odd n: S₁ = 1.2732, S₃ = 0.8488, S₅ = 1.1035, S₇ = 0.9216, S₉ = 1.0631 — converging to 1 by alternating overshoots. Setting the limit equal to 1 gives 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … = π/4, Leibniz's series, as a free by-product.
  • At the jump x = 0, every sin nx is 0, so every partial sum is exactly 0 — the midpoint of the jump from −1 to 1, not either one-sided value. §05 proves this is what always happens at a jump.

Worked: the sawtooth

Let f(x) = x on (−π, π), repeated with period 2π: a ramp that falls back from π to −π at every odd multiple of π. It is odd, so again aₙ = 0. One integration by parts:

sawtooth coefficients∫₀^π x·sin nx dx = [−x·cos nx / n]₀^π + (1/n)∫₀^π cos nx dx = −π·(−1)ⁿ/n + 0 = π·(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n bₙ = (2/π)·π·(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n = 2·(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n x ~ 2·( sin x − sin 2x/2 + sin 3x/3 − sin 4x/4 + … ) complex formc₀ = 0, cₙ = (1/2π)∫₋π^π x·e^(−inx) dx = i·(−1)ⁿ/n (n ≠ 0)
  • All harmonics appear (no half-wave symmetry), with alternating signs and amplitudes 2/n: 2, 1, 0.667, 0.5, 0.4.
  • At x = π/2: sin(nπ/2) is 0 for even n and alternates 1, −1 over odd n, so the series reads 2(1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …) = 2·π/4 = π/2, as it must. The partial sums S₁ … S₅ are 2, 2, 1.3333, 1.3333, 1.7333 against the target 1.5708: even harmonics contribute nothing at this particular point.
  • At the jump x = π: every sin nπ = 0, so the series gives 0, the midpoint of π (left limit) and −π (right limit).
  • The sawtooth is smooth inside, but its periodic extension is not. On (−π, π) the function x is as smooth as a function can be, yet its coefficients decay only like 1/n, as slowly as the square wave's. The series does not see x on an interval; it sees the 2π-periodic extension, and that has a jump of size 2π at every odd multiple of π. Every non-periodic function you expand on a window acquires such a jump unless its two ends happen to match.

Two more expansions worth having

triangle and parabola|x| ~ π/2 − (4/π)·( cos x + cos 3x/3² + cos 5x/5² + … ) aₙ = −4/(πn²) for odd n x² ~ π²/3 + 4·Σₙ₌₁^∞ (−1)ⁿ·cos nx / n² (from aₙ = (1/π)∫ x²·cos nx dx, two integrations by parts)

Both are continuous after periodic extension (|x| and x² take equal values at ±π), and both have coefficients falling like 1/n², a full order faster than the square wave. Because the periodic extension of x² is continuous and piecewise smooth, the series converges to it at every point (§05), including x = π, where cos nπ = (−1)ⁿ: π² = π²/3 + 4·Σ 1/n², so Σ 1/n² = π²/6. §07 reaches the same sum by a completely different route.

What the coefficients mean physically

  • a₀/2 is the mean of f over one period — the "DC" level that the oscillating terms average around.
  • Aₙ = √(aₙ² + bₙ²) is how much of frequency n the function contains; Aₙ²/2 is that component's mean-square contribution. §07 shows these contributions add up exactly to the mean square of f.
  • The phase φₙ records where each component's peaks sit. Two functions with the same amplitudes and different phases can look completely different (a square wave and a smeared, asymmetric blob), which is the one-dimensional version of the demonstration in Frequency Domain · magnitude and phase.
  • The pattern of harmonics is the "timbre". A sustained musical note of fundamental frequency f₀ is close to periodic, so it is a Fourier series in f₀, 2f₀, 3f₀, …. Two instruments playing the same pitch differ in their harmonic amplitudes: a square-like waveform has only odd harmonics and sounds hollow, a sawtooth-like one has all harmonics and sounds bright.
  • How fast the amplitudes fall is how smooth the function is. Jumps produce 1/n, corners 1/n², and smoother functions faster still. This is the subject of the second half of §05.

The builder adds harmonics one slider step at a time. Top: the partial sum S_N (orange) over the target wave (grey) on one period, with the value at x = π/2 marked. Bottom left: the coefficients as a stem plot, filled up to the current N. Bottom right: the energy ledger — the fraction of the mean square of f captured by the harmonics included so far, [a₀²/2 + Σₙ≤N (aₙ² + bₙ²)] ÷ (1/π)∫f², which by the error formula of §03 is 1 − ‖f − S_N‖²/‖f‖² and which Parseval's theorem (§07) says must reach 100%. The default is the sawtooth with N = 5: the partial sum is wiggly but recognisable, S₅(π/2) = 1.7333 against π/2 = 1.5708, and the ledger stands at 88.98% ((6/π²)·(1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + 1/25)), leaving a relative L² error of √(1 − 0.8898) = 33.2%. The square wave reaches 81.06% with its very first harmonic alone.
