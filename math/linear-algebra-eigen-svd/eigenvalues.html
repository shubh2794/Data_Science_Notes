09 Complex numbers

A short but necessary detour: real matrices can have complex eigenvalues (any rotation does), so we need the machinery — plus the small set of rules that change when matrix entries leave ℝ.

Why they show up at all

  • The fundamental theorem of algebra says a degree-n polynomial has exactly n roots — if you allow complex ones. Eigenvalues are roots of a polynomial built from a matrix, so a perfectly real-valued matrix can hand you complex eigenvalues (§15).
  • The archetype: R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]] rotates the plane, so no real vector keeps its direction — there can be no real eigenvector. Its eigenvalues are cos θ ± i·sin θ = e^(±iθ). Rotation is complex multiplication in disguise.
  • Elsewhere in applied math the same machinery runs the Fourier transform and the FFT, where a signal is decomposed into complex exponentials carrying both amplitude and phase.

The complex plane

  • The reals form a line; the complex numbers ℂ form a plane. Horizontal = real axis (Re), vertical = imaginary axis (Im), and i = √(−1), so i² = −1.
  • Write z = a + ib (real part first, always). Variations you'll see: a + bi, [a bi], (a, bi). z is the default letter, then w. Engineers write j for i; Python agrees — the literal is 1j, and 3+4j is a complex scalar.
  • Vocabulary worth keeping straight: a complex number has both parts; an imaginary number has no real part (ib); the imaginary operator is the bare symbol i; the imaginary component of a + ib is the real number b, not ib.
  • Because a complex number is a point in a plane it carries four readings at once: real part, imaginary part, magnitude |z| = √(a² + b²) (distance from the origin), and angle from the positive real axis. Hence the polar form z = r·e^(iθ), in which multiplication is beautifully simple: magnitudes multiply, angles add.
  • Complex matrices are ordinary matrices whose entries are drawn from ℂ. Beware of miscounting: [[6 − i2], [3 + i4]] is a 2×1 column vector, not a 2×2 matrix. If you see an i, slow down and count entries.
i² = −1 z = a + ib |z| = √(a² + b²) z = r·e^(iθ), r = |z|

The complex conjugate

  • z̄ (or z*) flips the sign of the imaginary part — it does not make it negative. The conjugate of a − ib is a + ib. In polar form r·e^(iθ) → r·e^(−iθ).
  • Geometrically it is a reflection across the real axis. A real number is its own conjugate — which is precisely why real diagonals show up in Hermitian matrices below.
  • The key identity: z̄z = (a − ib)(a + ib) = a² + b² = |z|² — real and non-negative. This one line is the reason the complex dot product is defined the way it is.
  • Conjugate pairs travel together. A real-coefficient polynomial with a complex root has them in pairs: x² + 9 = 0 gives ±3i. Same story from the quadratic formula whenever b² < 4ac. Consequently, complex eigenvalues of a real matrix always come in conjugate pairs.

Arithmetic

  • Add / subtract component-wise: (a + ib) ± (c + id) = (a ± c) + i(b ± d).
  • Multiply by expanding, then use i² = −1: (a + ib)(c + id) = (ac − bd) + i(ad + bc). There is no "multiply the parts separately" shortcut — the cross terms matter. Example: (3 + 6i)(−2 − 5i) = 24 − 27i.
  • Divide by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator, which makes the denominator real: z/w = zw̄ / (ww̄) = zw̄ / |w|². Example: (3 + 6i)/(−2 − 5i) = (−36 + 3i)/29.
  • Conjugation distributes over everything: conj(z + w) = z̄ + w̄ and conj(zw) = z̄·w̄.
arithmetic(a + ib)(c + id) = (ac − bd) + i(ad + bc) z̄z = |z|² z/w = zw̄ / |w|²

The Hermitian transpose and the complex dot product

  • The Hermitian transpose vᴴ is conjugate + transpose. For real matrices conjugation does nothing, so ᴴ = ᵀ — every real result you know is the special case of a complex one.
  • The dot product is mechanically unchanged (multiply element-wise, sum), but in practice you always use the Hermitian dot product zᴴw rather than zᵀw.
  • Why: length must stay positive. For v = [0, i]ᵀ, which clearly has length 1, the naive vᵀv = 0 + i² = −1 — a negative squared length. Conjugating one side gives vᴴv = 0 + (−i)(i) = 1. In general vᴴv = ∑|vₖ|² is real and non-negative, so ‖v‖ = √(vᴴv) still makes sense.
  • It doesn't matter mathematically which vector you conjugate; vᴴw is convention. Note that swapping the arguments conjugates the result: wᴴv = conj(vᴴw), so the complex inner product is not symmetric but conjugate-symmetric.
  • In code this is a real trap: numpy's np.dot does not conjugate, np.vdot conjugates the first argument, and .conj().T spells out the Hermitian explicitly.
vᴴ = v̄ᵀ vᴴv = ∑ₖ |vₖ|² ≥ 0 wᴴv = conj(vᴴw) real A ⟹ Aᴴ = Aᵀ

Special complex matrices

  • Hermitian A = Aᴴ — the complex analogue of symmetric. Mirror entries have equal magnitude and opposite-sign imaginary parts, e.g. [[2, 3−2i, 2+2i], [3+2i, 5, 8], [2−2i, 8, 9]]. The diagonal must be real, since only real numbers equal their own conjugate. Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors — exactly the properties that make symmetric matrices so pleasant.
  • Unitary UᴴU = I — the complex analogue of orthogonal (QᵀQ = I): columns are mutually orthogonal and unit length, so U preserves lengths and angles. Canonical example: U = ½·[[1+i, 1−i], [1−i, 1+i]], for which UᴴU = I but UᵀU ≠ I — proof that the conjugate is doing real work.
  • Skew-Hermitian A = −Aᴴ — the analogue of skew-symmetric. Its diagonal is purely imaginary and its eigenvalues are purely imaginary.
  • The symmetry-manufacturing tricks of §06 carry over with the same substitution: A + Aᴴ and AᴴA are Hermitian, not symmetric. Use the ordinary transpose by mistake and you get neither.

The one rule to internalize: everywhere a transpose appears in real linear algebra, the complex version wants a Hermitian transpose. Do that and lengths stay positive, symmetry becomes Hermitian-ness, orthogonal becomes unitary, and every theorem survives the crossing into ℂ.

15 Eigendecomposition

A matrix's special directions — the vectors it only stretches, never rotates. Find them and the matrix stops being a transformation and becomes a list of numbers.

Eigen-whatnow?

  • Defined only for square matrices. They may be singular or invertible, symmetric or triangular — but not rectangular. (The SVD, §16, lifts that restriction.)
  • An M×M matrix hides M eigenvalues and M paired eigenvectors. Eigenvalues are scalars (λ), eigenvectors are vectors (v).
  • Geometry: a matrix is an input-output transformer. For almost every vector w, Aw points somewhere else — the matrix rotates it. For a select few directions the output is parallel to the input: the matrix acted like a single number. That is the whole idea.
  • The relation belongs to the pair, not to the matrix or the vector alone. Change one element of A and v is generally no longer an eigenvector.
  • Eigenvalues can be zero, negative, or complex. λ < 0 means the direction is preserved but flipped; λ = 0 means the direction is annihilated (it's in the null space).
  • Eigenvectors are defined only up to scale — an eigenvector is really a basis vector for a 1D subspace. If v works, so does αv for any α ≠ 0. Convention (and every numerical library) picks ‖v‖ = 1; the sign is still arbitrary, so two runs of the same routine may return v or −v.
  • Where this shows up: plot two correlated measurements and the "natural axis" through the cloud is an eigenvector of the covariance matrix — the first principal component. PCA (§19) is nothing but an eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix.
the fundamental eigenvalue equationAv = λv v ≠ 0 "the matrix acts on v exactly as the scalar λ does"

Finding the eigenvalues

  • Move everything to one side and factor out v — inserting I to keep the shapes legal. The result says: the shifted matrix A − λI sends v to zero, so v lives in its null space.
  • We refuse the trivial v = 0, so A − λI must have a non-trivial null space ⟹ it is reduced-rank ⟹ its determinant is 0. That single condition is the machine that produces eigenvalues.
  • Expanding det(A − λI) = 0 gives the characteristic polynomial, degree M in λ. By the fundamental theorem of algebra it has exactly M roots (counting multiplicity, allowing complex ones) — hence M eigenvalues for an M×M matrix.
derivationAv − λv = 0 ⟹ Av − λIv = 0 ⟹ (A − λI)v = 0 characteristic equationdet(A − λI) = 0 2×2 shortcut (only 2×2!)λ² − tr(A)·λ + det(A) = 0 ⟹ λ = ( tr(A) ± √(tr(A)² − 4·det(A)) ) / 2
  • Worked 2×2: for [[1, 2], [2, 1]], det([[1−λ, 2], [2, 1−λ]]) = (1−λ)² − 4 = λ² − 2λ − 3 = (λ−3)(λ+1) ⟹ λ = 3, −1. Always try to factor before reaching for the quadratic formula — for [[1, 2], [2, 3]] you can't, and you get λ = 2 ± √5.
  • Free eigenvalues: for a triangular or diagonal matrix the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries — the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal, so the characteristic polynomial arrives pre-factored. E.g. [[9, 0, −8], [0, 3, −15], [0, 0, 1]] has λ = 9, 3, 1.
  • Two free sanity checks that work for any size: the eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant. Use them to find a missing eigenvalue, or to catch an arithmetic slip.
  • Eigenvalues are intrinsically unsorted. Software sorts them (by value or magnitude) for convenience — if you re-sort Λ, you must re-sort the columns of V identically or the pairing breaks.
sanity checksΣᵢ λᵢ = tr(A) Πᵢ λᵢ = det(A)

Finding the eigenvectors

  • Each eigenvalue is the key that unlocks its eigenvector: shift the matrix by that λ and find a vector in the null space of what's left.
  • For [[1, 2], [2, 1]]: λ₁ = 3 ⟹ A − 3I = [[−2, 2], [2, −2]], whose null space is spanned by [1, 1]ᵀ. λ₂ = −1 ⟹ A + I = [[2, 2], [2, 2]], null space spanned by [−1, 1]ᵀ.
  • Any scalar multiple works — [2, 2]ᵀ, [−13, −13]ᵀ, [π, π]ᵀ are the same eigenvector. Normalised, the pair is [1, 1]ᵀ/√2 and [−1, 1]ᵀ/√2. By hand, keep integers; in code, expect unit vectors.
finding eigenvectorsvᵢ ∈ N(A − λᵢI) (A − λᵢI)vᵢ = 0 convention: ‖vᵢ‖ = 1

Diagonalization

  • M separate equations Avᵢ = λᵢvᵢ collapse into one matrix equation by stacking the eigenvectors as columns of V and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ.
  • It must be VΛ, not ΛV. Post-multiplying by a diagonal matrix scales each column (one λ per eigenvector); pre-multiplying would scale each row, hitting every eigenvector's i-th element with the same λ. Also, AV = ΛV would let you cancel V and conclude A = Λ, which is nonsense.
  • Right-multiply by V⁻¹ and you get the punchline: A is a diagonal matrix when written in the basis of its own eigenvectors. Diagonalization = finding the coordinate system in which the transformation is pure per-axis scaling.
  • Read A = VΛV⁻¹ right-to-left as a three-step process: V⁻¹ rewrites the input in eigen-coordinates, Λ stretches each eigen-axis independently, V translates back to the original coordinates.
diagonalizationAV = VΛ ⟹ A = VΛV⁻¹ ⟺ V⁻¹AV = Λ

When diagonalization fails

  • The step A = VΛV⁻¹ assumes V is invertible, i.e. the matrix has M linearly independent eigenvectors. A matrix that doesn't is called defective.
  • Good news for applied work: every symmetric matrix is diagonalizable, and most matrices arising in statistics, ML and simulation are diagonalizable.
  • Bad news: [[1, 1], [−1, −1]] is rank-1 yet has λ = {0, 0}. With Λ = 0 there is no way to rebuild the matrix. It is nilpotent: A² = 0. Every triangular matrix with a zero diagonal is nilpotent, has all-zero eigenvalues, and cannot be diagonalized — e.g. [[0, 1], [0, 0]].
  • Nilpotence explains the zero eigenvalues directly: if Aᵏ = 0 then 0 = Aᵏv = λᵏv, and since v ≠ 0, λ = 0.
  • The escape hatch is the SVD, which exists for every matrix. Those two defective matrices have perfectly ordinary singular values, {2, 0} and {1, 0}.

Distinct vs. repeated eigenvalues

  • Distinct eigenvalues always give linearly independent eigenvectors — so a matrix with M distinct λs is guaranteed diagonalizable.
  • Proof by contradiction: assume β₁v₁ + β₂v₂ = 0 with both β ≠ 0. Multiply through by A to get β₁λ₁v₁ + β₂λ₂v₂ = 0; multiply the original by λ₁ and subtract. The v₁ terms cancel, leaving (λ₁ − λ₂)β₂v₂ = 0. Three factors multiplying to zero: λ₁ ≠ λ₂ by assumption and v₂ ≠ 0 by definition, so β₂ = 0 — contradiction. Feed that back and β₁ = 0 too, so the only combination giving zero is the trivial one: independence.
  • Repeated eigenvalues are ambiguous — the same proof stalls, because (λ₁ − λ₂) = 0 already satisfies the equation and tells you nothing. Both outcomes are possible, and which one occurs depends on the matrix entries.
  • Case A — defective. [[6, −4], [1, 2]] has (λ − 4)² = 0, so λ = 4 twice, and A − 4I = [[2, −4], [1, −2]] has a 1D null space: only one eigenvector, [2, 1]ᵀ. Nothing is wrong with the matrix (rank 2, non-zero determinant) — it just doesn't have enough eigenvectors.
  • Case B — a full eigenspace. 4I = [[4, 0], [0, 4]] also has λ = 4 twice, but A − 4I = 0, so every vector is an eigenvector. Software returns the standard basis (orthogonal, unit length), but any full-rank V would do.
  • Geometrically: a distinct eigenvalue owns a 1D eigenline. A repeated eigenvalue either collapses onto a single line (defective — the eigenvectors fail to span ℝᴹ) or owns a whole eigenplane, in which case any two independent vectors in that plane serve as eigenvectors; convention picks an orthogonal pair.
  • Not just a curiosity: real datasets produce eigenvalues that are exactly repeated or statistically indistinguishable often enough to be worth watching for.

Algebraic vs. geometric multiplicity — the exact test

  • "Repeated eigenvalue" is really two different counts wearing one name, and the entire diagonalizability question is the gap between them.
  • Algebraic multiplicity alg(λ) = how many times λ appears as a root of the characteristic polynomial — the exponent on (x − λ) once det(A − xI) is factored. It is a statement about a polynomial.
  • Geometric multiplicity geo(λ) = dim N(A − λI) — the dimension of the eigenspace S_A(λ), i.e. how many linearly independent eigenvectors that λ actually owns. It is a statement about a subspace. Equivalently geo(λ) = M − rank(A − λI) by rank–nullity (§07).
  • The two are never unrelated: 1 ≤ geo(λ) ≤ alg(λ) for every eigenvalue. The lower bound is free — det(A − λI) = 0 guarantees a non-trivial null space, so at least one eigenvector exists. The upper bound says you can never harvest more independent directions than the polynomial promised.
  • The theorem. Let the distinct eigenvalues be μ₁ … μ_h with algebraic multiplicities r₁ … r_h (so r₁ + … + r_h = M). Then A is diagonalizable ⟺ rank(A − μᵢI) = M − rᵢ for every i — that is, geo(μᵢ) = alg(μᵢ) for every eigenvalue, with no exceptions allowed.
  • Why that is the whole story: eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are automatically independent (proved above), so the eigenvectors of A span a subspace of dimension exactly Σᵢ geo(μᵢ). That total hits M — giving an invertible V — precisely when every geometric multiplicity is maxed out at its algebraic value.
  • Vocabulary: a λ with geo(λ) < alg(λ) is called defective, and so is any matrix that has one. alg(λ) − geo(λ) is the defect — literally the number of eigenvectors you are short.
  • Simple eigenvalues are safe. If alg(λ) = 1 then 1 ≤ geo(λ) ≤ 1 forces equality. So a matrix with M distinct eigenvalues cannot be defective — which is the earlier "distinct ⟹ diagonalizable" result, now as a one-line corollary.
the two multiplicitiesalg(λ) = exponent of (x − λ) in det(A − xI) geo(λ) = dim N(A − λI) = M − rank(A − λI) the inequality that decides everything1 ≤ geo(λ) ≤ alg(λ) A diagonalizable ⟺ geo(λᵢ) = alg(λᵢ) for every i ⟺ Σᵢ geo(μᵢ) = M
  • Worked, defective: A = [[6, −4], [1, 2]]. det(A − λI) = (6 − λ)(2 − λ) + 4 = λ² − 8λ + 16 = (λ − 4)², so alg(4) = 2. But A − 4I = [[2, −4], [1, −2]] has rank 1, so geo(4) = 2 − 1 = 1. Defect 1 ⟹ defective, not diagonalizable — even though det(A) = 16 ≠ 0, so the matrix is perfectly invertible. Invertibility and diagonalizability are unrelated properties.
  • Worked, healthy: 4I. Same characteristic polynomial (λ − 4)², so alg(4) = 2 again — but A − 4I = 0 has rank 0, so geo(4) = 2. Multiplicities agree, matrix diagonalizable (it already is diagonal). Identical spectrum, opposite verdict: the eigenvalues alone never tell you, you must look at the rank of the shifted matrix.
  • Worked, 3×3: A = [[0, 1, 0], [2, −1, 0], [0, 0, 1]] has eigenvalues 1, 1, 2, so alg(1) = 2. A − I = [[−1, 1, 0], [2, −2, 0], [0, 0, 0]] has rank 1 ⟹ geo(1) = 2. Multiplicities match, so this matrix is diagonalizable despite the repeated eigenvalue — with eigenvectors [0, 0, 1]ᵀ and [1, 1, 0]ᵀ spanning the eigenplane, plus [1, 0, 0]ᵀ for λ = 2.
  • Rank vs. non-zero eigenvalues, settled. The earlier gotcha now has a precise form: rank(A) = M − geo(0) always (the null space is the eigenspace of λ = 0). So rank(A) equals the count of non-zero eigenvalues exactly when geo(0) = alg(0) — the zero eigenvalue is the only one that has to behave. [[1, 1], [0, 1]] is not diagonalizable, yet 0 is not an eigenvalue at all, so its rank (2) does equal its count of non-zero eigenvalues.
  • And a one-sided bound holds for every square matrix: rank(A) ≥ (number of non-zero eigenvalues). The proof is one line from the Schur decomposition below.

The minimal polynomial: a sharper diagonalizability test

  • Cayley–Hamilton: every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial — substitute the matrix for the variable and you get the zeros matrix, c(A) = 0. (Careful: the slick "proof" c(A) = det(A − AI) = det(0) = 0 is nonsense — it substitutes a matrix into a scalar determinant. The real proof runs through the adjugate identity adj(C)·C = det(C)·I with C = A − xI, treating adj(C) as a polynomial with matrix coefficients and matching powers of x.)
  • Because some polynomial annihilates A, there is a unique monic one of least degree: the minimal polynomial m(x). Every polynomial that annihilates A is divisible by m, so in particular m divides c.
  • m and c have exactly the same roots — the eigenvalues — only the exponents differ. If c(x) = Πᵢ (x − μᵢ)^rᵢ then m(x) = Πᵢ (x − μᵢ)^qᵢ with 1 ≤ qᵢ ≤ rᵢ. (Every root of m is an eigenvalue because 0 = m(A)v = m(λ)v forces m(λ) = 0; the converse is the second half of Cayley–Hamilton.)
  • The clean criterion: A is diagonalizable ⟺ its minimal polynomial has no repeated roots, i.e. every qᵢ = 1, i.e. m(x) = Πᵢ (x − μᵢ) over the distinct eigenvalues. This is the multiplicity test in disguise — qᵢ turns out to be the size of the largest Jordan block for μᵢ (below), and all blocks are 1×1 exactly when the matrix is diagonalizable.
  • Worked: T = [[2, 0, 0, 1], [1, 2, 0, 2], [0, 0, 2, −1], [0, 0, 0, 1]] has c(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2)³, so m is one of (x−1)(x−2), (x−1)(x−2)², (x−1)(x−2)³. Multiplying out, (T − I)(T − 2I) ≠ 0 but (T − I)(T − 2I)² = 0 — hence m(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2)². Repeated root ⟹ not diagonalizable.
  • Instant corollaries. A projection satisfies P² = P, so m divides x² − x = x(x − 1) — squarefree, hence every projection is diagonalizable with λ ∈ {0, 1}. An involution R² = I has m | (x−1)(x+1), so λ = ±1 and it is diagonalizable. A nilpotent Aᵏ = 0 has m(x) = xᵖ for some p ≥ 2 (unless A = 0), which is never squarefree — so a non-zero nilpotent matrix is always defective.

The Jordan form — the best you can do when diagonalization fails

  • Two matrices are similar if A = CBC⁻¹ for some invertible C — the same linear map written in two bases. Similar matrices share everything basis-free: eigenvalues (with multiplicities), characteristic and minimal polynomial, determinant, trace, rank. Diagonalization is the question "is A similar to a diagonal matrix?", and we've just seen the answer is sometimes no.
  • The converse fails: identical eigenvalues do not imply similarity. [[0, 1], [0, 0]] and [[0, 0], [0, 0]] both have λ = 0 twice, but nothing is similar to the zeros matrix except itself (C·0·C⁻¹ = 0). Eigenvalues are a coarse invariant; the Jordan form is the fine one.
  • Jordan block: J_h(λ) is h×h with λ on the diagonal and 1s on the superdiagonal. It is triangular, so its only eigenvalue is λ with alg = h; but J_h(λ) − λI is the shift matrix of rank h − 1, so geo = 1. A Jordan block of size h > 1 is maximally defective: one eigenvector, no matter how large.
  • Jordan decomposition theorem: every square A is similar to a block-diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks, B⁻¹AB = J = diag(J_{h₁}(λ₁), …, J_{h_r}(λ_r)), unique up to the order of the blocks. Diagonalizable is the special case where every hᵢ = 1.
  • Reading the block structure off the multiplicities: for a given eigenvalue μ, the number of blocks carrying μ is geo(μ) (one eigenvector per block), the total size of those blocks is alg(μ), and the largest block has size q, the exponent of (x − μ) in the minimal polynomial. The full block sizes are recovered from the ranks of (A − μI)ᵏ as k climbs.
  • Counting: a 4×4 matrix whose only eigenvalue is λ (multiplicity 4) is similar to one of exactly five Jordan forms — one per partition of 4: 1+1+1+1 (diagonal, 4 eigenvectors), 2+1+1 (3 eigenvectors), 2+2 and 3+1 (2 eigenvectors each), and 4 (1 eigenvector). The partition, not the spectrum, is the complete similarity invariant.
  • The generalized eigenvectors. The columns of B belonging to a block form a Jordan chain: Av₁ = λv₁, then Av₂ = λv₂ + v₁, Av₃ = λv₃ + v₂, … Only the first is a true eigenvector; the rest satisfy (A − λI)ᵏvₖ = 0 without satisfying (A − λI)vₖ = 0. They live in the generalized eigenspace N((A − λI)^alg(λ)), whose dimension is always exactly alg(λ) — which is why generalized eigenvectors always give a full basis even when ordinary ones don't.
  • What it buys you: functions of a matrix. Jᵏ, e^J, and friends are computable block by block, and a Jordan block contributes terms like kλ^(k−1) and t·e^(λt) — the polynomial-times-exponential solutions of a repeated-root differential equation. Defectiveness is exactly why a critically damped oscillator has a t·e^(−ωt) term.
  • Why no numerical library computes it. The Jordan structure is discontinuous in the matrix entries: [[λ, 1], [0, λ]] is one 2×2 block, but perturbing one entry by ε to [[λ, 1], [ε, λ]] gives distinct eigenvalues λ ± √ε and a diagonalizable matrix. Rounding error alone destroys the answer — and note that a perturbation of size ε moved the eigenvalues by √ε, which for ε = 10⁻¹⁶ is 10⁻⁸. Defective eigenvalues are ill-conditioned eigenvalues. (By contrast, the eigenvalues themselves depend continuously on the entries — it is the eigenvectors and the block structure that jump.) In floating point, use the SVD or the Schur form instead.

The hierarchy, in one line: symmetric matrices get an orthogonal eigenbasis (QΛQᵀ); diagonalizable matrices get some eigenbasis (VΛV⁻¹); every matrix gets a generalized eigenbasis (BJB⁻¹) — and, if you insist on a numerically trustworthy factorization, a unitary one with a triangular core (QTQᴴ, below).

Complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  • If the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial goes negative, the eigenvalues are complex — and complex eigenvalues force complex eigenvectors. A real matrix can absolutely do this; nothing is broken.
  • The archetype is a rotation: [[0, −1], [1, 0]] turns every real vector by 90°, so no real direction survives. Its eigenvalues are ±i, with eigenvectors [1, ∓i]ᵀ. Any rotation-like block behaves the same way.
  • For a real matrix, complex solutions always arrive in conjugate pairs: λ and λ̄, with eigenvectors v and v̄. Take the conjugate of Av = λv; since Ā = A for real A, you get Av̄ = λ̄v̄ for free. For 2×2 it's visible in the quadratic formula — only the ±√(negative) differs between the two roots.
  • Complex eigen-solutions are awkward when your data are real, but they carry real information: the modulus of λ is the scaling and its argument is the rotation angle per application of A.

Symmetric matrices: the best case

  • Real eigenvalues. Take the Hermitian transpose of Av = λv: since Aᴴ = Aᵀ = A for a real symmetric matrix, vᴴA = λ̄vᴴ. Right-multiply by v: λvᴴv = λ̄vᴴv, and vᴴv = ‖v‖² ≠ 0 divides out, leaving λ = λ̄ — a number equal to its own conjugate is real.
  • Orthogonal eigenvectors (for distinct λ). Chase the equalities λ₁v₁ᵀv₂ = (Av₁)ᵀv₂ = v₁ᵀAᵀv₂ = v₁ᵀAv₂ = λ₂v₁ᵀv₂. Subtract and factor: (λ₁ − λ₂)v₁ᵀv₂ = 0. The eigenvalues differ, so the dot product must vanish — v₁ ⟂ v₂. The proof needs Aᵀ = A at the middle step and collapses without it.
  • Unit-normalise those orthogonal eigenvectors and V becomes an orthogonal matrix: VᵀV = I, so V⁻¹ = Vᵀ. The inverse in the decomposition becomes a free transpose — this is the spectral theorem.
  • Repeated eigenvalues don't spoil it: symmetric matrices are never defective, and the eigenvectors within a repeated eigenspace can be chosen orthogonal (that's what software returns).
  • Some texts write the same thing as A = PDP⁻¹ or A = UDU⁻¹; the letters change, the content doesn't.
spectral theorem (real symmetric A)A = QΛQᵀ QᵀQ = I λᵢ ∈ ℝ vᵢᵀvⱼ = 1 if i = j, else 0

The Schur decomposition — what libraries actually compute

  • Two of the three factorizations so far trade away the nice factor to get a nicer core: VΛV⁻¹ and BJB⁻¹ both need a general, possibly badly conditioned similarity matrix. Schur asks the opposite question: if you insist on a unitary (for real matrices, orthogonal) change of basis — the only kind that costs nothing numerically — what is the simplest core you can reach?
  • Answer: upper triangular, always. For every square matrix A there is a unitary Q with QᴴAQ = T upper triangular, and the diagonal of T is the eigenvalues of A. No hypotheses, no exceptions — defective, singular, non-normal, complex-spectrum, all fine.
  • The proof is a deflation, and it is worth carrying in your head. Take any unit eigenvector y₁ (one always exists) and extend it to a unitary Y = [y₁ | Y₂]. Then YᴴAY = [[λ₁, y₁ᴴAY₂], [0, B]] — the first column is Yᴴ(Ay₁) = λ₁Yᴴy₁ = λ₁e₁, which zeroes the whole sub-column because the other columns of Y are orthogonal to y₁. Cofactor expansion shows B's eigenvalues are λ₂ … λ_M, so induct on B and stack the unitary factors. Each step "peels off" one eigenvalue.
  • Non-uniqueness: T is not determined — the eigenvalues can appear on the diagonal in any order, and the strictly upper part changes completely with the ordering. That freedom is a feature: numerical software can reorder the Schur form to bring a wanted cluster of eigenvalues to the top left, which is how invariant subspaces (and stable/unstable splittings) are extracted.
  • Real Schur form: a real matrix with complex eigenvalues cannot be triangularized by a real orthogonal matrix — the diagonal would have to hold complex numbers. The fix is quasi-triangular: real orthogonal Q with QᵀAQ block upper triangular, 1×1 blocks for real eigenvalues and 2×2 blocks for each conjugate pair (each block a scaled rotation, exactly the picture from the complex-eigenvalue subsection above). If all eigenvalues are real, a real orthogonal Q triangularizes outright.
  • Three free corollaries. Since Q is unitary and T triangular: det(A) = det(T) = Πλᵢ; tr(A) = tr(QTQᴴ) = tr(QᴴQT) = tr(T) = Σλᵢ — both of the "free sanity checks" from earlier, now proved for every square matrix in two lines. And rank(A) ≥ (number of non-zero λᵢ), because deleting the rows and columns of T that carry a zero diagonal entry leaves a triangular submatrix with non-zero diagonal, hence non-singular, of that size.
  • Why this and not the eigendecomposition. Numerically, the quantity that matters is the condition number of the change of basis. For a unitary Q that number is exactly 1 (§16) — errors are neither amplified nor rotated into the wrong place. For the eigenvector matrix V of a nearly defective matrix it can be astronomically large, and the computed Λ is then worthless. So LAPACK's geev/gees and numpy's eig do not solve the characteristic polynomial (root-finding on a degree-M polynomial is itself unstable); they reduce A to upper Hessenberg form by orthogonal similarity, then run the shifted QR iteration, which converges to the Schur form. Eigenvalues are read off the diagonal; eigenvectors, if you ask for them, come from back-substitution afterwards.
  • Same story for the symmetric case: eigh reduces to tridiagonal form by orthogonal similarity and then diagonalizes — a symmetric-aware Schur reduction. Always call eigh (not eig) on a symmetric matrix: it is roughly twice as fast and returns exactly real eigenvalues and exactly orthogonal eigenvectors, whereas eig will hand you eigenvalues with 1e-17j attached.

The spectral theorem, finished

  • The two proofs above (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors) left one hole: they only separate eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues. Repeated eigenvalues were waved through with "symmetric matrices are never defective". Schur closes the hole in one line.
  • Proof. Write the Schur form A = QTQᵀ — legitimate with real Q because a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues. Now transpose: Aᵀ = QTᵀQᵀ. Since A = Aᵀ, we get T = Tᵀ. But T is upper triangular, and a triangular matrix that equals its own transpose is diagonal. So T = Λ and A = QΛQᵀ — done, with no case analysis, no assumption about multiplicities, and the columns of Q orthonormal by construction.
  • That gives the full statement: every real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and real eigenvalues, repeated or not. Within an eigenspace of dimension d > 1 any orthonormal basis will do — the theorem guarantees one exists, not that it is unique. (Software returns whichever one its algorithm lands on; two runs on the same matrix can return different bases of the same eigenplane, which is why PCA loadings for near-tied eigenvalues are unstable in exactly this way.)
  • Same argument, same conclusion in ℂ: a Hermitian matrix satisfies T = Tᴴ, hence T diagonal with real entries, hence A = UΛUᴴ. And more generally the argument works for any normal matrix (AᴴA = AAᴴ): normality forces the triangular T to be diagonal too. So the normal matrices are exactly the unitarily diagonalizable ones — a family that includes symmetric, Hermitian, skew-symmetric, orthogonal and unitary matrices, and explains in one stroke why they all have such well-behaved spectra.
  • Corollary — the eigenspaces of a symmetric matrix are mutually orthogonal and decompose the space: ℝᴹ = S_A(μ₁) ⊕ … ⊕ S_A(μ_h) with dim S_A(μᵢ) = alg(μᵢ), and A = Σᵢ μᵢ Pᵢ where Pᵢ is the orthogonal projector onto the i-th eigenspace (§13). That projector form is the basis-independent statement of the eigenlayers below: the individual eigenvectors are arbitrary inside a repeated eigenspace, but the projector is unique.

Eigenvalues of singular matrices

  • Every singular matrix has at least one λ = 0; every full-rank matrix has none. Quick argument: det(A) = Πλᵢ, and a singular matrix has determinant 0, so some λ must be 0.
  • The insightful argument runs through (A − λI)v = 0. Set λ = 0 and it reads Av = 0 with v ≠ 0 — that is the definition of a non-trivial null space. So the eigenvectors for λ = 0 are exactly a basis for N(A).
  • Example: [[1, 2], [1, 2]] gives λ² − 3λ = 0 ⟹ λ = 0, 3, with eigenvectors [−2, 1]ᵀ (the null space) and [1, 1]ᵀ. Nothing exotic about the zero-eigenvalue eigenvector — it's an ordinary vector.
  • It isn't a fluke of hand-picked numbers: for the general rank-1 matrix [[a, σa], [b, σb]] the constant term of the characteristic polynomial cancels for any a, b, σ, so λ(λ − (a + σb)) = 0 and λ = 0 is always a root. The same cancellation happens for a rank-deficient 3×3.
  • Gotcha: "rank = number of non-zero eigenvalues" holds for diagonalizable matrices only. [[0, 1], [0, 0]] is rank 1 with both eigenvalues zero. The clean statement belongs to singular values, not eigenvalues (§16).

Eigenlayers: a matrix as a stack of rank-1 sheets

  • The outer product vᵢvᵢᵀ of a unit eigenvector with itself is an M×M rank-1 matrix. Summing all M of them gives nothing useful — eigenvectors carry direction but no magnitude. Weight each by its eigenvalue and the sum exactly reconstructs the matrix.
  • This is just A = VΛVᵀ written out layer by layer, so it is valid for symmetric matrices (where V⁻¹ = Vᵀ) with unit-normalised eigenvectors.
  • For a non-symmetric diagonalizable matrix the layer form still works, but the right-hand vector must be the i-th row of V⁻¹ instead of vᵢᵀ. That version needs no normalisation, because VV⁻¹ = I regardless of eigenvector magnitudes, whereas VVᵀ = I only for unit vectors.
  • Worked example: [[2, 3], [3, 2]] has λ₁ = −1, v₁ = [−1, 1]ᵀ/√2 and λ₂ = 5, v₂ = [1, 1]ᵀ/√2. Layers: A₁ = [[−0.5, 0.5], [0.5, −0.5]] and A₂ = [[2.5, 2.5], [2.5, 2.5]]. Each is rank 1; their sum is rank 2 and equals A.
  • Why anyone cares: you don't have to add all the layers. Keep the k largest-λ layers and you get a low-rank approximation; drop the layers you believe are noise and you get a denoised matrix. This is the seed of PCA and of the low-rank machinery in §16.
eigenlayers (symmetric A, unit eigenvectors)A = Σᵢ λᵢ vᵢvᵢᵀ = VΛVᵀ general diagonalizable A, with W = V⁻ᵀA = Σᵢ λᵢ vᵢwᵢᵀ = VΛV⁻¹

Powers, inverses, and shifts

  • Powers are free. In (VΛV⁻¹)ⁿ every interior V⁻¹V collapses to I, leaving VΛⁿV⁻¹ — and a diagonal matrix to the n-th power is just its entries to the n-th power. Repeated matrix multiplication becomes M scalar exponentiations.
  • Equivalently, per eigenvector: A²v = A(λv) = λ(Av) = λ²v, and by induction Aⁿv = λⁿv. Powers change the eigenvalues, never the eigenvectors. This is why |λ| < 1 directions die out and |λ| > 1 directions dominate in iterated dynamics — the basis of the power method and of Markov-chain steady states.
  • Inverses are just as easy: A⁻¹ = VΛ⁻¹V⁻¹, inverting only the diagonal. Impossible if any λ = 0 — which is exactly the singular case.
  • Is that a real shortcut, given that V still needs inverting? Yes, twice over: for symmetric A, V⁻¹ = Vᵀ for free; and because the eigenvectors are normalised, V has a low condition number (§16), so inverting it is numerically safer than inverting A directly.
  • Shifting A + sI adds s to every eigenvalue and leaves the eigenvectors untouched: (A + sI)v = Av + sv = (λ + s)v. That one line is why ridge regularization works — shifting nudges the smallest eigenvalue away from zero and makes a near-singular matrix invertible.
operations that keep the eigenvectorsAⁿ = VΛⁿV⁻¹ Aⁿv = λⁿv A⁻¹ = VΛ⁻¹V⁻¹ A⁻¹v = (1/λ)v (A + sI)v = (λ + s)v

Eigenvalues as extrema — Rayleigh, Courant–Fischer, interlacing

  • So far eigenvalues have been roots of a polynomial. There is a second, far more useful definition: for a symmetric matrix they are the extreme values of a normalized energy. Almost every practical use of eigenvalues — PCA, LDA, spectral clustering, perturbation bounds, matrix approximation — runs through this characterization rather than through det(A − λI) = 0.
  • The object is the Rayleigh quotient R(x) = xᵀAx / xᵀx for x ≠ 0, treated in full on the sibling page (§17) with an interactive contour map. It is scale-invariant (R(αx) = R(x)), so it is a function of direction only.
  • The one-line proof of the bounds. Write A = QΛQᵀ and substitute y = Qᵀx. Since Q is orthogonal, xᵀx = yᵀy, and R(x) = Σᵢ λᵢyᵢ² / Σᵢ yᵢ². The weights yᵢ²/Σyⱼ² are non-negative and sum to 1 — so the Rayleigh quotient is a weighted average (a convex combination) of the eigenvalues. An average can never escape its extremes, giving λ_min ≤ R(x) ≤ λ_max, and it hits an extreme exactly when all the weight sits on that eigenvector.
  • Deflation form (the PCA recursion). Constrain x to be orthogonal to the top h − 1 eigenvectors and the maximum drops to the next eigenvalue: λ_h = max{R(x) : x ⟂ q₁ … q_{h−1}}. Dually, λ_h = min{R(x) : x ∈ span(q₁ … q_h)}. This is the "PC2 maximizes the residual variance subject to being orthogonal to PC1" rule — it is a theorem, not a design choice.
  • Courant–Fischer min–max. The deflation form has an ugly dependence on the eigenvectors you haven't computed yet. Courant–Fischer removes it: minimize over all constraint sets of the right size.
    Courant–Fischer (A symmetric, λ₁ ≥ … ≥ λ_M)λ_h = min_{B: M×(h−1), BᵀB = I}   max_{x ≠ 0, Bᵀx = 0}   xᵀAx / xᵀx λ_h = max_{C: M×(M−h), CᵀC = I}   min_{x ≠ 0, Cᵀx = 0}   xᵀAx / xᵀx
    Read the first line as: however adversarially you cut the space down by h − 1 dimensions, some direction survives with energy at least λ_h — and the eigenvector cut is the one that does best at holding it down. The proof of "≥" is a dimension count: BᵀX_h is (h−1)×h, so it has a non-trivial null vector, so some combination of the top h eigenvectors survives the constraint, and on that span R ≥ λ_h.
  • Why it matters: the min–max form mentions no eigenvectors at all. That makes it the right tool for comparing the eigenvalues of two different matrices, which is where every perturbation and interlacing result comes from.
  • Poincaré separation. Let H be M×h with HᵀH = I_h — an orthonormal set of h directions — and compress A onto them as the h×h matrix HᵀAH. Then for i = 1 … h: λ_{M−h+i}(A) ≤ λᵢ(HᵀAH) ≤ λᵢ(A). In words: compressing a symmetric matrix onto a subspace can only pull its eigenvalues inward, and each compressed eigenvalue is trapped between the eigenvalue of the same rank counted from the top and from the bottom.
  • Cauchy interlacing is the case H = the first M−1 standard basis vectors — i.e. delete the last row and column. Writing A_{M−1} for that leading principal submatrix: λ₁(A) ≥ λ₁(A_{M−1}) ≥ λ₂(A) ≥ λ₂(A_{M−1}) ≥ … ≥ λ_{M−1}(A_{M−1}) ≥ λ_M(A). The two spectra interlace like the teeth of a zip. Deleting M − k rows and columns generalizes it to λ_{M−i+1}(A) ≤ λ_{k−i+1}(A_k) ≤ λ_{k−i+1}(A).
  • Worked interlacing: A = [[2, 1, 0], [1, 3, 1], [0, 1, 2]] factors as det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)(λ − 1)(λ − 4), so λ(A) = 4, 2, 1. Its leading 2×2 block [[2, 1], [1, 3]] has λ = (5 ± √5)/2 ≈ 3.618, 1.382. Check: 4 ≥ 3.618 ≥ 2 ≥ 1.382 ≥ 1 ✓ — the smaller spectrum sits strictly inside the larger one.
  • What it tells a practitioner. Drop a feature from a covariance matrix and the variances of the principal components can only interlace — no eigenvalue can leapfrog two positions, so PC1 cannot suddenly become tiny. Conversely, adding a feature can raise the top eigenvalue but cannot push the second one above the old first. It also bounds the eigenvalues of any submatrix by those of the whole, which is how "the largest diagonal entry is at most λ_max" (take H = eᵢ) and "λ_min ≤ aᵢᵢ ≤ λ_max" fall out.
  • Weyl's inequality — symmetric eigenvalues are perfectly conditioned. For symmetric A, B: λ_h(A) + λ_min(B) ≤ λ_h(A + B) ≤ λ_h(A) + λ_max(B). Rearranged, |λ_h(A + B) − λ_h(A)| ≤ max|λ(B)| = ‖B‖₂. So perturbing a symmetric matrix by E moves every eigenvalue by at most ‖E‖₂ — no amplification, ever, unlike the √ε blow-up of the defective case above. This is the theoretical reason symmetric eigenproblems are the easy ones, and why sample covariance eigenvalues are stable estimates even when their eigenvectors are not.
  • Monotonicity. If B is positive semidefinite then λ_h(A + B) ≥ λ_h(A) for every h, strictly if B is positive definite. Adding sI with s > 0 — ridge regularization, covariance shrinkage (§19) — therefore lifts the whole spectrum, which is exactly why it rescues a near-singular matrix.
  • Sums of the top k. Summing the deflation form gives Σ_{i≤k} λᵢ(A) = max{tr(HᵀAH) : HᵀH = I_k} — the largest total energy any k-dimensional subspace can capture, attained by the top-k eigenvectors. That single identity is the engine behind PCA's optimality and behind the Eckart–Young theorem in §16; the analogue for singular values is von Neumann's trace inequality, also in §16.

Generalized eigendecomposition

  • Note that Av = λv and Av = λIv say the same thing. Replace I with another matrix B and you get the generalized eigenvalue problem, a.k.a. simultaneous diagonalization of two matrices.
  • When B is invertible it is equivalent to the ordinary eigendecomposition of B⁻¹A. Think of B⁻¹A as a matrix-valued ratio of A to B — "which directions maximise A relative to B?"
  • Gotcha: even if A and B are both symmetric, B⁻¹A generally is not, so the symmetric guarantees (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors) do not carry over.
  • In practice, never form B⁻¹: inverting a large or ill-conditioned matrix injects numerical error. Solvers handle the pair directly (scipy.linalg.eig(A, B); numpy's eig takes only one matrix).
  • Where it earns its keep: maximise between-class over within-class scatter and you have linear discriminant analysis; maximise signal covariance over noise covariance and you have spatial filters for source separation. Both are one generalized eigendecomposition.

Simultaneous diagonalization of two symmetric matrices

  • The generalized problem Av = λBv has a much better answer than "eigendecompose B⁻¹A" — one that repairs the gotcha above. Theorem: if A is symmetric and B is positive definite, there is a single invertible C that diagonalizes both at once: CACᵀ = Λ and CBCᵀ = I, where Λ holds the eigenvalues of B⁻¹A. Those eigenvalues are real and their eigenvectors are linearly independent — the symmetric guarantees come back, they were only hidden.
  • The construction is "whiten, then rotate", and it is worth memorizing. Because B is symmetric positive definite it has a symmetric positive definite square root T = B^(1/2) = PD^(1/2)Pᵀ, so B = TT and T⁻¹ exists. The matrix T⁻¹AT⁻¹ is symmetric, so the ordinary spectral theorem applies: T⁻¹AT⁻¹ = QΛQᵀ. Set C = QᵀT⁻¹. Then CBCᵀ = QᵀT⁻¹(TT)T⁻¹Q = QᵀQ = I and CACᵀ = Qᵀ(T⁻¹AT⁻¹)Q = Λ. Equivalently, with G = C⁻¹: A = GΛGᵀ and B = GGᵀ.
  • Why B⁻¹A can be non-symmetric and still have real eigenvalues: it is similar to the symmetric matrix T⁻¹AT⁻¹ (since B⁻¹A = T⁻¹(T⁻¹AT⁻¹)T), and similarity preserves eigenvalues. The eigenvectors are related by xᵢ = T⁻¹yᵢ, so they inherit independence from the orthonormal yᵢ — but not orthogonality. What they satisfy instead is B-orthogonality: xᵢᵀBxⱼ = 0 for i ≠ j, and xᵢᵀBxᵢ = 1. They are orthonormal in the inner product defined by B rather than the Euclidean one — the whole point of "the eigenvalues of A in the metric of B".
  • The generalized Rayleigh quotient. Left-multiplying Axᵢ = λᵢBxᵢ by xᵢᵀ gives λᵢ = xᵢᵀAxᵢ / xᵢᵀBxᵢ, and the whole extremal theory transfers verbatim: λ_max(B⁻¹A) = max_x xᵀAx / xᵀBx, the deflation form uses B-orthogonality (Xᵀ_{h−1}Bx = 0), and there is a Courant–Fischer min–max. The substitution x = T⁻¹y turns it into the ordinary quotient of T⁻¹AT⁻¹, which is the proof.
  • Weaker hypotheses still work. (i) If merely some linear combination αA + βB is positive definite, a common C diagonalizing both still exists. (ii) If A and B are both merely nonnegative definite — no invertibility anywhere — a nonsingular C making both CACᵀ and CBCᵀ diagonal still exists. What you lose is the clean CBCᵀ = I normalization and the interpretation of the diagonal as eigenvalues of anything.
  • When can one orthogonal matrix do it? Exactly when the matrices commute: there is an orthogonal P with PᵀAP and PᵀBP both diagonal ⟺ AB = BA. (⟸ is a block argument: diagonalize A, and commuting forces the transformed B to be block-diagonal on A's eigenspaces, where it can be diagonalized without disturbing A.) So commuting symmetric matrices share an eigenbasis, and conversely. It extends to any family A₁ … A_k that pairwise commute. Since (AB)ᵀ = BA for symmetric factors, the test is simply "is AB symmetric?" — and the same result for commuting projection matrices is what makes the sum-of-squares decompositions of ANOVA work (§18).
  • Where this actually shows up. Whitening: take A = anything, B = C the covariance matrix; C^(−1/2)x has identity covariance, and the generalized problem becomes an ordinary one in the whitened coordinates. LDA / canonical variate analysis: A = between-class scatter, B = within-class scatter; the top generalized eigenvectors are the discriminant directions, and the analysis is literally "whiten by the within-class covariance, then do PCA on the class means". Spatial filters and source separation: A = signal covariance, B = noise covariance, maximizing the generalized quotient maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio. Generalized least squares: minimizing (y − Xβ)ᵀΩ⁻¹(y − Xβ) is ordinary least squares after whitening by Ω^(−1/2).
  • In code: scipy.linalg.eigh(A, B) solves the symmetric-definite generalized problem directly — it Cholesky-factors B = LLᵀ and reduces to the standard problem for L⁻¹AL⁻ᵀ, which is the construction above with the Cholesky factor in place of the square root (cheaper, same effect). It returns real eigenvalues and B-orthonormal eigenvectors. Never assemble B⁻¹A yourself: you would lose symmetry, double the condition number, and hand a symmetric problem to a non-symmetric solver.
