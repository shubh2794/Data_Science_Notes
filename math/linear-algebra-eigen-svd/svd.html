16 The SVD

The master decomposition: every matrix, any shape, any rank, factors into rotate → stretch → rotate. Eigendecomposition is the special case where the matrix is square and well-behaved; the SVD never fails.

What the SVD is

  • Its job is to hand you an orthonormal basis for each of the four subspaces (§08) plus a set of scalars — the singular values — that rank those basis vectors by importance.
  • A is M×N, any rank. U is M×M orthogonal and spans ℝᴹ (column space + left-null space). V is N×N orthogonal and spans ℝᴺ (row space + null space). Σ is M×N, diagonal, with real, non-negative entries sorted descending.
  • Because the decomposition uses Vᵀ, the right singular vectors are the columns of V but appear as the rows of Vᵀ — a constant source of confusion, and of transposed code.
  • Geometry: Vᵀ rotates the input into the "natural" input axes, Σ stretches axis i by σᵢ (and pads or truncates dimensions), U rotates into the output frame. The unit sphere in ℝᴺ is therefore mapped to an ellipsoid in ℝᴹ whose semi-axis lengths are the σᵢ and whose axis directions are the uᵢ.
  • Single-vector form Avᵢ = σᵢuᵢ is the SVD's answer to Av = λv: unlike eigenvectors, the input and output directions are allowed to be different vectors, which is why rectangular matrices are no obstacle.
  • Economy (thin) SVD: for a tall M×N matrix, columns N+1…M of U get multiplied by zero rows of Σ and contribute nothing to A. Dropping them gives U of size M×N and Σ of size N×N — same product, far less storage (numpy.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)). You lose the explicit basis for the left-null space.

Computing the SVD

  • A may not be square, but AᵀA and AAᵀ always are — and always symmetric, so they are always diagonalizable with real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (§15). Substitute the SVD into each and the orthogonal factors annihilate each other in the middle.
  • So: V = eigenvectors of AᵀA, U = eigenvectors of AAᵀ, and the singular values are the square roots of the shared non-zero eigenvalues. Non-negativity is immediate — they're square roots of eigenvalues of a symmetric PSD matrix; realness comes from the symmetric-matrix proof in §15.
  • You only need one of the two eigendecompositions: get V and Σ, then read off U column by column as uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ. By hand, decompose whichever of AᵀA (N×N) or AAᵀ (M×M) is smaller.
  • Sizes gotcha: ΣᵀΣ is square even when A isn't. The real Σ is M×N; take the diagonal of the square version and trim the excess rows or columns. That's why a 2×3 matrix has three eigenvalues in AᵀA but only two singular values.
  • Normalisation and sign are not optional here. In VΛV⁻¹ any stray magnitude in V is cancelled by V⁻¹, so eigenvectors are magnitude- and sign-invariant. In UΣVᵀ the two outer matrices are not inverses of each other — they may not even be the same size — so nothing cancels. Singular vectors must be unit-length, and since all σ ≥ 0, the signs of uᵢ and vᵢ must be consistent (flip one, flip the other) or UΣVᵀ ≠ A.
  • In practice, never form AᵀA. It squares the condition number and throws away precision. Real algorithms (Golub–Kahan bidiagonalization + QR iteration) work on A directly: numpy.linalg.svd returns U, a vector of singular values, and Vᵀ.
why the recipe worksAᵀA = (UΣVᵀ)ᵀ(UΣVᵀ) = VΣᵀUᵀUΣVᵀ = V Σ² Vᵀ AAᵀ = (UΣVᵀ)(UΣVᵀ)ᵀ = UΣVᵀVΣᵀUᵀ = U Σ² Uᵀ recipeσᵢ = √λᵢ(AᵀA) uᵢ = Avᵢ / σᵢ i.e. AVΣ⁻¹ = U
  • Worked check: A = [[1, 1, 0], [0, 1, 1]] ⟹ σ = √3, 1 and Σ = [[√3, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]]. Build the singular vectors as integers first if you like, but you must normalise every one of them before UΣVᵀ reproduces A.

  • Why the apparent contradiction (identical for AᵀA, unrelated for a general A)? Because both decompositions are exact, all of the matrix's "energy" must live inside the three factors. In the SVD, U and V are orthogonal — norm 1, they can store no energy — so all of it sits in Σ. In an eigendecomposition of a non-symmetric matrix, V is not orthogonal, so energy is shared between V and Λ. Symmetry is what makes the two coincide.
  • Scale-dependence: multiply the matrix by c and every singular value multiplies by |c|. The orthogonal factors can't absorb a scalar, so Σ must. Raw singular values are therefore not comparable across matrices — normalise them first (below).

SVD of a symmetric matrix

  • Write the SVD of A and of Aᵀ: A = UΣVᵀ and Aᵀ = VΣUᵀ. If A = Aᵀ these must agree, forcing the left and right singular vectors to coincide up to sign.
  • Precisely: with the eigendecomposition A = QΛQᵀ, the SVD is U = Q, Σ = |Λ|, V = Q·sign(Λ). If A is positive semi-definite all signs are + and U = V = Q exactly: A = UΣUᵀ. Negative eigenvalues flip the corresponding column of V.
  • This is why covariance matrices, Gram matrices XᵀX, and kernel matrices behave so nicely: PSD and symmetric, so eigen and singular decompositions are literally the same object.

The SVD and the four subspaces

  • Split U and V at column r = rank(A) and all four subspaces fall out with orthonormal bases — the single most useful thing the SVD does.
  • The logic is Avᵢ = σᵢuᵢ: if σᵢ ≠ 0 the right-hand side is a non-zero vector reachable from the columns of A, so uᵢ ∈ C(A). If σᵢ = 0 the right-hand side is the zeros vector, so uᵢ ∈ N(Aᵀ). Run the same argument on uᵢᵀA = σᵢvᵢᵀ for the row space and null space.
  • Orthogonality between the pairs is automatic: any subset of columns of an orthogonal matrix is orthogonal to any non-overlapping subset. U spans all of ℝᴹ and V all of ℝᴺ, even when A is rank-deficient — unlike eigenvectors, which can fail to span anything (defective matrices, §15).
  • The ordering is not automatic: computed by hand, singular values emerge in no particular order. Software sorts Σ descending and permutes the columns of U and V to match.

Rank — and "effective" rank

  • rank(A) = the number of non-zero singular values. Unlike the eigenvalue version of this claim (§15), this one is always true. It is literally how software computes rank.
  • Two arguments. (1) From the subspace reading above: a non-zero σ contributes a basis vector to the column space, a zero σ contributes one to the left-null space. (2) From rank rules: rank(ABC) ≤ min of the ranks, U and V are full-rank by construction, so the rank of the product is pinned to rank(Σ) — which for a diagonal matrix is its count of non-zero entries.
  • Effective rank: a computer can't tell "small but non-zero" from "zero plus rounding error". The recipe is rank = count(σ > tol) with a tolerance scaled to the matrix: roughly max(M, N) × eps(σ_max), where eps is the spacing between representable floats near that value. Too small a tolerance over-estimates rank; too large under-estimates it.
  • A tiny-but-non-zero σ means near rank-deficiency — technically invertible, practically not. That is the same phenomenon as multicollinearity in least-squares (§14).

Spectral theory: a matrix as a stack of ranked layers

  • Every "spectral theory" says the same thing: a complicated object is a sum of simple ones, the way white light is a sum of colours. Here, every matrix is a sum of rank-1 matrices, and the SVD supplies the best possible set of them.
  • Rewrite UΣVᵀ as a sum of outer products: layer i is uᵢσᵢvᵢᵀ, an M×N rank-1 matrix. Layers past r are multiplied by zero and can be dropped.
  • Contrast with plain layer-wise matrix multiplication (§06), where each layer is defined by the accident of a column's position. Rows and columns of a data matrix can usually be shuffled without changing the information; the SVD instead ranks layers by how much of the matrix they actually explain.
  • Each uᵢvᵢᵀ is a pure direction — both vectors are unit length, so the outer product has norm 1. The singular value is the magnitude attached to that direction. The signpost points; the number on it tells you how far.
  • Layer 1 typically captures the most prominent structure in the matrix, layer 2 the next, and so on — with the tail layers contributing changes you can barely see.

Matrix norms — and why they are all functions of the singular values

  • To say a matrix is "big", or that one matrix is "close to" another, you need a norm. A matrix norm is a vector norm on the M×N entries plus one extra axiom: submultiplicativity, ‖AB‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖B‖. That extra condition is what lets you bound products, powers and iterations, and it is why not every vector norm on the flattened entries qualifies.
  • Induced (operator) norms. Given any vector norm, ‖A‖ = max_{‖x‖=1} ‖Ax‖ is a matrix norm — the maximum stretch the matrix applies to any unit vector. Submultiplicativity is immediate from ‖Ay‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖y‖, and ‖I‖ = 1 for every induced norm. The three you meet: ‖A‖₁ = max absolute column sum, ‖A‖_∞ = max absolute row sum, and ‖A‖₂ = σ_max.
  • Why ‖A‖₂ = σ_max. Substitute the SVD and set y = Vᵀx. Orthogonal matrices preserve length, so ‖Ax‖₂ = ‖UΣVᵀx‖₂ = ‖Σy‖₂ = √(Σᵢ σᵢ²yᵢ²) with ‖y‖ = 1. That is a weighted average of the σᵢ² — maximized at σ₁², attained at y = e₁, i.e. x = v₁. It is the circle→ellipse picture in one equation: the longest semi-axis is the operator norm. Same argument at the other end gives min_{‖x‖=1} ‖Ax‖₂ = σ_min.
  • Frobenius ‖A‖_F = √(Σᵢⱼ aᵢⱼ²) = √(tr(AᵀA)) = √(Σᵢ σᵢ²) — the Euclidean length of the flattened matrix. It is submultiplicative, so it is a legitimate matrix norm, but it is not induced by any vector norm: ‖I_M‖_F = √M ≠ 1. It is the only one of these that comes from an inner product, ⟨A, B⟩ = tr(AᵀB), which is why least-squares-flavoured matrix problems almost always use it.
  • Nuclear (trace) norm ‖A‖_* = Σᵢ σᵢ. Together these are the Schatten p-norms (Σσᵢᵖ)^(1/p): p = 1 nuclear, p = 2 Frobenius, p = ∞ spectral. They satisfy the same ordering as the vector p-norms applied to the singular-value vector, so σ_max ≤ ‖A‖_F ≤ ‖A‖_* ≤ √r · ‖A‖_F ≤ r · σ_max.
  • Unitary invariance is the deep property. A norm is unitarily invariant if ‖UAV‖ = ‖A‖ for all orthogonal/unitary U, V — rotating the input frame or the output frame changes nothing. Spectral, Frobenius, nuclear and every Schatten norm are unitarily invariant; the column-sum and row-sum norms are not. A theorem of von Neumann says a unitarily invariant norm is exactly a symmetric gauge function of the singular values: it can only depend on (σ₁, …, σ_r), and symmetrically at that. So the SVD is not just one way to measure a matrix — it is the complete list of ingredients any rotation-blind measurement can use. This is precisely why the Eckart–Young theorem below holds for all of them at once.
  • The spectral radius ρ(A) = max|λᵢ| is not a norm — [[0, 1], [0, 0]] has ρ = 0 but is not the zeros matrix, breaking "‖A‖ = 0 ⟹ A = 0". But it is bounded by every one of them: ρ(A) ≤ ‖A‖ for any matrix norm (apply ‖Ax1ᵀ‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖x1ᵀ‖ to a dominant eigenvector), and conversely, for any ε > 0 there is some matrix norm with ‖A‖ < ρ(A) + ε (rescale the Schur form by diag(c, c², …) to shrink the off-diagonal). So ρ is the infimum of all norms.
  • Consequence you will actually use: Aᵏ → 0 as k → ∞ ⟺ ρ(A) < 1, and then (I − A)⁻¹ = I + A + A² + … converges. ‖A‖ < 1 in any norm is sufficient but not necessary — the nilpotent example above has ‖A‖₂ = 1 yet A² = 0. This is the stability criterion for linear dynamical systems, Markov chains, iterative solvers, and the vanishing/exploding gradient of a recurrent network: ρ of the recurrent weight matrix decides whether the signal dies or blows up, while σ_max decides how badly it can transiently amplify along the way.
  • For symmetric matrices the two collapse: σᵢ = |λᵢ|, so ‖A‖₂ = ρ(A) = max|λᵢ| and ‖A‖_F = √(Σλᵢ²). That equality is another way to say symmetric matrices are the well-behaved ones.
  • Von Neumann's trace inequality is the Cauchy–Schwarz of matrix analysis: |tr(AᵀB)| ≤ Σᵢ σᵢ(A)·σᵢ(B), with equality exactly when A and B share singular vectors in matching order. Since tr(AᵀB) is the Frobenius inner product, this says two matrices are maximally aligned when their singular directions line up. It is the engine behind the low-rank theorem below, behind orthogonal Procrustes, and behind the fact that max{tr(AU) : U orthogonal} = Σσᵢ(A) = ‖A‖_*.

Low-rank approximation

  • Truncate the sum at k < r and you get Ã, a rank-k stand-in for A. A rank-30 smoothed data matrix can be visually indistinguishable from its rank-4 approximation.
  • Eckart–Young: this truncation is not merely a good rank-k approximation, it is the best one — no rank-k matrix is closer to A in either the Frobenius norm or the spectral (2-)norm. The errors are exactly the discarded singular values.
  • Choosing k, method 1 — the scree plot. Plot the singular values descending and look for the "elbow" where they flatten out. Quick, visual, and genuinely ambiguous: two people can reasonably disagree by a component or two.
  • Method 2 — a variance threshold. Normalise the singular values (next block) and keep every layer contributing at least, say, 1% (or 0.1% to retain more detail). Still a judgement call, but reproducible.
  • Why bother: (1) Denoising — if signal lives in the large singular values and noise in the small ones, truncation "projects out" the noise. (2) Feature extraction for ML — train on the top-k singular vectors instead of raw columns: faster, often more accurate, and the core of PCA (§19). (3) Compression — a 10,000 × 100,000 float matrix is ~8 GB; if it is rank 100, storing 100 columns of U, 100 of V and 100 scalars costs under 0.1 GB. Image compression is the same trick on a pixel matrix.
best rank-k approximation (Eckart–Young)Ã_k = Σ_(i=1..k) σᵢ uᵢ vᵢᵀ ‖A − Ã_k‖₂ = σ_(k+1) ‖A − Ã_k‖_F = √( Σ_(i>k) σᵢ² )

Eckart–Young–Mirsky, stated and proved

  • The truncation above is not merely convenient — it is provably optimal, and it is optimal in a very strong sense. Theorem (Eckart–Young 1936, Mirsky 1960): let A have rank r and singular value decomposition A = Σ_(i=1..r) σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ, and let Ã_k be the sum truncated at k < r. Then for every unitarily invariant norm, Ã_k minimizes ‖A − X‖ over all matrices X of rank at most k — not just over truncations, not just over matrices built from A's own singular vectors, but over the entire set of rank-k matrices in ℝ^(M×N).
  • Because it holds for every unitarily invariant norm at once, the two headline cases come free: ‖A − Ã_k‖₂ = σ_(k+1) and ‖A − Ã_k‖_F = √(Σ_(i>k) σᵢ²). The error is exactly the discarded spectrum. There is no separate theorem for each norm and no possibility that a different rank-k matrix wins on Frobenius while losing on spectral — the same Ã_k wins everywhere.
  • Proof, Frobenius case — worth reading once, because it is where the extremal theory of §15 cashes out. Any rank-k matrix factors as X = QY with Q an M×k matrix of orthonormal columns. Expand the squared norm, complete the square in Y, and permute inside the trace:
    ‖A − QY‖²_F = tr(AᵀA) + tr((Y − QᵀA)ᵀ(Y − QᵀA)) − tr(QᵀAAᵀQ) ≥ tr(AᵀA) − tr(QᵀAAᵀQ) (middle term is a Gram matrix, ≥ 0, killed by Y = QᵀA)
    The first term is Σᵢ σᵢ². The second is the top-k trace problem for the symmetric matrix AAᵀ, whose eigenvalues are the σᵢ² — and Ky Fan's identity says tr(QᵀAAᵀQ) ≤ Σ_(i≤k) σᵢ², with equality when Q = [u₁ … u_k]. Hence ‖A − X‖²_F ≥ Σ_(i>k) σᵢ², and direct expansion shows Ã_k attains it. The best rank-k approximation problem and the "maximize variance in a k-dimensional subspace" problem are the same problem — which is exactly why PCA and truncated SVD are the same algorithm.
  • Proof sketch, spectral case (Mirsky's route, which covers every unitarily invariant norm at once): for any rank-k matrix B one shows σᵢ(A − B) ≥ σ_(i+k)(A) — the residual A − B is always at least as "big", singular value by singular value, as what truncation leaves behind. Since a unitarily invariant norm is a monotone symmetric function of the singular values, that inequality upgrades to a statement about every such norm simultaneously. The i = 1 case is the spectral statement ‖A − B‖₂ ≥ σ_(k+1).
  • Uniqueness. Ã_k is the unique minimizer iff σ_k > σ_(k+1). If the spectrum is tied at the cut point, any orthonormal choice inside the tied singular subspace does equally well — the same degeneracy that makes PCA loadings unstable when two eigenvalues are near-equal. Truncating in the middle of a tie is a modelling smell.
  • What the theorem does not say. Optimality is relative to a unitarily invariant norm on a fully observed matrix. Change either and the truncated SVD stops being optimal: (i) weighted or missing-entry losses (recommender systems with unobserved ratings) are not unitarily invariant — you need alternating least squares or gradient methods; (ii) constrained factorizations (non-negative matrix factorization, sparse PCA, binary factors) live outside the feasible set the theorem optimizes over; (iii) if you care about a downstream loss rather than reconstruction error, the top-k directions need not be the informative ones (see the PCA caveats in §19).
  • Rank is discrete; the nuclear norm is its convex hull. "Find the lowest-rank matrix fitting my data" is NP-hard in general, but ‖·‖_* is the tightest convex relaxation of rank(·) on the spectral-norm ball — the same relationship ℓ¹ has to ℓ⁰ for sparsity. That is why matrix completion and robust PCA minimize the nuclear norm, and why the proximal operator of λ‖·‖_* is singular value soft-thresholding σᵢ ↦ max(σᵢ − λ, 0) — a smooth cousin of hard truncation that shrinks every layer instead of deleting a suffix.
  • Worked example. A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]]. Then AᵀA = [[25, 20], [20, 25]] with eigenvalues 45, 5, so σ₁ = √45 ≈ 6.708, σ₂ = √5 ≈ 2.236, v₁ = [1, 1]ᵀ/√2, v₂ = [1, −1]ᵀ/√2, u₁ = [1, 3]ᵀ/√10, u₂ = [3, −1]ᵀ/√10. The best rank-1 approximation is Ã₁ = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ = 1.5·[[1, 1], [3, 3]] = [[1.5, 1.5], [4.5, 4.5]], so A − Ã₁ = [[1.5, −1.5], [−0.5, 0.5]]. Its Frobenius norm is √(2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25) = √5 = σ₂ ✓, and being rank 1 its spectral norm is the same √5 = σ₂ ✓. No other rank-1 matrix on Earth gets closer.

Normalizing singular values

  • Is a largest singular value of 8 big? Unanswerable — σ scales with the numbers in the matrix. 10A has singular values 10σ. So convert to a share of the total.
  • The sum of all singular values is the nuclear norm (Schatten 1-norm) and can be read as the total "energy" or variance in the matrix. Dividing each σᵢ by that sum gives a percentage that is comparable across matrices of different scales.
  • For variance-explained in the PCA sense, use the squared singular values, σᵢ²/Σσⱼ² — because σ² are the eigenvalues of the covariance-like matrix AᵀA. (The Schatten-2 norm, √Σσᵢ², is just the Frobenius norm.)
  • Interpretation: a normalised top singular value of 82% says one component explains nearly everything — a simple matrix, maybe one pattern plus noise. A top value of 35% says the information is spread across several directions — a genuinely complicated matrix.
normalised singular values‖A‖_p = ( Σ_(i=1..r) σᵢᵖ )^(1/p) (Schatten p-norm; p=1 nuclear, p=2 Frobenius) σ̃ᵢ = 100·σᵢ / Σⱼ σⱼ variance explained: σᵢ² / Σⱼ σⱼ²

Condition number

  • The condition number κ measures the "spread" of a matrix — how differently it stretches its most- and least-favoured directions. In the circle→ellipse picture it is simply the ellipse's aspect ratio.
  • κ(I) = 1. In fact every orthogonal matrix has κ = 1, because QᵀQ = I means all its singular values are 1 — a rotation stretches nothing.
  • κ = ∞ (or undefined) for a singular matrix, since σ_min = 0. There's no universal cutoff for "ill-conditioned"; κ > 10⁴ is a common rule of thumb but it is application-specific.
  • What it predicts: sensitivity to perturbation. A high κ means small changes in the input (noise, rounding) produce large changes in anything that involves an inverse — regression coefficients, solutions of linear systems. Roughly, you lose log₁₀ κ digits of accuracy.
  • Don't over-read it. A matrix can carry enormous information and still be singular (κ undefined), or be near-singular and still be exactly the matrix you want. κ is a stability warning, not a quality score.
condition numberκ = σ_max / σ_min κ(Q) = 1 for orthogonal Q κ = ∞ for singular A

What the condition number really measures

  • Definition, properly: κ(A) = ‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖ in whatever norm you chose. In the spectral norm that is σ_max·(1/σ_min) = σ_max/σ_min, which is the formula above — the aspect ratio of the ellipse. Other norms give other condition numbers (κ₁, κ_∞) that differ by at most a factor of M, which is why nobody worries much about which one is quoted.
  • The bound it certifies. Solve Ax = b and perturb the right-hand side to b + δb. Then δx = A⁻¹δb, so ‖δx‖ ≤ ‖A⁻¹‖‖δb‖, and since ‖b‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖x‖:
    ‖δx‖ / ‖x‖ ≤ κ(A) · ‖δb‖ / ‖b‖ and the same bound holds for a perturbation of A itself, to first order
    Relative input error times κ = relative output error. With κ = 10⁸ and inputs good to 16 digits, the answer is good to 8. That is the "you lose log₁₀κ digits" rule, now with a proof.
  • The most useful reframing: 1/κ is the relative distance to singularity. Apply Eckart–Young at k = M − 1: the smallest perturbation that makes A rank-deficient has spectral norm exactly σ_min. So
    min{ ‖E‖₂ : A + E singular } = σ_min ⟹ min{ ‖E‖₂/‖A‖₂ : A + E singular } = σ_min/σ_max = 1/κ(A)
    A matrix with κ = 10¹⁰ is one part in 10¹⁰ away from being singular. It is not "almost singular" as a vague feeling — it is a measured distance, and the low-rank approximation theorem is what measures it.
  • Squaring is the classic own-goal. κ₂(AᵀA) = κ₂(A)², because the singular values square. A design matrix with κ = 10⁸ — entirely ordinary for correlated features — produces normal equations with κ = 10¹⁶, at which point double precision has nothing left. This is the formula-level reason to solve least-squares by QR or SVD on A rather than by forming AᵀA (§14), and it is the same reason to compute the SVD from A rather than from AᵀA, and to run PCA via svd(X) rather than eigh(XᵀX) when the data are ill-conditioned.
  • Ridge regularization, read off the spectrum. Shifting to AᵀA + λI maps σᵢ² ↦ σᵢ² + λ, so κ(AᵀA + λI) = (σ₁² + λ)/(σ_r² + λ), which decreases monotonically from κ(A)² at λ = 0 toward 1 as λ → ∞. The ridge estimator in SVD coordinates is β̂ = Σᵢ [σᵢ/(σᵢ² + λ)]·(uᵢᵀy)·vᵢ: directions with large σᵢ are barely touched, while directions with σᵢ² ≪ λ are shrunk by roughly σᵢ²/λ. Ridge does not shrink coefficients uniformly — it shrinks the badly determined directions. Compare truncated-SVD regression, which applies a hard 0/1 filter to the same coefficients; ridge is the soft version of the same idea.
  • Sanity limits. κ ≥ 1 always (the max stretch is at least the min stretch), with equality exactly for scalar multiples of orthogonal matrices. κ is invariant under multiplication by orthogonal matrices and under overall scaling — κ(cA) = κ(A) — which is what makes it a relative measure. And κ(A) = κ(A⁻¹): an ill-conditioned matrix has an ill-conditioned inverse.
  • Diagnose before you regularize. A large κ can often be fixed by column scaling alone: a design matrix holding a variable in metres alongside one in nanometres is ill-conditioned for a trivial reason. Standardize the columns first, then look at κ again; whatever is left is genuine collinearity. (This is the same argument as covariance- vs. correlation-based PCA in §18.) In code: numpy.linalg.cond(A) defaults to the 2-norm and computes it from the SVD.

The inverse and the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse

  • For a square full-rank matrix, inverting the SVD is nearly free: reverse the order, transpose the orthogonal factors (their inverses are their transposes), and reciprocate the diagonal.
  • This is where instability comes from. If some σ ≈ 10⁻¹⁵, then 1/σ ≈ 10¹⁵ and that one layer swamps everything. The explicit inverse of an ill-conditioned matrix amplifies noise by exactly κ — a formula-level explanation of why numerical people say "never invert a matrix".
  • It also proves that the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric: A = VΣVᵀ ⟹ A⁻¹ = VΣ⁻¹Vᵀ, same singular vectors, still a diagonal core.
  • The pseudoinverse (§12, part 1) is the same procedure with one change: invert only the non-zero singular values, leave the zeros as zeros, and transpose the result to fix the shape. This works for any matrix — square or rectangular, full-rank or singular.
  • When A is square and full-rank, A⁺ = A⁻¹ exactly. When A is tall with full column rank, A⁺ equals the left inverse (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ — substitute the SVD and everything collapses to VΣ⁻¹Uᵀ. Same story for the right inverse of a wide matrix. So the one-sided inverses need no explicit (AᵀA)⁻¹.
  • Least-squares, finished: β = A⁺y always minimises ‖Aβ − y‖, and when there are infinitely many minimisers it returns the one with the smallest norm. This is the clean answer to the rank-deficient case that broke §14's normal equations.
  • In code: numpy.linalg.pinv implements exactly this, thresholding singular values below rcond × σ_max to zero so that near-zero values don't explode. numpy.linalg.lstsq uses the same machinery for regression.

The polar decomposition — pulling the rotation out

  • Insert VᵀV = I into the middle of the SVD and regroup: A = UΣVᵀ = (UVᵀ)(VΣVᵀ). The first factor W = UVᵀ is orthogonal (a product of orthogonal matrices); the second P = VΣVᵀ is symmetric positive semidefinite with the same eigenvalues as Σ. That is the polar decomposition A = WP — the matrix analogue of writing a complex number as r·e^(iθ): a pure rotation/reflection times a pure stretch.
  • Grouping the other way gives the left-handed version A = (UΣUᵀ)(UVᵀ) = P'W with P' = UΣUᵀ = √(AAᵀ), while P = √(AᵀA). P is always unique; W is unique when A is invertible.
  • The nearest orthogonal matrix. Of all orthogonal matrices, the one closest to A in any unitarily invariant norm is exactly W = UVᵀ — the orthogonal factor of the polar decomposition. Equivalently max{tr(AᵀQ) : QᵀQ = I} = Σσᵢ = ‖A‖_*, attained at Q = UVᵀ, which is von Neumann's trace inequality with equality. This is the orthogonal Procrustes problem: to find the rotation best aligning point cloud X with point cloud Y, take the SVD of YᵀX and use UVᵀ.
  • Where you meet it: rigid-body registration and shape analysis (Procrustes alignment), aligning two word-embedding spaces across languages, computing rotations from noisy sensor data (Kabsch algorithm), enforcing orthogonality on a neural network's weight matrix by projecting it back after each step, and optimizers that precondition a gradient by replacing it with its own orthogonal factor — all of which are "run an SVD, throw away Σ, keep UVᵀ".
  • Note what discarding Σ means: UVᵀ is the matrix that does A's rotation with every singular value forced to 1 — all directional bias removed, all anisotropy flattened. It is the most aggressive form of conditioning possible, since κ(UVᵀ) = 1 by construction.
