13 Projections & orthogonalization

Drop a vector onto a subspace and keep the closest point. One geometric idea — "minimum distance means perpendicular" — generates the left inverse, least-squares, Gram-Schmidt, and QR.

Projection onto a line in ℝ²

  • Setup: a vector a spanning a line, a point b off it. Find the scalar β so that βa is as close to b as possible without leaving the line.
  • The geometry hands you the algebra. The connector b − βa and the line form a right triangle in which the connector is a leg and the distance from b to any other point on the line is a hypotenuse. A hypotenuse always exceeds a leg — so the shortest connector is the perpendicular one.
  • Perpendicular means the dot product vanishes: aᵀ(b − βa) = 0. Expand and solve; dividing by aᵀa is legal because it's a scalar.
  • Read the formula as mapping over magnitude: how much of b aligns with a, normalised by a's squared length. Correlation, convolution, and normalisation all wear this same shape.
  • β can be negative — the point projects "backwards" along a. It is 0 exactly when b ⟂ a, in which case the line explains nothing about b.
  • Mnemonic for the notation proj_a(b): the subspace goes in the subscript.
derivationaᵀ(b − βa) = 0 ⟹ aᵀb − β aᵀa = 0 ⟹ β = aᵀb / aᵀa proj_a(b) = (aᵀb / aᵀa) · a error e = b − proj_a(b), e ⟂ a examplea = [−2, −1]ᵀ, b = [3, −1]ᵀ ⟹ β = (−6 + 1)/(4 + 1) = −1 ⟹ p = [2, 1]ᵀ

Projection onto a subspace in ℝᴺ

  • Same derivation, promoted: the line a becomes a matrix A whose columns span a subspace, and the scalar β becomes a coefficient vector x. Orthogonality now means the error is perpendicular to every column: Aᵀ(b − Ax) = 0.
  • That rearranges to the normal equations AᵀA x = Aᵀb. We can't divide by AᵀA — but we can left-multiply by its inverse, and out falls the left inverse from §12. Two topics with unrelated motivations, one formula.
  • Validity condition: AᵀA must be invertible, i.e. A is square full rank or tall with full column rank. Linearly dependent columns ⟹ singular AᵀA ⟹ the projection coefficients aren't unique (the projected point still is).
  • If b already lies in C(A), the error is the zero vector and Ax = b exactly. If A is square and full rank, C(A) is the whole space, and the formula collapses: (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ = A⁻¹A⁻ᵀAᵀ = A⁻¹.
  • The map b ↦ Ax is the projection matrix P. It is symmetric (Pᵀ = P) and idempotent (P² = P — projecting something already in the subspace changes nothing). Its rank equals dim C(A), and I − P projects onto the orthogonal complement (the error).
  • Computationally: never form (AᵀA)⁻¹ — use np.linalg.lstsq, which factorizes instead (and is far better conditioned, since forming AᵀA squares the condition number).
projection onto the column space of AAᵀ(b − Ax) = 0 ⟹ AᵀA x = Aᵀb ⟹ x = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ b ← the left inverse p = Ax = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ b = P b P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ Pᵀ = P, P² = P if A has orthonormal columns (AᵀA = I): P = AAᵀ ← no inverse needed at all

Parallel and perpendicular components

  • Any vector w splits uniquely relative to a reference v into a piece along v and a piece perpendicular to it. The parallel piece is just the projection; the perpendicular piece is whatever's left over.
  • The two pieces really are orthogonal — take their dot product, note that wᵀv and vᵀv are scalars, and the two terms cancel identically.
  • This is the atom of Gram-Schmidt, of removing a confound from a signal, and of "residualizing" one regressor against another.
decomposition of w relative to vw = w∥ᵥ + w⟂ᵥ w∥ᵥ = (vᵀw / vᵀv) · v w⟂ᵥ = w − w∥ᵥ proof of orthogonality(w∥ᵥ)ᵀ(w⟂ᵥ) = (wᵀv/vᵀv)·vᵀw − (wᵀv/vᵀv)²·vᵀv = (wᵀv)²/vᵀv − (wᵀv)²/vᵀv = 0 example: decompose v = [2, 3]ᵀ relative to w = [4, 0]ᵀv∥w = (8/16)·[4, 0]ᵀ = [2, 0]ᵀ v⟂w = [2, 3]ᵀ − [2, 0]ᵀ = [0, 3]ᵀ (visibly orthogonal, and they sum to v)

Projections from an orthonormal basis

  • The decomposition theorem. Let S be an r-dimensional subspace of ℝᵐ. Every x ∈ ℝᵐ splits uniquely as x = u + v with u ∈ S and v orthogonal to every vector in S. The proof is a one-liner once you have an orthonormal basis: extend {z₁, …, z_r} for S to an orthonormal basis {z₁, …, z_m} for all of ℝᵐ, write x = ∑ αᵢzᵢ, and cut the sum in two. The first r terms are u, the rest are v.
  • Orthonormality makes the coordinates free. For a general basis, finding the αᵢ in x = ∑ αᵢzᵢ means solving a linear system. For an orthonormal basis, hit both sides with zᵢᵀ and every term but one dies: αᵢ = zᵢᵀx. A dot product per coordinate, no system to solve. This is the reason orthonormal bases are worth the trouble of constructing.
  • Stack the basis as the columns of an m×r matrix Z₁. Then u = Z₁Z₁ᵀx, so the projection matrix is P_S = Z₁Z₁ᵀ — no inverse anywhere. The complementary basis gives P_{S⊥} = Z₂Z₂ᵀ, and since ZZᵀ = Iₘ for the full basis, P_{S⊥} = I − P_S.
  • The projection matrix does not depend on which orthonormal basis you chose, even though the basis itself is wildly non-unique. Proof: if W₁ is another orthonormal basis for the same S, then W₁ = Z₁P for some r×r P, and I = W₁ᵀW₁ = PᵀZ₁ᵀZ₁P = PᵀP, so P is orthogonal and W₁W₁ᵀ = Z₁PPᵀZ₁ᵀ = Z₁Z₁ᵀ. The subspace determines the projection; the basis is scaffolding.
  • Why the projection is the closest point, in two lines. Take any other y ∈ S. Then u − y ∈ S and x − u = v ⟂ S, so (x − u)ᵀ(u − y) = 0 and Pythagoras applies: ‖x − y‖² = ‖x − u‖² + ‖u − y‖² ≥ ‖x − u‖². Equality only when y = u. That is the entire content of least-squares, and it needed no calculus.
  • Gram-Schmidt, re-read as repeated projection. The (i+1)-th orthogonal vector is yᵢ₊₁ = (I − Z₍ᵢ₎Z₍ᵢ₎ᵀ)xᵢ₊₁ — the projection of the next raw vector onto the orthogonal complement of what you have built so far. The algorithm is not "subtract some dot products"; it is "project onto what is left".
orthonormal-basis projectionx = u + v, u ∈ S, v ∈ S⊥ unique αᵢ = zᵢᵀx u = Z₁Z₁ᵀx = P_S x v = (I − Z₁Z₁ᵀ)x = P_{S⊥} x P_S + P_{S⊥} = I closest point, by Pythagoras‖x − y‖² = ‖x − u‖² + ‖u − y‖² ≥ ‖x − u‖² for every y ∈ S, equality iff y = u Gram-Schmidt as projection onto the complementyᵢ₊₁ = (Iₘ − Z₍ᵢ₎Z₍ᵢ₎ᵀ) xᵢ₊₁ Z₍ᵢ₎ = [z₁ … zᵢ]

Projection matrices, characterized

  • Every orthogonal projection matrix is symmetric and idempotent. Immediate from P = Z₁Z₁ᵀ: transposing gives itself, and P² = Z₁(Z₁ᵀZ₁)Z₁ᵀ = Z₁I_rZ₁ᵀ = P.
  • And the converse holds — that is the useful half. If P is m×m, symmetric, idempotent and of rank r, then P is the projection matrix of some r-dimensional subspace. Sketch: take a rank factorization P = FG with F of full column rank r and G of full row rank r. Idempotence gives FGFG = FG; cancel the invertible FᵀF on the left and GGᵀ on the right to get GF = I_r. Symmetry then forces G = (FᵀF)⁻¹Fᵀ, so P = F(FᵀF)⁻¹Fᵀ — the projector onto C(F).
  • So "symmetric and idempotent" is a complete test. You never need to produce the subspace to know you have a projector; and once you know you have one, its subspace is just C(P).
  • Corollaries you use constantly. The eigenvalues of a projection matrix are only 0 and 1 (from λ² = λ), so trace(P) = rank(P) = dim S — a rank you can read by summing a diagonal, with no elimination and no SVD. And ‖P‖₂ = 1: an orthogonal projection can only shorten a vector, never lengthen it.
  • Reading the subspace off a projector. Given a symmetric idempotent P, the columns Pe₁, Pe₂, … all lie in S; pick rank(P) independent ones and you have a basis.
characterizationP is an orthogonal projection matrix ⟺ Pᵀ = P and P² = P eigenvalues ∈ {0, 1} trace(P) = rank(P) = dim S ‖P‖₂ = 1 ‖Px‖ ≤ ‖x‖ worked: is this a projector, and onto what?P = (1/6)·[[5, −1, 2], [−1, 5, 2], [2, 2, 2]] Pᵀ = P ✓ P² = P ✓ trace = (5 + 5 + 2)/6 = 2 ⟹ it projects ℝ³ onto a plane basis for S: Pe₁ = (5, −1, 2)ᵀ/6, Pe₂ = (−1, 5, 2)ᵀ/6 (the third column is their average — dependent, as rank 2 requires) worked: the projector of a 4×3 basisX₁ = [[1, 1, 3], [1, −2, 1], [1, 1, 1], [1, −2, −1]] P = X₁(X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹X₁ᵀ = ¼·[[3, 1, 1, −1], [1, 3, −1, 1], [1, −1, 3, 1], [−1, 1, 1, 3]] x = [1, −1, 2, 1]ᵀ u = Px = ¼·[3, −3, 9, 3]ᵀ v = (I − P)x = ¼·[1, −1, −1, 1]ᵀ u + v = x ✓, uᵀv = 0 ✓

Projecting with an arbitrary basis — and with a rank-deficient one

  • You rarely start with an orthonormal basis. Suppose the columns of X₁ merely span S and are independent. Any orthonormalisation is Z₁ = X₁A for some invertible r×r matrix A with AᵀX₁ᵀX₁A = I — i.e. AAᵀ = (X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹, so A is a square root of (X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹. Substituting into P = Z₁Z₁ᵀ gives P = X₁AAᵀX₁ᵀ = X₁(X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹X₁ᵀ, and the arbitrary A vanishes.
  • That is a genuinely nice result: the projector formula X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ is not a separate construction from Z₁Z₁ᵀ — it is the same construction with the orthonormalisation left un-performed. Which orthonormalisation you would have used cancels out.
  • Gram-Schmidt is one particular choice of A. In matrix form the orthogonalisation is Y₁ = X₁T with T unit upper triangular, and the normalisation is Z₁ = X₁TD⁻¹. So A = TD⁻¹ is the upper triangular square root of (X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹ with positive diagonal — which is exactly what makes Gram-Schmidt produce the QR factorization rather than some other orthonormalisation.
  • What if the columns are dependent? Then XᵀX is singular and the inverse is illegal — but the projection still exists, because the subspace is perfectly well defined. Replace the inverse with any generalized inverse: P = X(XᵀX)⁻Xᵀ. Three facts make this work, and they are worth stating precisely:
    • X(XᵀX)⁻XᵀX = X — so (XᵀX)⁻Xᵀ is a genuine generalized inverse of X;
    • X(XᵀX)⁻Xᵀ is invariant to which generalized inverse you pick (it always equals XX⁺);
    • X(XᵀX)⁻Xᵀ is symmetric even when (XᵀX)⁻ is not.
    So the projected point ŷ — and therefore every fitted value and every residual — is unique even when the coefficients that produce it are not. Hold on to that: it is the entire story of rank-deficient regression in §14.
an arbitrary basis gives the same projectorZ₁ = X₁A, AAᵀ = (X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹ ⟹ P = Z₁Z₁ᵀ = X₁AAᵀX₁ᵀ = X₁(X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹X₁ᵀ Gram-Schmidt = the choice of A that is upper triangular with positive diagonal = QR rank-deficient basis: use any generalized inverseP = X(XᵀX)⁻Xᵀ = XX⁺ independent of the choice of (XᵀX)⁻ and symmetric regardless

Oblique projections

  • Everything so far has been orthogonal projection: split ℝᵐ = S ⊕ S⊥ and drop the perpendicular. But the split does not have to be perpendicular. Take any two subspaces S₁, S₂ with S₁ ∩ S₂ = {0} and S₁ ⊕ S₂ = ℝᵐ. Every x still decomposes uniquely as x = x₁ + x₂; x₁ is then the projection of x onto S₁ along S₂.
  • Geometrically: instead of dropping a perpendicular onto the target line, you slide along the direction of S₂ until you hit it. Same target subspace, different landing point.
  • Why it is still a projection. Change coordinates by X = [X₁ X₂] (bases of the two subspaces side by side, necessarily invertible). In the new coordinates the two subspaces become the first m₁ and last m₂ coordinate axes — which are orthogonal. So an oblique projection is an orthogonal projection viewed in a skewed coordinate system, and the projector is P_{S₁|S₂} = X[[I, 0], [0, 0]]X⁻¹.
  • Idempotent yes, symmetric no. P² = P falls straight out of the block form. But symmetry holds only when X₁ᵀX₂ = 0, i.e. only when the two subspaces are actually orthogonal, in which case the formula collapses back to X₁(X₁ᵀX₁)⁻¹X₁ᵀ. That sharpens the earlier characterization:
    • idempotent ⟺ a projection (onto C(P) along N(P));
    • idempotent + symmetric ⟺ an orthogonal projection.
    Symmetry is precisely the extra ingredient that means "perpendicular".
  • Oblique projectors can lengthen a vector. For a rank-1 projector onto span(a) along span(c), ‖P‖₂ = 1/sin θ where θ is the angle between the two directions. Orthogonal (θ = 90°) gives ‖P‖₂ = 1; as the two subspaces approach each other, ‖P‖₂ → ∞. That is the same blow-up as an ill-conditioned system (conditioning) — and it is not a coincidence, because a near-parallel decomposition is a near-singular change of basis.
  • Where oblique projections come from in practice. Change the inner product. Fix a positive definite A and define ⟨x, y⟩_A = xᵀAy. The projection onto S₁ that is orthogonal with respect to this inner product has matrix X₁(X₁ᵀAX₁)⁻¹X₁ᵀA — oblique in the ordinary Euclidean sense, perfectly orthogonal in the A sense, and still the minimiser of the corresponding distance (x − y)ᵀA(x − y). The proof is the same Pythagoras argument with A inserted.
  • This is exactly generalized least squares and whitening: with A = C⁻¹ for a known error covariance C, the A-orthogonal projection of y onto C(X) is the GLS fit X(XᵀC⁻¹X)⁻¹XᵀC⁻¹y. Weighted least squares is the diagonal special case. The §14 payoff: OLS is optimal when the errors are spherical, and when they are not, the right answer is to project obliquely — or equivalently, to make the errors spherical first and then project perpendicularly.
oblique projection onto S₁ along S₂S₁ ∩ S₂ = {0}, S₁ ⊕ S₂ = ℝᵐ x = x₁ + x₂ uniquely X = [X₁ X₂] P_{S₁|S₂} = X · [[I_{m₁}, 0], [0, 0]] · X⁻¹ P_{S₂|S₁} = I − P_{S₁|S₂} P² = P always Pᵀ = P ⟺ X₁ᵀX₂ = 0 ⟺ the projection is orthogonal worked in ℝ²: onto span[2, 1]ᵀ along span[1, 2]ᵀX = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] X⁻¹ = ⅓·[[2, −1], [−1, 2]] P = ⅓·[[4, −2], [2, −1]] P² = P ✓ Pᵀ ≠ P ✗ trace = 1 = rank ✓ eigenvalues 1 and 0 ✓ x = [1, 1]ᵀ oblique: x₁ = [⅔, ⅓]ᵀ, x₂ = [⅓, ⅔]ᵀ orthogonal onto the same line: ⅕·[[4, 2], [2, 1]]·x = [1.2, 0.6]ᵀ ‖P_oblique‖₂ = 5/3 = 1/sin θ (cos θ = ⅘) ‖P_orthogonal‖₂ = 1 A-inner-product (oblique) projection = generalized least squares⟨x, y⟩_A = xᵀAy, A positive definite P_A = X(XᵀAX)⁻¹XᵀA XᵀA(y − P_A y) = 0 A = C⁻¹ ⟹ β̂_GLS = (XᵀC⁻¹X)⁻¹XᵀC⁻¹y A = diag(1/wᵢ) ⟹ weighted least squares

Orthogonal matrices

  • Conventionally written Q. Two requirements on the columns: pairwise orthogonal, and each of unit norm — i.e. ⟨qᵢ, qⱼ⟩ = 1 if i = j, else 0. Both conditions compress into one statement: QᵀQ = I.
  • That definition is the definition of an inverse. For square Q: Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ, and QᵀQ = QQᵀ = I. Enormously convenient — inverting becomes free and exact, where a general inverse is expensive and unstable.
  • Isometry. ‖Qx‖² = (Qx)ᵀ(Qx) = xᵀQᵀQx = xᵀx = ‖x‖², and likewise (Qx)ᵀ(Qy) = xᵀy. So orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles — they are pure rotations, reflections, and permutations, never stretches or shears. Consequently det(Q)² = 1, so det(Q) = ±1 (+1 for a rotation, −1 when a reflection is included).
  • Examples: I itself; every 2×2 rotation [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]; (1/√2)·[[1, −1], [1, 1]] — note that the unnormalised version has orthogonal columns but norm √2, so it needs the scaling to qualify.
  • Rectangular case. A tall matrix can have orthonormal columns; then Qᵀ is only a left inverse — QᵀQ = Iₙ but QQᵀ ≠ Iₘ. A wide matrix cannot have orthonormal columns at all (more than m columns in ℝᵐ can't be independent, let alone orthogonal); the best it can do is orthonormal rows, giving QQᵀ = Iₘ, a right inverse.
tall Q: one-sided only — and QQᵀ is a projection matrixQ = ⅓·[[2, 1], [−2, 2], [1, 2]] QᵀQ = I₂ ✓ QQᵀ = (1/9)·[[5, −2, 4], [−2, 8, 2], [4, 2, 5]] ≠ I₃ but (QQᵀ)ᵀ = QQᵀ and (QQᵀ)² = QQᵀ, trace = 2 = rank ← it projects ℝ³ onto the plane C(Q)

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

  1. Take the first vector and normalise it: q₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖.
  2. For each later vector vₖ: subtract its projections onto all previously produced q's — that's the perpendicular component from above, applied repeatedly.
  3. Normalise the remainder, and move on.
Gram-Schmidt stepṽₖ = vₖ − ∑ⱼ₌₁..ₖ₋₁ (qⱼᵀvₖ) qⱼ qₖ = ṽₖ / ‖ṽₖ‖ worked example on A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, 1]]q₁ = [1, 1, 0]ᵀ/√2 ṽ₂ = [0, 1, 1]ᵀ − (1/√2)·q₁ = [−½, ½, 1]ᵀ ‖ṽ₂‖ = √6/2 q₂ = [−1, 1, 2]ᵀ/√6 check: q₁ᵀq₂ = (−1 + 1 + 0)/√12 = 0 ✓
  • Orthogonalize against the already-orthogonalized vectors, not the raw originals — that ordering is the whole algorithm.
  • If the inputs are linearly dependent, some remainder comes out as the zero vector (you can't have more than n orthogonal directions in ℝⁿ), and normalising it is undefined. Libraries simply drop that column.
  • Numerically unstable: rounding errors accumulate down the sequence, and the normalisation step amplifies them — a remainder that should be 0 comes out as 1e−16 and gets blown up to unit length, producing a spurious "orthogonal" direction. Production code uses modified Gram-Schmidt, Householder reflections, or Givens rotations instead.
  • Sign is arbitrary: qₖ and −qₖ are equally valid basis vectors, so your answer may differ from a library's by column signs. The same ambiguity reappears with eigenvectors and the SVD.

QR decomposition

  • Gram-Schmidt turns A into Q and appears to throw information away. QR keeps it: A = QR, where R is the "residual" bookkeeping. Recovering it is one line — left-multiply by Qᵀ and use QᵀQ = I.
  • R is always upper triangular. Entry Rᵢⱼ = qᵢᵀaⱼ. Below the diagonal (i > j) we pair a later q with an earlier column of A — and qᵢ was constructed to be orthogonal to exactly those, so the entry is zero. Above the diagonal, no such orthogonality was imposed.
  • Rank shows up on the diagonal of R. Q always attains its maximum possible rank, even if A is rank-deficient (you can always invent a new direction orthogonal to the previous ones). R, however, inherits rank(R) = rank(A): if column k of A is a combination of earlier columns, then qₖ ⟂ aₖ and Rₖₖ = 0.
  • Sizes. Square A ⟹ square Q, R. Tall A (m > n) admits two flavours: economy (Q is m×n, R is n×n) or full (Q is m×m — pad with m − n extra orthogonal directions — and R is m×n). Wide A has no economy form. Watch the defaults: np.linalg.qr returns the economy version unless asked otherwise.
A = QR, and R = QᵀAA = QR ⟹ QᵀA = QᵀQR = R A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, 1]] Q = [[1/√2, −1/√6], [1/√2, 1/√6], [0, 2/√6]] R = QᵀA = [[√2, 1/√2], [0, √6/2]] ← upper triangular, as guaranteed

Inverse (and least-squares) via QR

A = QR ⟹ A⁻¹ = (QR)⁻¹ = R⁻¹Q⁻¹ = R⁻¹Qᵀ
  • You still invert something — but R is triangular, so it inverts by back-substitution alone: cheap, and numerically far better behaved than inverting a dense A.
  • The same trick removes the (AᵀA)⁻¹ from the projection formula: substitute A = QR and everything cancels down to solving the triangular system Rx = Qᵀb. This is what lstsq actually does, and it's the bridge into §14.
The one idea: "closest point in a subspace" = "error orthogonal to the subspace". Everything here — the left inverse, projection matrices, Gram-Schmidt, QR — is that sentence written in different notation.

14 Least-squares the linear-algebra view

When Ax = b has no exact solution (more equations than unknowns), find the x̂ that comes closest. Statistics calls the same equation Xβ = y and calls the answer regression. This is the linear-algebra derivation — for the ML angle (loss, gradient descent, logistic, evaluation) see the dedicated Linear & Logistic Regression page.

Why least-squares exists: models of data

  • A model is a deliberately simplified set of equations that captures the parts of a system we think matter and abstracts away the rest. It is not meant to reproduce reality — it is meant to be understandable.
  • Every model splits into fixed features (which terms are in the equation — the modeller's hypothesis) and free parameters (how strongly each term counts — read off the data). Model-fitting = choosing the free parameters so the model matches the data as closely as possible.
  • Example hypothesis: adult height h depends on sex s, parents' height p, and childhood nutrition n. The βs are unknown; ε soaks up everything the three terms cannot explain.
a model = fixed features · free parameters + errorh = β₁s + β₂p + β₃n + ε
  • Each row of data turns the model template into one equation. Many rows ⟹ a system of equations ⟹ a matrix equation. With 1000 people and 3 predictors you get 1000 equations in 3 unknowns — hopelessly overdetermined, hence "closest" rather than "exact".
  • "Linear" in linear least-squares constrains the parameters, not the model. n³, log p, or an interaction s·p are all fine — they are just columns. What breaks it is a parameter multiplying a parameter, e.g. β₂√β₃·p, because then the system can no longer be written as a matrix times a coefficient vector.

The five steps of model-fitting

  1. Write the model equation. Decide which terms are in and which are out — this is science, not algebra.
  2. Work the data into the model. One row of the data table ⟹ one equation. Binary variables get dummy-coded (female = 0, male = 1), which fixes the sign convention of the corresponding β.
  3. Convert the system into a matrix equation Xβ = y: split coefficients (into X), unknowns (into β), constants (into y).
  4. Solve for β — "fitting the model", "estimating the parameters". This is the linear-algebra step, and the rest of this section.
  5. Statistically evaluate the model. Is it good? Does it generalise or is it over-fit? Do all terms earn their place? This step produces p-, t- and F-values and belongs to probability theory, not linear algebra — see the ML page for the practical version (train/test splits, cross-validation, R²).

  • The design matrix is nearly always tall: many observations (rows M), few predictors (columns N), N ≪ M. Wide design matrices (more variables than observations) show up in deep learning and genomics and need regularization to be solvable at all.
  • The intercept is a column of ones. Without it the fit is forced through the origin; with it, the line can cross the y-axis anywhere. Its literal meaning ("expected y when every predictor is 0") is often absurd, but it is structurally necessary unless all data and regressors are mean-centred.

Route 1 — via the left inverse (algebra)

  • X is not square, so X⁻¹ doesn't exist. Use the left inverse (§12): multiply both sides by (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ.
  • Validity condition: X must be full column rank, i.e. the predictors are linearly independent. A repeated column, or one predictor that is a linear combination of others, makes XᵀX singular and the formula illegal. Statistics calls this multicollinearity.
least-squares via left inverseXβ = y (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀX β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (requires rank(X) = N)
  • Is the solution ever exact? Only if y ∈ C(X) — asking whether a specific vector in ℝᴹ happens to lie in an N-dimensional subspace (say 3D inside ℝ¹⁰⁰⁰). Essentially never: even a whisper of noise pushes y off the subspace. So we look for the reachable ŷ ∈ C(X) that is as close to y as possible.
the GLM, honestly statedy = Xβ + ε ŷ = Xβ̂ ∈ C(X) ε = y − ŷ

Route 2 — via orthogonal projection (geometry)

  • Picture ambient ℝᴹ with an N-dimensional subspace inside it whose basis is the columns of X. That subspace is the mathematical statement of your theory: it contains every prediction the model is capable of making.
  • The data y is a point in ℝᴹ, almost certainly off the subspace. The closest point inside it is the orthogonal projection of y onto C(X) (§13) — and "closest" is exactly what least-squares means.
  • The residual ε = y − Xβ is the leftover that points from the subspace to the data. Being the shortest such connector forces it to be ⟂ to every column of X, which is one equation per predictor: Xᵀε = 0.
orthogonality ⟹ the normal equationsXᵀε = 0 Xᵀ(y − Xβ) = 0 Xᵀy − XᵀXβ = 0 XᵀXβ = Xᵀy (normal equations) β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy
  • Same answer, different starting point. The geometry also hands you a diagnostic: prediction errors must be uncorrelated with the model. If your residuals still show structure that the predictors could explain, the fit is wrong or the model is mis-specified.

Route 3 — via row reduction, and Route 4 — via calculus

  • Naïve row reduction fails. rref([X | y]) is legal (both have M rows) but useless: the augmented matrix is tall with rank N + 1, so RREF returns [I ; 0] — identity on top, zeros beneath. No β anywhere.
  • Fix: pre-multiply by Xᵀ first. Then [XᵀX | Xᵀy] is N × (N+1) — wide and full row-rank — and Gauss-Jordan does deliver β on the right.
  • Why it works: X and XᵀX share a row space (§8), and if X has independent columns then XᵀX spans all of ℝᴺ. Xᵀy is just some point in ℝᴺ, so it is guaranteed to be in C(XᵀX). The whole "is y in the column space?" problem evaporates because we never leave ℝᴺ. (Price: you must compute ε separately afterwards — β solves the normal equation, not the original one.)
  • Route 4: state it as pure optimisation — minimise the squared error and set the derivative to zero. Chain rule on ‖Xβ − y‖² gives 2Xᵀ(Xβ − y) = 0, i.e. the normal equations again. This is the version that generalises to gradient descent.
four roads, one destinationrref([XᵀX | Xᵀy]) ⟹ [I | β] min_β ‖Xβ − y‖² ⟹ d/dβ ‖Xβ − y‖² = 2Xᵀ(Xβ − y) = 0 ⟹ XᵀXβ = Xᵀy

Predicted values, residuals, and fit quality

  • Predictions: ŷ = Xβ — the projection of the data onto what the model can express. Residuals: ε = y − ŷ, one per observation.
  • We don't care about individual εᵢ so much as the norm ‖ε‖², which is what the fit minimises. That single number is the objective of the whole procedure.
  • Orthogonality check: εᵀŷ = 0 and Xᵀε = 0 should hold to machine precision. In practice you'll see something like 1e−14 instead of exactly 0 — that's rounding error, not a bug.
  • R² normalises the residual sum of squares by the total variance of y: the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the design matrix. R² = 1 is a perfect fit; R² = 0 means the model does no better than the mean.
minimise this‖ε‖² = ‖y − Xβ‖² = Σᵢ εᵢ² fit qualityR² = 1 − (Σᵢ εᵢ²) / (Σᵢ (yᵢ − ȳ)²) = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot

Drag the line off the best fit and watch the squared error grow — it bottoms out exactly at the least-squares solution.

Worked example: three models on eight numbers

Take a signal recorded at eight successive times: y = [−4, 0, −3, 1, 2, 8, 5, 8], x = 1…8. Watch the design matrix grow.

model 1 — a constant: y = β₁ + εX = column of eight 1s XᵀX = 8 Xᵀy = 17 β = 17/8 = 2.125 model 2 — a slope with no intercept: y = β₁x + εX = [1,2,…,8]ᵀ XᵀX = 204 Xᵀy = 148 β = 148/204 = 0.7255 model 3 — intercept + slope: y = β₁ + β₂x + εXᵀX = [[8, 36], [36, 204]] Xᵀy = [17, 148]ᵀ β = [−5.5357, 1.7024]ᵀ
  • Model 1 re-derives the mean. Xᵀy sums the data, XᵀX counts the observations, so β = Σyᵢ/M — the arithmetic mean falls out of least-squares when your hypothesis is "one number explains everything". It fits badly here: the data clearly trend upward.
  • Why the column of 1s? Any constant would work — set the column to 9.8 and β becomes 2.125/9.8 = 0.2168, meaning "the level is β × 9.8". Mathematically identical, interpretively awful. Ones keep β in the units of the data.
  • Model 2 over-predicts early and under-predicts late, because with no intercept the line is nailed to the origin.
  • Model 3 gives ŷ = [−3.833, −2.131, −0.429, 1.274, 2.976, 4.679, 6.381, 8.083] and residuals ε = [−0.167, 2.131, −2.571, −0.274, −0.976, 3.321, −1.381, −0.083]. Then SS_res = 25.15, SS_tot = 146.88, so R² = 0.829 — the line explains 83% of the variance and the leftovers look like noise. Confirm εᵀŷ ≈ 0: you'll get ~1e−14.
  • The intercept −5.54 is where the line would cross x = 0; the data start at x = 1, so you never see it, but it is what lets the line sit where it belongs.

Route 5 — via QR, and why this is the one libraries use

  • All four routes above end at the normal equations, and the normal equations require forming XᵀX. That step is numerically expensive in a way that has nothing to do with arithmetic cost: κ₂(XᵀX) = κ₂(X)², so the condition number is squared and you lose half your significant digits before the solve even begins (conditioning). A design matrix with κ = 10⁸ — unremarkable with correlated predictors — turns into a 10¹⁶ problem, which double precision cannot solve at all.
  • The fix is to never form XᵀX. Factor X = QR (§13) with Q orthogonal and R = [R₁ ; 0], R₁ upper triangular. Because Q is orthogonal it preserves norms, so you may measure the residual after rotating by Qᵀ — and the problem falls apart into a piece you can zero and a piece you cannot touch.
  • The derivation is four lines and delivers a bonus. Split Qᵀy = [c₁ ; c₂] with c₁ of length N. Then ‖y − Xβ‖² = ‖Qᵀy − Rβ‖² = ‖c₁ − R₁β‖² + ‖c₂‖². The second term contains no β at all. So the minimum is attained by killing the first term — solve the triangular system R₁β = c₁ by back-substitution — and the minimum residual sum of squares is exactly ‖c₂‖², free of charge, with no need to form residuals at all.
  • The pseudoinverse also reads straight off: for full column rank, X⁺ = R₁⁻¹Q₁ᵀ, hence β̂ = X⁺y. The conceptual formula (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ and the computational one are the same object approached from opposite ends.
  • The Cholesky route is the honest middle ground: form XᵀX, factor it as TᵀT, solve two triangular systems. It is the fastest option (about half the work of QR) and is what you want when N ≪ M and the predictors are well behaved. But it has already paid the squared-condition-number price. Note the identity from factorizations: XᵀX = RᵀR, so R from the QR is the Cholesky factor of XᵀX — QR reaches the same triangular matrix without ever squaring anything.
  • What lstsq actually does. NumPy's lstsq uses an SVD-based driver by default (maximally robust, handles rank deficiency, returns singular values); LAPACK also offers QR with column pivoting. Neither forms XᵀX. Explicitly writing np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y is slower and less accurate — there is no argument for it outside a derivation.
  • Updating a fit. If new observations arrive, you do not refactor. Stack the new rows under [R₁ ; 0] and apply a sequence of Givens rotations to zero them out, rotating R₁ into the updated R₁*. Cost is O(N²) per new row instead of O(MN²) for a full refit — the mechanism behind recursive/online least squares.
least squares via QRX = QR, R = [R₁ ; 0], R₁ upper triangular N×N Qᵀy = [c₁ ; c₂], c₁ ∈ ℝᴺ, c₂ ∈ ℝᴹ⁻ᴺ ‖y − Xβ‖² = ‖Qᵀ(y − Xβ)‖² = ‖Qᵀy − Rβ‖² = ‖c₁ − R₁β‖² + ‖c₂‖² ⟹ minimise by solving R₁β = c₁ (back-substitution) and SS_res = ‖c₂‖² falls out for free X⁺ = R₁⁻¹Q₁ᵀ β̂ = X⁺y worked: X = [[1,1],[1,2],[1,3],[1,4]], y = [2, 3, 5, 4]ᵀQᵀy = [−7, −1.7889, 1.2, −0.6]ᵀ c₂ = [1.2, −0.6]ᵀ ‖c₂‖² = 1.80 β̂ = [1.5, 0.8]ᵀ direct check: ‖y − Xβ̂‖² = 1.80 ✓ (column signs of Q are conventional — flip a column of Q and the matching row of R and nothing observable changes) the conditioning argument, stated onceκ₂(XᵀX) = κ₂(X)² normal equations: lose half your digits QR: keep them

When X is rank-deficient: the minimum-norm least-squares solution

  • Collinear predictors make XᵀX singular, so (XᵀX)⁻¹ does not exist and the recipe above is illegal. But the problem is still perfectly well posed — "find the closest reachable point" always has an answer. What fails is uniqueness of the coefficients, not existence of the fit.
  • Replace the inverse with any generalized inverse (§12): every β = (XᵀX)⁻Xᵀy is a least-squares solution, and every least-squares solution has that form. Equivalently, a {1,3}-inverse X_L (satisfying XX_LX = X with XX_L symmetric) is called a least-squares inverse precisely because β = X_Ly minimises ‖Xβ − y‖².
  • What is unique and what is not. The fitted values are unique: Xβ̂ = XX_Ly = XX⁺y for every choice, so ŷ, the residuals, and SS_res do not depend on which solution you found. The coefficients themselves do. The full solution set is β = X_Ly + (I − X_LX)z over all z — a particular solution plus the null space of X, exactly as in §10. It collapses to a point ⟺ rank(X) = N.
  • The pseudoinverse picks one canonically: the shortest. Among all least-squares solutions, β⁺ = X⁺y has the strictly smallest ‖β‖₂. The proof is one Pythagoras step: any other solution is β⁺ + (I − X⁺X)z, the correction lies in N(X), β⁺ lies in the row space C(Xᵀ), and those two subspaces are orthogonal — so the norms add and any nonzero correction strictly increases the length. Minimum-norm least squares is a double projection: project y onto C(X), then project 0 onto the solution set.
  • The statistically important consequence: estimability. A linear combination aᵀβ̂ is the same number for every least-squares solution ⟺ a lies in the row space of X. Combinations inside the row space are estimable and are safe to report; combinations with a component in N(X) are arbitrary artefacts of which generalized inverse the software happened to pick. This is why a rank-deficient ANOVA design gives you meaningful contrasts but meaningless individual level effects — and why dropping a dummy column is not a statistical decision but a choice of generalized inverse.
  • Ridge does something different and worth contrasting. The pseudoinverse chooses the minimum-norm solution among the exact minimisers of ‖Xβ − y‖². Ridge changes the objective to ‖Xβ − y‖² + λ‖β‖², giving a unique solution that is not a least-squares minimiser at all. As λ → 0⁺ the ridge solution converges to X⁺y, so the pseudoinverse is the limit of ridge — but for any λ > 0 you have traded fit for stability on purpose (regularization).

The hat matrix and leverage

  • Give the projection matrix its statistical name. H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ is the hat matrix — it puts the hat on y: ŷ = Hy, and ε = (I − H)y. It is symmetric and idempotent, and so is I − H. Everything below is a consequence of those two words.
  • Degrees of freedom are traces. trace(H) = rank(X) = N (the number of parameters) and trace(I − H) = M − N (the residual degrees of freedom). The mysterious "M − N" divisor in the variance estimate is just the dimension of the subspace the residual is free to live in.
  • Leverage. The diagonal entry hᵢᵢ is the leverage of observation i: literally ∂ŷᵢ/∂yᵢ, how much observation i pulls its own fitted value. Because H is a projection, 0 ≤ hᵢᵢ ≤ 1, and with an intercept in the model hᵢᵢ ≥ 1/M. They sum to N, so the average leverage is N/M — which is what makes the usual rule of thumb ("flag hᵢᵢ > 2N/M") a statement about being twice as influential as the typical point, not an arbitrary threshold.
  • Leverage depends only on X, never on y. It is a property of where an observation sits in predictor space — how far its row is from the centre of the design — and can be computed before a single response is collected. High leverage is not the same as being an outlier: it is potential influence, realised only if the point also has a large residual.
  • Residual variance is not constant, even under homoscedastic errors. Var(ε) = σ²(I − H), so Var(εᵢ) = σ²(1 − hᵢᵢ). High-leverage points have smaller residual variance — the fit is dragged toward them, so they look deceptively well fitted. In the limit hᵢᵢ = 1 the residual is forced to exactly 0 and the point is fitted perfectly regardless of its value. That is precisely why raw residuals are a poor diagnostic and why you standardise: rᵢ = εᵢ / (σ̂√(1 − hᵢᵢ)). Cook's distance combines the two ingredients — the standardised residual and the leverage — into a single influence measure.
  • Also note ε ⟂ ŷ and SS_res = yᵀ(I − H)y: the residual sum of squares is a quadratic form in the projector onto the orthogonal complement, which is the fact that makes its distribution chi-squared under normal errors and hands you the F-test.
  • Comparing nested models is a projection decomposition. Fitting X₁ alone leaves SS₂; fitting [X₁ X₂] leaves SS₁. If the two blocks are orthogonal the reduction is simply yᵀX₂(X₂ᵀX₂)⁻¹X₂ᵀy. If they are not — the usual case — you must first residualise X₂ against X₁: X₂* = (I − H₁)X₂, and the reduction is yᵀX₂*(X₂*ᵀX₂*)⁻¹X₂*ᵀy. That is the projection-geometry statement of "controlling for X₁", and it is why a coefficient's meaning depends on what else is in the model.

Gauss-Markov: why least squares, and not some other line

  • Everything so far says least squares gives the closest point. That is a geometric claim, and geometry alone does not explain why "closest in the Euclidean sense" should be the right thing to want. The Gauss-Markov theorem supplies the missing reason, and it is a statistical one.
  • The model. Assume only two things about the data-generating process: E[y] = Xβ (the model is correctly specified — the mean really is linear in the predictors) and Var(y) = σ²I (errors have equal variance and are uncorrelated). No distributional assumption whatsoever — no normality, no independence beyond zero correlation.
  • Estimability first. A parametric function Qβ has any linear unbiased estimator ⟺ Q = AX for some A, i.e. ⟺ the rows of Q lie in the row space of X. Same condition as above: unbiased estimation and uniqueness-of-the-coefficient are the same requirement wearing different clothes.
  • The theorem. Let P be the orthogonal projector onto C(X). Among all linear unbiased estimators Fy of an estimable Qβ, the estimator APy — the one built from the projection — has the smallest variance. In the multi-parameter case "smallest" means smallest dispersion matrix in the Löwner order: Var(Fy) − Var(APy) is nonnegative definite, which implies minimum variance simultaneously for every scalar combination. This is BLUE: Best Linear Unbiased Estimator.
  • The proof is two lines and is pure projection algebra. Unbiasedness forces FX = Q = AX, hence FP = AP. Then Var(Fy) − Var(APy) = σ²(FFᵀ − APPᵀAᵀ) = σ²(FFᵀ − FPFᵀ) = σ²F(I − P)Fᵀ = σ²F(I − P)(I − P)ᵀFᵀ, which is a Gram matrix and therefore nonnegative definite. The entire optimality of least squares rests on I − P being a symmetric idempotent — that is, on the projection being orthogonal.
  • Read that last sentence again. The reason least squares is statistically optimal is exactly the reason it is geometrically natural, and the hinge is Var(y) = σ²I: spherical error contours make the Euclidean perpendicular the right notion of "closest". Change the error covariance to σ²C and the contours become ellipsoids; the optimal estimator becomes the oblique projection in the C⁻¹ inner product (§13), i.e. generalized least squares. OLS is still unbiased under a non-spherical C, just no longer best.
  • A free unbiased variance estimator comes with it: σ̂² = yᵀ(I − P)y/(M − r), where r = rank(X). Proof by trace: E[yᵀ(I − P)y] = σ²·trace(I − P) = σ²(M − r). The denominator is the dimension of the residual subspace — nothing to do with "correcting for bias" in any hand-wavy sense.
  • What Gauss-Markov does not say. It does not say least squares is the best estimator, only the best linear unbiased one. Drop linearity and, under heavy-tailed errors, robust L₁ or M-estimators beat it badly. Drop unbiasedness and ridge, lasso and shrinkage estimators can beat it in mean-squared error even under exactly these assumptions — that is the bias-variance trade in one sentence. And normality is needed for none of this, only for the t- and F-distributions of the tests you run afterwards.

Other norms, other fits

  • "Closest" was a choice. Minimise a different norm of the residual and you get a different, equally legitimate, fit — with different properties and a much harder computation.
  • L₁ (least absolute deviations). Minimise ∑|εᵢ|. Robust to outliers because a wild point contributes linearly instead of quadratically; the solution is a linear program, not a projection; no closed form; the fit passes exactly through N data points. It estimates the conditional median, not the mean — which is why quantile regression is its natural generalization.
  • L∞ (Chebyshev / minimax). Minimise maxᵢ|εᵢ|. Also a linear program; maximally sensitive to outliers, but the right criterion when what you care about is a worst-case guarantee (function approximation, engineering tolerances).
  • Regularized solutions. Minimise ‖y − Xβ‖² + λ‖β‖² (ridge / Tikhonov) or + λ‖β‖₁ (lasso). Ridge keeps the closed form with XᵀX + λI in place of XᵀX, is always invertible for λ > 0, and strictly improves the condition number. Lasso has no closed form but sets coefficients exactly to zero.
  • Total least squares (orthogonal distance regression). Ordinary least squares minimises vertical distances — it assumes the predictors are measured without error and only y is noisy. If both are noisy, minimise the perpendicular distance to the fitted line instead. The solution is not a projection of y onto C(X) but the smallest singular vector of the augmented matrix [X | y] (§16) — which is also exactly what PCA computes. The difference between regression and PCA on the same two-column data is entirely the difference between vertical and perpendicular distance.
  • Weighted least squares puts a positive definite W in the middle: minimise (y − Xβ)ᵀW(y − Xβ). Because W applies to both y and Xβ it is not really a new problem — substitute the Cholesky factor W_C and it is ordinary least squares on W_C y against W_C X. Whiten, then project.

When it breaks

  • Collinear predictors ⟹ XᵀX singular ⟹ no left inverse. Symptoms: wildly unstable coefficients, huge condition number (§16). Fixes: drop a redundant column, or ridge-regularize by shifting to XᵀX + λI.
  • Wide design matrix (N > M) ⟹ infinitely many exact solutions; you need a criterion to pick one (minimum-norm via the pseudoinverse, §16, or a penalty).
  • Nonlinear-in-parameters models are outside linear least-squares entirely; they need iterative nonlinear optimisation.
  • Numerics: forming XᵀX explicitly squares the condition number. Production solvers use QR (§13) or the SVD (§16) instead — numpy.linalg.lstsq rather than an explicit inverse.
This is regression: the fitted coefficients are the pseudoinverse times the data, and "best fit" literally means the projection of the targets onto what the features can express. The residual is what the theory cannot reach. Full ML treatment: Linear & Logistic Regression.
