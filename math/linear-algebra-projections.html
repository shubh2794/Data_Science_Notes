13 Projections & orthogonalization

Drop a vector onto a subspace and keep the closest point. One geometric idea — "minimum distance means perpendicular" — generates the left inverse, least-squares, Gram-Schmidt, and QR.

Projection onto a line in ℝ²

  • Setup: a vector a spanning a line, a point b off it. Find the scalar β so that βa is as close to b as possible without leaving the line.
  • The geometry hands you the algebra. The connector b − βa and the line form a right triangle in which the connector is a leg and the distance from b to any other point on the line is a hypotenuse. A hypotenuse always exceeds a leg — so the shortest connector is the perpendicular one.
  • Perpendicular means the dot product vanishes: aᵀ(b − βa) = 0. Expand and solve; dividing by aᵀa is legal because it's a scalar.
  • Read the formula as mapping over magnitude: how much of b aligns with a, normalised by a's squared length. Correlation, convolution, and normalisation all wear this same shape.
  • β can be negative — the point projects "backwards" along a. It is 0 exactly when b ⟂ a, in which case the line explains nothing about b.
  • Mnemonic for the notation proj_a(b): the subspace goes in the subscript.
derivationaᵀ(b − βa) = 0 ⟹ aᵀb − β aᵀa = 0 ⟹ β = aᵀb / aᵀa proj_a(b) = (aᵀb / aᵀa) · a error e = b − proj_a(b), e ⟂ a examplea = [−2, −1]ᵀ, b = [3, −1]ᵀ ⟹ β = (−6 + 1)/(4 + 1) = −1 ⟹ p = [2, 1]ᵀ

Drag b (orange). Its shadow p on the line stays put as the closest point; the dashed error always meets the line at a right angle.

Projection onto a subspace in ℝᴺ

  • Same derivation, promoted: the line a becomes a matrix A whose columns span a subspace, and the scalar β becomes a coefficient vector x. Orthogonality now means the error is perpendicular to every column: Aᵀ(b − Ax) = 0.
  • That rearranges to the normal equations AᵀA x = Aᵀb. We can't divide by AᵀA — but we can left-multiply by its inverse, and out falls the left inverse from §12. Two topics with unrelated motivations, one formula.
  • Validity condition: AᵀA must be invertible, i.e. A is square full rank or tall with full column rank. Linearly dependent columns ⟹ singular AᵀA ⟹ the projection coefficients aren't unique (the projected point still is).
  • If b already lies in C(A), the error is the zero vector and Ax = b exactly. If A is square and full rank, C(A) is the whole space, and the formula collapses: (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ = A⁻¹A⁻ᵀAᵀ = A⁻¹.
  • The map b ↦ Ax is the projection matrix P. It is symmetric (Pᵀ = P) and idempotent (P² = P — projecting something already in the subspace changes nothing). Its rank equals dim C(A), and I − P projects onto the orthogonal complement (the error).
  • Computationally: never form (AᵀA)⁻¹ — use np.linalg.lstsq, which factorizes instead (and is far better conditioned, since forming AᵀA squares the condition number).
projection onto the column space of AAᵀ(b − Ax) = 0 ⟹ AᵀA x = Aᵀb ⟹ x = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ b ← the left inverse p = Ax = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ b = P b P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ Pᵀ = P, P² = P if A has orthonormal columns (AᵀA = I): P = AAᵀ ← no inverse needed at all

Parallel and perpendicular components

  • Any vector w splits uniquely relative to a reference v into a piece along v and a piece perpendicular to it. The parallel piece is just the projection; the perpendicular piece is whatever's left over.
  • The two pieces really are orthogonal — take their dot product, note that wᵀv and vᵀv are scalars, and the two terms cancel identically.
  • This is the atom of Gram-Schmidt, of removing a confound from a signal, and of "residualizing" one regressor against another.
decomposition of w relative to vw = w∥ᵥ + w⟂ᵥ w∥ᵥ = (vᵀw / vᵀv) · v w⟂ᵥ = w − w∥ᵥ proof of orthogonality(w∥ᵥ)ᵀ(w⟂ᵥ) = (wᵀv/vᵀv)·vᵀw − (wᵀv/vᵀv)²·vᵀv = (wᵀv)²/vᵀv − (wᵀv)²/vᵀv = 0 example: decompose v = [2, 3]ᵀ relative to w = [4, 0]ᵀv∥w = (8/16)·[4, 0]ᵀ = [2, 0]ᵀ v⟂w = [2, 3]ᵀ − [2, 0]ᵀ = [0, 3]ᵀ (visibly orthogonal, and they sum to v)

Orthogonal matrices

  • Conventionally written Q. Two requirements on the columns: pairwise orthogonal, and each of unit norm — i.e. ⟨qᵢ, qⱼ⟩ = 1 if i = j, else 0. Both conditions compress into one statement: QᵀQ = I.
  • That definition is the definition of an inverse. For square Q: Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ, and QᵀQ = QQᵀ = I. Enormously convenient — inverting becomes free and exact, where a general inverse is expensive and unstable.
  • Isometry. ‖Qx‖² = (Qx)ᵀ(Qx) = xᵀQᵀQx = xᵀx = ‖x‖², and likewise (Qx)ᵀ(Qy) = xᵀy. So orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles — they are pure rotations, reflections, and permutations, never stretches or shears. Consequently det(Q)² = 1, so det(Q) = ±1 (+1 for a rotation, −1 when a reflection is included).
  • Examples: I itself; every 2×2 rotation [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]; (1/√2)·[[1, −1], [1, 1]] — note that the unnormalised version has orthogonal columns but norm √2, so it needs the scaling to qualify.
  • Rectangular case. A tall matrix can have orthonormal columns; then Qᵀ is only a left inverse — QᵀQ = Iₙ but QQᵀ ≠ Iₘ. A wide matrix cannot have orthonormal columns at all (more than m columns in ℝᵐ can't be independent, let alone orthogonal); the best it can do is orthonormal rows, giving QQᵀ = Iₘ, a right inverse.
tall Q: one-sided only — and QQᵀ is a projection matrixQ = ⅓·[[2, 1], [−2, 2], [1, 2]] QᵀQ = I₂ ✓ QQᵀ = (1/9)·[[5, −2, 4], [−2, 8, 2], [4, 2, 5]] ≠ I₃ but (QQᵀ)ᵀ = QQᵀ and (QQᵀ)² = QQᵀ, trace = 2 = rank ← it projects ℝ³ onto the plane C(Q)

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

  1. Take the first vector and normalise it: q₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖.
  2. For each later vector vₖ: subtract its projections onto all previously produced q's — that's the perpendicular component from above, applied repeatedly.
  3. Normalise the remainder, and move on.
Gram-Schmidt stepṽₖ = vₖ − ∑ⱼ₌₁..ₖ₋₁ (qⱼᵀvₖ) qⱼ qₖ = ṽₖ / ‖ṽₖ‖ worked example on A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, 1]]q₁ = [1, 1, 0]ᵀ/√2 ṽ₂ = [0, 1, 1]ᵀ − (1/√2)·q₁ = [−½, ½, 1]ᵀ ‖ṽ₂‖ = √6/2 q₂ = [−1, 1, 2]ᵀ/√6 check: q₁ᵀq₂ = (−1 + 1 + 0)/√12 = 0 ✓
  • Orthogonalize against the already-orthogonalized vectors, not the raw originals — that ordering is the whole algorithm.
  • If the inputs are linearly dependent, some remainder comes out as the zero vector (you can't have more than n orthogonal directions in ℝⁿ), and normalising it is undefined. Libraries simply drop that column.
  • Numerically unstable: rounding errors accumulate down the sequence, and the normalisation step amplifies them — a remainder that should be 0 comes out as 1e−16 and gets blown up to unit length, producing a spurious "orthogonal" direction. Production code uses modified Gram-Schmidt, Householder reflections, or Givens rotations instead.
  • Sign is arbitrary: qₖ and −qₖ are equally valid basis vectors, so your answer may differ from a library's by column signs. The same ambiguity reappears with eigenvectors and the SVD.

QR decomposition

  • Gram-Schmidt turns A into Q and appears to throw information away. QR keeps it: A = QR, where R is the "residual" bookkeeping. Recovering it is one line — left-multiply by Qᵀ and use QᵀQ = I.
  • R is always upper triangular. Entry Rᵢⱼ = qᵢᵀaⱼ. Below the diagonal (i > j) we pair a later q with an earlier column of A — and qᵢ was constructed to be orthogonal to exactly those, so the entry is zero. Above the diagonal, no such orthogonality was imposed.
  • Rank shows up on the diagonal of R. Q always attains its maximum possible rank, even if A is rank-deficient (you can always invent a new direction orthogonal to the previous ones). R, however, inherits rank(R) = rank(A): if column k of A is a combination of earlier columns, then qₖ ⟂ aₖ and Rₖₖ = 0.
  • Sizes. Square A ⟹ square Q, R. Tall A (m > n) admits two flavours: economy (Q is m×n, R is n×n) or full (Q is m×m — pad with m − n extra orthogonal directions — and R is m×n). Wide A has no economy form. Watch the defaults: np.linalg.qr returns the economy version unless asked otherwise.
A = QR, and R = QᵀAA = QR ⟹ QᵀA = QᵀQR = R A = [[1, 0], [1, 1], [0, 1]] Q = [[1/√2, −1/√6], [1/√2, 1/√6], [0, 2/√6]] R = QᵀA = [[√2, 1/√2], [0, √6/2]] ← upper triangular, as guaranteed

Inverse (and least-squares) via QR

A = QR ⟹ A⁻¹ = (QR)⁻¹ = R⁻¹Q⁻¹ = R⁻¹Qᵀ
  • You still invert something — but R is triangular, so it inverts by back-substitution alone: cheap, and numerically far better behaved than inverting a dense A.
  • The same trick removes the (AᵀA)⁻¹ from the projection formula: substitute A = QR and everything cancels down to solving the triangular system Rx = Qᵀb. This is what lstsq actually does, and it's the bridge into §14.
The one idea: "closest point in a subspace" = "error orthogonal to the subspace". Everything here — the left inverse, projection matrices, Gram-Schmidt, QR — is that sentence written in different notation.
Next chapter: least-squares is nothing but projecting the data vector b onto the column space of A — the closest reachable point, leaving the smallest error. Orthonormal columns (§16, PCA) are the version where the projection needs no inverse at all.

14 Least-squares the linear-algebra view

When Ax = b has no exact solution (more equations than unknowns), find the x̂ that comes closest. Statistics calls the same equation Xβ = y and calls the answer regression. This is the linear-algebra derivation — for the ML angle (loss, gradient descent, logistic, evaluation) see the dedicated Linear & Logistic Regression page.

Why least-squares exists: models of data

  • A model is a deliberately simplified set of equations that captures the parts of a system we think matter and abstracts away the rest. It is not meant to reproduce reality — it is meant to be understandable.
  • Every model splits into fixed features (which terms are in the equation — the modeller's hypothesis) and free parameters (how strongly each term counts — read off the data). Model-fitting = choosing the free parameters so the model matches the data as closely as possible.
  • Example hypothesis: adult height h depends on sex s, parents' height p, and childhood nutrition n. The βs are unknown; ε soaks up everything the three terms cannot explain.
a model = fixed features · free parameters + errorh = β₁s + β₂p + β₃n + ε
  • Each row of data turns the model template into one equation. Many rows ⟹ a system of equations ⟹ a matrix equation. With 1000 people and 3 predictors you get 1000 equations in 3 unknowns — hopelessly overdetermined, hence "closest" rather than "exact".
  • "Linear" in linear least-squares constrains the parameters, not the model. n³, log p, or an interaction s·p are all fine — they are just columns. What breaks it is a parameter multiplying a parameter, e.g. β₂√β₃·p, because then the system can no longer be written as a matrix times a coefficient vector.

The five steps of model-fitting

  1. Write the model equation. Decide which terms are in and which are out — this is science, not algebra.
  2. Work the data into the model. One row of the data table ⟹ one equation. Binary variables get dummy-coded (female = 0, male = 1), which fixes the sign convention of the corresponding β.
  3. Convert the system into a matrix equation Xβ = y: split coefficients (into X), unknowns (into β), constants (into y).
  4. Solve for β — "fitting the model", "estimating the parameters". This is the linear-algebra step, and the rest of this section.
  5. Statistically evaluate the model. Is it good? Does it generalise or is it over-fit? Do all terms earn their place? This step produces p-, t- and F-values and belongs to probability theory, not linear algebra — see the ML page for the practical version (train/test splits, cross-validation, R²).

Terminology: two languages, one equation

Linear algebraStatisticsWhat it is
Ax = bXβ = yThe general linear model (GLM)
AXDesign matrix — columns are independent variables / predictors / regressors
xβRegression coefficients, a.k.a. beta weights (a vector, despite the Greek letter)
byDependent variable, outcome measure, the observed data
  • The design matrix is nearly always tall: many observations (rows M), few predictors (columns N), N ≪ M. Wide design matrices (more variables than observations) show up in deep learning and genomics and need regularization to be solvable at all.
  • The intercept is a column of ones. Without it the fit is forced through the origin; with it, the line can cross the y-axis anywhere. Its literal meaning ("expected y when every predictor is 0") is often absurd, but it is structurally necessary unless all data and regressors are mean-centred.

Route 1 — via the left inverse (algebra)

  • X is not square, so X⁻¹ doesn't exist. Use the left inverse (§12): multiply both sides by (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ.
  • Validity condition: X must be full column rank, i.e. the predictors are linearly independent. A repeated column, or one predictor that is a linear combination of others, makes XᵀX singular and the formula illegal. Statistics calls this multicollinearity.
least-squares via left inverseXβ = y (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀX β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy (requires rank(X) = N)
  • Is the solution ever exact? Only if y ∈ C(X) — asking whether a specific vector in ℝᴹ happens to lie in an N-dimensional subspace (say 3D inside ℝ¹⁰⁰⁰). Essentially never: even a whisper of noise pushes y off the subspace. So we look for the reachable ŷ ∈ C(X) that is as close to y as possible.
the GLM, honestly statedy = Xβ + ε ŷ = Xβ̂ ∈ C(X) ε = y − ŷ

Route 2 — via orthogonal projection (geometry)

  • Picture ambient ℝᴹ with an N-dimensional subspace inside it whose basis is the columns of X. That subspace is the mathematical statement of your theory: it contains every prediction the model is capable of making.
  • The data y is a point in ℝᴹ, almost certainly off the subspace. The closest point inside it is the orthogonal projection of y onto C(X) (§13) — and "closest" is exactly what least-squares means.
  • The residual ε = y − Xβ is the leftover that points from the subspace to the data. Being the shortest such connector forces it to be ⟂ to every column of X, which is one equation per predictor: Xᵀε = 0.
orthogonality ⟹ the normal equationsXᵀε = 0 Xᵀ(y − Xβ) = 0 Xᵀy − XᵀXβ = 0 XᵀXβ = Xᵀy (normal equations) β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy
  • Same answer, different starting point. The geometry also hands you a diagnostic: prediction errors must be uncorrelated with the model. If your residuals still show structure that the predictors could explain, the fit is wrong or the model is mis-specified.

Route 3 — via row reduction, and Route 4 — via calculus

  • Naïve row reduction fails. rref([X | y]) is legal (both have M rows) but useless: the augmented matrix is tall with rank N + 1, so RREF returns [I ; 0] — identity on top, zeros beneath. No β anywhere.
  • Fix: pre-multiply by Xᵀ first. Then [XᵀX | Xᵀy] is N × (N+1) — wide and full row-rank — and Gauss-Jordan does deliver β on the right.
  • Why it works: X and XᵀX share a row space (§8), and if X has independent columns then XᵀX spans all of ℝᴺ. Xᵀy is just some point in ℝᴺ, so it is guaranteed to be in C(XᵀX). The whole "is y in the column space?" problem evaporates because we never leave ℝᴺ. (Price: you must compute ε separately afterwards — β solves the normal equation, not the original one.)
  • Route 4: state it as pure optimisation — minimise the squared error and set the derivative to zero. Chain rule on ‖Xβ − y‖² gives 2Xᵀ(Xβ − y) = 0, i.e. the normal equations again. This is the version that generalises to gradient descent.
four roads, one destinationrref([XᵀX | Xᵀy]) ⟹ [I | β] min_β ‖Xβ − y‖² ⟹ d/dβ ‖Xβ − y‖² = 2Xᵀ(Xβ − y) = 0 ⟹ XᵀXβ = Xᵀy

Predicted values, residuals, and fit quality

  • Predictions: ŷ = Xβ — the projection of the data onto what the model can express. Residuals: ε = y − ŷ, one per observation.
  • We don't care about individual εᵢ so much as the norm ‖ε‖², which is what the fit minimises. That single number is the objective of the whole procedure.
  • Orthogonality check: εᵀŷ = 0 and Xᵀε = 0 should hold to machine precision. In practice you'll see something like 1e−14 instead of exactly 0 — that's rounding error, not a bug.
  • R² normalises the residual sum of squares by the total variance of y: the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the design matrix. R² = 1 is a perfect fit; R² = 0 means the model does no better than the mean.
minimise this‖ε‖² = ‖y − Xβ‖² = Σᵢ εᵢ² fit qualityR² = 1 − (Σᵢ εᵢ²) / (Σᵢ (yᵢ − ȳ)²) = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot

Drag the line off the best fit and watch the squared error grow — it bottoms out exactly at the least-squares solution.

Worked example: three models on eight numbers

Take a signal recorded at eight successive times: y = [−4, 0, −3, 1, 2, 8, 5, 8], x = 1…8. Watch the design matrix grow.

model 1 — a constant: y = β₁ + εX = column of eight 1s XᵀX = 8 Xᵀy = 17 β = 17/8 = 2.125 model 2 — a slope with no intercept: y = β₁x + εX = [1,2,…,8]ᵀ XᵀX = 204 Xᵀy = 148 β = 148/204 = 0.7255 model 3 — intercept + slope: y = β₁ + β₂x + εXᵀX = [[8, 36], [36, 204]] Xᵀy = [17, 148]ᵀ β = [−5.5357, 1.7024]ᵀ
  • Model 1 re-derives the mean. Xᵀy sums the data, XᵀX counts the observations, so β = Σyᵢ/M — the arithmetic mean falls out of least-squares when your hypothesis is "one number explains everything". It fits badly here: the data clearly trend upward.
  • Why the column of 1s? Any constant would work — set the column to 9.8 and β becomes 2.125/9.8 = 0.2168, meaning "the level is β × 9.8". Mathematically identical, interpretively awful. Ones keep β in the units of the data.
  • Model 2 over-predicts early and under-predicts late, because with no intercept the line is nailed to the origin.
  • Model 3 gives ŷ = [−3.833, −2.131, −0.429, 1.274, 2.976, 4.679, 6.381, 8.083] and residuals ε = [−0.167, 2.131, −2.571, −0.274, −0.976, 3.321, −1.381, −0.083]. Then SS_res = 25.15, SS_tot = 146.88, so R² = 0.829 — the line explains 83% of the variance and the leftovers look like noise. Confirm εᵀŷ ≈ 0: you'll get ~1e−14.
  • The intercept −5.54 is where the line would cross x = 0; the data start at x = 1, so you never see it, but it is what lets the line sit where it belongs.

When it breaks

  • Collinear predictors ⟹ XᵀX singular ⟹ no left inverse. Symptoms: wildly unstable coefficients, huge condition number (§16). Fixes: drop a redundant column, or ridge-regularize by shifting to XᵀX + λI.
  • Wide design matrix (N > M) ⟹ infinitely many exact solutions; you need a criterion to pick one (minimum-norm via the pseudoinverse, §16, or a penalty).
  • Nonlinear-in-parameters models are outside linear least-squares entirely; they need iterative nonlinear optimisation.
  • Numerics: forming XᵀX explicitly squares the condition number. Production solvers use QR (§13) or the SVD (§16) instead — numpy.linalg.lstsq rather than an explicit inverse.
This is regression: the fitted coefficients are the pseudoinverse times the data, and "best fit" literally means the projection of the targets onto what the features can express. The residual is what the theory cannot reach. Full ML treatment: Linear & Logistic Regression.
