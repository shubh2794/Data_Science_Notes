17 Quadratic form

Squeeze a whole matrix into one number: xᵀAx. Sweep x over the plane and that number traces an energy landscape — a bowl, a dome, a saddle, or a trough — whose shape is fixed entirely by A's eigenvalues.

Algebraic perspective

  • Pre- and post-multiply a square A (m×m) by the same vector x (m×1). Sizes: (1×m)(m×m)(m×1) = 1×1. So m² + 2m numbers collapse into a single scalar — call it ζ.
  • Expanded elementwise it is a second-degree polynomial — hence "quadratic": xᵀAx = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢⱼ xᵢxⱼ. Diagonal entries are the coefficients on the squared terms; off-diagonals are the coefficients on the cross terms.
  • Only the symmetric part of A matters: the cross-term xy picks up b + c, so A and (A + Aᵀ)/2 give the identical surface. Any skew-symmetric part contributes exactly zero. This is why the quadratic form is usually stated for symmetric matrices — nothing is lost.
  • Two structural facts, free of charge: q(0) = 0 always (every surface passes through zero at the origin), and q(cx) = c²·q(x) — scaling x scales the form quadratically, and the sign never flips. So the form only cares about direction plus a c² stretch.
  • When A = I, xᵀIx = ‖x‖². Putting a different matrix in the middle modulates the length measurement — this is exactly how non-Euclidean (and Mahalanobis) distances are defined.
  • Complex case: use the Hermitian transpose, xᴴAx. If A is Hermitian (Aᴴ = A) the form is real-valued — and since Hermitian eigenvalues are real too, everything below about definiteness carries over unchanged.
2×2 expansion[x y] [[a, b], [c, d]] [x; y] = ax² + (b + c)xy + dy² 3×3, symmetric [[a, f, d], [f, b, e], [d, e, c]]ax² + by² + cz² + 2f·xy + 2d·xz + 2e·yz

Geometric perspective — the energy surface

  • Treat x = [x₁, x₂] as a coordinate plane and ζ = xᵀAx as height above it. The resulting 3-D surface is the quadratic form surface: a map of how much "energy" the matrix has in each direction.
  • Moving away from the origin, every direction does exactly one of three things: climb to +∞, fall to −∞, or stay flat at 0. Combinations give the four archetypes: bowl (up everywhere), dome (down everywhere), saddle (up along some directions, down along others), trough (up in one direction, flat along another).
  • Directions climb at different rates — the surface is anisotropic. Its level sets are ellipses (bowl/dome) or hyperbolas (saddle), and the ellipse axes are exactly the eigenvector directions.
  • The picture only literally works for 2×2. For m×m the surface is an m-D hypersheet over an m-D space, but the story is identical: zero at the origin, and up/down/flat in different directions.
  • Spot check: A = [[1, 1], [1, 1]] at x = [−2, 1]ᵀ gives xᵀA = [−1, −1] and ζ = 1. Being rank 1, this matrix is a trough: flat zero along [1, −1], climbing perpendicular to it.

The normalized quadratic form (Rayleigh quotient)

  • The problem: Cauchy–Schwarz twice (once on (x)ᵀ(Ax), once on Ax) gives |xᵀAx| ≤ ‖A‖_F ‖x‖². The form's magnitude is contaminated by the length of the vector we chose to probe with — argmax_x {xᵀAx} is trivially unbounded.
  • The fix: divide out the c². Normalizing by xᵀx = ‖x‖² makes the form scale-invariant — it depends only on the direction of x. This ratio is the Rayleigh quotient. Undefined only at x = 0.
  • Now the bound reads |xᵀAx| / xᵀx ≤ ‖A‖_F — bounded by the matrix alone. Equivalently: maximizing the normalized form is the same as maximizing xᵀAx subject to ‖x‖ = 1.
  • Sanity check: for A = I the normalized surface is the constant sheet 1 — the identity has the same energy in every direction, which is what "no preferred direction" should look like.
  • The payoff: for symmetric A the quotient is bounded by the extreme eigenvalues, λ_min ≤ R(x) ≤ λ_max, and the bounds are attained at the corresponding eigenvectors. This variational characterization — eigenvectors as maximizers of a normalized energy — is literally the derivation of PCA (§19).
normalized quadratic form / Rayleigh quotientR(x) = xᵀAx / xᵀx, x ≠ 0 λ_min ≤ R(x) ≤ λ_max argmax_x R(x) = eigenvector of λ_max
check itA = [[2, 2], [2, 3]]: λ = (5 ± √17)/2 ≈ 0.438, 4.562 x = [1, 1]ᵀ: R = 9/2 = 4.50 x = [1, 0]ᵀ: R = 2 both inside [0.438, 4.562] ✓

Eigenvectors are the ridges and valleys

  • The normalized surface has a ridge (direction of maximal energy) and a valley (minimal), plus m − 2 intermediate saddle directions. An m×m matrix has m important directions.
  • For a symmetric A, those directions are exactly the eigenvectors: the eigenvector of λ_max runs along the ridge, the eigenvector of λ_min along the valley, and intermediate eigenvalues give saddle lines. Non-symmetric matrices lose this guarantee.
  • Symmetry also makes the principal axes mutually orthogonal (§15) — so the contour ellipses are a clean, un-sheared set of axes. The eigenvalue is the curvature along its axis: large λ ⟹ steep climb, λ = 0 ⟹ flat trough, λ < 0 ⟹ descent.
  • Read a contour ellipse backwards and you recover the matrix: axis orientation = eigenvectors, axis length ∝ 1/√λ for a level set of xᵀAx = 1. Small eigenvalue ⟹ long, floppy axis.

Definiteness — the matrix's multidimensional sign

  • Definition (algebraic): the sign of xᵀAx over all x ≠ 0. (Geometric): whether the surface sits above, below, or straddles the plane away from the origin. Categories are exclusive — one label per matrix (with the zeros matrix as the perverse exception: it is both positive and negative semidefinite).

  • Staring at surfaces is fun but not scalable — read the eigenvalue signs. Magnitudes are irrelevant; only signs matter. Singular values cannot do this job: they are non-negative by construction, so they carry no sign information.
  • Definite ⟹ no zero eigenvalue ⟹ invertible. Semidefinite ⟹ at least one zero eigenvalue ⟹ singular. Indefinite is unpredictable.
  • Random square matrices are almost always indefinite — definiteness is special structure, not the default. It shows up when a matrix is built a certain way (next).

Why AᵀA is always positive (semi)definite

  • One re-parenthesization does the whole proof: xᵀ(AᵀA)x = (xᵀAᵀ)(Ax) = (Ax)ᵀ(Ax) = ‖Ax‖² ≥ 0. Squared magnitudes can't be negative, so AᵀA can never be indefinite or negative (semi)definite.
  • When is it exactly zero for some x ≠ 0? Only when Ax = 0 — i.e. A has a non-trivial null space. So: AᵀA is PD ⟺ A has full column rank; PSD (and singular) otherwise. Same argument, same conclusion, for AAᵀ (PD ⟺ full row rank).
  • Careful with the converse: every AᵀA is symmetric, but not every symmetric matrix is some AᵀA. Symmetry alone does not imply positive (semi)definiteness — [[−9, 4], [4, 9]] is symmetric with λ = ±√97 ≈ ±9.85: indefinite, a saddle.
  • Factoring a symmetric PSD matrix as LLᵀ is precisely the Cholesky decomposition, and it exists only for positive (semi)definite matrices — a practical definiteness test in itself.

Eigenvalues ⟺ definiteness (the proof)

  • Start from the eigenvalue equation and left-multiply by the eigenvector: Av = λv ⟹ vᵀAv = λ vᵀv = λ‖v‖². Since ‖v‖² > 0, the sign of the quadratic form is the sign of λ.
  • Assume PD: the left side is positive for all v ≠ 0, so every λ must be positive. Assume PSD: the left side is non-negative, so no λ can be negative (and at least one is zero, since the matrix is singular). Flip the assumption and you get the negative categories for free.
  • But that only used eigenvectors — the form is defined for all vectors. The bridge: if the eigenvectors span the space (guaranteed for symmetric A), then any x = Σ αᵢvᵢ, and orthogonality collapses the form to xᵀAx = Σ αᵢ²λᵢ. The αᵢ² are non-negative, so the eigenvalue signs alone dictate the sign of the whole form. The implication runs both ways.
Av = λv ⟹ vᵀAv = λ‖v‖² x = Σᵢ αᵢvᵢ ⟹ xᵀAx = Σᵢ αᵢ²λᵢ PD ⟺ all λ > 0
Why ML cares: near a minimum, any smooth loss is a quadratic form in its Hessian — positive definite ⟹ locally convex, a unique minimum, and gradient descent that converges (with the condition number λ_max/λ_min setting how badly it zig-zags). Indefinite Hessian ⟹ a saddle, the real obstacle in deep-net optimization. And the covariance matrix (§18) is a XᵀX-shaped matrix, hence always PSD — which is what makes PCA (§19) work at all.

18 Covariance matrices

Statistics turns out to be linear algebra in disguise: variance is a dot product with itself, covariance is a dot product, correlation is a cosine. One symmetric PSD matrix then holds every pairwise relationship in the dataset.

Correlation: what we are actually after

  • The goal is to quantify commonality between variables — do height and weight rise and fall together? Plot one against the other and ask how well a straight line describes the cloud.
  • Correlation is bivariate: defined between exactly two variables. A correlation matrix is just a tidy container for all the pairwise numbers.
  • r ∈ [−1, +1]: −1 = perfect inverse relationship, 0 = no linear relationship, +1 = perfect direct relationship.
  • Gotcha — linearity only. A strong but non-monotonic relationship (a parabola, a ring) can produce r ≈ 0 while the variables are fully determined by one another. Mutual information catches what correlation misses; alternatively transform the variables to linearize the relationship first.

Variance and standard deviation

  • Variance is a measure of dispersion: the average squared distance of each value from the mean. Small when the data huddle near the mean, large when they scatter.
  • Mean-centering — subtracting x̄ so the average is 0 — is the move that turns a statistic into a vector operation. Write x̃ = x − x̄ and variance becomes x̃ᵀx̃/(n − 1): a vector's dot product with itself, i.e. its squared length, rescaled.
  • Why n − 1 and not n: dividing by something that grows with the data is what keeps variance from inflating just because you collected more points (same logic as the mean). Using n − 1 (the sample variance) accounts for the degree of freedom spent estimating the mean — once you know n − 1 centered values and the mean, the last one is determined. Use n only when the data are the whole population. Watch your library defaults: some standard-deviation functions divide by n unless you ask for ddof=1.
  • Standard deviation σ = √(σ²) — the principal (positive) root, because a measure of spread can no more be negative than a length can. It lives in the units of the data, which variance does not.
  • Symbol clash to keep straight: σ is a standard deviation in statistics and a singular value in the SVD (§16). Context disambiguates.
variance (sample)σ² = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)² = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)(xᵢ − x̄) as linear algebra, with x mean-centeredσ² = xᵀx / (n − 1) = ‖x‖² / (n − 1) σ = √(σ²)

Covariance — the dot product of two centered variables

  • "Covariance" = variance between two variables. Take the variance formula and replace one copy of the centered x with a centered y. Both vectors must have the same number of observations.
  • Sign is the message: positive when the two tend to sit on the same side of their means at the same time, negative when they sit on opposite sides, near zero when the products cancel.
  • Magnitude is not comparable across variables — covariance carries the product of the two units, so multiplying the data by 1000 multiplies the covariance by 10⁶. That scale-dependence is exactly what correlation fixes.
  • Mean-centered, the whole thing is xᵀy/(n − 1). In many applications (comparing covariances that share the same n) the 1/(n − 1) is a constant that cancels, and covariance is just a dot product. Every geometric intuition about dot products (§03) is now an intuition about statistics.
  • Center first, always. Skipping the centering step leaves the means baked into the products; the diagonal stops being variance and PCA (§19) produces components that point at the data cloud's offset from the origin rather than at its structure.
covariancec_xy = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = xᵀy / (n − 1) (x, y mean-centered)

The correlation coefficient is a cosine

  • Divide the covariance by the two standard deviations and the units cancel: r = c_xy / (σ_x σ_y). The 1/(n − 1) appears once above and twice under the roots below, so it cancels outright in the raw-sum form.
  • In vector terms this is mapping over magnitude: r = xᵀy / (‖x‖‖y‖) — precisely the geometric dot-product formula for cos θ. Pearson correlation is the cosine of the angle between the two mean-centered data vectors.
  • That immediately explains the bounds: Cauchy–Schwarz gives |xᵀy| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖, so −1 ≤ r ≤ 1, with equality exactly when the two centered vectors are collinear. Set y = x and numerator = denominator ⟹ r = 1.
  • If the data are unit-normed the denominator is 1 and correlation is the dot product. If the vectors are not mean-centered, the same formula is called cosine similarity — identical algebra, different interpretation (and different answers whenever the means are non-zero).

Covariance matrices

  • Stack the variables into a data matrix X — observations in rows, features in columns, exactly the design-matrix layout of least-squares (§14). Mean-center each column.
  • All pairwise covariances at once: C = XᵀX/(n − 1). Entry Cᵢⱼ is the dot product of centered feature i with centered feature j — matrix multiplication is a machine for computing every dot product simultaneously.
  • Orientation is the #1 bug. There is nothing sacred about transposing on the left: if the data are stored features-by-observations, use C = XXᵀ. The rule that never changes: the covariance matrix must come out features-by-features. Check the output size before believing the numbers.
  • Diagonal = variances (each feature dotted with itself), off-diagonal = covariances. This holds only if the columns were mean-centered.
  • C is symmetric — algebraically obvious from (XᵀX)ᵀ = XᵀX, and statistically sensible: the relationship of height to weight is the relationship of weight to height.
  • C is positive semidefinite, being of the form XᵀX (§17) — PD when the features are linearly independent, singular the moment one feature is a linear combination of others (or when features outnumber observations). Symmetric + PSD is the ideal case for eigendecomposition: real non-negative eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.

Correlation matrix ⟷ covariance matrix

  • Let S = diag(σ₁, …, σ_m) hold the feature standard deviations. Then R = S⁻¹ C S⁻¹ and, inverting, C = S R S. R has ones on its diagonal by construction, since each feature correlates perfectly with itself.
  • Push the parentheses around and the meaning appears: R = (XS⁻¹)ᵀ(XS⁻¹)/(n − 1). The correlation matrix is the covariance matrix of the rescaled data X̃ = XS⁻¹ — each feature divided by its own standard deviation. (Do not collapse that to a norm — XS⁻¹ is a matrix, not a vector.)
  • Since the data were already centered, dividing by σ is exactly z-scoring. Hence: on z-scored data, covariance and correlation are the same matrix.
  • Practical consequence: covariance-based PCA is dominated by whichever feature happens to have the biggest units; correlation-based PCA (equivalently, PCA on z-scored data) puts all features on equal footing. Choose deliberately — see §19.
S = diag(σ₁, …, σ_m)R = S⁻¹ C S⁻¹ = (XS⁻¹)ᵀ(XS⁻¹)/(n − 1) C = S R S diag(R) = [1, 1, …, 1]
The bridge: covariance is the dot product, correlation is the cosine — statistics inherits every geometric fact you already know about vectors. And because C is symmetric and PSD, its eigendecomposition is guaranteed clean: real, non-negative eigenvalues with orthogonal eigenvectors. That decomposition is PCA (§19).

19 PCA the linear-algebra view

Where everything converges: the quadratic form, covariance, eigendecomposition, and the SVD are four views of one algorithm. PCA finds a new basis whose axes are ordered by variance and mutually orthogonal. This is the linear-algebra derivation — for the ML angle (uses, scaling, when it fails) see the dedicated Dimensionality Reduction (PCA) page.

What PCA is looking for

  • A dataset with m features is a cloud of points in ℝ^m, and the raw features are its basis vectors. PCA asks: is this the best basis? Its answer: pick basis vectors that successively maximize variance while staying orthogonal to all previous ones.
  • "Maximize variance" concretely means: find weights w, one per feature, such that the weighted combination Xw has the largest possible variance. Two positively correlated channels get weights of the same sign (they reinforce); two negatively correlated channels get opposite signs, flipping one so it adds instead of cancels.
  • Without a constraint this is trivial — scale w up and variance grows forever. PCA therefore requires ‖w‖ = 1: the sum of squared weights is 1, so only the direction of the weighting is free. This is exactly the normalization of §17.
  • PC2 is the direction that maximizes the residual variance — everything PC1 did not explain — subject to being orthogonal to PC1. In 2-D there is only one such direction; in higher dimensions there are many, and the constraint chain continues.
  • Two outputs, don't confuse them. Loadings (weights) = the eigenvectors: how much each original feature contributes to a component. Scores = the data projected onto those eigenvectors: the new coordinates of each observation. Eigenvalues = the variance along each component.
  • Geometrically PCA is a pure rotation. The components are orthonormal, so the score plot is the mean-centered cloud rigidly rotated onto its own principal axes — no stretching, no distortion. And because the axes are now the eigenvectors of C, the covariance matrix in the new basis is diagonal: the components are uncorrelated. (Dividing each score by √λᵢ on top of that is whitening.)

Applications, and how many components to keep

  • Dimensionality reduction / compression: keep the top k < r components and discard the rest, on the assumption that low-variance directions are noise. This is the low-rank approximation of §16 wearing a statistics hat.
  • Denoising (reconstruct from the top components), visualization (plot scores on PC1 vs PC2), decorrelation/whitening (feed uncorrelated features to a downstream model), and exploration (read the loadings to see which features move together).
  • An m-feature dataset yields exactly m components, because the eigendecomposition of an m×m covariance matrix does. Choosing how many to keep is partly a judgement call: the qualitative tool is the scree plot of sorted eigenvalues (§16) — look for the elbow — or a cumulative-variance threshold. Quantitative thresholds exist but are statistical machinery beyond the algebra.
  • Eigenvalues are occasionally called latent factor scores; converted to percentages they are the familiar percent variance explained.

How to perform a PCA

  1. Mean-center every feature. Non-negotiable — see the failure mode below.
  2. Form the covariance matrix C = XᵀX/(n − 1) (§18). The data matrix may be observations-by-features or features-by-observations; C must come out features-by-features.
  3. Eigendecompose C. It is symmetric PSD, so the eigenvalues are real and non-negative and the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
  4. Sort eigenvalues descending and reorder the eigenvectors to match. Eigenvalues have no intrinsic ordering and are glued to their eigenvectors — sorting one without the other silently corrupts everything downstream.
  5. Compute component scores: project the data onto the eigenvectors, scores = XW (column j of W weighting all features for component j). If the data are time series these are the "component time series".
  6. Convert eigenvalues to percent variance explained: 100·λᵢ/Σλ — the same recipe used for singular values.
the whole algorithmX ← X − mean(X) → C = XᵀX/(n − 1) → C = WΛWᵀ → scores = XW → var explained_i = λᵢ/Σλ

Equivalently: the SVD of the centered data

  • Take X = UΣVᵀ on the mean-centered data matrix. Then XᵀX/(n − 1) = V(ΣᵀΣ)Vᵀ/(n − 1), which is already an eigendecomposition. So the right singular vectors V are the principal components, and the eigenvalues are λᵢ = σᵢ²/(n − 1). Scores are XV = UΣ.
  • Neither route is theoretically preferred. SVD is numerically more stable (it never forms XᵀX, which squares the condition number). Eigendecomposition of C wins on large datasets: the covariance matrix is small, cheap to store, and easy to build incrementally or from a filtered subset of the data.
  • numpy: np.linalg.eigh on the covariance matrix (use eigh, not eig, for symmetric input), or np.linalg.svd on the centered data — then square the singular values and divide by n − 1 to line them up with the eigenvalues.
two roads, one destinationC = XᵀX/(n − 1) = WΛWᵀ X = UΣVᵀ ⟹ W = V, λᵢ = σᵢ²/(n − 1) scores = XW = UΣ

The algebra: why eigenvectors are the answer

  • State the goal formally: the variance of the projection Xw is wᵀCw — the quadratic form of the covariance matrix. So PCA is argmax_w {wᵀCw} subject to ‖w‖ = 1, equivalently argmax_w {wᵀCw / wᵀw}: maximize the normalized quadratic form of §17.
  • Calculus route: the Lagrangian L = wᵀCw − λ(wᵀw − 1) has gradient 2Cw − 2λw. Setting it to zero gives Cw = λw — the eigenvalue equation falls out of the optimization. Left-multiply by wᵀ: the achieved variance is wᵀCw = λ. So the maximizer is the top eigenvector and the maximum is λ₁.
  • Linear-algebra route (all m solutions at once): collect candidate vectors as columns of W. A matrix can't sit in a denominator, so multiply by an inverse instead — Λ = (WᵀW)⁻¹(WᵀCW) = W⁻¹W⁻ᵀWᵀCW = W⁻¹CW, hence CW = WΛ. The eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix is the solution to the maximization problem.
  • Interpretation of the two outputs of that equation: Λ holds the maximal energy values of the quadratic form surface — the variances — and the columns of W are the ridge/valley directions of that surface: the principal components.
  • Because C is PD (full-rank case), its normalized quadratic form surface is positive everywhere — a bowl. PC1 runs along the ridge, PC2 along the valley, exactly as §17 predicts.
the optimizationw_max = argmax_w {wᵀCw}, s.t. ‖w‖ = 1 ⟺ argmax_w { wᵀCw / wᵀw } the solutionCw = λw CW = WΛ max variance = λ₁

Mean-centering is not optional

  • Principal components are vectors from the origin. If the cloud is offset from the origin, the direction of largest wᵀ(XᵀX)w is the direction pointing from the origin to the center of the cloud — which says nothing about the cloud's internal structure.
  • The result is mathematically correct and analytically useless: PC1 points at the mean, PC2 is forced orthogonal to it, and both ignore the covariance you actually wanted. Centering removes the offset so the components describe shape rather than location.
  • Centering is also what makes diag(C) equal the variances (§18) — without it the eigenvalues aren't variances at all.

Regularization: shrinkage

  • The problem: covariance matrices are often near-singular — more features than observations, collinear features, or tiny noise-dominated eigenvalues. The condition number explodes, the inverse is unstable, and the smallest components are numerical fiction.
  • Shrinkage replaces C with a blend of C and a scaled identity: C̃ = (1 − γ)C + γαI, with α the mean eigenvalue of C (= tr(C)/m). That α matters: it puts the shift on the same numerical scale as the data, so "1% regularization" means the same thing whatever the units.
  • What it does to the spectrum: eigenvectors are unchanged, and each eigenvalue maps to (1 − γ)λᵢ + γα — every eigenvalue is pulled toward the common value α. Big ones shrink, small ones grow. Hence the name, and hence a smaller condition number. Any γ > 0 turns a rank-deficient covariance matrix into a full-rank one.
  • Extremes: γ = 0 leaves C untouched; γ = 1 replaces it entirely with αI, destroying all covariance structure. The working rule is as little as possible, as much as necessary; γ is usually tuned by cross-validation.
  • This is the same "shift the diagonal" move as ridge regression and Tikhonov regularization — a bias-for-variance trade that buys numerical stability and better generalization.
shrinkage regularizationC̃ = (1 − γ)C + γαI, α = (1/m) Σᵢ λᵢ = tr(C)/m λ̃ᵢ = (1 − γ)λᵢ + γα on the worked example: α = 4, γ = 0.1λ: 7.727, 0.273 → 7.354, 0.646 condition number: 28.3 → 11.4

Is PCA always the best?

  • Variance ≠ relevance. PCA assumes the interesting structure lives in the directions of greatest spread. With noisy measurements the leading components can be pure noise; and a real effect confined to a few channels can be buried below components that merely track a loud, uninformative one.
  • The classic failure: data forming an "X" of two crossing arms. PC1 splits the difference and points between the two arms, PC2 sits orthogonal to it — technically the maximum-variance direction, but not the structure a human sees. PCA recovers decorrelated axes, not the underlying sources.
  • PCA is linear and constrained to orthogonal components. If the true latent factors are correlated, or the manifold is curved, no rotation will separate them — reach for kernel PCA, autoencoders, or a manifold method.
  • ICA optimizes a different criterion: statistical independence (not just zero correlation), with no orthogonality requirement. On the "X" data it recovers the two arms — the data come out demixed rather than merely decorrelated. Different criterion, different assumptions, not universally better.
  • PCA is unsupervised — it never sees the labels. If the goal is class separation, a supervised projection like LDA (maximize between-class over within-class scatter) can find a low-variance direction that PCA would have discarded.
  • Scaling is a modelling decision: raw-covariance PCA is dominated by whichever feature has the largest units; correlation-based PCA (z-scored data, §18) treats every feature equally. Neither is automatically right.
Everything converges here: PCA = maximizing a normalized quadratic form (§17) = eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix (§18) = SVD of the centered data (§16). Vectors, projection, basis, orthogonality, eigenvalues, and the SVD all meet in one algorithm. Full ML treatment: Dimensionality Reduction (PCA).
