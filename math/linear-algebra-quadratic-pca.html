17 Quadratic form

Squeeze a whole matrix into one number: xᵀAx. Sweep x over the plane and that number traces an energy landscape — a bowl, a dome, a saddle, or a trough — whose shape is fixed entirely by A's eigenvalues.

Algebraic perspective

  • Pre- and post-multiply a square A (m×m) by the same vector x (m×1). Sizes: (1×m)(m×m)(m×1) = 1×1. So m² + 2m numbers collapse into a single scalar — call it ζ.
  • Expanded elementwise it is a second-degree polynomial — hence "quadratic": xᵀAx = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢⱼ xᵢxⱼ. Diagonal entries are the coefficients on the squared terms; off-diagonals are the coefficients on the cross terms.
  • Only the symmetric part of A matters: the cross-term xy picks up b + c, so A and (A + Aᵀ)/2 give the identical surface. Any skew-symmetric part contributes exactly zero. This is why the quadratic form is usually stated for symmetric matrices — nothing is lost.
  • Two structural facts, free of charge: q(0) = 0 always (every surface passes through zero at the origin), and q(cx) = c²·q(x) — scaling x scales the form quadratically, and the sign never flips. So the form only cares about direction plus a c² stretch.
  • When A = I, xᵀIx = ‖x‖². Putting a different matrix in the middle modulates the length measurement — this is exactly how non-Euclidean (and Mahalanobis) distances are defined.
  • Complex case: use the Hermitian transpose, xᴴAx. If A is Hermitian (Aᴴ = A) the form is real-valued — and since Hermitian eigenvalues are real too, everything below about definiteness carries over unchanged.

Geometric perspective — the energy surface

  • Treat x = [x₁, x₂] as a coordinate plane and ζ = xᵀAx as height above it. The resulting 3-D surface is the quadratic form surface: a map of how much "energy" the matrix has in each direction.
  • Moving away from the origin, every direction does exactly one of three things: climb to +∞, fall to −∞, or stay flat at 0. Combinations give the four archetypes: bowl (up everywhere), dome (down everywhere), saddle (up along some directions, down along others), trough (up in one direction, flat along another).
  • Directions climb at different rates — the surface is anisotropic. Its level sets are ellipses (bowl/dome) or hyperbolas (saddle), and the ellipse axes are exactly the eigenvector directions.
  • The picture only literally works for 2×2. For m×m the surface is an m-D hypersheet over an m-D space, but the story is identical: zero at the origin, and up/down/flat in different directions.
  • Spot check: A = [[1, 1], [1, 1]] at x = [−2, 1]ᵀ gives xᵀA = [−1, −1] and ζ = 1. Being rank 1, this matrix is a trough: flat zero along [1, −1], climbing perpendicular to it.

The normalized quadratic form (Rayleigh quotient)

  • The problem: Cauchy–Schwarz twice (once on (x)ᵀ(Ax), once on Ax) gives |xᵀAx| ≤ ‖A‖_F ‖x‖². The form's magnitude is contaminated by the length of the vector we chose to probe with — argmax_x {xᵀAx} is trivially unbounded.
  • The fix: divide out the c². Normalizing by xᵀx = ‖x‖² makes the form scale-invariant — it depends only on the direction of x. This ratio is the Rayleigh quotient. Undefined only at x = 0.
  • Now the bound reads |xᵀAx| / xᵀx ≤ ‖A‖_F — bounded by the matrix alone. Equivalently: maximizing the normalized form is the same as maximizing xᵀAx subject to ‖x‖ = 1.
  • Sanity check: for A = I the normalized surface is the constant sheet 1 — the identity has the same energy in every direction, which is what "no preferred direction" should look like.
  • The payoff: for symmetric A the quotient is bounded by the extreme eigenvalues, λ_min ≤ R(x) ≤ λ_max, and the bounds are attained at the corresponding eigenvectors. This variational characterization — eigenvectors as maximizers of a normalized energy — is literally the derivation of PCA (§19).

Eigenvectors are the ridges and valleys

  • The normalized surface has a ridge (direction of maximal energy) and a valley (minimal), plus m − 2 intermediate saddle directions. An m×m matrix has m important directions.
  • For a symmetric A, those directions are exactly the eigenvectors: the eigenvector of λ_max runs along the ridge, the eigenvector of λ_min along the valley, and intermediate eigenvalues give saddle lines. Non-symmetric matrices lose this guarantee.
  • Symmetry also makes the principal axes mutually orthogonal (§15) — so the contour ellipses are a clean, un-sheared set of axes. The eigenvalue is the curvature along its axis: large λ ⟹ steep climb, λ = 0 ⟹ flat trough, λ < 0 ⟹ descent.
  • Read a contour ellipse backwards and you recover the matrix: axis orientation = eigenvectors, axis length ∝ 1/√λ for a level set of xᵀAx = 1. Small eigenvalue ⟹ long, floppy axis.

Definiteness — the matrix's multidimensional sign

  • Definition (algebraic): the sign of xᵀAx over all x ≠ 0. (Geometric): whether the surface sits above, below, or straddles the plane away from the origin. Categories are exclusive — one label per matrix (with the zeros matrix as the perverse exception: it is both positive and negative semidefinite).

  • Definite ⟹ no zero eigenvalue ⟹ invertible. Semidefinite ⟹ at least one zero eigenvalue ⟹ singular. Indefinite is unpredictable.
  • Random square matrices are almost always indefinite — definiteness is special structure, not the default. It shows up when a matrix is built a certain way (next).

Why AᵀA is always positive (semi)definite

  • One re-parenthesization does the whole proof: xᵀ(AᵀA)x = (xᵀAᵀ)(Ax) = (Ax)ᵀ(Ax) = ‖Ax‖² ≥ 0. Squared magnitudes can't be negative, so AᵀA can never be indefinite or negative (semi)definite.
  • When is it exactly zero for some x ≠ 0? Only when Ax = 0 — i.e. A has a non-trivial null space. So: AᵀA is PD ⟺ A has full column rank; PSD (and singular) otherwise. Same argument, same conclusion, for AAᵀ (PD ⟺ full row rank).
  • Careful with the converse: every AᵀA is symmetric, but not every symmetric matrix is some AᵀA. Symmetry alone does not imply positive (semi)definiteness — [[−9, 4], [4, 9]] is symmetric with λ = ±√97 ≈ ±9.85: indefinite, a saddle.
  • Factoring a symmetric PSD matrix as LLᵀ is precisely the Cholesky decomposition, and it exists only for positive (semi)definite matrices — a practical definiteness test in itself.

Eigenvalues ⟺ definiteness (the proof)

  • Start from the eigenvalue equation and left-multiply by the eigenvector: Av = λv ⟹ vᵀAv = λ vᵀv = λ‖v‖². Since ‖v‖² > 0, the sign of the quadratic form is the sign of λ.
  • Assume PD: the left side is positive for all v ≠ 0, so every λ must be positive. Assume PSD: the left side is non-negative, so no λ can be negative (and at least one is zero, since the matrix is singular). Flip the assumption and you get the negative categories for free.
  • But that only used eigenvectors — the form is defined for all vectors. The bridge: if the eigenvectors span the space (guaranteed for symmetric A), then any x = Σ αᵢvᵢ, and orthogonality collapses the form to xᵀAx = Σ αᵢ²λᵢ. The αᵢ² are non-negative, so the eigenvalue signs alone dictate the sign of the whole form. The implication runs both ways.
Av = λv ⟹ vᵀAv = λ‖v‖² x = Σᵢ αᵢvᵢ ⟹ xᵀAx = Σᵢ αᵢ²λᵢ PD ⟺ all λ > 0

Sylvester's criterion — a determinant test for definiteness

  • Eigenvalues answer the definiteness question completely, but they cost an O(m³) iterative decomposition and give you far more information than the question needs. There is a determinant test that needs no eigenvalues at all.
  • The leading principal minors of an m×m matrix are the determinants D₁, D₂, …, D_m of the top-left 1×1, 2×2, …, m×m blocks. Sylvester's criterion: a symmetric A is positive definite ⟺ every Dₖ > 0. Flip signs for the other end: A is negative definite ⟺ (−1)ᵏDₖ > 0, i.e. the minors alternate −, +, −, +, … (which is just Sylvester applied to −A).
  • Why it works — an induction on the block determinant identity |A| = |A_{m−1}|·(a_{mm} − aᵀA_{m−1}⁻¹a) for the partition A = [[A_{m−1}, a], [aᵀ, a_{mm}]]. If A_{m−1} is positive definite then |A_{m−1}| > 0, so |A| > 0 forces the Schur complement a_{mm} − aᵀA_{m−1}⁻¹a to be positive — and a positive definite block plus a positive Schur complement is a positive definite matrix. Start the induction at 1×1 and climb.
  • The trap: this does not extend to the semidefinite case. For PSD you must check all 2^m − 1 principal minors (every symmetric choice of rows and columns), not just the leading ones. The standard counterexample is A = [[0, 0], [0, −1]]: its leading minors are D₁ = 0 and D₂ = 0, both non-negative, yet xᵀAx = −y² — the matrix is negative semidefinite. The leading minors sample only one nested chain of submatrices and can miss trouble hiding elsewhere on the diagonal.
  • Free necessary conditions you can check by eye, from the definition alone with x = eᵢ and x = eᵢ + yeⱼ: every diagonal entry of a PSD matrix satisfies aᵢᵢ ≥ 0, and every off-diagonal satisfies aᵢⱼ² ≤ aᵢᵢaⱼⱼ (strict for PD). A symmetric matrix with a negative diagonal entry, or with an off-diagonal entry larger in magnitude than the geometric mean of its two diagonal partners, is immediately disqualified. For a correlation matrix the second condition is just |r| ≤ 1.
  • 2×2, the case you will use most. A = [[a, b], [b, d]] is positive definite ⟺ a > 0 and ad − b² > 0; indefinite ⟺ det < 0; semidefinite ⟺ det = 0 with a, d ≥ 0. Note that det = λ₁λ₂ and tr = λ₁ + λ₂, so the determinant alone separates "saddle" from "bowl or dome" and the trace then picks which — which is exactly the tr and det readout under the landscape above. Drag the sliders until det crosses zero and watch the contours flip from ellipses to hyperbolas.
  • Worked. [[2, 2], [2, 3]]: D₁ = 2 > 0, D₂ = 6 − 4 = 2 > 0 ⟹ positive definite ✓ (its eigenvalues ≈ 0.438, 4.562 agree). [[1, 2], [2, 1]]: D₁ = 1 > 0, D₂ = 1 − 4 = −3 < 0 ⟹ indefinite ✓ (λ = 3, −1). [[−9, 4], [4, 9]]: D₁ = −9 < 0 and D₂ = −81 − 16 < 0 — neither pattern ⟹ indefinite ✓.
  • Cost, honestly. Sylvester is a clean theoretical test but a poor computational one: m determinants naively cost O(m⁴), and determinants of large matrices overflow or underflow long before they lose accuracy. In practice nobody computes minors — they run a Cholesky factorization and watch for it to fail, which is the same test done in O(m³/3) flops.

Cholesky — definiteness you can compute

  • Every symmetric positive definite matrix factors uniquely as A = LLᵀ with L lower triangular and strictly positive diagonal. Conversely, if such an L exists then xᵀAx = ‖Lᵀx‖² > 0 for x ≠ 0 (since Lᵀ is invertible), so A is positive definite. The factorization exists ⟺ the matrix is positive definite — the algorithm is the test.
  • The algorithm, and where it breaks. Compare entries on both sides of A = LLᵀ and solve column by column:
    l₁₁ = √a₁₁ lᵢ₁ = aᵢ₁ / l₁₁ lᵢᵢ = √( aᵢᵢ − Σ_(k<i) lᵢₖ² ) lⱼᵢ = ( aⱼᵢ − Σ_(k<i) lⱼₖlᵢₖ ) / lᵢᵢ for j > i
    Every step needs a square root of a quantity that must be positive and a division by it. The moment aᵢᵢ − Σ lᵢₖ² comes out zero or negative, the matrix is not positive definite and the algorithm stops. That failure is not a bug — it is the answer, delivered in m³/3 flops (half the cost of an LU factorization, and numerically stable with no pivoting required).
  • Worked. A = [[4, 2], [2, 3]]: l₁₁ = 2, l₂₁ = 2/2 = 1, l₂₂ = √(3 − 1) = √2, so L = [[2, 0], [1, √2]]. Check LLᵀ = [[4, 2], [2, 1 + 2]] = A ✓. Now try A = [[4, 5], [5, 3]]: l₁₁ = 2, l₂₁ = 2.5, l₂₂ = √(3 − 6.25) = √(−3.25) — fails, and indeed det = 12 − 25 < 0, so the matrix is indefinite.
  • The root-free variant. A = LDLᵀ with L unit lower triangular and D diagonal avoids square roots entirely (so it never fails on a negative pivot) and extends to indefinite matrices with symmetric pivoting. Its diagonal D is exactly what the inertia discussion below needs. A = LLᵀ is recovered as L·D^(1/2) when all dᵢ > 0.
  • Singular PSD matrices still factor: if rank(A) = r there is a triangular L with r positive diagonal entries and m − r zero rows. Uniqueness survives; the algorithm just zeroes out a whole row whenever a pivot comes out zero.
  • Relation to QR and to the SVD. If X = QR is the QR factorization of a full-column-rank data matrix (§13), then XᵀX = RᵀR — so R is the Cholesky factor of the Gram matrix (up to sign conventions on the diagonal), computed without ever forming XᵀX. And X = UΣVᵀ gives XᵀX = VΣ²Vᵀ: the three factorizations are three routes to the same object, with different numerical costs and different stability.
  • What Cholesky is actually used for, well beyond the definiteness test:
    • Sampling from N(μ, Σ): draw z ~ N(0, I) and return μ + Lz, whose covariance is L·I·Lᵀ = Σ. The Cholesky factor is the "square root" that turns white noise into correlated noise.
    • Whitening: L⁻¹(x − μ) has identity covariance — the cheap alternative to the symmetric square root Σ^(−1/2).
    • Mahalanobis distance (x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ) = ‖L⁻¹(x − μ)‖² by one triangular solve, never an explicit inverse.
    • Log-determinants: log|Σ| = 2·Σᵢ log lᵢᵢ. This is how Gaussian, GMM and Gaussian-process log-likelihoods are evaluated without overflow — det of a 1000×1000 covariance matrix is unrepresentable, its log is not.
    • Solving PD systems (normal equations, Newton steps, Kalman updates) at half the cost of LU, and detecting a non-convex Newton step for free — if the Hessian's Cholesky fails, you are not at a local minimum.
  • In code: numpy.linalg.cholesky returns the lower factor and raises LinAlgError on a non-PD input; scipy.linalg.cho_factor/cho_solve keep the factor for repeated solves. The idiomatic definiteness test in Python is a try/except around cholesky, not an eigenvalue call.

Congruence and Sylvester's law of inertia

  • Change variables in a quadratic form. Put x = Cy with C invertible, and xᵀAx = yᵀ(CᵀAC)y. So a change of coordinates does not act on A by similarity C⁻¹AC — it acts by congruence CᵀAC. Two symmetric matrices related this way describe the same energy surface seen in two different coordinate systems.
  • Congruence and similarity coincide only when C is orthogonal (Cᵀ = C⁻¹) — which is why the spectral theorem, an orthogonal congruence and an orthogonal similarity, is so powerful, and why non-orthogonal changes of basis are a different animal.
  • Congruence destroys eigenvalues. A = [[1, 0], [0, 1]] and C = diag(1, 3) give CᵀAC = diag(1, 9) — same surface, rescaled axes, completely different spectrum. So "what does a quadratic form really mean, independent of coordinates?" cannot be answered with eigenvalues. It can be answered with their signs.
  • Inertia. Define In(A) = (n₊, n₋, n₀): the number of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues of a symmetric A, counted with multiplicity. Then n₊ + n₋ + n₀ = m, the rank is n₊ + n₋, and the signature is n₊ − n₋.
  • Sylvester's law of inertia: two symmetric matrices are congruent ⟺ they have the same inertia. Equivalently, every symmetric A is congruent to the canonical form diag(I_{n₊}, −I_{n₋}, 0), and no invertible change of coordinates can alter how many directions climb, how many fall, and how many stay flat. Magnitudes are coordinate artefacts; the three counts are the invariant.
  • Read the definiteness table through this lens and it snaps into place: PD is (m, 0, 0), ND is (0, m, 0), PSD is (n₊, 0, n₀) with n₀ > 0, NSD is (0, n₋, n₀), and indefinite is anything with both n₊ > 0 and n₋ > 0. Definiteness is precisely the congruence-invariant content of a symmetric matrix — it is the one thing about the energy surface that survives any relabelling of the axes, which is why it, and not the eigenvalues themselves, is the property that gets a name.
  • The practical payoff: you can read the inertia off an LDLᵀ factorization without computing a single eigenvalue, because A = LDLᵀ is a congruence (C = L⁻¹ gives CACᵀ = D) and D is diagonal, so the signs of D's entries are the inertia. That is how trust-region and interior-point optimizers decide, in O(m³/3), whether a Hessian has negative curvature and in how many directions — the number of negative pivots is the dimension of the descent cone at a saddle.
  • Congruence also explains AᵀA and whitening. If B is positive definite and C = B^(−1/2), the congruence CᵀAC = B^(−1/2)AB^(−1/2) is exactly the "whiten first" move of the generalized eigenvalue problem (§15). By the law of inertia, A and B^(−1/2)AB^(−1/2) have the same inertia — so whitening cannot turn a saddle into a bowl, it only changes the curvature magnitudes. It rescales the problem; it does not change its nature.

The nearest positive-definite matrix

  • Real covariance and correlation estimates come back indefinite more often than anyone would like: pairwise-deleted missing data, correlations elicited from different sources, ad-hoc smoothing, or simply m > n plus round-off. Downstream code then fails — Cholesky raises, a Gaussian log-likelihood returns nan, an optimizer diverges.
  • The fix has a closed form. The nearest symmetric positive semidefinite matrix to A in the Frobenius norm is obtained in two steps: symmetrize, B = (A + Aᵀ)/2, then clip the negative eigenvalues to zero: with B = QΛQᵀ, take Â = Q·diag(max(λᵢ, 0))·Qᵀ. The minimum distance is ‖A − Â‖²_F = Σ_(λᵢ < 0) λᵢ² + ‖(A − Aᵀ)/2‖²_F, and Â is unique.
  • Why symmetrizing comes first, and for free: Frobenius norm splits orthogonally into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts, ‖D + E‖²_F = ‖D‖²_F + ‖E‖²_F when D = Dᵀ and E = −Eᵀ, because the cross term Σᵢⱼ dᵢⱼeᵢⱼ cancels pairwise. Since every candidate X is symmetric, the skew part of A is an unavoidable constant — so minimizing over PSD matrices reduces to the symmetric part alone. This is the same "the skew part contributes nothing" fact that opened this section, now doing real work.
  • Why clipping is optimal: rotate into B's eigenbasis (Frobenius is unitarily invariant, so nothing changes) and let Y = QᵀXQ. Then ‖B − X‖²_F = Σ_(i≠j) yᵢⱼ² + Σᵢ (λᵢ − yᵢᵢ)² ≥ Σᵢ (λᵢ − yᵢᵢ)² ≥ Σ_(λᵢ < 0) λᵢ², the last step because Y PSD forces yᵢᵢ ≥ 0. Clipping attains both inequalities.
  • Caveats worth stating. Clipping gives a PSD matrix, not a positive definite one — add a small εI if you need invertibility. It also does not preserve a unit diagonal, so the nearest correlation matrix (unit diagonal and PSD) is a genuinely harder, constrained problem with no closed form; it is solved by alternating projections onto the two constraint sets.
  • Contrast with shrinkage (§19), which blends toward αI and lifts every eigenvalue: shrinkage is a statistically motivated estimator that trades bias for variance, whereas eigenvalue clipping is a purely geometric repair that touches only the offending directions. Both are one line of eigen-arithmetic; they answer different questions.

The generalized Rayleigh quotient

  • Normalizing by xᵀx asks "which direction has the most energy, measured with a ruler that treats all axes alike?" Often that is the wrong ruler — the axes have different units, or different noise levels, or a covariance structure you want to discount. Replace the denominator with a second quadratic form and you get the generalized Rayleigh quotient R(x; A, B) = xᵀAx / xᵀBx, with A symmetric and B positive definite (so the denominator is never zero for x ≠ 0).
  • The bounds, and the trick that proves them. Substitute x = B^(−1/2)y. Then xᵀBx = yᵀy and xᵀAx = yᵀ(B^(−1/2)AB^(−1/2))y, so the generalized quotient becomes the ordinary Rayleigh quotient of the symmetric matrix B^(−1/2)AB^(−1/2). Everything transfers verbatim: λ_min ≤ R ≤ λ_max, the extremes are the eigenvalues of B⁻¹A, and they are attained at the generalized eigenvectors solving Ax = λBx. Left-multiplying that equation by xᵀ gives λ = xᵀAx / xᵀBx directly — the eigenvalue is the achieved ratio.
  • "Whiten, then rotate" is the whole idea. B^(−1/2) deforms space so that B's unit ball becomes a sphere; once the ruler is isotropic, the ordinary eigenvector argument applies. The successive maximizers are orthogonal in the B inner product (xᵢᵀBxⱼ = 0), not the Euclidean one — they generally look oblique when you plot them, and that is correct, not a bug. The full machinery, including simultaneous diagonalization, is on the sibling page (§15).
  • Where the ratio shows up:
    • Linear discriminant analysis — Fisher's criterion wᵀS_Bw / wᵀS_Ww: maximize between-class scatter relative to within-class scatter. PCA maximizes wᵀCw / wᵀw; LDA swaps the denominator for something that knows about the noise.
    • Canonical correlation analysis, where the ratio compares cross-covariance to the two within-set covariances.
    • Signal-to-noise filters: A = signal covariance, B = noise covariance; the top generalized eigenvector is the spatial filter with the best SNR.
    • Mahalanobis geometry: xᵀΣ⁻¹x is the quadratic form that measures distance in units of the data's own spread — the same idea with A = Σ⁻¹, B = I, or equivalently a Euclidean form after whitening.
    • Second-order optimality in a metric: natural-gradient and trust-region methods ask whether dᵀ∇²f·d / dᵀG·d is positive, where G is a Fisher-information or scaling matrix — the generalized version of "is the Hessian positive definite?"
    • Spectral clustering: the normalized-cut objective is a generalized Rayleigh quotient of the graph Laplacian over the degree matrix, xᵀLx / xᵀDx.
  • By the law of inertia above, the sign pattern of R cannot be changed by the choice of B: whitening rescales the ratio but cannot turn a negative direction positive. What the denominator buys you is a reordering of which directions look most important — never a change in which ones climb.

Why ML cares: near a minimum, any smooth loss is a quadratic form in its Hessian — positive definite ⟹ locally convex, a unique minimum, and gradient descent that converges (with the condition number λ_max/λ_min setting how badly it zig-zags). Indefinite Hessian ⟹ a saddle, the real obstacle in deep-net optimization. And the covariance matrix (§18) is a XᵀX-shaped matrix, hence always PSD — which is what makes PCA (§19) work at all.

18 Covariance matrices

Statistics turns out to be linear algebra in disguise: variance is a dot product with itself, covariance is a dot product, correlation is a cosine. One symmetric PSD matrix then holds every pairwise relationship in the dataset.

Correlation: what we are actually after

  • The goal is to quantify commonality between variables — do height and weight rise and fall together? Plot one against the other and ask how well a straight line describes the cloud.
  • Correlation is bivariate: defined between exactly two variables. A correlation matrix is just a tidy container for all the pairwise numbers.
  • r ∈ [−1, +1]: −1 = perfect inverse relationship, 0 = no linear relationship, +1 = perfect direct relationship.
  • Gotcha — linearity only. A strong but non-monotonic relationship (a parabola, a ring) can produce r ≈ 0 while the variables are fully determined by one another. Mutual information catches what correlation misses; alternatively transform the variables to linearize the relationship first.

Variance and standard deviation

  • Variance is a measure of dispersion: the average squared distance of each value from the mean. Small when the data huddle near the mean, large when they scatter.
  • Mean-centering — subtracting x̄ so the average is 0 — is the move that turns a statistic into a vector operation. Write x̃ = x − x̄ and variance becomes x̃ᵀx̃/(n − 1): a vector's dot product with itself, i.e. its squared length, rescaled.
  • Why n − 1 and not n: dividing by something that grows with the data is what keeps variance from inflating just because you collected more points (same logic as the mean). Using n − 1 (the sample variance) accounts for the degree of freedom spent estimating the mean — once you know n − 1 centered values and the mean, the last one is determined. Use n only when the data are the whole population. Watch your library defaults: some standard-deviation functions divide by n unless you ask for ddof=1.
  • Standard deviation σ = √(σ²) — the principal (positive) root, because a measure of spread can no more be negative than a length can. It lives in the units of the data, which variance does not.
  • Symbol clash to keep straight: σ is a standard deviation in statistics and a singular value in the SVD (§16). Context disambiguates.

Covariance — the dot product of two centered variables

  • "Covariance" = variance between two variables. Take the variance formula and replace one copy of the centered x with a centered y. Both vectors must have the same number of observations.
  • Sign is the message: positive when the two tend to sit on the same side of their means at the same time, negative when they sit on opposite sides, near zero when the products cancel.
  • Magnitude is not comparable across variables — covariance carries the product of the two units, so multiplying the data by 1000 multiplies the covariance by 10⁶. That scale-dependence is exactly what correlation fixes.
  • Mean-centered, the whole thing is xᵀy/(n − 1). In many applications (comparing covariances that share the same n) the 1/(n − 1) is a constant that cancels, and covariance is just a dot product. Every geometric intuition about dot products (§03) is now an intuition about statistics.
  • Center first, always. Skipping the centering step leaves the means baked into the products; the diagonal stops being variance and PCA (§19) produces components that point at the data cloud's offset from the origin rather than at its structure.

The correlation coefficient is a cosine

  • Divide the covariance by the two standard deviations and the units cancel: r = c_xy / (σ_x σ_y). The 1/(n − 1) appears once above and twice under the roots below, so it cancels outright in the raw-sum form.
  • In vector terms this is mapping over magnitude: r = xᵀy / (‖x‖‖y‖) — precisely the geometric dot-product formula for cos θ. Pearson correlation is the cosine of the angle between the two mean-centered data vectors.
  • That immediately explains the bounds: Cauchy–Schwarz gives |xᵀy| ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖, so −1 ≤ r ≤ 1, with equality exactly when the two centered vectors are collinear. Set y = x and numerator = denominator ⟹ r = 1.
  • If the data are unit-normed the denominator is 1 and correlation is the dot product. If the vectors are not mean-centered, the same formula is called cosine similarity — identical algebra, different interpretation (and different answers whenever the means are non-zero).

Covariance matrices

  • Stack the variables into a data matrix X — observations in rows, features in columns, exactly the design-matrix layout of least-squares (§14). Mean-center each column.
  • All pairwise covariances at once: C = XᵀX/(n − 1). Entry Cᵢⱼ is the dot product of centered feature i with centered feature j — matrix multiplication is a machine for computing every dot product simultaneously.
  • Orientation is the #1 bug. There is nothing sacred about transposing on the left: if the data are stored features-by-observations, use C = XXᵀ. The rule that never changes: the covariance matrix must come out features-by-features. Check the output size before believing the numbers.
  • Diagonal = variances (each feature dotted with itself), off-diagonal = covariances. This holds only if the columns were mean-centered.
  • C is symmetric — algebraically obvious from (XᵀX)ᵀ = XᵀX, and statistically sensible: the relationship of height to weight is the relationship of weight to height.
  • C is positive semidefinite, being of the form XᵀX (§17) — PD when the features are linearly independent, singular the moment one feature is a linear combination of others (or when features outnumber observations). Symmetric + PSD is the ideal case for eigendecomposition: real non-negative eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.

Correlation matrix ⟷ covariance matrix

  • Let S = diag(σ₁, …, σ_m) hold the feature standard deviations. Then R = S⁻¹ C S⁻¹ and, inverting, C = S R S. R has ones on its diagonal by construction, since each feature correlates perfectly with itself.
  • Push the parentheses around and the meaning appears: R = (XS⁻¹)ᵀ(XS⁻¹)/(n − 1). The correlation matrix is the covariance matrix of the rescaled data X̃ = XS⁻¹ — each feature divided by its own standard deviation. (Do not collapse that to a norm — XS⁻¹ is a matrix, not a vector.)
  • Since the data were already centered, dividing by σ is exactly z-scoring. Hence: on z-scored data, covariance and correlation are the same matrix.
  • Practical consequence: covariance-based PCA is dominated by whichever feature happens to have the biggest units; correlation-based PCA (equivalently, PCA on z-scored data) puts all features on equal footing. Choose deliberately — see §19.

Quadratic forms in random vectors — the mean, and why n − 1

  • Everything so far treated x as a fixed vector. Let it be a random vector instead, with mean μ and covariance Ω, and xᵀAx becomes a random scalar — a sum of squares. Sample variances, regression residual sums of squares, ANOVA sums of squares, and every χ² statistic you have ever computed are quadratic forms in a random vector. So it is worth knowing their moments exactly.
  • The expectation, by the trace trick. xᵀAx is a scalar, so it equals its own trace, and the trace is cyclic:
    E(xᵀAx) = E[tr(Axxᵀ)] = tr(A·E[xxᵀ]) = tr(A(Ω + μμᵀ)) = tr(AΩ) + μᵀAμ
    No distributional assumption at all — only that the second moments exist. Two terms with two readings: tr(AΩ) is the contribution of the spread, μᵀAμ the contribution of the location. When Ω = σ²I, the first collapses to σ²·tr(A).
  • Bessel's correction, derived rather than asserted. Let x = (x₁ … x_n)ᵀ be a sample with E(x) = μ1_n and var(x) = σ²I_n. The centering matrix is H = I − (1/n)11ᵀ: it subtracts the mean from every entry, it is symmetric, and it is idempotent (H² = H), so it is the orthogonal projector onto the space orthogonal to 1 (§13). Then Σᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)² = xᵀHx is a quadratic form, and the formula above gives it immediately:
    E[Σᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)²] = tr(H·σ²I) + μ²·1ᵀH1 = σ²·tr(H) + 0 = σ²(n − 1) because tr(H) = tr(I) − (1/n)tr(11ᵀ) = n − 1, and H1 = 1 − 1 = 0
    Dividing by n − 1 therefore gives E(s²) = σ² exactly, while dividing by n gives σ²(n−1)/n — biased low by a factor (n−1)/n. The "lost degree of freedom" is literally tr(H): the rank of the projection that removed the mean. The μᵀHμ term vanishing is the algebraic statement that centering is blind to location.
  • The same argument fixes the residual variance in regression. With ŷ = Py and the projection ("hat") matrix P = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ (§14), the residual sum of squares is yᵀ(I − P)y. I − P is symmetric idempotent of rank n − p, and (I − P)Xβ = 0, so E[SSE] = σ²(n − p) and the unbiased estimator is σ̂² = SSE/(n − p). Every "degrees of freedom" in classical statistics is the rank of a projection matrix, and every "divide by df" is this one identity.
  • The variance, when x is normal. Higher moments need a distribution; for x ~ N(μ, Ω):
    var(xᵀAx) = 2·tr(AΩAΩ) + 4·μᵀAΩAμ cov(xᵀAx, xᵀBx) = 2·tr(AΩBΩ) + 4·μᵀAΩBμ
    Note the covariance vanishes when AΩB = 0 — a hint of the independence criterion below, and the reason the sum-of-squares terms in an ANOVA table are uncorrelated.
  • The covariance matrix estimator is unbiased for the same reason. With the rows of X independent and identically distributed with covariance Σ, applying the identity entry by entry gives E[XᵀHX/(n − 1)] = Σ — the matrix version of the scalar computation above.

Chi-square, Cochran's theorem, and where degrees of freedom come from

  • Knowing the mean of a quadratic form is not enough to test anything; you need its distribution. The bridge from linear algebra to inference is one theorem, and its hypothesis is idempotence.
  • The base case. If z ~ N(0, I_m) and A is symmetric and idempotent with rank(A) = r, then zᵀAz ~ χ²_r. Proof in three lines: a symmetric idempotent matrix has eigenvalues only 0 and 1 (from λ² = λ), so A = P·diag(I_r, 0)·Pᵀ for some orthogonal P, with exactly r ones. Put y = Pᵀz; since P is orthogonal, y ~ N(0, PᵀP) = N(0, I) — the components are still independent standard normals. Then zᵀAz = yᵀdiag(I_r, 0)y = Σ_(i≤r) yᵢ², a sum of r independent squared standard normals, which is the definition of χ²_r. Note that rank = trace for an idempotent matrix, so the degrees of freedom can be read off by summing a diagonal.
  • General covariance. If x ~ N(0, Ω) with Ω positive definite, then xᵀAx ~ χ²_r ⟺ AΩ is idempotent with rank(AΩ) = r. (Whiten by Ω = TTᵀ, set z = T⁻¹x, and check that TᵀAT is idempotent exactly when AΩ is — the same "whiten, then apply the clean theorem" move as everywhere else on this page.) With Ω = σ²I this reduces to "σ²A is idempotent".
  • Non-zero mean. Same hypotheses with x ~ N(μ, Ω) give the noncentral chi-square, xᵀAx ~ χ²_r(λ) with noncentrality λ = ½·μᵀAμ. The central case is exactly Aμ = 0 — the mean lies in the null space of the form. That single condition is why a test statistic is central under the null hypothesis and noncentral under the alternative, and the noncentrality parameter is what determines the test's power.
  • Independence. For x ~ N(μ, Ω) with A, B symmetric: xᵀAx and xᵀBx are independent if AΩB = 0; and xᵀAx is independent of the linear form Bx if BΩA = 0. With Ω = σ²I both reduce to AB = 0 — orthogonal projections onto orthogonal subspaces produce independent sums of squares. The geometry is doing all the probabilistic work.
  • Cochran's theorem ties it together. Let A₁ + … + A_k = I_m with every Aᵢ symmetric and idempotent. Then AᵢAⱼ = 0 for all i ≠ j automatically — you never have to check it. More generally, for symmetric A₁ … A_k summing to A, any two of the following imply the third, and all of them imply the fourth:
    1. each Aᵢ is idempotent;
    2. A is idempotent;
    3. AᵢAⱼ = 0 for all i ≠ j;
    4. rank(A) = Σᵢ rank(Aᵢ).
    Conditions (2) and (4) alone also suffice. The whole statement is a decomposition of a space into mutually orthogonal subspaces: symmetric + idempotent = orthogonal projector, AᵢAⱼ = 0 = the ranges are orthogonal, and the rank condition = the dimensions add up. Cochran says these three descriptions are the same description.
  • What it delivers: if x ~ N(0, σ²I_n) and I = A₁ + … + A_k with the Aᵢ symmetric idempotent, then the quadratic forms xᵀA₁x/σ², …, xᵀA_kx/σ² are mutually independent, each χ² with rank(Aᵢ) degrees of freedom, and their degrees of freedom sum to n. That is the ANOVA table: a total sum of squares split into pieces whose degrees of freedom add up, because the identity matrix was split into projectors whose ranks add up.
  • Worked — the t-test, from scratch. Take x ~ N(μ1_n, σ²I_n) and split the identity into two projectors:
    I_n = (1/n)11ᵀ + (I − (1/n)11ᵀ) rank 1 + rank (n − 1) = n ✓
    Both are symmetric and idempotent, and their product is zero (11ᵀ(I − 11ᵀ/n) = 11ᵀ − 11ᵀ = 0). Cochran therefore gives: n·x̄²/σ² ~ χ²₁(λ) with λ = nμ²/(2σ²); (n − 1)s²/σ² ~ χ²_(n−1) centrally, because H·μ1 = 0; and — the crucial part — they are independent. Independence of x̄ from s² is what makes t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) a ratio of independent normal and √χ² pieces, i.e. an actual t distribution. Every introductory course asserts that independence; this is the proof, and it is a statement about two orthogonal subspaces of ℝⁿ.
  • Worked — the F test in regression. Compare a full model to a nested reduced one. The extra sum of squares is yᵀ(P − P₁)y and the residual is yᵀ(I − P)y, where P and P₁ are the projection matrices of the full and reduced designs. Both middle matrices are symmetric idempotent — differences of nested projectors are projectors — of ranks p − p₁ and n − p, and their product is zero because the subspaces are orthogonal. Hence two independent chi-squares, and their scaled ratio is exactly F_(p−p₁, n−p). The F statistic is a ratio of squared lengths in two orthogonal subspaces, and σ² cancels between numerator and denominator — which is why you never have to know it.
  • The geometric summary, worth keeping. Normal noise is spherically symmetric. Project a sphere onto orthogonal subspaces and the squared lengths of the pieces are independent, with distributions depending only on the dimensions of those subspaces. That is the entire content of "degrees of freedom", and it is why so much of classical statistics is really linear algebra in disguise — the same message as "covariance is a dot product" above, one level up.

19 PCA the linear-algebra view

Where everything converges: the quadratic form, covariance, eigendecomposition, and the SVD are four views of one algorithm. PCA finds a new basis whose axes are ordered by variance and mutually orthogonal. This is the linear-algebra derivation — for the ML angle (uses, scaling, when it fails) see the dedicated Dimensionality Reduction (PCA) page.

What PCA is looking for

  • A dataset with m features is a cloud of points in ℝ^m, and the raw features are its basis vectors. PCA asks: is this the best basis? Its answer: pick basis vectors that successively maximize variance while staying orthogonal to all previous ones.
  • "Maximize variance" concretely means: find weights w, one per feature, such that the weighted combination Xw has the largest possible variance. Two positively correlated channels get weights of the same sign (they reinforce); two negatively correlated channels get opposite signs, flipping one so it adds instead of cancels.
  • Without a constraint this is trivial — scale w up and variance grows forever. PCA therefore requires ‖w‖ = 1: the sum of squared weights is 1, so only the direction of the weighting is free. This is exactly the normalization of §17.
  • PC2 is the direction that maximizes the residual variance — everything PC1 did not explain — subject to being orthogonal to PC1. In 2-D there is only one such direction; in higher dimensions there are many, and the constraint chain continues.
  • Two outputs, don't confuse them. Loadings (weights) = the eigenvectors: how much each original feature contributes to a component. Scores = the data projected onto those eigenvectors: the new coordinates of each observation. Eigenvalues = the variance along each component.
  • Geometrically PCA is a pure rotation. The components are orthonormal, so the score plot is the mean-centered cloud rigidly rotated onto its own principal axes — no stretching, no distortion. And because the axes are now the eigenvectors of C, the covariance matrix in the new basis is diagonal: the components are uncorrelated. (Dividing each score by √λᵢ on top of that is whitening.)

Applications, and how many components to keep

  • Dimensionality reduction / compression: keep the top k < r components and discard the rest, on the assumption that low-variance directions are noise. This is the low-rank approximation of §16 wearing a statistics hat.
  • Denoising (reconstruct from the top components), visualization (plot scores on PC1 vs PC2), decorrelation/whitening (feed uncorrelated features to a downstream model), and exploration (read the loadings to see which features move together).
  • An m-feature dataset yields exactly m components, because the eigendecomposition of an m×m covariance matrix does. Choosing how many to keep is partly a judgement call: the qualitative tool is the scree plot of sorted eigenvalues (§16) — look for the elbow — or a cumulative-variance threshold. Quantitative thresholds exist but are statistical machinery beyond the algebra.
  • Eigenvalues are occasionally called latent factor scores; converted to percentages they are the familiar percent variance explained.

How to perform a PCA

  1. Mean-center every feature. Non-negotiable — see the failure mode below.
  2. Form the covariance matrix C = XᵀX/(n − 1) (§18). The data matrix may be observations-by-features or features-by-observations; C must come out features-by-features.
  3. Eigendecompose C. It is symmetric PSD, so the eigenvalues are real and non-negative and the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
  4. Sort eigenvalues descending and reorder the eigenvectors to match. Eigenvalues have no intrinsic ordering and are glued to their eigenvectors — sorting one without the other silently corrupts everything downstream.
  5. Compute component scores: project the data onto the eigenvectors, scores = XW (column j of W weighting all features for component j). If the data are time series these are the "component time series".
  6. Convert eigenvalues to percent variance explained: 100·λᵢ/Σλ — the same recipe used for singular values.

The algebra: why eigenvectors are the answer

  • State the goal formally: the variance of the projection Xw is wᵀCw — the quadratic form of the covariance matrix. So PCA is argmax_w {wᵀCw} subject to ‖w‖ = 1, equivalently argmax_w {wᵀCw / wᵀw}: maximize the normalized quadratic form of §17.
  • Calculus route: the Lagrangian L = wᵀCw − λ(wᵀw − 1) has gradient 2Cw − 2λw. Setting it to zero gives Cw = λw — the eigenvalue equation falls out of the optimization. Left-multiply by wᵀ: the achieved variance is wᵀCw = λ. So the maximizer is the top eigenvector and the maximum is λ₁.
  • Linear-algebra route (all m solutions at once): collect candidate vectors as columns of W. A matrix can't sit in a denominator, so multiply by an inverse instead — Λ = (WᵀW)⁻¹(WᵀCW) = W⁻¹W⁻ᵀWᵀCW = W⁻¹CW, hence CW = WΛ. The eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix is the solution to the maximization problem.
  • Interpretation of the two outputs of that equation: Λ holds the maximal energy values of the quadratic form surface — the variances — and the columns of W are the ridge/valley directions of that surface: the principal components.
  • Because C is PD (full-rank case), its normalized quadratic form surface is positive everywhere — a bowl. PC1 runs along the ridge, PC2 along the valley, exactly as §17 predicts.

The other derivation: PCA as the best-fitting hyperplane

  • "Maximize variance" is one way to motivate PCA. There is a completely different-looking objective that turns out to define the same thing, and it is the one PCA was originally posed with: find the k-dimensional flat that the point cloud lies closest to, measuring distance perpendicular to the flat.
  • The problem, stated. A k-dimensional hyperplane (affine subspace) is {α + By : y ∈ ℝᵏ} — an offset α plus the span of k basis directions B. Given points x₁ … x_n stacked into X, choose α, B and the coordinates Z to minimize ‖X − α1ᵀ − BZ‖. Everything on the right has rank at most k after the offset is removed — so this is a rank-k matrix approximation problem with an affine shift.
  • The solution — and it is exact, not an approximation: the optimal offset is the mean, α = x̄; the optimal directions are the top-k singular vectors of the mean-centred data; and the optimal coordinates are the projections onto them. The achieved minimum, in Frobenius norm, is
    min ‖X − α1ᵀ − BZ‖²_F = σ²_(k+1) + σ²_(k+2) + … + σ²_r (σᵢ = singular values of the centred data)
    This is Eckart–Young applied to the centred data matrix (§16) — the low-rank approximation theorem, wearing a geometry hat. The standard "closeness of fit" index is the leftover share (σ²_(k+1) + … + σ²_r)/(σ²₁ + … + σ²_r), i.e. exactly 1 − the variance explained.
  • Why the offset has to be the mean, in one line. For any fixed subspace, the residual sum of squares as a function of α is a quadratic bowl whose minimizer is the centroid of the residuals — and the centroid of a cloud, projected out of any subspace, is the projection of the centroid. Centring first is therefore not a preprocessing convention imposed from outside; it is the first-order condition of the fitting problem. (Which is another way to read the "mean-centering is not optional" subsection above.)
  • Why maximizing variance and minimizing distance are the same problem — Pythagoras. For each centred point x̃ᵢ and any unit direction w, the projection and the residual are orthogonal, so
    ‖x̃ᵢ‖² = (x̃ᵢᵀw)² + ‖x̃ᵢ − (x̃ᵢᵀw)w‖² total = explained + residual, point by point Σᵢ ‖x̃ᵢ‖² = Σᵢ (x̃ᵢᵀw)² + Σᵢ ‖residualᵢ‖² ⟹ (n−1)·tr(C) = (n−1)·wᵀCw + SS_perp
    The left-hand side — the total scatter — does not depend on w at all. So maximizing the projected variance wᵀCw is identically minimizing the sum of squared perpendicular distances. They are not two objectives that happen to agree; they are the same equation rearranged. This is the single most useful sentence about PCA: it explains why the same eigenvectors serve both the "find the directions of greatest spread" story and the "compress with least reconstruction error" story.
  • This is total least squares (orthogonal regression), and it is not ordinary least squares. OLS (§14) minimizes vertical residuals Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²; PCA minimizes perpendicular ones. The differences are not cosmetic:
    • Asymmetry. OLS treats x as error-free and y as noisy, so regressing y on x and x on y give two different lines. TLS treats both symmetrically and gives one line.
    • Where PC1 sits. The first principal axis always lies between the two OLS lines, and all three coincide only when r = ±1. The gap between the two regression lines is a direct visual measure of 1 − r², since r² = b_(y|x) · b_(x|y).
    • Regression dilution. If x is itself measured with noise, the OLS slope is biased toward zero (attenuated) — the classic errors-in-variables problem. TLS corrects the attenuation exactly when the two error variances are equal, which is the assumption you buy into by choosing perpendicular distance.
    • Equivariance. TLS/PCA is rotation-equivariant (rotate the cloud, the fit rotates with it) but not scale-equivariant — stretching one axis changes which direction is "closest", because perpendicular distance is measured in the raw units. OLS is exactly the other way round. That is the real reason z-scoring is a modelling decision: choosing units is choosing what "perpendicular" means.
    • Supervision. OLS has a designated target; PCA has none. If you want to predict y, PC1 is not the answer, and a low-variance direction may carry all the signal.

Mean-centering is not optional

  • Principal components are vectors from the origin. If the cloud is offset from the origin, the direction of largest wᵀ(XᵀX)w is the direction pointing from the origin to the center of the cloud — which says nothing about the cloud's internal structure.
  • The result is mathematically correct and analytically useless: PC1 points at the mean, PC2 is forced orthogonal to it, and both ignore the covariance you actually wanted. Centering removes the offset so the components describe shape rather than location.
  • Centering is also what makes diag(C) equal the variances (§18) — without it the eigenvalues aren't variances at all.

Regularization: shrinkage

  • The problem: covariance matrices are often near-singular — more features than observations, collinear features, or tiny noise-dominated eigenvalues. The condition number explodes, the inverse is unstable, and the smallest components are numerical fiction.
  • Shrinkage replaces C with a blend of C and a scaled identity: C̃ = (1 − γ)C + γαI, with α the mean eigenvalue of C (= tr(C)/m). That α matters: it puts the shift on the same numerical scale as the data, so "1% regularization" means the same thing whatever the units.
  • What it does to the spectrum: eigenvectors are unchanged, and each eigenvalue maps to (1 − γ)λᵢ + γα — every eigenvalue is pulled toward the common value α. Big ones shrink, small ones grow. Hence the name, and hence a smaller condition number. Any γ > 0 turns a rank-deficient covariance matrix into a full-rank one.
  • Extremes: γ = 0 leaves C untouched; γ = 1 replaces it entirely with αI, destroying all covariance structure. The working rule is as little as possible, as much as necessary; γ is usually tuned by cross-validation.
  • This is the same "shift the diagonal" move as ridge regression and Tikhonov regularization — a bias-for-variance trade that buys numerical stability and better generalization.

Whitening — and why the rotation is not unique

  • PCA rotates the cloud onto its own axes, which makes the covariance diagonal. Divide each score by √λᵢ on top of that and the covariance becomes the identity: every direction has unit variance and all correlations are zero. That is whitening (sphering), and it is what "C^(−1/2)" means concretely.
  • PCA whitening: Z = X W Λ^(−1/2), using the eigenvectors W and eigenvalues Λ of C. Check: cov(Z) = Λ^(−1/2)Wᵀ C W Λ^(−1/2) = Λ^(−1/2)ΛΛ^(−1/2) = I ✓.
  • The key structural fact: a whitening matrix is only determined up to an orthogonal factor. If cov(XM) = I and Q is any orthogonal matrix, then cov(XMQ) = QᵀIQ = I as well. So there is an entire rotation group's worth of whitening transforms, and choosing one is a modelling decision, not a computation:
    • PCA whitening WΛ^(−1/2) — axes along the principal directions, the natural choice if you also want to truncate.
    • ZCA whitening WΛ^(−1/2)Wᵀ — the symmetric square root C^(−1/2). Among all whitening matrices it is the one closest to the identity in Frobenius norm, so it whitens with the least possible rotation and keeps the whitened data looking like the original. This is why ZCA is what image pipelines use: whitened image patches still look like image patches.
    • Cholesky whitening L⁻ᵀ from C = LLᵀ (§17) — triangular, so it is the cheapest to compute and to invert, at the cost of being order-dependent: permute the features and you get a different transform.
  • This is exactly where ICA enters. Whitening removes second-order structure and leaves the rotation free; ICA spends that leftover rotation on a higher-order criterion — maximizing non-Gaussianity or minimizing mutual information. So the standard pipeline is literally "PCA to whiten, then search over Q", and it explains cleanly why ICA can succeed where PCA fails on the crossing-arms data below: the arms differ in a fourth-moment property that no second-order method can see, and the covariance matrix — being second-order by construction — is blind to it.
  • Where whitening is doing quiet work elsewhere: the Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after whitening; generalized least squares is OLS after whitening by the error covariance; LDA is PCA on the class means after whitening by the within-class covariance (the generalized eigenproblem of §15); batch normalization is a cheap diagonal-only whitening of layer activations; and natural gradient descent is gradient descent after whitening by the Fisher information matrix.
  • The catch nobody mentions. Whitening divides by √λᵢ, so the smallest eigenvalue — the least reliable, most noise-dominated direction — is amplified the most. On an ill-conditioned covariance matrix, whitening actively manufactures noise. Standard defences: truncate to the top k components before whitening, add a floor √(λᵢ + ε), or shrink C first (below). The amplification factor is precisely √κ(C), so the condition number of §16 tells you in advance how bad it will be.

Is PCA always the best?

  • Variance ≠ relevance. PCA assumes the interesting structure lives in the directions of greatest spread. With noisy measurements the leading components can be pure noise; and a real effect confined to a few channels can be buried below components that merely track a loud, uninformative one.
  • The classic failure: data forming an "X" of two crossing arms. PC1 splits the difference and points between the two arms, PC2 sits orthogonal to it — technically the maximum-variance direction, but not the structure a human sees. PCA recovers decorrelated axes, not the underlying sources.
  • PCA is linear and constrained to orthogonal components. If the true latent factors are correlated, or the manifold is curved, no rotation will separate them — reach for kernel PCA, autoencoders, or a manifold method.
  • ICA optimizes a different criterion: statistical independence (not just zero correlation), with no orthogonality requirement. On the "X" data it recovers the two arms — the data come out demixed rather than merely decorrelated. Different criterion, different assumptions, not universally better.
  • PCA is unsupervised — it never sees the labels. If the goal is class separation, a supervised projection like LDA (maximize between-class over within-class scatter) can find a low-variance direction that PCA would have discarded.
  • Scaling is a modelling decision: raw-covariance PCA is dominated by whichever feature has the largest units; correlation-based PCA (z-scored data, §18) treats every feature equally. Neither is automatically right.
