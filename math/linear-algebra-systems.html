10 Systems of equations

Ax = b is the central problem. A pile of equations becomes one matrix equation — and then a mechanical, geometry-backed procedure (elimination) solves it, computes rank, and exposes a basis for the row space along the way.

Algebra and geometry of equations

  • The number of unknowns sets the dimension of the picture: one variable ⇒ a point on the number line, two ⇒ a line in ℝ², three ⇒ a plane in ℝ³. Every point on that object solves the equation.
  • Row picture: a system of m equations is m such objects, and the solution set is their intersection. Two lines crossing at one point, two parallel lines, or two copies of the same line — that trichotomy is the theory of solvability.
  • Column picture: read Ax = b the other way and it asks for the weights x that mix the columns of A to land exactly on b. Solvable ⟺ b ∈ C(A) — the column space of §08. The row picture explains where the solution is; the column picture explains whether one exists.
  • Within a system you may scale an individual equation (by a nonzero scalar) and add/subtract whole equations from one another. The individual lines move, but the intersection point never does — the solution set is invariant. That single fact licenses everything below.
  • Why bother: some presentations of the same system are far easier than others. 2x + 3y = 8 with −2x + 2y = −3 is awkward; add the equations and the second becomes 5y = 5, which decouples y from x and hands you the answer. Elimination is the systematic hunt for that decoupling.

From systems to matrices

  • Three ingredients: variables (the unknowns), coefficients (the numbers multiplying them — 1 if the variable stands alone, 0 if it's absent), and constants (everything not attached to a variable, collected on the right).
  • Coefficients go into the coefficients matrix A: one row per equation, one column per variable. Variables become a column vector x right-multiplying A; constants become the column vector b.
  • The order of variables across the columns must be consistent for every row. The classic blunder is dropping an implicit zero — write missing variables in explicitly with coefficient 0 before transcribing.
  • For elimination, glue b on as an extra column: the augmented matrix [A | b]. Row operations then act on the constants too, automatically.

Row reduction and elementary matrices

  • Row reduction = replacing rows with linear combinations of other rows, with the goal of manufacturing zeros. Zeros are cheap to compute with and make structure visible.
  • Three elementary row operations: (1) swap two rows, (2) scale a row by a nonzero constant, (3) add a multiple of one row to another.
  • Each operation is itself a matrix, applied on the left. Start from I and edit it: putting σ in position (j,i) builds the R that adds σ·(row i) to row j; permuting rows of I builds a permutation matrix P that swaps rows.
  • So the echelon form is not information loss, just a change of coordinates on the rows: E = Rₙ…R₁PA. Keep the factors and the process is fully reversible; discard them and you keep only the reduced form.
  • Left-multiply for rows, right-multiply for columns. The same P applied on the right permutes columns instead — a clean demonstration that matrix multiplication doesn't commute.
  • In practice, this bookkeeping is packaged as the LU decomposition: PA = LU with U an echelon form and L⁻¹ the accumulated elimination steps (scipy: lu).

Echelon form, pivots, and rank

  • A matrix is in echelon form when (1) the leading nonzero entry of each row sits strictly to the right of the leading entry of the row above, and (2) all-zero rows are pushed to the bottom. It's a staircase — upper-triangular in spirit, whatever the shape.
  • The pivots are those leading nonzero entries. A zero can never be a pivot (pivots become denominators). Not every row or column has one, and you cannot see a matrix's pivots until you've reduced it.
  • Strategy: clear below the top-left pivot first, then move right and down. Tactics that reduce arithmetic pain — divide a row to make its leading entry 1, scale a row before subtracting to keep integers, multiply through to kill fractions.
  • If a leading entry lands too far left, swap rows. (Each swap flips the sign of the determinant — see §11 — without changing its magnitude.)
  • rank = number of pivots = number of nonzero rows in the echelon form. Any row that is a combination of the others gets annihilated into a zeros row — the definition of linear dependence. The surviving nonzero rows are a basis for the row space.
  • The echelon form is not unique (scale any row and it's still echelon), but the number of pivots and the number of zero rows (m − r) are invariant.

Gaussian elimination and back-substitution

  • Three steps: augment [A | b] → row-reduce to echelon form → back-substitute from the bottom row upward.
  • Apply every operation across the entire row, constants column included — forgetting the last column is the most common mistake.
  • Always substitute the answer back into the original equations. If it doesn't check out, the error is in the reduction or the substitution, and you'll catch it in seconds.
worked example: 2x + 3y = 8, −2x + 2y = −3[[2, 3 | 8], [−2, 2 | −3]] ──R₁+R₂──▸ [[2, 3 | 8], [0, 5 | 5]] ──R₂/5──▸ [[2, 3 | 8], [0, 1 | 1]] back-substitute: y = 1 ⟹ 2x + 3(1) = 8 ⟹ x = 5/2

Reduced row echelon form and Gauss-Jordan

  • RREF pushes further: every pivot equals 1, and each pivot is the only nonzero entry in its column — zeros above and below. Get there by working downward to echelon form, then upward from the last pivot row, dividing each pivot row by its pivot.
  • Unlike echelon form, the RREF of a matrix is unique. (Many different matrices can share one RREF, though: every square full-rank matrix reduces to I.)
  • Shape tells you the pattern: a full-rank square matrix → I; a full-row-rank wide matrix → I followed by extra columns; a full-column-rank tall matrix → I stacked on zero rows; column dependencies → zero rows and pivot-free columns.
  • Gauss-Jordan elimination = the same solve, but reduce [A | b] all the way to RREF. More reduction work, zero back-substitution: the last column simply is the solution.
  • Computationally: sympy's rref (Python) does it exactly; it is a symbolic/teaching tool, not the numerical route for large systems.
Gauss-Jordan finishes the same example[[2, 3 | 8], [0, 1 | 1]] ──−3R₂+R₁──▸ [[2, 0 | 5], [0, 1 | 1]] ──R₁/2──▸ [[1, 0 | 5/2], [0, 1 | 1]] read off: x = 5/2, y = 1

The algorithm, its cost, and the pivoting problem

  • Gauss's method is two nested sweeps. Forward reduction walks k = 1…n−1; at step k it divides by the pivot a⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾ₖₖ to form the multipliers, then updates every entry below and to the right of it. Three nested loops ⟹ ≈ n³/3 multiply-adds. Back-substitution is one triangular sweep, ≈ n²/2 — negligible beside the reduction.
  • Gauss-Jordan is strictly more work. Clearing above each pivot as well pushes the elimination to ≈ n³/2. You buy a solution you can read straight off the last column; you pay about 50% more arithmetic. For a single right-hand side that's a bad trade, which is exactly why production solvers stop at echelon form and back-substitute.
  • The cubic is empirical, not just asymptotic. Time a naive row reducer on random n×n matrices and the plot is a clean cubic — double n, multiply the runtime by roughly 8. That single fact is why elimination, and not the definition of the determinant, is how library code evaluates det (§11).
  • A pivot is a denominator, so a tiny pivot is a catastrophe. Solve 0.0001x₁ + x₂ = 1 together with x₁ + x₂ = 2 carrying three significant digits. Eliminating with the tiny pivot produces −10000x₂ = −10000, so x₂ = 1.00, and then back-substitution gives x₁ = (1 − 1.00)/0.0001 = 0.00. The true answer is x₁ = 1.0001: every digit is wrong.
  • Notice what that example is not. The coefficient matrix is perfectly healthy — its condition number is 2.62 (see conditioning). The failure belongs to the algorithm, not to the problem. That is precisely the difference between an ill-conditioned problem and an unstable method, and it is the single most useful distinction in numerical linear algebra.
  • Partial pivoting fixes it: at step k, scan column k from row k downward and swap the largest-magnitude entry into the pivot seat. Complete pivoting scans the entire remaining submatrix and swaps a row and a column. Complete pivoting is provably stable; partial pivoting has contrived counterexamples but is stable on essentially everything that arises in practice — and it costs O(n²) comparisons instead of O(n³). So partial pivoting is the universal default, and partial pivoting plus one step of iterative refinement is unconditionally stable.
  • In code the "swap" is virtual: you permute an index vector, never the data. That accumulated permutation is the P in PA = LU.

The three possibilities for a solution

  • Exactly one — the lines/planes meet at a single point. Every variable has a pivot: rank(A) = rank([A|b]) = n.
  • None — the system is inconsistent. Elimination produces a row reading 0 = 1, i.e. a pivot in the constants column: rank(A) < rank([A|b]). Geometrically, parallel lines; algebraically, b ∉ C(A).
  • Infinitely many — the equations are redundant (collinear/coplanar). Some column has no pivot, so its variable is a free variable you may set arbitrarily: rank(A) = rank([A|b]) < n, with n − r free variables. The solution set is one particular solution plus the whole null space N(A).

Consistency stated with rank — and the shape of the whole solution set

  • The consistency theorem. Ax = b has at least one solution ⟺ b ∈ C(A) ⟺ rank([A | b]) = rank(A). The proof is one line: Ax is the combination ∑ⱼ xⱼaⱼ of the columns, so a solution exists exactly when b is such a combination — in which case gluing b on as an extra column adds nothing new to the span.
  • An equivalent test that also hands you a solution. Let A⁻ be any generalized inverse (any matrix with AA⁻A = A, see §12). Then the system is consistent ⟺ AA⁻b = b, and in that case x = A⁻b is a solution. Better still: the test doesn't depend on which generalized inverse you pick — if it holds for one it holds for all — so you may as well use the pseudoinverse A⁺.
  • Two names for the two failures of squareness. A consistent system with rank(A) < n is underdetermined — it has infinitely many solutions, either because there are fewer equations than unknowns or because the coefficient matrix simply isn't full rank. A system with m > n and rank([A|b]) > rank(A) is overdetermined — no x satisfies it at all, and you fall back on approximate solutions (§14).
  • The complete solution set is one particular solution plus the null space. If Ax = b is consistent, then for every vector z ∈ ℝⁿ, A⁻b + (I − A⁻A)z is a solution — check it: A(A⁻b + (I − A⁻A)z) = AA⁻b + (A − AA⁻A)z = b + 0. And conversely every solution has that form, because if Ay = b then y = A⁻b + (I − A⁻A)y identically. So the solution set is an affine subspace: a point translated by N(A).
  • How big is that null space? I − A⁻A is idempotent and rank(I − A⁻A) = n − rank(A), so the free directions number exactly n − r — the rank-nullity theorem falling out of generalized-inverse algebra rather than out of pivot counting (§07).
  • Uniqueness has a crisp algebraic form: the solution A⁻b is unique ⟺ I − A⁻A = 0 ⟺ A⁻A = Iₙ ⟺ rank(A) = n. Full column rank, not squareness, is what makes a consistent system determinate.
  • Careful — the solution set of a consistent inhomogeneous system is not a subspace. It doesn't contain 0 (since b ≠ 0), and a combination α₁x₁ + … + α_r x_r of solutions is a solution only when the weights sum to 1: A(∑αᵢxᵢ) = (∑αᵢ)b. That's an affine (in fact convex-closed) set, not a linear one — and it's why two solutions being distinct instantly implies infinitely many, since αx₁ + (1 − α)x₂ solves it for every α.
  • Consequently the right count is: every solution of a consistent system with b ≠ 0 is a combination of exactly n − rank(A) + 1 linearly independent solutions — the +1 being the particular solution that the null space alone cannot supply.

Homogeneous systems

  • Ax = 0 is the homogeneous case. It is always consistent — x = 0, the trivial solution, is free. The only question is whether anything else solves it.
  • Nontrivial solutions exist ⟺ rank(A) < n ⟺ A⁻A ≠ Iₙ ⟺ the columns of A are linearly dependent. For a square A that is the same as det(A) = 0 (§11), and the same as A having no inverse (§12). Three chapters, one condition.
  • Unlike the inhomogeneous case, the solution set here is a genuine vector subspace — it contains 0 and is closed under arbitrary linear combinations, since A(∑αᵢxᵢ) = ∑αᵢ·0 = 0 with no constraint on the weights. That subspace is the null space N(A), of dimension n − rank(A), and every solution is a combination of n − r independent ones.
  • The geometry of where the null space sits. If a ∈ C(Aᵀ) (the row space) and b ∈ N(A), then a = Aᵀx for some x, so bᵀa = bᵀAᵀx = (Ab)ᵀx = 0. Every null-space vector is orthogonal to every row-space vector — and since their dimensions add to n, N(A) ⊕ C(Aᵀ) = ℝⁿ: the null space is the orthogonal complement of the row space, not merely disjoint from it (§08).
  • Homogeneous and inhomogeneous are the same problem. Fix any particular solution x* of Ax = b. Then x solves Ax = b ⟺ x − x* solves Ax = 0. Solving the inhomogeneous system reduces to one particular solution plus the entire homogeneous solution set — which is the "particular + null space" statement above, restated.
  • Nesting, for square A: N(A) ⊆ N(A²) ⊆ N(A³) ⊆ … and dually C(A) ⊇ C(A²) ⊇ C(A³) ⊇ …. Repeated application keeps destroying dimensions and never creates one; where the chain stabilises is the story of §15's defective matrices.

Solvability is a rank statement — the geometry (intersect / parallel / coincident) is exactly the three rank cases above. In data work, the tall m > n systems we actually face are almost always inconsistent, so we stop demanding Ax = b and ask for the closest reachable point instead — that's §13 and §14.
Theory vs. practice: hand elimination assumes exact arithmetic. With floating-point data, a pivot that "should" be zero comes out as 1e−16, so rank-by-counting-pivots is fragile; production code uses partial pivoting (LU) or counts singular values above a tolerance (§16).

11 Determinant

One number per square matrix that answers three questions at once: is it invertible, how much does it scale volume, and does it flip orientation? It's also the gateway to eigenvalues.

Four things to know

  • Square only. The determinant is defined for n×n matrices — never for rectangular ones. (Rectangular matrices get singular values instead, §16.)
  • One per matrix. It's a single scalar summarising the whole matrix. Many matrices share a determinant value, but no matrix has two.
  • det = 0 ⟺ singular. Zero determinant means rank-deficient (r < n), means a linear dependency among the columns (equivalently the rows), means no inverse. Check the determinant before you attempt an inverse.
  • Notation: det(A), or single vertical bars |A|, or the symbol Δ. Single bars distinguish it from the double bars of a norm ‖A‖.
  • Geometry: |det(A)| is the factor by which the transform scales area (2D) / volume (nD). The sign reports orientation: negative means the transform includes a reflection. det = 0 means the unit cube gets flattened into something of zero volume — a dimension was destroyed.

What the determinant actually is: four defining properties

The 2×2 and 3×3 recipes below are conveniences, not definitions — and there is no analogous recipe you'd want to memorise for 7×7. The honest construction goes the other way round: state the properties the function must have, prove exactly one function has them, and read the algorithm off the properties. Think of det as a function of the n rows ρ₁, …, ρₙ.

definition — det : ℝⁿˣⁿ → ℝ is the unique function satisfying(1) row combination det(…, k·ρᵢ + ρⱼ, …) = det(…, ρⱼ, …) for i ≠ j ← adding a multiple of one row to another changes nothing (2) alternating det(…, ρⱼ, …, ρᵢ, …) = −det(…, ρᵢ, …, ρⱼ, …) ← swapping two rows flips the sign (3) homogeneous det(…, k·ρᵢ, …) = k · det(…, ρᵢ, …) ← scaling one row scales the determinant (4) normalisation det(I) = 1
  • These are exactly the three elementary row operations plus a scale-fixing convention. That is the whole trick: the determinant is defined by how it reacts to elimination, so elimination computes it by construction. Compare the table in "Determinant and row reduction" below — that table is not a set of derived facts, it is the definition, read backwards.
  • (2) is redundant. A swap is three combination steps and a negation: ρᵢ + ρⱼ → ρⱼ, then −ρⱼ + ρᵢ → ρᵢ, then ρᵢ + ρⱼ → ρⱼ, then −ρᵢ. It's listed anyway so the axioms line up one-for-one with the row operations.
  • (3) has no k ≠ 0 restriction, unlike the row operation. It doesn't need one — the consequences below cover k = 0.
  • Everything else is a corollary, and each proof is two lines.
    • Two identical rows ⟹ det = 0. Swap them: by (2) the determinant negates; but the matrix is unchanged, so Δ = −Δ, hence Δ = 0.
    • A zero row ⟹ det = 0. Multiply that row by 2: by (3) the determinant doubles; but the matrix is unchanged, so Δ = 2Δ, hence Δ = 0.
    • det(echelon) = ∏ diagonal. If a diagonal entry is zero the matrix has a zero row, so both sides vanish. Otherwise factor each pivot out with (3), leaving 1s on the diagonal; the Jordan half of the elimination then clears everything above with (1), leaving I, whose determinant is 1 by (4).
    • det = 0 ⟺ singular. Gauss-Jordan reduce. A full-rank matrix reaches I, determinant 1, and (1)–(3) only ever multiply by nonzero factors — so the original was nonzero. A rank-deficient matrix reaches a form with a zero row, determinant 0.
  • Multilinearity. Properties (1) and (3) together force det to be linear in each row separately — additive as well as homogeneous: det(…, v + w, …) = det(…, v, …) + det(…, w, …). Note the word separately: det is emphatically not a linear function of the matrix. Scaling the whole matrix scales all n rows, which is why det(βA) = βⁿ det(A), and det(A + B) ≠ det(A) + det(B) in general.
  • Uniqueness is easy; existence is the real theorem. Uniqueness: any two functions with these properties must agree, because you can compute the value of either one by fixing a reduction to echelon form, tracking sign flips and scale factors, and multiplying the diagonal — a procedure that only ever invokes the axioms. Existence is harder, because different eliminations of the same matrix could conceivably disagree; the permutation expansion below settles it by exhibiting a formula that provably satisfies (1)–(4).
  • Why this beats "det = the cofactor recipe". Three reasons. (i) The axioms give you the O(n³) algorithm immediately, whereas the recipe gives you an O(n!) one. (ii) The properties are what proofs actually use — det(AB) = det(A)det(B) drops out by checking that T ↦ |TS|/|S| satisfies (1)–(4) and invoking uniqueness. (iii) The axioms are the ones that generalise: multilinear + alternating + normalised is what makes the determinant the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the rows, and the Jacobian factor in change-of-variables.

2×2 and the singularity condition

det([[a, b], [c, d]]) = ad − bc (diagonal product minus off-diagonal product) det([[1, 2], [0, 1]]) = 1 det([[5, −3], [−4, 2]]) = −2 det([[1, 2], [2, 4]]) = 0 ← rank 1
  • Don't be seduced: ad − bc is a 2×2 shortcut, not the definition.
  • Why dependency forces zero: build a matrix whose second column is λ times the first, [[a, λa], [c, λc]]. Then det = a·λc − λa·c = 0, identically. Every singular 2×2 satisfies ad = bc.
  • Transpose invariance: det(Aᵀ) = det(A). For 2×2 it's immediate (ad − bc vs. ad − cb); it holds at every size, because the determinant is a property of the matrix, not of its rows or its columns.

The characteristic polynomial

  • Read det(A) = Δ as an equation and you can solve for an unknown matrix entry. Put the unknown λ in several diagonal slots and you get a polynomial in λ — degree n for an n×n matrix, hence n roots (possibly complex, by the fundamental theorem of algebra).
  • Put λ on the whole diagonal — that is, shift the matrix by −λI — and demand singularity. The result is the characteristic polynomial, and its roots are exactly the eigenvalues (§15).
characteristic polynomialdet(A − λI) = 0 A = [[1, 3], [3, 1]] ⟹ (1 − λ)² − 9 = 0 ⟹ λ² − 2λ − 8 = 0 ⟹ (λ − 4)(λ + 2) = 0 λ = 4, −2 check: A − 4I = [[−3, 3], [3, −3]] and A + 2I = [[3, 3], [3, 3]] — both visibly rank 1
The bridge to eigenvalues: an eigenvalue is precisely a shift that makes a matrix singular. Everything in §15 is this equation solved systematically.

3×3: the diagonal shortcut

  • Sum the products of the three "right-going" diagonals (wrapping around, or equivalently after gluing copies of columns 1 and 2 onto the right), then subtract the products of the three "left-going" diagonals. Remember the picture, not the string of letters.
  • This shortcut is genuinely 3×3-only — it does not generalise to 4×4.
3×3 (Sarrus)det([[a, b, c], [d, e, f], [g, h, i]]) = aei + bfg + cdh − ceg − bdi − afh det([[1, 3, 5], [2, 4, 2], [1, 2, 3]]) = (12 + 6 + 20) − (20 + 18 + 4) = 38 − 42 = −4

The full procedure: cofactor (Laplace) expansion

  1. Pick a row (or a column) — any one. Walking the first row is conventional; a row with many zeros is smarter.
  2. For each entry aᵢⱼ, form its minor Mᵢⱼ: the determinant of the submatrix left after deleting row i and column j.
  3. Attach the sign (−1)^(i+j) — a checkerboard [[+, −, +], [−, +, −], [+, −, +]] — to get the cofactor.
  4. Sum entry × cofactor along the chosen line.
expansion along row idet(A) = ∑ⱼ (−1)^(i+j) · aᵢⱼ · Mᵢⱼ det([[1, 3, 5], [2, 4, 2], [1, 2, 3]]) = 1·(12 − 4) − 3·(6 − 2) + 5·(4 − 4) = 8 − 12 + 0 = −4
  • It's recursive: a 4×4 costs four 3×3s, a 5×5 costs five 4×4s. Cost grows like O(n!) — nobody computes a 5×5 determinant this way twice.
  • Both the 2×2 and 3×3 shortcuts fall out of this procedure as special cases (a 1×1 determinant is just its single entry).
  • Column-wise expansion works identically — unsurprising, since det(Aᵀ) = det(A).

The permutation expansion — the formula that proves existence

  • Cofactor expansion is recursive; unrolling the recursion all the way down gives a single closed formula. Every surviving term picks exactly one entry from each row and each column — that is, it is indexed by a permutation σ of {1, …, n} — and carries the sign of that permutation (+1 if it is a product of an even number of transpositions, −1 if odd).
  • This is the formula that proves a determinant function exists. Checking that it satisfies the four axioms is mechanical; uniqueness then says it must be the same number every elimination produces, no matter which elimination you chose. Without it, "reduce and multiply the diagonal" would be an algorithm with no guarantee that two different reductions agree.
  • It is also spectacularly useless as an algorithm. There are n! permutations, each requiring n multiplications. At n = 10 that is 10! = 3 628 800 terms; elimination needs about n³/3 ≈ 333 operations. Four orders of magnitude at a size that isn't even large.
  • Because det(Aᵀ) = det(A), everything said about rows holds verbatim for columns — the axioms could equally have been stated column-wise.
  • Two by-products worth knowing. Cramer's rule writes each unknown as a ratio of determinants, xᵢ = det(Aᵢ)/det(A) with Aᵢ being A with its i-th column replaced by b: theoretically pretty, practically hopeless (it costs n + 1 determinants). And the rank characterisation: rank(A) = r ⟺ r is the largest size of a square submatrix with nonzero determinant — a definition of rank that never mentions elimination.

Block matrices: the Schur complement

  • Partition a square A into four blocks with A₁₁ square and invertible. The matrix Z = A₂₂ − A₂₁A₁₁⁻¹A₁₂ is the Schur complement of A₁₁ in A, and it makes both the determinant and the inverse factor cleanly.
  • The determinant is the product of the two: |A| = |A₁₁|·|Z|. It follows from a block LU factorization that is worth memorising in its own right — A equals a block-lower-triangular matrix times a block-unit-upper-triangular one.
  • Why a statistician cares. Take A = [X y]ᵀ[X y] with X of full column rank. The Schur complement of XᵀX is yᵀy − yᵀX(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy — literally the residual sum of squares of the least-squares fit (§14). And in a Gaussian, the Schur complement of a covariance block is the conditional covariance: block algebra is conditioning.
  • The same partitioned formula holds with generalized inverses substituted throughout, which is how it survives the rank-deficient case.

Triangular matrices

  • The determinant of a triangular matrix (upper, lower, or diagonal) is the product of its diagonal entries. Hugely useful — and it's why det(I) = 1 at every size.
  • Intuition: every term in the determinant expansion is a product picking exactly one entry from each row and each column. Every such term except the main-diagonal one must reach into both the upper and lower triangle, so it contains a zero and dies.
det([[a, b, c], [0, e, f], [0, 0, i]]) = a·e·i det(I) = 1

Determinant and row reduction

Since triangular determinants are trivial, the practical route is: eliminate to echelon form, multiply the diagonal, then undo the bookkeeping. Each elementary operation has a known effect.

Algebraic properties

det(AB) = det(A)·det(B) det(Aᵀ) = det(A) det(A⁻¹) = 1 / det(A) det(βA) = βⁿ·det(A) for A ∈ ℝⁿˣⁿ det(I) = 1 det(triangular) = ∏ diagonal
  • det(AB) = det(A)det(B) is the volume-scaling statement: apply two transforms, the volume factors multiply. It also makes det(A⁻¹) = 1/det(A) automatic, since det(A)det(A⁻¹) = det(I) = 1 — and instantly re-proves that a singular matrix has no inverse (you can't divide by 0).
  • Scalars are not gentle: det(βA) = βⁿ det(A), not β det(A), because scaling the matrix scales all n rows. Doubling a 10×10 matrix multiplies its determinant by 1024.
  • A zero on the diagonal does not by itself force det = 0 — that implication holds only for triangular matrices. [[0, 0, 1], [1, 0, 0], [2, 1, 0]] has an all-zero diagonal and det = 1.

Theory vs. practice

  • The determinant is conceptually central (invertibility, eigenvalues, change-of-variables in integration, the Gaussian likelihood's normalising constant) but a poor numerical tool. In floating point, a genuinely singular matrix returns a determinant of 1e−15, not 0 — so "is det == 0?" is never a valid singularity test.
  • Worse, the magnitude scales multiplicatively with size: build random singular matrices and their computed determinants grow exponentially with n, drowning the signal you wanted. Test rank via singular values above a tolerance, or gauge invertibility with the condition number (§16), instead.
  • Shifting helps in practice: adding λI with a small λ to a near-singular matrix pushes the determinant away from zero and stabilises the inverse — the same trick as ridge regularization in regularization.
  • Computationally: np.linalg.det (via LU); for likelihoods prefer np.linalg.slogdet, which returns sign and log-magnitude and doesn't overflow.
det = 0 is the alarm bell: the matrix is singular — rank-deficient, non-invertible, it collapses a dimension, and 0 is one of its eigenvalues. (Drag the 2×2 transform in §06 until the area vanishes.)

12 Matrix inverse

The "undo" matrix: A⁻¹ reverses exactly what A did, so A⁻¹A = AA⁻¹ = I. Matrix division doesn't exist — this is what replaces it.

Concepts and applications

  • The scalar analogy: to solve 3x = 1 you multiply both sides by 3⁻¹, because 3·3⁻¹ = 1 and 1 is the multiplicative identity. For matrices the identity is I, and the inverse is whatever matrix multiplies A to give it. And just as 0 has no reciprocal, not every matrix has an inverse.
  • The headline application: Ax = b ⟹ A⁻¹Ax = A⁻¹b ⟹ x = A⁻¹b. One matrix, one multiplication, the whole system solved.
  • Multiply on the same side of both sides. Since AB ≠ BA in general, A⁻¹Ax = bA⁻¹ is nonsense; here it isn't even conformable, because b is a column vector.
  • Existence requires exactly two things: the matrix is square, and it is full rank (equivalently det ≠ 0, equivalently no linear dependencies, equivalently a trivial null space). A rank-deficient transform destroyed a dimension — that information is gone, so no undo can exist.
  • Uniqueness: if an inverse exists it is the only one. One-line proof: suppose B and C both invert A; then C = CI = C(AB) = (CA)B = IB = B.
  • The "full" (two-sided) inverse is one of the rare places matrix multiplication does commute: AA⁻¹ = A⁻¹A = I. Rectangular matrices only get one-sided inverses.
algebra of the inverse(A⁻¹)⁻¹ = A (Aᵀ)⁻¹ = (A⁻¹)ᵀ = A⁻ᵀ (cA)⁻¹ = (1/c)·A⁻¹ (ABC)⁻¹ = C⁻¹B⁻¹A⁻¹ ← LIVE EVIL: the order reverses, exactly like the transpose A = Aᵀ ⟹ A⁻¹ = A⁻ᵀ ← the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric

  • A product can be invertible even when its factors aren't square or individually invertible — AᵀA is the standing example, and the reason least-squares works at all.

Two shortcuts: diagonal and 2×2

  • Diagonal: reciprocate each diagonal entry, leave the zeros alone. It fails exactly when some diagonal entry is 0 — the singular case, staring you in the face as a division by zero.
  • Do not generalise that to "reciprocate every element". Even one nonzero off-diagonal entry breaks it: [[3, 0, 2], [0, ½, 0], [0, 0, 4]] element-wise-reciprocated gives a product with a stray 8/3 in the corner, not I.
  • 2×2: compute the determinant first and stop if it's zero; otherwise swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by Δ. It's often cleanest to leave 1/Δ outside the matrix rather than distribute fractions.
2×2 inverse[[a, b], [c, d]]⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc) · [[d, −b], [−c, a]] [[1, 2], [2, 3]]⁻¹ = 1/(−1) · [[3, −2], [−2, 1]] = [[−3, 2], [2, −1]] [[1, 2], [2, 4]]⁻¹ : Δ = 4 − 4 = 0 ⟹ stop, no inverse

The MCA algorithm — Minors, Cofactors, Adjugate

  1. Minors matrix M: entry mᵢⱼ is the determinant of the submatrix left after deleting row i and column j. Tedious and the main time sink — don't rush it.
  2. Cofactors matrix C = G ⊙ M: Hadamard-multiply by the checkerboard sign grid gᵢⱼ = (−1)^(i+j), starting + at the top-left. Signs come from both the minor and the grid — most errors live here.
  3. Adjugate: transpose the cofactors matrix. Then divide by det(A) — the determinant of the original matrix, not of M or C.

  • The 2×2 shortcut is MCA: the minors of a 2×2 are just the opposite diagonal entries, the sign grid swaps the off-diagonal signs, the transpose swaps the diagonal.
  • Same O(n!) blow-up as cofactor expansion for the determinant (§11), so MCA is a pencil-and-paper tool for n ≤ 3, not an algorithm.

Inverse via row reduction

  • Augment with the identity and reduce: rref([A | I]) → [I | A⁻¹]. If the left block cannot be driven to I, the matrix is singular and you're done.
  • Why it works: it's n systems solved at once. Solving Axᵢ = eᵢ for each column eᵢ of I gives the columns of X in AX = I — i.e. X = A⁻¹. A solution is guaranteed for every eᵢ precisely when C(A) spans all of ℝⁿ, which is what full rank means.

One-sided inverses for rectangular matrices

  • Left inverse (tall, m > n, full column rank). A itself isn't invertible, but AᵀA is n×n, and rank(AᵀA) = rank(A) = n, so AᵀA is invertible. Then (AᵀA)⁻¹(AᵀA) = I; drop the second pair of parentheses and regroup, and the first three factors form a matrix that left-multiplies A into I.
  • Right inverse (wide, m < n, full row rank). The mirror image: start from AAᵀ, which is m×m and invertible, and attach the inverse on the right.
  • One-sided means one-sided. A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ is a perfectly legal product with matching shapes — but it is not I. It's the projection matrix onto C(A) (§13), which is why least-squares (§14) is the very next idea.

The pseudoinverse

  • Every matrix — square or not, full rank or not — has a Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse A⁺ (also written A† or A*). A singular matrix can have many "a pseudoinverse", but the Moore–Penrose one is unique, which is why it's the default.
  • It degrades gracefully: it is A⁻¹ when the full inverse exists, and is the left/right inverse when those exist. When neither exists, AA⁺ is only approximately I — but the defining identity A A⁺ A = A always holds exactly.
  • It's sided like the one-sided inverses: AA⁺ ≠ A⁺A in general. Both products are symmetric projection matrices — A⁺A projects onto the row space, AA⁺ onto the column space.
  • Computed from the SVD (§16): invert the nonzero singular values, leave the zeros alone. That's also why it is numerically well behaved where explicit inversion is not. In code: np.linalg.pinv.

Generalized inverses: what survives when nothing is invertible

  • Start from the weakest possible demand. A matrix G is a generalized inverse (or g-inverse, written A⁻) of A if AGA = A — nothing more. If A is m×n then A⁻ is n×m. Every matrix has one, of any shape and any rank, and if A is square and nonsingular then A⁻ = A⁻¹ and it is the only one.
  • It is usually far from unique — and that is the point, not a defect. The freedom is exactly the freedom in the solution set of Ax = b. Example: A = [[1, 2], [2, 4]] is rank 1, and G = [[1, 0], [0, 0]] satisfies AGA = A. So does the pseudoinverse A⁺ = (1/25)·[[1, 2], [2, 4]]. Two genuinely different inverses of the same matrix.
  • The properties that hold for any A⁻, no assumptions: (A⁻)ᵀ is a generalized inverse of Aᵀ; rank(A⁻A) = rank(AA⁻) = rank(A); both A⁻A and I − A⁻A are idempotent; A(I − A⁻A) = 0; and rank(I − A⁻A) = n − rank(A). That last identity is what powers the "particular + null space" description of a solution set in §10.
  • Two useful rank tests fall out: rank(A) = m (full row rank) ⟺ AA⁻ = Iₘ, and rank(A) = n (full column rank) ⟺ A⁻A = Iₙ. The one-sided inverses above are the special cases where a particular generalized inverse happens to be one-sided.
  • Where A⁻ comes from in practice: from the SVD (§16) with A = P[[Δ, 0], [0, 0]]Qᵀ, every generalized inverse has the form Q[[Δ⁻¹, E], [F, G]]Pᵀ for arbitrary blocks E, F, G. Only the top-left block is forced; the rest is pure slack. Setting E = F = G = 0 recovers the Moore-Penrose inverse.
  • Equally, every generalized inverse can be written from any one of them: A⁻ + C − A⁻ACAA⁻ is a generalized inverse for every n×m matrix C, and every generalized inverse arises this way.

computing the solution Factorizations & how systems are really solved

Everything above is exact arithmetic on small matrices. Real solvers never form A⁻¹ and never row-reduce a fresh copy per right-hand side. They factor A once into pieces that are trivial to invert — triangular or orthogonal — and reuse the factors. This section is the bridge from the mathematics to what np.linalg.solve is actually doing.

Why factor at all

  • A factorization writes A = BC where B and C have a shape you can exploit. There are two shapes worth wanting: triangular, because a triangular system solves by substitution in O(n²), and orthogonal, because Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ is free and exact and ‖Qx‖ = ‖x‖ so no error gets amplified.
  • Amortisation. Factoring costs O(n³) once; each subsequent solve costs O(n²). Ten right-hand sides against the same A cost one factorization and ten cheap substitutions — never ten eliminations, and certainly not one inversion plus ten matrix-vector products.
  • The catalogue. Rank (full-rank) factorization for any matrix; LU / LDU for square (and, with care, rectangular) matrices; QR for any matrix; Cholesky and the matrix square root for nonnegative-definite matrices; the eigendecomposition for diagonalizable ones and the SVD for absolutely anything (§16). Each is generated by a sequence of elementary transformations and their inverses.
  • Where the choice actually falls: LU with partial pivoting is the default for a general square system; Cholesky if the matrix is symmetric positive definite (half the work); QR for least-squares and rectangular problems; SVD when the rank is in doubt or you need the most numerically forgiving answer.

LU and LDU

  • Each elimination step is a left-multiplication by a unit lower triangular Lₖ holding the negated multipliers in column k (§10). Collect them: U = Lₙ₋₁⋯L₁A. The product of unit lower triangular matrices is unit lower triangular, and so is its inverse — call that inverse L, and you have A = LU with L unit lower triangular (1s down the diagonal) and U upper triangular. LU is Gaussian elimination, with the receipts kept.
  • The multipliers you divided by during elimination are literally the entries of L. Nothing is discovered; nothing is thrown away. Cost: ≈ n³/3 multiply-adds, the same as the elimination it records.
  • LDU just pulls U's diagonal out into a diagonal D, leaving a unit upper triangular factor: A = LDU with 1s on both triangular diagonals. This form is symmetric-friendly — for symmetric A it becomes A = LDLᵀ, which needs no square roots.
  • Existence is not automatic and not implied by invertibility. The permutation matrix [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is perfectly invertible yet has no LU factorization (a unit lower triangular times upper triangular always has a nonzero (1,1) entry when nonsingular). Conversely, a singular matrix can have one: any upper trapezoidal matrix is its own U with L = I. A sufficient condition is that every leading principal submatrix A_k be nonsingular — and then det(A_k) = u₁₁u₂₂⋯u_kk, so the leading principal minors are readable straight off U's diagonal.
  • When it fails, permute: PA = LU always exists, and the P is exactly the partial-pivoting swap record. This is what scipy.linalg.lu returns and what np.linalg.solve uses internally. It is also how det is computed: det(A) = (−1)^(#swaps) · ∏ uᵢᵢ.
  • Two ways to compute the same thing: the outer-product form (each step is a rank-1 update of the trailing submatrix — cache-friendly, easy to block) and the inner-product form, which reads each entry off directly as lᵢⱼ = (aᵢⱼ − ∑ₖ lᵢₖuₖⱼ)/uⱼⱼ. Same n³/3, different memory traffic.

Cholesky — the specialist for symmetric positive definite matrices

  • If A is symmetric and positive definite (xᵀAx > 0 for all x ≠ 0, see §17), it factors as A = TᵀT with T upper triangular. Demand positive diagonal entries and T is unique. (The lower-triangular convention A = LLᵀ with L = Tᵀ is equally common; NumPy returns L.)
  • Half the work of LU — roughly n³/6 multiply-adds — because symmetry means you only ever touch one triangle. Storage drops from n² to n(n+1)/2. And the algorithm is unconditionally stable: no pivoting is needed, ever, for a positive definite matrix.
  • The algorithm is short enough to hold in your head: t₁₁ = √a₁₁; the rest of the first row is t₁ⱼ = a₁ⱼ/t₁₁; then for each i ≥ 2, tᵢᵢ = √(aᵢᵢ − ∑ₖ<ᵢ tₖᵢ²) and tᵢⱼ = (aᵢⱼ − ∑ₖ<ᵢ tₖᵢtₖⱼ)/tᵢᵢ. It doubles as a positive-definiteness test: if a quantity under a square root comes out negative, the matrix isn't positive definite, and you know it in O(n³) without touching an eigenvalue.
  • Root-free variant. Write A = TᵀDT with unit diagonal T and store the squares in D; no square roots at all, which matters in fixed-precision or when entries can be near zero. This is exactly the symmetric LDLᵀ.
  • Merely nonnegative definite? Then a unique T still exists with r = rank(A) positive diagonal entries and n − r zero rows — same algorithm, except that when tᵢᵢ comes out as 0 you zero the whole row.
  • Cholesky ≠ matrix square root. The square root A^½ = VC^½Vᵀ (from the eigendecomposition) is symmetric, and is likewise unique among nonnegative definite matrices. The Cholesky factor is triangular. The square root is the theoretically natural object; the Cholesky factor is the one you compute.
  • Everywhere in statistics: Cholesky of a covariance matrix generates correlated Gaussian samples (x = μ + Tᵀz), evaluates the multivariate normal density (log|Σ| = 2∑log tᵢᵢ, no overflow), and whitens data. It is also the fast route through the normal equations of §14.

Householder reflections

  • A Householder reflector is H = I − 2uuᵀ for a unit vector u. It reflects space through the hyperplane perpendicular to u: Hu = −u, and Hv = v for every v ⟂ u. It is symmetric, orthogonal, and its own inverse (H² = I), and det(H) = −1.
  • uuᵀ is a rank-1 symmetric idempotent, so a reflector is a "rank-one update" of the identity — cheap to store (just u) and cheap to apply (Hx = x − 2u(uᵀx) is two vector operations, never an n×n matrix product).
  • The killer property: one reflector zeros an entire column below a chosen entry. To send x to ±‖x‖e₁, form q = x + sign(x₁)‖x‖e₁ and set u = q/‖q‖. The sign is chosen to add quantities of like sign — picking the other sign invites catastrophic cancellation when x is already nearly aligned with e₁.
  • Being orthogonal, H preserves lengths, so the target is forced to be ±‖x‖ in its single surviving slot. That is also why the process cannot amplify rounding error: every intermediate matrix has the same norm as the original.

Givens rotations

  • Where a reflector clears a whole column at once, a Givens rotation G_pq clears one entry, touching only rows p and q. It is the identity with four entries changed: c at (p,p) and (q,q), s at (p,q), −s at (q,p), with c = x_p/r, s = x_q/r, r = √(x_p² + x_q²).
  • The angle θ is never needed — only a pair (c, s) with c² + s² = 1. No trigonometry is evaluated anywhere in the algorithm.
  • When to prefer rotations over reflections: when the matrix is sparse (a rotation disturbs only two rows, so it creates far less fill-in), when the data arrive one row at a time (streaming/online least squares), or when you need to update an existing factorization after adding observations. Applied naively they cost about twice as much as reflectors; fast Givens rotations factor out the cos θ and work with tangents, halving the multiplications, at the price of having to rescale periodically to stop the accumulated diagonal factors from drifting apart in magnitude.
  • Sandwiched as GᵀXG they preserve symmetry, which is how symmetric eigenvalue algorithms (Jacobi) chase off-diagonal entries to zero.

QR, and how the factorizations relate to one another

  • A = QR with Q orthogonal and R upper triangular/trapezoidal exists for every matrix. For tall A the "skinny" or economy form A = Q₁R₁ — Q₁ is m×n with orthonormal columns, R₁ is n×n upper triangular — is the one you almost always want. Full details and the Gram-Schmidt construction are in §13.
  • Three ways to build it, all ≈ 2n³/3 multiply-adds — about twice the cost of LU, which is what you pay for orthogonality: Householder reflections (the default), Givens rotations (sparse or streaming), and modified Gram-Schmidt (conceptually clearest, least stable).
  • Rank-revealing QR. Add column pivoting and you get A = QTEπᵀ with T = [[T₁, T₂], [0, 0]] and T₁ an r×r upper triangular block — the rank is now visible. One more round of orthogonal transformations from the right gives the unique A = QRUᵀ with R = [[R₁, 0], [0, 0]], from which the Moore-Penrose inverse reads off immediately. In practice this is a good and much cheaper alternative to the SVD for determining rank — though the pivoting has to be done carefully, since the computations are sensitive to rounding.
  • The identity that ties QR to Cholesky: AᵀA = (QR)ᵀQR = RᵀR. So the R of the QR factorization of A is the Cholesky factor of AᵀA (once you fix the signs by demanding a nonnegative diagonal, which also makes both factorizations unique). Two algorithms, one triangular matrix — but QR reaches it without ever forming AᵀA, which is the entire numerical argument for QR least-squares.
  • And to the SVD: A = UΣVᵀ gives AᵀA = VΣ²Vᵀ, the eigendecomposition of the Gram matrix. Singular values of A are square roots of eigenvalues of AᵀA; condition numbers square.
  • Incomplete factorizations. For huge sparse systems an exact factorization destroys sparsity ("fill-in"). Deliberately approximate factors — incomplete LU, incomplete Cholesky — preserve the sparsity pattern at the cost of exactness and are used as preconditioners for iterative methods rather than as solvers.

Direct vs iterative, and updating a solution

  • Direct methods (everything above) finish in a fixed number of steps determined only by the size of the matrix, and would be exact in exact arithmetic. Iterative methods generate a sequence x⁽⁰⁾, x⁽¹⁾, … converging to the solution, and you stop when a convergence criterion — ‖x⁽ᵏ⁾ − x⁽ᵏ⁻¹⁾‖ or the residual norm ‖Ax⁽ᵏ⁾ − b‖ — is small enough.
  • When iterative wins: very large systems, sparse systems (you may never need A in memory at all, only the ability to compute Av), and parallel hardware, which iterative schemes exploit far more readily than the inherently sequential elimination.
  • Gauss-Seidel, the simplest: split A = D + L + U into diagonal, strictly lower, strictly upper, and iterate x⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = (D + L)⁻¹(b − Ux⁽ᵏ⁾) — i.e. update each coordinate in turn using the newest values available. It converges from any starting point ⟺ the spectral radius of (D + L)⁻¹U is < 1, and converges faster the smaller that radius is. Successive over-relaxation extrapolates each update to shrink it.
  • Conjugate gradient, for symmetric positive definite A: minimise the quadratic ½xᵀAx − bᵀx, whose negative gradient at x⁽ᵏ⁾ is exactly the residual r⁽ᵏ⁾ = b − Ax⁽ᵏ⁾. Steepest descent zigzags on an elongated bowl; CG instead picks search directions that are A-conjugate, (p⁽ᵏ⁾)ᵀAp⁽ⁱ⁾ = 0, which in exact arithmetic reaches the solution in at most n steps and in practice reaches a good one in far fewer. Same geometry as the optimizers in optimization.
  • Iterative refinement is different from both: it starts from an already-computed direct solution x⁽⁰⁾, forms the residual r = b − Ax⁽⁰⁾ in higher precision, solves Ah = r with the factors you already have, and sets x⁽¹⁾ = x⁽⁰⁾ + h. The higher precision in the residual step is the whole point — at working precision r is pure noise. Cheap (the factorization is reused), and it is what makes Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting unconditionally stable.
  • Updating. If A changes by a rank-one perturbation Ã = A − uvᵀ, the Sherman-Morrison formula gives the new inverse without refactoring; the rank-k version is the Woodbury formula. Note the caution: you do not actually form inverses — having already factored A, you solve Ay = uᵢ for the few columns of U, and the remaining system is only k×k. This is the backbone of online/recursive least squares, Kalman filtering, and leave-one-out cross-validation.

numerical reality Conditioning & numerical accuracy

A matrix can be invertible and still numerically useless. The condition number is the one number that says how much a small error in your data can be magnified in your answer — and it is the reason "is det(A) == 0?" is never the right question.

Ill-conditioning, demonstrated

  • A problem is ill-conditioned when small changes to the input produce large changes to the output. For Ax = b that means a tiny perturbation of A or b moves x a lot. Nothing about this is a computer artefact — it is a property of the problem, and it would ruin hand calculation just as thoroughly.
  • Take the system below. Its exact solution is x = (1, 1). Change the second constant by 0.001 and the solution jumps to (0, 3). Change one coefficient by 0.001 instead and it jumps to (2, −1). Perturbations of order 10⁻³ produce changes of order 1: an amplification of about 3000×.
  • The geometry. The two lines are almost parallel — the cosine of the angle between them is 1 − 2×10⁻⁷, an angle of about 0.034°. Two nearly parallel lines have an intersection point that slides enormously when either line is nudged. In statistics the same picture is called collinearity or multicollinearity: an angle between one predictor and the span of the others that is small but not zero.
  • Note that this is the exact opposite failure to the small-pivot example in §10. There, a healthy problem was wrecked by a careless algorithm. Here, no algorithm can save you — the answer genuinely is that sensitive, and the best you can hope for is to be told so.

The condition number

  • The derivation is three lines. Perturb b to b + δb; the new solution is x + δx with δx = A⁻¹δb. Take norms: ‖δx‖ ≤ ‖A⁻¹‖‖δb‖. Also b = Ax gives ‖b‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖x‖, i.e. 1/‖x‖ ≤ ‖A‖/‖b‖. Multiply the two inequalities and the absolute errors cancel into relative ones.
  • That bound defines the condition number with respect to inversion, κ(A) = ‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖. It is the worst-case amplification factor from relative input error to relative output error — and it is sharp (take A = I and it is attained). Because it bounds something you do not want to be large, a large condition number is bad.
  • The definition needs an induced matrix norm but doesn't pin down which; different choices give different values that are usually the same order of magnitude, and all the structural properties are norm-independent. The L₂ or spectral condition number is the standard one.
  • κ₂ is a ratio of singular values, σ_max/σ_min — the most-stretched direction over the least-stretched one, since κ₂(A) = max‖Ax‖/‖x‖ ÷ min‖Ax‖/‖x‖. Stated this way it extends immediately to singular and rectangular matrices: κ₂ = σ_max / (smallest nonzero σ). Careful with a common slip — κ₂ is the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue magnitude only for symmetric (more generally normal) matrices. For the example above, the eigenvalue ratio gives 3556 while the correct κ₂ is 3612.
  • Artificial ill-conditioning. A = diag(1, ε) has κ = 1/ε, huge for small ε — yet nothing about solving Ax = b is delicate, and simply multiplying the second row by 1/ε makes κ = 1. A condition number inflated purely by rows or columns living on wildly different scales is telling you to rescale, not to panic. Always ask whether a large κ is real collinearity or just units.
  • The determinant answers none of this. det(diag(ε, ε, …, ε)) for a 100×100 matrix is ε¹⁰⁰ — astronomically small for a perfectly conditioned matrix — while a genuinely singular matrix computed in floating point returns 10⁻¹⁵, not 0. Determinant magnitude conflates scale with singularity; the condition number does not.

Forward error, backward error, and how many digits survive

  • Forward error is what you want to know and usually cannot: how far the computed x̂ is from the true x. Backward error is what you can usually prove: how much you would have to perturb the problem for x̂ to be its exact answer — the smallest δA, δb with (A + δA)x̂ = b + δb.
  • An algorithm is backward stable if it always returns the exact solution of a nearby problem, i.e. its backward error is of the order of machine precision. That is the strongest promise a solver can make, because no algorithm can beat the sensitivity of the problem itself. forward error ≲ condition number × backward error — the algorithm supplies the second factor, the problem supplies the first, and you are stuck with the product.
  • The digit-counting rule of thumb. If the data are accurate to p decimal digits and κ(A) ≈ 10ᵗ, then the relative error in the solution is roughly 10^(t − p): you lose about t digits. With double precision (p ≈ 16) and κ ≈ 10⁸ you keep about 8 digits — usually fine. At κ ≈ 10¹⁶ you keep none, and the computed answer is noise.
  • Caveats worth carrying. The bound is a worst case over perturbation directions and is often pessimistic. The condition number is itself expensive to compute and the usual estimators are not always reliable. And it is a norm-wise statement — individual components of x can be far better or far worse determined than the bound suggests.
  • Empirically, for a matrix of independent zero-mean unit-variance random entries, the condition number grows roughly linearly in the size of the matrix. Bigger problems are harder, but not catastrophically so unless there is structure driving the collinearity.

What to do about it

  • Never square the condition number for free. κ₂(XᵀX) = κ₂(X)², so solving the normal equations halves the number of correct digits compared with working on X directly. That is the whole argument for the QR route to least squares (§14) and why np.linalg.lstsq exists alongside — and instead of — an explicit inverse.
  • Rescale before you diagnose. Centre and standardise columns; a condition number driven by units is not a modelling problem.
  • Regularize. For λ > 0, the eigenvalues of XᵀX + λI are dᵢ + λ, and max(dᵢ + λ)/min(dᵢ + λ) < max(dᵢ)/min(dᵢ) — ridge strictly improves the condition number, and it does so most where it is needed most, because adding λ to a near-zero eigenvalue changes it proportionally far more than adding λ to a large one. That is the linear-algebra content of ridge regularization: not a statistical hack, a conditioning fix that happens to also trade variance for bias.
  • Prefer orthogonal transformations. They preserve κ₂ exactly, so a chain of Householder reflections or Givens rotations can never make a problem worse conditioned than it started. This is the deep reason QR, the SVD, and orthogonal iterations dominate serious numerical linear algebra.
  • Use iterative refinement when you have a factorization already and want a few more digits — cheap, and it converts partial pivoting into an unconditionally stable method.
  • Test rank with singular values, not with det. Count the σᵢ above a tolerance (np.linalg.matrix_rank does exactly this), and report np.linalg.cond alongside any fitted model whose coefficients you intend to interpret.

Three different sentences, often confused: "A is singular" is a statement about exact rank; "A is ill-conditioned" is a statement about sensitivity; "the algorithm is unstable" is a statement about the method. A well-conditioned problem can be ruined by a bad algorithm (a small pivot), and an ill-conditioned problem defeats even a perfect one. Diagnose which you have before reaching for a fix.
