02 Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers — and, equivalently, an arrow with a length and a direction. Holding both pictures at once is the whole game, and every operation below has a formula and a picture.

Scalars — the things that stretch

  • A scalar is a single number: 4, −17.3, π. Notationally scalars get lowercase Greek letters (λ, α, β, γ) precisely so they can never be mistaken for vectors or matrices.
  • The name is earned, not decorative: a scalar scales — stretches or shrinks — a vector without rotating it. That one property is the seed of subspaces, linear combinations, and eigendecomposition.
  • A scalar is a point on the number line. An arrow from 0 to that point is a 1-element vector, not a scalar. Keep the two apart from day one.

Two definitions of the same object

  • Algebra: an ordered list of n numbers. Order matters — [3, 1] and [1, 3] are different vectors even though they share elements and dimensionality.
  • Geometry: a line segment with a magnitude (length) and a direction, living in an n-axis coordinate system. Element vᵢ says how far to travel along axis i.
  • Dimensionality = number of elements. [3 4 6 1 −4 5] is a 6-D vector; algebra scales to any n, geometry stops being drawable past 3. Use geometry for intuition in 2-D/3-D, then trust the algebra everywhere else.
  • Careful: "dimension" of a vector (element count) is not the same as its length ‖v‖ (a magnitude). A 6-D vector can be short; a 2-D vector can be enormous.
  • Brackets vs. parentheses is pure style: [2 5 5] = (2 5 5). But |2 5 5| and ‖2 5 5‖ are not vectors — those bars mean magnitude.

Position-independence & standard position

  • A vector's definition contains no starting point. [1 −2] means "one unit along axis 1, two units backwards along axis 2" — drawn anywhere on the page it is still the same vector.
  • This is the key difference from a coordinate, which is a unique point in space. Infinitely many arrows, one vector; one coordinate, one point.
  • Put the tail at the origin and the vector is in standard position — only then does the head coincide with the coordinate of the same name. Assume standard position unless told otherwise.

Orientation, transpose, notation

  • Vectors stand up (column) or lie down (row). Orientation does not change dimensionality — it changes what operations are legal.
  • Convention: a vector is a column unless stated otherwise, so a row vector is written vᵀ. Inside running text a column is often typed as a transposed row: w = [1 2 3]ᵀ.
  • Transpose swaps rows and columns; values and their order are untouched. Double transpose returns the original — (vᵀ)ᵀ = v — a step that shows up in a surprising number of proofs.
  • Written notation: bold lowercase v for the vector (an arrow overhead when handwritten), non-bold vᵢ for its i-th element. Confusingly, vᵢ can also mean the i-th vector in a list v₁, v₂, … — context decides.
  • In code, a plain 1-D array has no orientation; you impose one with an extra bracket layer (numpy: np.array([[2],[5]]) is a column, .T transposes).
transpose[4 3 0]ᵀ = [4, 3, 0] as a column (vᵀ)ᵀ = v

Addition and subtraction

  • Algebra: element-wise. Valid only for equal dimensionality — there is nothing to add the extra elements to.
  • Geometry of a + b: tip-to-tail. Slide b's tail to a's head; the arrow from a's tail to b's head is the sum. Because you can slide either one first, addition is commutative: a + b = b + a.
  • Geometry of a − b, view 1: flip b (multiply by −1, a 180° rotation) and add tip-to-tail.
  • Geometry of a − b, view 2: leave both in standard position and draw the arrow from the head of b to the head of a. Not in standard position — irrelevant, since vectors are position-free. The two views give the same arrow.
  • Order matters: a − b = −(b − a). Same length, opposite direction — you have just swapped which end carries the arrowhead.
element-wisec = a + b = [a₁+b₁, a₂+b₂, …, aₙ+bₙ]ᵀ worked[1, 2, 3]ᵀ − [9, −8, 7]ᵀ = [−8, 10, −4]ᵀ

Vector-scalar multiplication

  • Algebra: multiply every element by the scalar. Element-wise ⇒ commutative: λv = vλ.
  • Geometry: stretch (|λ| > 1) or shrink (0 < |λ| < 1) without rotating. Direction is invariant to scaling.
  • Negative λ makes the arrow point backwards — a 180° flip. The scaled vector always meets the original at 0° or 180°.
  • The deeper reason: every scalar multiple λv lands on the same infinite line through the origin. That line is a 1-D subspace (§04), and "direction" really means "which line," not "which way along it."
  • λ = 0 collapses the vector to a point at the origin. Asking for its angle is meaningless.
  • Try it: draw λv for ten random λ — every arrow lies on one line. That picture is the 1-D subspace.
scalar-vectorλv = [λv₁, λv₂, …, λvₙ]ᵀ 3·[−1, 3, 0, 2] = [−3, 9, 0, 6]

Special vectors and magnitude

  • Zeros vector 0: every element is zero (a vector with some zeros is just a vector with some zeros). It sits at the origin, and its direction is not zero — it is undefined. It also drags any set containing it into linear dependence (§04).
  • Magnitude / length / norm ‖v‖ is the geometric length: root of the sum of squares. It is a scalar, always ≥ 0, and zero only for the zeros vector.
  • ‖v‖² = vᵀv — the dot product of a vector with itself (§03). On mean-centred data that same quantity is (up to a divisor) the variance, which is why norms and statistics keep colliding.
  • Dividing by the norm gives a unit vector (§03); the operation is undefined for 0.

03 Vector multiplication

There are four ways to multiply two vectors — dot, outer, element-wise, and cross. The dot product is the one that runs nearly all of machine learning, and it deserves most of the space.

The dot product — algebra

  • Also called the inner product or the scalar product (not to be confused with scalar-vector multiplication). Two vectors go in, one number comes out.
  • Recipe: multiply corresponding elements, then sum. Four notations mean exactly the same thing — a·b, ⟨a, b⟩, aᵀb, and the explicit sum. aᵀb is the most useful because it exposes the dot product as a special case of matrix multiplication (§06): a 1×n row times an n×1 column is 1×1.
  • Requires equal dimensionality. With a 5-element and a 4-element vector there is simply nothing to pair the fifth element with.
  • A vector with itself gives the sum of squares — the squared norm ‖a‖². On mean-centred data that is (up to a divisor) the variance.
  • It is the computational atom underneath convolution, correlation, the Fourier transform, matrix multiplication, filtering, and every dense layer of a neural network.
definitionα = a·b = ⟨a, b⟩ = aᵀb = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ aᵢbᵢ worked[1 2 3 4]·[5 6 7 8] = 5 + 12 + 21 + 32 = 70 selfaᵀa = Σᵢ aᵢ² = ‖a‖²

Properties — and the one that fails

  • Commutative: aᵀb = bᵀa. Each term is a product of two scalars, and scalar multiplication commutes.
  • Distributive: wᵀ(u + v) = wᵀu + wᵀv — you may split one vector into a sum and split the dot product with it, or run it backwards and merge two dot products that share a vector. This is the property that unlocks the algebra ⇄ geometry proof below.
  • Scalars factor freely: γ(uᵀv) = (γu)ᵀv = uᵀ(γv) = (uᵀv)γ. Handy in proofs, otherwise unremarkable.
  • NOT associative: uᵀ(vᵀw) ≠ (uᵀv)ᵀw. Neither side is even a dot product — each is a scalar times a vector, and the two sides come out with different orientations. With mismatched dimensionalities one side may not exist at all. (Matrix multiplication is associative — §06 — so don't over-generalise from here.)
associativity fails — u = [1,2], v = [1,3], w = [2,3]uᵀ(vᵀw) = [1 2]·11 = [11 22] (a row) (uᵀv)ᵀw = 7·[2, 3]ᵀ = [14, 21]ᵀ (a column) distributivity holds — same u, v, wwᵀ(u + v) = [2 3]·[2, 5]ᵀ = 19 wᵀu + wᵀv = 8 + 11 = 19 ✓

Applying distributivity to a vector with itself gives an identity used constantly in least-squares and in the proof below:

The dot product — geometry

  • Geometrically the dot product is length × length × cosine of the angle between them. Rearranged, it defines the angle between two vectors — and it does so in any number of dimensions, generalising a 2-D notion of angle to ℝⁿ.
  • Norms are non-negative, so the sign of the dot product is decided entirely by the angle. Five cases worth memorising:

Why algebra and geometry agree

  1. Start from the law of cosines, c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos θ. It is the Pythagorean theorem with a correction term: drop a perpendicular to split the triangle into two right triangles, write the legs as a·sin θ and b − a·cos θ, apply Pythagoras, and simplify with sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. At θ = 90° the correction vanishes and Pythagoras returns.
  2. Now read the triangle's sides as vectors with the third side c = a − b, so ‖c‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2‖a‖‖b‖cos θ.
  3. Separately expand ‖a − b‖² with distributivity and commutativity: ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2aᵀb.
  4. Set the two expressions equal. The ‖a‖², ‖b‖² and the factor −2 cancel from both sides, leaving aᵀb = ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ. The element-wise sum and the trigonometric product were never two operations — they are one.
the two expansions that must match‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2aᵀb ‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2‖a‖‖b‖cos θ ⟹ aᵀb = ‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

  • The dot product is bounded by the product of the norms. The proof is now one line: take absolute values in the geometric formula and note |cos θ| ≤ 1.
  • Equality holds exactly when the two vectors are collinear (θ = 0° or 180°, i.e. a linearly dependent pair, a = λb). So the inequality is really a statement about linear dependence.
  • Consequence: −1 ≤ cos θ ≤ 1 always, so the "angle" formula never asks for the arccosine of an out-of-range number — correlations are automatically bounded by ±1.
  • It reappears when normalising the quadratic form (§17), which underpins PCA (§19).

Linear weighted combination

  • Scalar-multiply a set of vectors and add them up. Also called a linear mixture or weighted combination; the scalars are weights or coefficients.
  • All vectors must share a dimensionality. Subtraction needs no separate rule — use negative weights. Weights may be zero.
  • This deceptively simple operation is the engine of span, subspaces, and basis (§04), and it is exactly what matrix-vector multiplication computes (§06). Every linear model prediction ŷ = w₁x₁ + … + wₙxₙ is one of these.
definitionw = λ₁v₁ + λ₂v₂ + … + λₙvₙ worked — λ = (1, 2, −3)1·[4, 5, 1]ᵀ + 2·[−4, 0, −4]ᵀ − 3·[1, 3, 2]ᵀ = [−7, −4, −13]ᵀ

The outer product

  • Shapes are everything. For v with M elements and w with N elements: vᵀw is 1×1 (a scalar, needs M = N), while vwᵀ is M×N (a matrix, and M ≠ N is perfectly fine).
  • Element view: entry (i, j) is vᵢwⱼ.
  • Column view: every column is the left column vector rescaled by one element of the right row vector.
  • Row view: every row is the right row vector rescaled by one element of the left column vector.
  • Because every column is a multiple of one vector, the outer product is always a rank-1 matrix (§07) — it is the atom out of which eigendecomposition and the SVD rebuild any matrix (§15, §16), and the basis of low-rank image compression.
  • Swapping the order transposes the result: wvᵀ = (vwᵀ)ᵀ. So the outer product of a vector with itself, vvᵀ, is always symmetric — that is a covariance matrix in miniature (§18).
  • Neat identity worth knowing: tr(vwᵀ) = vᵀw — the trace of the outer product is the dot product (§05).
definition & shapes(vwᵀ)ᵢⱼ = vᵢwⱼ vᵀw → 1×1 vwᵀ → M×N worked — column view (each column ∝ [1,2,3,4]ᵀ)[1, 2, 3, 4]ᵀ [4 1 −1] = [[4, 1, −1], [8, 2, −2], [12, 3, −3], [16, 4, −4]]

Element-wise (Hadamard) product

  • Multiply corresponding elements; the result is a vector of the same size. Requires equal dimensionality. Common notation: a ⊙ b.
  • Arguably not a linear-algebra operation at all — it is a compact way to organise a batch of ordinary scalar multiplications. But it is everywhere in code: gating in LSTMs/GRUs, dropout masks, and element-wise scaling of features.
  • Language gotcha: * means element-wise in some environments and matrix multiplication in others. Read the docs before trusting an asterisk.
Hadamardc = a ⊙ b = [a₁b₁, a₂b₂, …, aₙbₙ] [3, 6, 9, 0] ⊙ [2, −2, 4, −4] = [6, −12, 36, 0]

Cross product

  • Defined only for two 3-element vectors, and it returns a 3-element vector (unlike the dot product, which returns a scalar).
  • The result is orthogonal to both inputs — i.e. normal to the plane they span. Its direction follows the right-hand rule: point the fingers along a, curl them toward b, and the thumb gives a × b. Consequently the cross product anti-commutes: b × a = −(a × b).
  • Its magnitude uses sine where the dot product used cosine, so ‖a × b‖ is the area of the parallelogram spanned by a and b — and it is zero exactly when the two vectors are collinear.
  • Heavily used in geometry, graphics, and physics (surface integrals, torque, normals). Almost never used in data analysis, statistics, or ML — included for completeness.
definitiona × b = [a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, a₃b₁ − a₁b₃, a₁b₂ − a₂b₁]ᵀ ‖a × b‖ = ‖a‖‖b‖ sin(θ_ab) worked[3, 5, 1] × [4, −2, −1] = [−3, 7, −26] check: (−3,7,−26)·(3,5,1) = 0 ✓ and ·(4,−2,−1) = 0 ✓

Unit vectors and normalisation

  • A unit vector has ‖v̂‖ = 1; the hat is the standard mark. To build one, scale by the reciprocal of the norm — the norm is a scalar, so division is legal and the direction is unchanged.
  • Divide by ‖v‖, not by vᵀv. Proof it works: ‖(1/‖v‖)v‖ = (1/‖v‖)‖v‖ = 1.
  • The zeros vector cannot be normalised — algebraically it is division by zero, geometrically a vector with no length has no direction to preserve.
  • If both vectors are unit length, the dot product is the cosine of the angle: ûᵀv̂ = cos θ. That is exactly "cosine similarity" in retrieval and embedding search.
  • Unit vectors keep numerics well-behaved and are the ingredient of orthogonal matrices (mutually orthogonal, unit-length columns — §05, §13).
normalisationv̂ = v / ‖v‖ = v / √(Σᵢ vᵢ²) workedv = [3, 4]ᵀ, ‖v‖ = 5, v̂ = [0.6, 0.8]ᵀ, ‖v̂‖ = √(0.36 + 0.64) = 1 ✓ unit vectorsûᵀv̂ = cos θ
The dot product is the workhorse of the whole subject — length, angle, projection, correlation, orthogonality, and matrix multiplication are all dot products in disguise. The other three products each have one job: outer builds rank-1 matrices, Hadamard batches scalar multiplies, cross finds a normal in ℝ³.
Shape discipline: before writing any product, say the shapes out loud. aᵀb collapses to 1×1, abᵀ expands to M×N, a ⊙ b keeps the shape, a × b exists only in ℝ³. Most "linear algebra bugs" are shape bugs.

04 Vector spaces

The "space" a vector lives in, described by five ideas that recur for the rest of these notes: dimension, subspace, span, linear independence, basis. Everything later — rank, the four fundamental subspaces, least-squares, PCA — is these five ideas wearing different clothes.

Dimension and fields

  • Algebraically the dimensionality of a vector is its number of elements. Position is meaningful (the 2nd element is the 2nd dimension) but not hierarchical — no dimension is intrinsically more important than another.
  • Geometrically it is the number of coordinate axes the vector needs. A 2-D vector and a 3-D vector are both lines; they just live in different ambient spaces.
  • Axes need not be orthogonal. The Cartesian right-angle grid is a convenient default, not a requirement — skewed axes are legal and sometimes better (data compression, components analyses). This freedom is what makes "basis" interesting.
  • A field is a set of numbers on which addition, subtraction, multiplication and division all behave: closed under + and ×, with additive inverses and multiplicative inverses for every non-zero element, plus associativity, commutativity, identities (0 and 1), and distributivity.
  • ℝ (real), ℚ (rational, numbers writable as p/q), and ℂ (complex — §09) are fields. Gotcha: ℤ (the integers) is not a field — 2 has no integer reciprocal. It is a ring. The hollow letters are all "number systems"; only some of them are fields.
  • In practice the field notation is used mostly to declare dimensionality: ℝ² is all real 2-element vectors, ℝ⁴ all real 4-element vectors. It also silently states legality: if w ∈ ℝᴹ and v ∈ ℝᴺ, then wᵀv exists only when M = N.

Vector spaces — the axioms

  • A vector space is any set of objects on which addition and scalar multiplication are defined and well behaved. The axioms are exactly the arithmetic rules you already assume.
  • Everything downstream leans on vector subspaces rather than on vector spaces themselves; the axioms are here mainly so the two terms never get confused.
vector-space axiomsadditive inverse v + (−v) = 0 associativity (v + w) + u = v + (w + u) commutativity v + w = w + v additive identity v + 0 = v multiplicative identity 1v = v distributivity (α + β)(v + w) = αv + αw + βv + βw

Subspaces and the ambient space

  • Geometry: a subspace is the set of all points reachable by stretching and adding a collection of vectors. One non-zero vector generates an infinitely long line through the origin; two non-collinear vectors generate an infinite plane; and so on.
  • A single vector is not a subspace — it is finite. The set {λv : λ ∈ ℝ} is. This is the deeper meaning of "scaling doesn't change direction" (§02): every λv lands on one line, and that line is the object that matters.
  • Algebra — two conditions: closed under addition and scalar multiplication, and contains the origin. "Closed" means what happens in the subspace stays in the subspace.
  • The origin condition is not an extra rule — it follows from closure with λ = 0. Practical consequence: a line or plane that misses the origin is not a subspace, no matter how flat it looks.
  • Ambient dimensionality is the N in ℝᴺ — the size of the vectors. A subspace of dimension d sits inside an ambient space of dimension N ≥ d. A plane through the origin in ℝ³ is a 2-D subspace in a 3-D ambient space.
  • Counting: an N-dimensional ambient space admits exactly N + 1 subspace dimensionalities (0 through N), but an infinite number of subspaces — except at the two extremes, where there is exactly one each: the single point {0} (0-D) and the whole ambient space itself (N-D).
algebraic definition∀ v, w ∈ V, ∀ λ, α ∈ ℝ: λv + αw ∈ V a 1-D subspace inside ℝ³V = { λ[1 3 4] , λ ∈ ℝ } [3 9 12] ∈ V (λ=3) [−2 −6 −8] ∈ V (λ=−2) [1 3 5] ∉ V

Subsets vs. subspaces

  • A subset is just any region of space. It may have boundaries, may be finite, and need not contain the origin. A subspace is a subset with the two closure properties — so every subspace is a subset, but almost no subset is a subspace.
  • Worked negatives: the upper half-plane (all points with y ≥ 0) contains the origin but fails closure — take v = [2, 3] and λ = −1 and you leave the set. The unit sphere x² + y² + z² = 1 fails both tests (no origin, not closed). The line y = 2x + 0.1 fails only the origin test — and that is enough.
  • Worked positive: the line y = 4x, extended infinitely both ways, is a subspace — an infinite line through the origin.
  • ML relevance: this is exactly why a linear model without an intercept can only fit predictions lying in a subspace through the origin, and why adding a bias term is a genuinely different (affine, not linear) object.

Span

  • Grammatically: a subspace is a noun, span is a verb. A set of vectors spans; the thing it produces is a subspace.
  • Formally, the span is the set of all linear weighted combinations (§03) of the vectors in the set.
  • "Is w in the span of S?" means: can w be built exactly from some weighted combination of the vectors in S? Sometimes the answer is instantly visible (if every vector in S has a zero in slot 3 and w does not, no combination can create something from nothing). Usually it is not — the general algorithm needs rank / Gaussian elimination (§07, §10).
  • The number of vectors is not the dimension of the span. Redundant vectors in the set are harmless — they just don't add reach. Five 4-element vectors can span a mere 2-D subspace.

Linear independence

  • Independence is a property of a set, never of a single vector or of one vector "inside" a set. It makes no sense to ask whether v₃ is independent.
  • Geometric definition: a set is independent if the dimensionality of the subspace it spans equals the number of vectors in it. Three vectors that all lie in one plane cannot build a cube — so that set is dependent.
  • Equivalent phrasing: no vector in the set lies in the span of the others.
  • Counting theorem: any set of M > N vectors in ℝᴺ is necessarily dependent. Any set of M ≤ N vectors could be independent — "could" is doing real work: 20 vectors in ℝ²⁵ that all lie on one line are still dependent.
  • Formal (algebraic) definition: the set is dependent if some weighted combination equals the zeros vector with at least one λ ≠ 0. If the only way to reach 0 is the trivial all-zeros weighting, the set is independent.
  • Why that definition? Move one term across and divide: v₁ = (λ₂/λ₁)v₂ + … + (λₙ/λ₁)vₙ. The ratios are just scalars — so "a non-trivial combination hits zero" and "one vector is a combination of the others" are the same statement.
  • Corollary: any set containing the zeros vector is dependent — weight the zeros vector by 1000, everything else by 0, and the sum is 0 with a non-zero coefficient.
  • Scalable test: stack the vectors as columns of a matrix and compute the rank (§07). Rank = number of vectors ⟹ independent; rank < number of vectors ⟹ dependent. Numerically: count singular values above a tolerance.
  • Terminology trap: "independent" in statistics means zero correlation; that case is called orthogonal in linear algebra. What linear algebra calls "independent," statistics would call "correlated." Different fields, opposite-sounding words.

A by-hand procedure, useful for building intuition (it does not scale — use rank for anything real):

  1. Count the vectors M and compare to the ambient N. If M > N, stop: dependent.
  2. Look for the zeros vector. If present, stop: dependent.
  3. Look for zeros in individual elements. A zero in one vector paired with non-zeros in that slot elsewhere is evidence toward independence — you cannot make something from nothing.
  4. Guess weights that reproduce one vector's first element from the others, then check whether the same weights work for every remaining element. One mismatched element kills that candidate; exhausting the candidates proves independence.

Basis

  • A basis = span + independence. The set must (1) span the target subspace and (2) be linearly independent. Its size is the dimension of that subspace.
  • Geometrically a basis is a ruler for the space: the fundamental units (directions and lengths) in which coordinates are measured.
  • The standard basis — all 0s with a single 1 — is popular because it is unit-length and mutually orthogonal (an orthonormal set), which makes coordinates trivial to read off.
  • A basis need not span all of ℝᴺ. {[1,0,0]ᵀ, [0,1,0]ᵀ} is a perfectly good basis — for the z = 0 plane, a 2-D subspace of ℝ³.
  • Why independence is required: so that coordinates are unique. Drop independence and a single vector gets infinitely many coordinate lists — see the worked example below. Mathematicians chose uniqueness.
  • Infinitely many bases describe the same space (any independent pair of 2-D vectors is a basis for ℝ²), but each vector has exactly one coordinate list within a given basis. Bases are not created equal: some make a problem trivial, others make it a nightmare.
  • Finding a good basis is the job in much of data science. PCA (§19) picks the basis that maximises variance along successive orthogonal axes; independent components analysis picks a (generally non-orthogonal) basis that maximises statistical independence. Same data, different rulers, different stories.

A basis is a coordinate system. The same vector has different number-lists in different bases — change-of-basis is what eigendecomposition (§15) and the SVD (§16) ultimately do, and choosing a good basis is what PCA (§19) is for.
Two tests to keep in your pocket: "does it contain the origin and survive scaling?" (subspace) and "can I reach 0 non-trivially?" (dependence). Almost every question in this section reduces to one of those two.
