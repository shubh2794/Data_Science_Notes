02 Vectors

A vector is an ordered list of numbers — and, equivalently, an arrow with a length and a direction. Holding both pictures at once is the whole game, and every operation below has a formula and a picture.

Scalars — the things that stretch

  • A scalar is a single number: 4, −17.3, π. Notationally scalars get lowercase Greek letters (λ, α, β, γ) precisely so they can never be mistaken for vectors or matrices.
  • The name is earned, not decorative: a scalar scales — stretches or shrinks — a vector without rotating it. That one property is the seed of subspaces, linear combinations, and eigendecomposition.
  • A scalar is a point on the number line. An arrow from 0 to that point is a 1-element vector, not a scalar. Keep the two apart from day one.

Two definitions of the same object

  • Algebra: an ordered list of n numbers. Order matters — [3, 1] and [1, 3] are different vectors even though they share elements and dimensionality.
  • Geometry: a line segment with a magnitude (length) and a direction, living in an n-axis coordinate system. Element vᵢ says how far to travel along axis i.
  • Dimensionality = number of elements. [3 4 6 1 −4 5] is a 6-D vector; algebra scales to any n, geometry stops being drawable past 3. Use geometry for intuition in 2-D/3-D, then trust the algebra everywhere else.
  • Careful: "dimension" of a vector (element count) is not the same as its length ‖v‖ (a magnitude). A 6-D vector can be short; a 2-D vector can be enormous.
  • Brackets vs. parentheses is pure style: [2 5 5] = (2 5 5). But |2 5 5| and ‖2 5 5‖ are not vectors — those bars mean magnitude.

Position-independence & standard position

  • A vector's definition contains no starting point. [1 −2] means "one unit along axis 1, two units backwards along axis 2" — drawn anywhere on the page it is still the same vector.
  • This is the key difference from a coordinate, which is a unique point in space. Infinitely many arrows, one vector; one coordinate, one point.
  • Put the tail at the origin and the vector is in standard position — only then does the head coincide with the coordinate of the same name. Assume standard position unless told otherwise.

Orientation, transpose, notation

  • Vectors stand up (column) or lie down (row). Orientation does not change dimensionality — it changes what operations are legal.
  • Convention: a vector is a column unless stated otherwise, so a row vector is written vᵀ. Inside running text a column is often typed as a transposed row: w = [1 2 3]ᵀ.
  • Transpose swaps rows and columns; values and their order are untouched. Double transpose returns the original — (vᵀ)ᵀ = v — a step that shows up in a surprising number of proofs.
  • Written notation: bold lowercase v for the vector (an arrow overhead when handwritten), non-bold vᵢ for its i-th element. Confusingly, vᵢ can also mean the i-th vector in a list v₁, v₂, … — context decides.
  • In code, a plain 1-D array has no orientation; you impose one with an extra bracket layer (numpy: np.array([[2],[5]]) is a column, .T transposes).
transpose[4 3 0]ᵀ = [4, 3, 0] as a column (vᵀ)ᵀ = v

Addition and subtraction

  • Algebra: element-wise. Valid only for equal dimensionality — there is nothing to add the extra elements to.
  • Geometry of a + b: tip-to-tail. Slide b's tail to a's head; the arrow from a's tail to b's head is the sum. Because you can slide either one first, addition is commutative: a + b = b + a.
  • Geometry of a − b, view 1: flip b (multiply by −1, a 180° rotation) and add tip-to-tail.
  • Geometry of a − b, view 2: leave both in standard position and draw the arrow from the head of b to the head of a. Not in standard position — irrelevant, since vectors are position-free. The two views give the same arrow.
  • Order matters: a − b = −(b − a). Same length, opposite direction — you have just swapped which end carries the arrowhead.

Vector-scalar multiplication

  • Algebra: multiply every element by the scalar. Element-wise ⇒ commutative: λv = vλ.
  • Geometry: stretch (|λ| > 1) or shrink (0 < |λ| < 1) without rotating. Direction is invariant to scaling.
  • Negative λ makes the arrow point backwards — a 180° flip. The scaled vector always meets the original at 0° or 180°.
  • The deeper reason: every scalar multiple λv lands on the same infinite line through the origin. That line is a 1-D subspace (§04), and "direction" really means "which line," not "which way along it."
  • λ = 0 collapses the vector to a point at the origin. Asking for its angle is meaningless.
  • Try it: draw λv for ten random λ — every arrow lies on one line. That picture is the 1-D subspace.

Special vectors and magnitude

  • Zeros vector 0: every element is zero (a vector with some zeros is just a vector with some zeros). It sits at the origin, and its direction is not zero — it is undefined. It also drags any set containing it into linear dependence (§04).
  • Magnitude / length / norm ‖v‖ is the geometric length: root of the sum of squares. It is a scalar, always ≥ 0, and zero only for the zeros vector.
  • ‖v‖² = vᵀv — the dot product of a vector with itself (§03). On mean-centred data that same quantity is (up to a divisor) the variance, which is why norms and statistics keep colliding.
  • Dividing by the norm gives a unit vector (§03); the operation is undefined for 0.

03 Vector multiplication

There are four ways to multiply two vectors — dot, outer, element-wise, and cross. The dot product is the one that runs nearly all of machine learning, and it deserves most of the space.

The dot product — algebra

  • Also called the inner product or the scalar product (not to be confused with scalar-vector multiplication). Two vectors go in, one number comes out.
  • Recipe: multiply corresponding elements, then sum. Four notations mean exactly the same thing — a·b, ⟨a, b⟩, aᵀb, and the explicit sum. aᵀb is the most useful because it exposes the dot product as a special case of matrix multiplication (§06): a 1×n row times an n×1 column is 1×1.
  • Requires equal dimensionality. With a 5-element and a 4-element vector there is simply nothing to pair the fifth element with.
  • A vector with itself gives the sum of squares — the squared norm ‖a‖². On mean-centred data that is (up to a divisor) the variance.
  • It is the computational atom underneath convolution, correlation, the Fourier transform, matrix multiplication, filtering, and every dense layer of a neural network.

Properties — and the one that fails

  • Commutative: aᵀb = bᵀa. Each term is a product of two scalars, and scalar multiplication commutes.
  • Distributive: wᵀ(u + v) = wᵀu + wᵀv — you may split one vector into a sum and split the dot product with it, or run it backwards and merge two dot products that share a vector. This is the property that unlocks the algebra ⇄ geometry proof below.
  • Scalars factor freely: γ(uᵀv) = (γu)ᵀv = uᵀ(γv) = (uᵀv)γ. Handy in proofs, otherwise unremarkable.
  • NOT associative: uᵀ(vᵀw) ≠ (uᵀv)ᵀw. Neither side is even a dot product — each is a scalar times a vector, and the two sides come out with different orientations. With mismatched dimensionalities one side may not exist at all. (Matrix multiplication is associative — §06 — so don't over-generalise from here.)

The dot product — geometry

  • Geometrically the dot product is length × length × cosine of the angle between them. Rearranged, it defines the angle between two vectors — and it does so in any number of dimensions, generalising a 2-D notion of angle to ℝⁿ.

Why algebra and geometry agree

  1. Start from the law of cosines, c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos θ. It is the Pythagorean theorem with a correction term: drop a perpendicular to split the triangle into two right triangles, write the legs as a·sin θ and b − a·cos θ, apply Pythagoras, and simplify with sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. At θ = 90° the correction vanishes and Pythagoras returns.
  2. Now read the triangle's sides as vectors with the third side c = a − b, so ‖c‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2‖a‖‖b‖cos θ.
  3. Separately expand ‖a − b‖² with distributivity and commutativity: ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2aᵀb.
  4. Set the two expressions equal. The ‖a‖², ‖b‖² and the factor −2 cancel from both sides, leaving aᵀb = ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ. The element-wise sum and the trigonometric product were never two operations — they are one.
the two expansions that must match‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2aᵀb ‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2‖a‖‖b‖cos θ ⟹ aᵀb = ‖a‖‖b‖ cos θ

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

  • The dot product is bounded by the product of the norms. The proof is now one line: take absolute values in the geometric formula and note |cos θ| ≤ 1.
  • Equality holds exactly when the two vectors are collinear (θ = 0° or 180°, i.e. a linearly dependent pair, a = λb). So the inequality is really a statement about linear dependence.
  • Consequence: −1 ≤ cos θ ≤ 1 always, so the "angle" formula never asks for the arccosine of an out-of-range number — correlations are automatically bounded by ±1.
  • It reappears when normalising the quadratic form (§17), which underpins PCA (§19).

Linear weighted combination

  • Scalar-multiply a set of vectors and add them up. Also called a linear mixture or weighted combination; the scalars are weights or coefficients.
  • All vectors must share a dimensionality. Subtraction needs no separate rule — use negative weights. Weights may be zero.
  • This deceptively simple operation is the engine of span, subspaces, and basis (§04), and it is exactly what matrix-vector multiplication computes (§06). Every linear model prediction ŷ = w₁x₁ + … + wₙxₙ is one of these.

The outer product

  • Shapes are everything. For v with M elements and w with N elements: vᵀw is 1×1 (a scalar, needs M = N), while vwᵀ is M×N (a matrix, and M ≠ N is perfectly fine).
  • Element view: entry (i, j) is vᵢwⱼ.
  • Column view: every column is the left column vector rescaled by one element of the right row vector.
  • Row view: every row is the right row vector rescaled by one element of the left column vector.
  • Because every column is a multiple of one vector, the outer product is always a rank-1 matrix (§07) — it is the atom out of which eigendecomposition and the SVD rebuild any matrix (§15, §16), and the basis of low-rank image compression.
  • Swapping the order transposes the result: wvᵀ = (vwᵀ)ᵀ. So the outer product of a vector with itself, vvᵀ, is always symmetric — that is a covariance matrix in miniature (§18).
  • Neat identity worth knowing: tr(vwᵀ) = vᵀw — the trace of the outer product is the dot product (§05).

Element-wise (Hadamard) product

  • Multiply corresponding elements; the result is a vector of the same size. Requires equal dimensionality. Common notation: a ⊙ b.
  • Arguably not a linear-algebra operation at all — it is a compact way to organise a batch of ordinary scalar multiplications. But it is everywhere in code: gating in LSTMs/GRUs, dropout masks, and element-wise scaling of features.
  • Language gotcha: * means element-wise in some environments and matrix multiplication in others. Read the docs before trusting an asterisk.

Cross product

  • Defined only for two 3-element vectors, and it returns a 3-element vector (unlike the dot product, which returns a scalar).
  • The result is orthogonal to both inputs — i.e. normal to the plane they span. Its direction follows the right-hand rule: point the fingers along a, curl them toward b, and the thumb gives a × b. Consequently the cross product anti-commutes: b × a = −(a × b).
  • Its magnitude uses sine where the dot product used cosine, so ‖a × b‖ is the area of the parallelogram spanned by a and b — and it is zero exactly when the two vectors are collinear.
  • Heavily used in geometry, graphics, and physics (surface integrals, torque, normals). Almost never used in data analysis, statistics, or ML — included for completeness.

Unit vectors and normalisation

  • A unit vector has ‖v̂‖ = 1; the hat is the standard mark. To build one, scale by the reciprocal of the norm — the norm is a scalar, so division is legal and the direction is unchanged.
  • Divide by ‖v‖, not by vᵀv. Proof it works: ‖(1/‖v‖)v‖ = (1/‖v‖)‖v‖ = 1.
  • The zeros vector cannot be normalised — algebraically it is division by zero, geometrically a vector with no length has no direction to preserve.
  • If both vectors are unit length, the dot product is the cosine of the angle: ûᵀv̂ = cos θ. That is exactly "cosine similarity" in retrieval and embedding search.
  • Unit vectors keep numerics well-behaved and are the ingredient of orthogonal matrices (mutually orthogonal, unit-length columns — §05, §13).

The one vector, the mean, and centring

  • The one vector 1 = [1, 1, …, 1]ᵀ (sometimes ı or 𝟙) is the vector every element of which is 1. It is the single most useful "constant" in applied linear algebra: 1ᵀx is the sum of x's elements, ‖1‖ = √n, and 1/√n is the corresponding unit vector.
  • So the mean is a dot product: x̄ = (1ᵀx)/n. The same number read geometrically: x̄·1 is the projection of x onto the one vector (§13) — the best constant approximation to x in the least-squares sense.
  • The centred vector is the residual of that projection: x_c = x − x̄1. Being a residual, it is orthogonal to 1, i.e. 1ᵀx_c = 0 — "a centred vector is one whose elements sum to zero". Centring is linear: (x + y)_c = x_c + y_c.
  • Orthogonality plus the expansion of ‖u + v‖² gives a Pythagorean split of a data vector into "level" and "shape": ‖x‖² = ‖x̄1‖² + ‖x_c‖² = n·x̄² + ‖x_c‖². Rearranged, that is the identity every data analyst knows as Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = Σxᵢ² − n·x̄².
  • Immediate consequence: centring can only shorten a vector, ‖x_c‖ ≤ ‖x‖, with equality exactly when the mean is already 0.
  • Sharper still, for any scalar a: ‖x − a1‖² = ‖x_c‖² + n(a − x̄)². The right-hand side is minimised at a = x̄ — a one-line proof that the mean is the least-squares constant.

Statistics is geometry on centred vectors

  • Once a vector is centred, the three headline statistics are not analogies for length, dot product and angle — they are length, dot product and angle, divided by n − 1.
  • Variance is a squared norm. V(x) = ‖x_c‖²/(n − 1), so the standard deviation s_x = ‖x_c‖/√(n − 1) is literally the length of the centred vector on a per-degree-of-freedom scale. It is invariant to adding a constant (that only moves the vector along 1) and scales as V(ax) = a²V(x) — because ‖λv‖ = |λ|‖v‖.
  • Covariance is a dot product. Cov(x, y) = ⟨x_c, y_c⟩/(n − 1), and Cov(x, x) = V(x) is just vᵀv = ‖v‖² again. Every algebraic property of covariance is a property of the inner product: symmetry, bilinearity, and Cov(a1, y) = 0 (a constant vector centres to 0).
  • Correlation is a cosine. Normalise both centred vectors and take the dot product: r = cos θ between x_c and y_c. Cauchy–Schwarz then proves −1 ≤ r ≤ 1, with equality precisely when x_c and y_c are collinear — i.e. when y = αx + β1 exactly. Perfect correlation is linear dependence, nothing more mystical.
  • Standardising a vector is centre-then-normalise, rescaled so the variance is 1: x_cs = √(n − 1)·x_c/‖x_c‖. Angles (and therefore correlations) are unchanged by scaling — but they are very much changed by centring.
  • That last point is a real trap: angle(x_c, y_c) ≠ angle(x, y) in general. Two all-positive vectors always have an acute angle (cosine similarity near 1), yet can be negatively correlated. Cosine similarity between raw embeddings and Pearson correlation are different measurements; only after centring do they coincide.
  • A non-zero vector can centre to the zero vector — that is exactly a constant feature. Its norm is 0, so it cannot be normalised, which is the geometric reason a constant column has undefined correlation, zero variance, and no predictive content (and why it makes XᵀX singular, §07).
  • Stacking these per-vector definitions for every column of a data matrix produces the covariance matrix (§18) — a whole grid of dot products, which is why it is symmetric and positive semi-definite by construction.

Why this section earns its place: it collapses two vocabularies into one. Mean = projection onto 1. Variance = squared length. Covariance = inner product. Correlation = cosine. The dot product is the workhorse of the whole subject — length, angle, projection, correlation, orthogonality, and matrix multiplication are all dot products in disguise. The other three products each have one job: outer builds rank-1 matrices, Hadamard batches scalar multiplies, cross finds a normal in ℝ³.
Shape discipline: before writing any product, say the shapes out loud. aᵀb collapses to 1×1, abᵀ expands to M×N, a ⊙ b keeps the shape, a × b exists only in ℝ³. Most "linear algebra bugs" are shape bugs.

04 Vector spaces

The "space" a vector lives in, described by five ideas that recur for the rest of these notes: dimension, subspace, span, linear independence, basis. Everything later — rank, the four fundamental subspaces, least-squares, PCA — is these five ideas wearing different clothes.

Dimension and fields

  • Algebraically the dimensionality of a vector is its number of elements. Position is meaningful (the 2nd element is the 2nd dimension) but not hierarchical — no dimension is intrinsically more important than another.
  • Geometrically it is the number of coordinate axes the vector needs. A 2-D vector and a 3-D vector are both lines; they just live in different ambient spaces.
  • Axes need not be orthogonal. The Cartesian right-angle grid is a convenient default, not a requirement — skewed axes are legal and sometimes better (data compression, components analyses). This freedom is what makes "basis" interesting.
  • A field is a set of numbers on which addition, subtraction, multiplication and division all behave: closed under + and ×, with additive inverses and multiplicative inverses for every non-zero element, plus associativity, commutativity, identities (0 and 1), and distributivity.
  • ℝ (real), ℚ (rational, numbers writable as p/q), and ℂ (complex — §09) are fields. Gotcha: ℤ (the integers) is not a field — 2 has no integer reciprocal. It is a ring. The hollow letters are all "number systems"; only some of them are fields.
  • In practice the field notation is used mostly to declare dimensionality: ℝ² is all real 2-element vectors, ℝ⁴ all real 4-element vectors. It also silently states legality: if w ∈ ℝᴹ and v ∈ ℝᴺ, then wᵀv exists only when M = N.

Vector spaces — the axioms

  • A vector space is any set of objects on which addition and scalar multiplication are defined and well behaved. The axioms are exactly the arithmetic rules you already assume.
  • Everything downstream leans on vector subspaces rather than on vector spaces themselves; the axioms are here mainly so the two terms never get confused.

The ten conditions, stated in full

  • The compact list above is the working version. The formal definition is ten conditions — five governing addition, five governing scalar multiplication — and it is worth meeting them once in full, because the two that do all the real work are easy to skip past: the two closure conditions.
  • A vector space is a set together with two operations, never a set on its own. The same set can be a vector space under one pair of operations and fail to be one under another. Almost always the operations are inherited from a larger space — you reuse that space's + and · on a subset.
  • Two kinds of addition and two kinds of multiplication hide inside these lines. In (r + s)v = rv + sv the left + adds two scalars, the right + adds two vectors; in (rs)v = r(sv) the rs is ordinary number multiplication. Nothing is ambiguous — the types decide — but it repays a slow first reading.
  • Closure says the operations are always defined and never escape: any two members add to a member, any scalar times a member is a member. Every other condition is arithmetic you already trust; closure is the condition a set actually fails.

  • What the axioms buy you is that the familiar cancellations are legal. Four small results, each provable in one line, are used silently everywhere below:
  • 0·v = 0 — the scalar zero times any vector is the zero vector. Proof: 0v = (0 + 0)v = 0v + 0v by (7); add −(0v) to both sides. This is exactly why every subspace must contain the origin.
  • (−1)·v = −v. Proof: v + (−1)v = 1v + (−1)v = (1 + (−1))v = 0v = 0, so (−1)v is the additive inverse. This is why "flip the arrow" and "scale by −1" are the same operation (§02).
  • The zero vector and each additive inverse are unique. Proof for zero: if 0 and 0′ both work then 0 = 0 + 0′ = 0′. So "the" origin is well defined.
  • λv = 0 ⟹ λ = 0 or v = 0. Proof: if λ ≠ 0 multiply by 1/λ and use (9), (10). This is the fact that makes the definition of linear independence below bite.
  • Condition (10) is not redundant. Drop it and you can define a "space" on ℝ² with r·v = 0 for every r; conditions (1)–(9) all hold, and the object is useless. The axioms are a minimal list, not a padded one.

Spaces that are not columns of numbers

  • The payoff for stating axioms instead of drawing arrows: things that are not lists of numbers satisfy them too, so every theorem below applies to them for free.
  • Matrices. ℝ^(m×n) under element-wise addition and scaling is a vector space of dimension mn. So "the space of all 2×2 matrices" is a legitimate 4-dimensional vector space — and that is why it makes sense to talk about a basis of matrices, or the distance between two matrices (§06).
  • Polynomials. P_n = {a₀ + a₁x + … + aₙxⁿ} has dimension n + 1 with the obvious basis ⟨1, x, x², …, xⁿ⟩. Differentiation and integration are then linear maps between such spaces.
  • Functions. All real functions on a set form a vector space; so does the two-dimensional space {a·cos θ + b·sin θ}, with basis ⟨cos θ, sin θ⟩. Fourier analysis is basis-choosing in a function space.
  • Solution sets. The solutions of a homogeneous system Ay = 0 form a vector space — this is precisely the null space N(A) (§08). Closure is the statement A(αy₁ + βy₂) = 0.
  • Non-examples worth internalising. The solutions of a non-homogeneous system Ax = b (b ≠ 0) are not a vector space — no zero vector, no closure; they are a flat (an affine space), a subspace shifted off the origin. Likewise the set of vectors with non-negative entries: closed under addition, not under scaling by −1. And the unit sphere: closed under neither.
  • That non-example is the reason a linear model with an intercept is affine rather than linear, and the reason we usually smuggle the intercept back into linearity by appending a column of ones (the one vector, §03).

Subspaces and the ambient space

  • Geometry: a subspace is the set of all points reachable by stretching and adding a collection of vectors. One non-zero vector generates an infinitely long line through the origin; two non-collinear vectors generate an infinite plane; and so on.
  • A single vector is not a subspace — it is finite. The set {λv : λ ∈ ℝ} is. This is the deeper meaning of "scaling doesn't change direction" (§02): every λv lands on one line, and that line is the object that matters.
  • Algebra — two conditions: closed under addition and scalar multiplication, and contains the origin. "Closed" means what happens in the subspace stays in the subspace.
  • The origin condition is not an extra rule — it follows from closure with λ = 0. Practical consequence: a line or plane that misses the origin is not a subspace, no matter how flat it looks.
  • Ambient dimensionality is the N in ℝᴺ — the size of the vectors. A subspace of dimension d sits inside an ambient space of dimension N ≥ d. A plane through the origin in ℝ³ is a 2-D subspace in a 3-D ambient space.
  • Counting: an N-dimensional ambient space admits exactly N + 1 subspace dimensionalities (0 through N), but an infinite number of subspaces — except at the two extremes, where there is exactly one each: the single point {0} (0-D) and the whole ambient space itself (N-D).

Subsets vs. subspaces

  • A subset is just any region of space. It may have boundaries, may be finite, and need not contain the origin. A subspace is a subset with the two closure properties — so every subspace is a subset, but almost no subset is a subspace.
  • Worked negatives: the upper half-plane (all points with y ≥ 0) contains the origin but fails closure — take v = [2, 3] and λ = −1 and you leave the set. The unit sphere x² + y² + z² = 1 fails both tests (no origin, not closed). The line y = 2x + 0.1 fails only the origin test — and that is enough.
  • Worked positive: the line y = 4x, extended infinitely both ways, is a subspace — an infinite line through the origin.
  • ML relevance: this is exactly why a linear model without an intercept can only fit predictions lying in a subspace through the origin, and why adding a bias term is a genuinely different (affine, not linear) object.

The subspace test — one line, and why it is enough

  • Checking all ten conditions to certify a subspace would be miserable. It is also unnecessary. Lemma. For a non-empty subset S of a vector space, under the inherited operations these are equivalent: (1) S is a subspace; (2) S is closed under linear combinations of pairs, r₁s₁ + r₂s₂ ∈ S; (3) S is closed under linear combinations of any number of its members.
  • Briefly: a non-empty subset is a subspace exactly when it is closed under linear combinations. That is the test to memorise, and statement (2) is the one to actually use.
  • Proof sketch of the only hard direction, (2) ⟹ (1). Closure under pairs immediately gives conditions (1) and (6). Conditions (2), (3), (7)–(10) are inherited: they are equations that already hold in the big space, and both sides live in S. That leaves the two existence conditions — and closure supplies them. Pick any s ∈ S (possible, since S is non-empty). Then 0·s + 0·s = 0 ∈ S, giving condition (4); and 0·s + (−1)·s = −s ∈ S, giving condition (5). ∎
  • Two things to notice. First, non-emptiness is doing work: the empty set is closed under everything vacuously but has no zero vector. Second, this proof is where the "must contain the origin" rule comes from — it is derived, not decreed.
  • Practical recipe: to show a set is a subspace, parametrise it. Rewrite the defining condition so the set appears as a set of unrestricted linear combinations — e.g. {[x,y,z]ᵀ : x − 2y + z = 0} = {y[2,1,0]ᵀ + z[−1,0,1]ᵀ}. A set of unrestricted combinations is a span, and a span is a subspace (next subsection). The same parametrisation hands you a basis for free.
  • Intersections behave; unions do not. The intersection of two subspaces is always a subspace (it inherits closure from both). The union almost never is: the x-axis ∪ y-axis in ℝ² contains [1,0] and [0,1] but not their sum. To combine subspaces properly you take the sum, not the union — see "Sums and direct sums" below.

Span

  • Grammatically: a subspace is a noun, span is a verb. A set of vectors spans; the thing it produces is a subspace.
  • Formally, the span is the set of all linear weighted combinations (§03) of the vectors in the set.
  • "Is w in the span of S?" means: can w be built exactly from some weighted combination of the vectors in S? Sometimes the answer is instantly visible (if every vector in S has a zero in slot 3 and w does not, no combination can create something from nothing). Usually it is not — the general algorithm needs rank / Gaussian elimination (§07, §10).
  • The number of vectors is not the dimension of the span. Redundant vectors in the set are harmless — they just don't add reach. Five 4-element vectors can span a mere 2-D subspace.

Span, proved — four lemmas you will reuse all book

  1. The span of any set is a subspace. Take two members of span(S), each a combination of vectors from S, and form p·(c₁s₁ + … + cₙsₙ) + r·(d₁t₁ + … + dₘtₘ). Multiply through: every term is still a scalar times a member of S. A combination of combinations is a combination, so the result is back in span(S), and the one-line subspace test applies. ∎
  2. The converse holds too: every subspace is a span — trivially, it is the span of itself. So "subspace" and "span of some set" describe exactly the same objects. The verb and the noun really are two views of one thing.
  3. Span is idempotent: span(span(S)) = span(S). Same argument. And putting the two lemmas together: span(S) is the smallest subspace containing S — any subspace holding S must, by closure, hold all its combinations.
  4. Adding a redundant vector changes nothing: span(S ∪ {v}) = span(S) if and only if v ∈ span(S). (⟸) expand v inside any combination and you get a combination of S alone. (⟹) is immediate, since v is always in span(S ∪ {v}). ∎
  5. Corollary (the removal test). For v ∈ S, deleting it leaves the span unchanged — span(S − {v}) = span(S) — exactly when v ∈ span(S − {v}), i.e. exactly when v is a "repeat". This corollary is the bridge to linear independence: a spanning set is minimal precisely when nothing in it is a repeat.
  • Convention worth knowing: span(∅) = {0}. The sum of zero-many vectors is defined to be 0, which keeps the lemmas above free of special cases and makes the trivial subspace the span of the empty basis.
  • A space can have many different spanning sets, of many different sizes. In P₂, the sets {x, x²}, {3x − x², 2x}, and {x, x², −x + 2x²} all span the same 2-dimensional subspace. Spanning fixes the subspace, never the set.
  • Alternative notation: square brackets [S] are common for the span, as are sp(S) and span(S). Nothing is standard; all three mean the same set.

Linear independence

  • Independence is a property of a set, never of a single vector or of one vector "inside" a set. It makes no sense to ask whether v₃ is independent.
  • Geometric definition: a set is independent if the dimensionality of the subspace it spans equals the number of vectors in it. Three vectors that all lie in one plane cannot build a cube — so that set is dependent.
  • Equivalent phrasing: no vector in the set lies in the span of the others.
  • Counting theorem: any set of M > N vectors in ℝᴺ is necessarily dependent. Any set of M ≤ N vectors could be independent — "could" is doing real work: 20 vectors in ℝ²⁵ that all lie on one line are still dependent.
  • Formal (algebraic) definition: the set is dependent if some weighted combination equals the zeros vector with at least one λ ≠ 0. If the only way to reach 0 is the trivial all-zeros weighting, the set is independent.
  • Why that definition? Move one term across and divide: v₁ = (λ₂/λ₁)v₂ + … + (λₙ/λ₁)vₙ. The ratios are just scalars — so "a non-trivial combination hits zero" and "one vector is a combination of the others" are the same statement.
  • Corollary: any set containing the zeros vector is dependent — weight the zeros vector by 1000, everything else by 0, and the sum is 0 with a non-zero coefficient.
  • Scalable test: stack the vectors as columns of a matrix and compute the rank (§07). Rank = number of vectors ⟹ independent; rank < number of vectors ⟹ dependent. Numerically: count singular values above a tolerance.
  • Terminology trap: "independent" in statistics means zero correlation; that case is called orthogonal in linear algebra. What linear algebra calls "independent," statistics would call "correlated." Different fields, opposite-sounding words.

A by-hand procedure, useful for building intuition (it does not scale — use rank for anything real):

  1. Count the vectors M and compare to the ambient N. If M > N, stop: dependent.
  2. Look for the zeros vector. If present, stop: dependent.
  3. Look for zeros in individual elements. A zero in one vector paired with non-zeros in that slot elsewhere is evidence toward independence — you cannot make something from nothing.
  4. Guess weights that reproduce one vector's first element from the others, then check whether the same weights work for every remaining element. One mismatched element kills that candidate; exhausting the candidates proves independence.

Independence — the standard results, proved

  1. The two definitions agree. A set is independent (no member is a combination of the others) iff the only linear relation among its members is the trivial one. (⟹) If some non-trivial relation held, isolate a vector with a non-zero coefficient and divide — you have written it in terms of the others. (⟸) If sᵢ = c₁s₁ + … then subtracting sᵢ gives a relation with coefficient −1 on sᵢ, which is non-trivial. ∎ The second phrasing is what you compute with; the first is what it means.
  2. Nothing is special about which vector you isolate. Given 0 = c₁s₁ + … + cₙsₙ, any sᵢ whose coefficient cᵢ ≠ 0 can be solved for: sᵢ = (−c₁/cᵢ)s₁ + … + (−cₙ/cᵢ)sₙ. That is why we prefer the symmetric "linear relation" form, with everything on one side.
  3. Independence ⟺ every removal shrinks the span. Immediate from the removal test of the previous subsection: S is independent iff no member lies in the span of the rest iff deleting any member strictly shrinks the span. So "minimal spanning set" and "independent spanning set" are the same idea — which is exactly why the word basis is about to appear.
  4. Growing an independent set. If S is independent and v ∉ S, then S ∪ {v} is independent iff v ∉ span(S). Proof (⟸ by contraposition): if v = c₁s₁ + … then 0 = c₁s₁ + … − 1·v is a non-trivial relation. Conversely, a non-trivial relation 0 = c₁s₁ + … + cₙ₊₁v must have cₙ₊₁ ≠ 0 (else it is a relation inside the independent S), so v can be solved for and lies in span(S). ∎
  5. Pruning theorem. Every finite set contains an independent subset with the same span. Proof: if the set is dependent, it has a member that is a combination of the others; delete it — the span is unchanged by the removal test. Iterate. The process stops, because the set is finite and the empty set is independent. ∎ This is the abstract version of "row-reduce and keep the pivot columns" (§10).
  6. The first offender. A list s₁, …, sₙ is dependent iff some sᵢ lies in the span of the vectors before it. Proof: walk the prefixes ∅, {s₁}, {s₁,s₂}, …; let i be the first index at which the prefix becomes dependent — then by the growing lemma sᵢ ∈ span(s₁,…,sᵢ₋₁). ∎ Practically: you never need to test every vector against every other; one left-to-right sweep suffices.
  7. Subsets preserve independence; supersets preserve dependence. Both are immediate: a relation among a subset is a relation among the whole set (pad with zero coefficients), and any relation survives adding more vectors. The other two combinations are genuinely open — see the table.

Basis

  • A basis = span + independence. The set must (1) span the target subspace and (2) be linearly independent. Its size is the dimension of that subspace.
  • Geometrically a basis is a ruler for the space: the fundamental units (directions and lengths) in which coordinates are measured.
  • The standard basis — all 0s with a single 1 — is popular because it is unit-length and mutually orthogonal (an orthonormal set), which makes coordinates trivial to read off.
  • A basis need not span all of ℝᴺ. {[1,0,0]ᵀ, [0,1,0]ᵀ} is a perfectly good basis — for the z = 0 plane, a 2-D subspace of ℝ³.
  • Why independence is required: so that coordinates are unique. Drop independence and a single vector gets infinitely many coordinate lists — see the worked example below. Mathematicians chose uniqueness.
  • Infinitely many bases describe the same space (any independent pair of 2-D vectors is a basis for ℝ²), but each vector has exactly one coordinate list within a given basis. Bases are not created equal: some make a problem trivial, others make it a nightmare.
  • Finding a good basis is the job in much of data science. PCA (§19) picks the basis that maximises variance along successive orthogonal axes; independent components analysis picks a (generally non-orthogonal) basis that maximises statistical independence. Same data, different rulers, different stories.

Basis ⟺ unique coordinates (the theorem, and its proof)

  • Theorem. A set is a basis if and only if every vector in the space is a linear combination of its members in one and only one way.
  • Proof. Spanning is exactly "at least one way". So the whole content of the theorem is: a spanning set is independent iff every vector is a combination in at most one way. Take two expansions of the same vector, pad with 0·βᵢ terms so they use the same basis vectors in the same order, and subtract:
v = c₁β₁ + … + cₙβₙ and v = d₁β₁ + … + dₙβₙ ⟺ 0 = (c₁ − d₁)β₁ + … + (cₙ − dₙ)βₙ independence says the only relation is trivial ⟺ cᵢ − dᵢ = 0 for every i ⟺ the two expansions were the same
  • Read the chain in one direction and independence gives uniqueness; read it in the other and uniqueness gives independence. Uniqueness of coordinates is not a bonus property of bases — it is what independence is, seen from the user's side.
  • This is why the dependent set above fails: [−2, 6] has infinitely many coordinate lists in {[1,1]ᵀ, [1,3]ᵀ, [0,2]ᵀ}, one for each vector in the null space of the matrix whose columns they are (§08). Independence is precisely the condition that this null space is trivial.
  • A basis is technically a sequence, not a set: ⟨[2,4]ᵀ, [1,1]ᵀ⟩ and ⟨[1,1]ᵀ, [2,4]ᵀ⟩ are different bases, because they assign coordinates in a different order. Angle brackets are the usual mark for "order matters here". In practice this only matters when you write coordinates down — but you always do.

Coordinates relative to a basis

  • Given a basis B = ⟨b₁, …, bₙ⟩, the coordinate vector (or representation) of v is the column of weights that rebuilds it. These notes write it v[B]; you will also see [v]_B and Rep_B(v). All three name the same list of numbers.
  • Computing coordinates is solving a linear system: Bc = v, where B is the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors. Because the basis is independent, B is square and invertible, so c = B⁻¹v exists and is unique (§12). In the standard basis B = I, so the coordinates are the entries — which is why we normally forget that a coordinate list is a basis-relative object at all.
  • The point of coordinates is that they are faithful: a linear relation holds among some vectors if and only if the same relation, with the same scalars, holds among their coordinate vectors. Formally, a₁v₁ + … + a_kv_k = 0 in V ⟺ a₁v₁[B] + … + a_kv_k[B] = 0 in ℝⁿ.
  • That single lemma is the licence for everything computational in these notes: to answer any linear question about polynomials, matrices or functions, take coordinates and answer it about columns of numbers instead. Independence, span, dimension, rank — all transfer unchanged.
  • The catch: coordinates depend on the basis you picked, and different bases tell different stories about the same data. Choosing the basis is the modelling decision; PCA (§19) is one automated way to make it.

The exchange lemma, and why "dimension" is well defined

  • Every result so far has been about one set at a time. The deepest fact in the subject compares two different bases for the same space — and says they must be the same size. That is what licenses the word dimension.
  • Exchange Lemma. Let B = ⟨b₁, …, bₙ⟩ be a basis and let v = c₁b₁ + … + cₙbₙ with some cᵢ ≠ 0. Then swapping bᵢ out for v yields another basis.
  • Proof. Write B̂ = ⟨b₁, …, bᵢ₋₁, v, bᵢ₊₁, …, bₙ⟩. Independence: take a relation d₁b₁ + … + dᵢv + … + dₙbₙ = 0 and substitute the expansion of v. The coefficient on bᵢ is dᵢcᵢ, which independence of B forces to 0; since cᵢ ≠ 0, dᵢ = 0. Feed that back and every other d is 0 too. Same span: one direction is clear because v ∈ span(B); for the other, the assumption cᵢ ≠ 0 lets you solve for bᵢ = (−c₁/cᵢ)b₁ + … + (1/cᵢ)v + … , so everything B reaches, B̂ reaches too. ∎
  • The cᵢ ≠ 0 condition is the whole trick, and it is where the lemma can fail: you may only swap out a basis vector that the newcomer actually uses. Swap out one it doesn't use and you lose that direction entirely.
  • Theorem. In a finite-dimensional space, all bases have the same number of elements. Proof. Pick a basis B of minimal size n and let D = ⟨d₁, d₂, …⟩ be any other basis, so |D| ≥ n. Expand d₁ in B and exchange it in, giving a basis with one d and n − 1 b's. Inductively, given a basis with k d's and n − k b's (k < n), expand d_{k+1} in it: at least one non-zero coefficient must sit on a b, because otherwise we would have written d_{k+1} in terms of other d's, contradicting the independence of D. Exchange there. After n steps no b's remain, so ⟨d₁, …, dₙ⟩ is already a basis — and any further d_{n+1} would lie in its span, contradicting D's independence. Hence |D| = n. ∎
  • Definition. The dimension of a vector space is the number of vectors in any of its bases. It is now a property of the space, not of the basis you happened to choose.
  • Notice what the proof never used: it never used that D spans, only that it is independent. That gives the next corollary for free.

Five corollaries you will use constantly

  1. No independent set can be larger than the dimension. (The exchange argument never used spanning.) This is the honest proof of the counting rule stated earlier: any M > N vectors in ℝᴺ are dependent.
  2. Any independent set extends to a basis. If it does not already span, there is a vector outside its span; adjoin it — the growing lemma keeps independence. Corollary 1 says the process must stop.
  3. Any spanning set shrinks to a basis. That is the pruning theorem. Together with (2): bases are exactly the maximal independent sets and exactly the minimal spanning sets, and those two descriptions never disagree.
  4. In an n-dimensional space, a set of exactly n vectors is independent ⟺ it spans ⟺ it is a basis. Proof: an independent n-set extends to a basis, but a basis has n elements, so the extension added nothing; a spanning n-set shrinks to a basis, which again has n elements, so nothing was removed. This halves your work forever: with the right count, check one property, get the other free.
  5. Every subspace W ⊆ V has dim W ≤ dim V, with equality only when W = V. Hence the complete inventory of subspaces of ℝ³: the origin (0-D), every line through the origin (1-D), every plane through the origin (2-D), and ℝ³ itself. There is nothing else — no 1.5-dimensional subspace, no five-dimensional subspace hiding inside three-space.

Every n-dimensional real space is ℝⁿ wearing a costume

  • An isomorphism between vector spaces V and W is a map f : V → W that is (i) a correspondence — one-to-one and onto — and (ii) structure-preserving: f(v₁ + v₂) = f(v₁) + f(v₂) and f(rv) = r·f(v). Written V ≅ W, read "V is isomorphic to W". "Morphism" means map; "iso" means same.
  • The two conditions collapse into one: a map preserves addition and scaling iff it preserves linear combinations of two vectors iff it preserves linear combinations of any number. And any such map sends 0 to 0 — because f(0) = f(0·v) = 0·f(v) = 0.
  • The coordinate map is an isomorphism. Fix a basis B for an n-dimensional V; then v ↦ v[B] is one-to-one (unique coordinates), onto (every column of scalars names a combination), and linear (that is the faithfulness lemma above). So V ≅ ℝⁿ.
  • Theorem. Two vector spaces are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. (⟸) both are isomorphic to ℝⁿ, and isomorphism is an equivalence relation — reflexive by the identity, symmetric because the inverse of an isomorphism is one, transitive by composition. (⟹) an isomorphism carries a basis of V to a basis of W: it spans because f is onto, and it is independent because f is one-to-one so only 0 maps to 0. ∎
  • Corollary. Every finite-dimensional real vector space is isomorphic to one and only one ℝⁿ. The ℝⁿ's are a complete list of representatives: dimension is a total classification, which is a startlingly strong result for so cheap a definition.
  • So P₃ ≅ ℝ⁴ (cubics are 4-tuples of coefficients), ℝ^(2×2) ≅ ℝ⁴ (2×2 matrices are 4-tuples of entries), and {a cos θ + b sin θ} ≅ ℝ². All three are "the same space" as far as linear algebra can tell, and every theorem proved for columns applies verbatim.
  • Two cautions. (a) The isomorphism is not canonical — it depends on the basis you chose, and different bases give genuinely different correspondences. (b) Isomorphism only preserves linear structure. Polynomials can be multiplied, differentiated and evaluated; a column of four numbers cannot. "The same" means "indistinguishable by addition and scaling", not "interchangeable".

Sums, direct sums, and complements

  • Subspaces are sets, and sets combine by union — but the union of subspaces is not a subspace. The right operation is the sum: W₁ + W₂ = span(W₁ ∪ W₂), equivalently {w₁ + w₂ : wᵢ ∈ Wᵢ}. It is the smallest subspace containing both.
  • The sum gets you at least one decomposition of every vector. What we usually want from "ℝ³ is built from three axes" is stronger: exactly one. Compare two ways of writing ℝ³ — as x-axis + y-axis + z-axis (a vector's "x part" is unambiguous) versus as xy-plane + yz-plane, where [1,2,3]ᵀ splits as [1,2,0] + [0,0,3] or [1,0,0] + [0,2,3] or infinitely many other ways. The second sum is a sum but not a direct sum.
  • Lemma (three equivalent conditions). For V = W₁ + … + W_k with bases B₁, …, B_k, the following are equivalent: (1) every v has a unique decomposition v = w₁ + … + w_k with wᵢ ∈ Wᵢ; (2) the concatenation B₁ ⌢ … ⌢ B_k is a basis for V; (3) among non-zero vectors drawn from different Wᵢ's, every linear relation is trivial. When they hold we call the subspaces independent and write V = W₁ ⊕ … ⊕ W_k, a direct sum.
  • Corollary. dim(W₁ ⊕ … ⊕ W_k) = dim W₁ + … + dim W_k — immediately, because condition (2) says the basis is the concatenation, and concatenation adds lengths.
  • The two-subspace case is the one you meet. V = W₁ ⊕ W₂ iff V = W₁ + W₂ and W₁ ∩ W₂ = {0}. Then W₁ and W₂ are called complements. Proof sketch: if the sum is direct and v lies in both, then v = v reads as "a member of W₁ equals a member of W₂", which independence forces to be 0; conversely a trivial intersection kills any cross-relation.
  • The general dimension formula covers the non-direct case: dim(W₁ + W₂) = dim W₁ + dim W₂ − dim(W₁ ∩ W₂). Sanity check: two distinct planes through the origin in ℝ³ give 2 + 2 − 1 = 3 — they sum to all of ℝ³ and meet in a line, so the sum is not direct. It also proves that two 3-dimensional subspaces of ℝ⁵ must intersect non-trivially (3 + 3 − dim(∩) ≤ 5).
  • Trap for k > 2: a trivial triple intersection is not enough, and neither are trivial pairwise intersections. In ℝ³ take W₁ = x-axis, W₂ = y-axis, W₃ = the plane {[q, q, r]ᵀ}. They sum to ℝ³ and W₁ ∩ W₂ ∩ W₃ = {0}, yet decompositions are not unique. For three or more summands you must check the real condition (1), (2) or (3).
  • Complements are not unique. In ℝ² the x-axis has the y-axis as a complement, and also the line y = x, and also y = 2x — any line other than itself. And "complement" here is not set complement: two complementary subspaces always share the origin.
  • Where this lands. The orthogonal complement of §08 is a direct-sum decomposition with an extra demand — the two pieces must be perpendicular, not merely independent. That is what makes C(Aᵀ) ⊕ N(A) = ℝⁿ and C(A) ⊕ N(Aᵀ) = ℝᵐ so much more useful than a generic complement: the unique decomposition is computable by projection (§13), and it is what least squares actually does (§14).

A basis is a coordinate system. The same vector has different number-lists in different bases — change-of-basis is what eigendecomposition (§15) and the SVD (§16) ultimately do, and choosing a good basis is what PCA (§19) is for.
Two tests to keep in your pocket: "does it contain the origin and survive scaling?" (subspace) and "can I reach 0 non-trivially?" (dependence). Almost every question in this section reduces to one of those two.
