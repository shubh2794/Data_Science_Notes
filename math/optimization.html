Optimization

How learning actually happens: every fitted model is the answer to a minimization problem. We build the picture from the ground up — the loss landscape, the gradient that points downhill, the step rules that walk it, and the geometry (convexity, conditioning) that decides whether the walk succeeds.

Math & Statistics Foundations Geometry-first Gradient descent

01 The optimization view of learning

Training a model is choosing parameters θ that make a loss as small as possible. Everything else — gradient descent, momentum, Adam, regularization — is machinery for solving that one problem efficiently.

  • The objective: a function J(θ) (the loss, cost, or objective) that scores how badly the model fits. We want the θ that minimizes it.
  • Unconstrained vs constrained: most ML is unconstrained — θ can be anything in ℝⁿ. Constraints (e.g. weights ≥ 0, ‖θ‖ ≤ t) add boundaries to respect.
  • Analytic vs iterative: a few problems (linear least-squares) have a closed form. The rest — anything with millions of parameters or a non-linear model — are solved by iterating: start somewhere, repeatedly step downhill.
  • What "downhill" means: the negative gradient. Calculus gives the direction; optimization decides how far and how fast to walk it.
The core problem θ* = argmin_θ J(θ) with J(θ) = (1/m) Σᵢ ℓ(f(xᵢ; θ), yᵢ) [+ regularizer]

One idea, everywhere: linear/logistic regression, SVMs, neural nets, and even k-means all reduce to "minimize an objective." Master the optimization picture once and it transfers to every model. The derivative machinery itself comes from Calculus, and these methods consume gradients produced by neural-network training (backprop).

02 The loss landscape

Picture J(θ) as a surface over parameter space — valleys are good solutions, hills are bad ones. Optimization is rolling a ball downhill on this surface.

  • Critical points are where ∇J = 0 — the surface is locally flat. They come in three flavors below.
  • Local vs global minimum: a local minimum is lowest in its neighborhood; the global minimum is lowest everywhere. Convex problems have only one (they coincide); non-convex problems may have many.
  • Saddle points are flat but go up in one direction and down in another. In high dimensions they vastly outnumber local minima — they are the real obstacle for deep nets, not bad minima.
  • Plateaus & ravines: nearly-flat regions stall progress; long narrow valleys cause zig-zagging (the conditioning problem in §07).

Why slope = direction: the derivative is the instantaneous rate of change. Where it's positive the function rises to the right, so we move left; where it's negative we move right. Step opposite the gradient and you always head downhill — that is gradient descent in one line.

03 The gradient — steepest ascent

In more than one dimension, the slope becomes a vector: the gradient. It collects the partial derivative in every coordinate and, remarkably, points in the direction of fastest increase.

  • Definition: stack the partial derivatives. Each ∂J/∂θⱼ measures how J changes as you nudge only θⱼ.
  • Direction of steepest ascent: among all unit directions u, the rate of change is the directional derivative ∇J · u, maximized when u aligns with ∇J. So −∇J is steepest descent.
  • Magnitude = steepness: ‖∇J‖ is the slope in that steepest direction. Near a minimum it shrinks toward zero — a natural "are we there yet" signal.
  • Level sets: the gradient is always perpendicular to contour lines of J (lines of constant value) — the shortest way off a contour is straight across it.
Gradient and the steepest-descent step ∇J(θ) = [ ∂J/∂θ₁, ∂J/∂θ₂, …, ∂J/∂θₙ ]ᵀ directional derivative D_u J = ∇J · u = ‖∇J‖ cos φ (maximal at φ = 0) update: θ ← θ − η ∇J(θ)

Worked example. Let J(θ₁, θ₂) = θ₁² + 3θ₂². Then ∇J = [2θ₁, 6θ₂]ᵀ. At θ = (2, 1), ∇J = [4, 6]ᵀ. With η = 0.1 the next point is (2, 1) − 0.1·(4, 6) = (1.6, 0.4) — both coordinates moved toward 0, and θ₂ moved faster because the surface is steeper there.

04 Gradient descent

The fundamental algorithm: start anywhere, repeatedly step a small amount in the negative gradient direction, stop when the gradient is (nearly) zero.

  • The update rule: θ ← θ − η ∇J(θ), applied until convergence. That single line trains most of machine learning.
  • The learning rate η is everything. Too small ⇒ painfully slow, may stall on plateaus. Too large ⇒ overshoots the valley, oscillates, or diverges (loss explodes). There is a sweet spot tied to the curvature.
  • Convergence on a quadratic: for J with curvature (Hessian) eigenvalues in [λ_min, λ_max], gradient descent converges iff 0 < η < 2/λ_max; the best fixed rate is η = 2/(λ_min + λ_max).
  • Stopping: when ‖∇J‖ is below a tolerance, when J stops improving, or after a fixed iteration / time budget.
Batch gradient descent repeat: θ ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = θ ⁽ᵗ⁾ − η ∇J(θ ⁽ᵗ⁾) converges (quadratic case) ⟺ 0 < η < 2 / λ_max(H)

Pick a start, set the learning rate, and step. Low η crawls; raise it and the path overshoots and zig-zags across the bowl; push it past the stability limit and it spirals outward and diverges.

The η dial is the whole craft: the same gradient with three learning rates gives slow-but-safe, fast-and-clean, or explosive. In practice we schedule η — start larger, decay over time — to get speed early and stability late.

05 Batch, stochastic & mini-batch

How much data do you look at before each step? The answer trades accuracy of the gradient against speed and noise — and the noisy version often wins.

  • Batch GD: use all m examples per step. The gradient is exact, the path is smooth — but each step costs a full pass, infeasible for large data.
  • Stochastic GD (SGD): one example per step. Cheap and fast, but each gradient is a noisy estimate — the path jitters and never fully settles, hovering around the minimum.
  • Mini-batch: a small batch (32–512) per step — the default. Gradients are accurate enough, the path is reasonably smooth, and it maps perfectly onto vectorized hardware (GPUs).
  • Noise as a feature: SGD/mini-batch noise can jump out of shallow local minima and saddle points, which is partly why deep nets train well despite non-convexity.
  • One epoch = one full pass over the data; mini-batch GD does ⌈m / batch⌉ steps per epoch.

06 Momentum, RMSProp & Adam

Plain gradient descent zig-zags through narrow valleys and crawls across plateaus. Modern optimizers fix this by giving the update a memory — of past directions, past magnitudes, or both.

  • Momentum: accumulate an exponentially-weighted average of past gradients (the "velocity") and step with that. Oscillations across the valley cancel out; the consistent down-valley component builds up — like a heavy ball rolling, it powers through plateaus.
  • RMSProp: keep a running average of squared gradients per parameter and divide the step by its root. This rescales each coordinate — directions with big, swingy gradients get damped; flat directions get amplified.
  • Adam = momentum (first moment m) + RMSProp (second moment v), with bias correction for the cold start. It is the de-facto default: robust to the learning rate, adaptive per parameter.
  • Bias correction: m and v start at 0, biasing early estimates toward 0; dividing by (1 − β^t) rescales them until the averages warm up.
Momentum v ← β v + ∇J(θ) θ ← θ − η v (β ≈ 0.9)
Adam (per-parameter, step t) m ← β₁ m + (1 − β₁) ∇J v ← β₂ v + (1 − β₂) (∇J)² m̂ = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ) v̂ = v / (1 − β₂ᵗ) θ ← θ − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, ε=10⁻⁸)

Why momentum helps: in a stretched valley the gradient mostly points across the valley, not along it. Averaging gradients cancels the back-and-forth across-component while the along-valley component survives and accumulates — convergence that scales with √(κ) instead of κ (the condition number, §07).

07 Convexity & conditioning

Two properties of the landscape decide whether optimization is easy: convexity (is there one minimum?) and conditioning (how round vs stretched is the bowl?).

Convexity — the global-minimum guarantee

  • A function is convex if the line segment between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph — no "dips" to get stuck in.
  • Consequence: every local minimum is the global minimum, and any critical point is optimal. Gradient descent (with a sane η) is guaranteed to find it.
  • Twice-differentiable test: convex ⟺ the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere (H ⪰ 0). In 1-D that's just J″(θ) ≥ 0 — the curve always bends upward.
  • Convex ML problems: linear & ridge regression, logistic regression, linear SVMs. Non-convex: neural networks, k-means, matrix factorization.
Convexity (Jensen's inequality form) J(t·θ₁ + (1−t)·θ₂) ≤ t·J(θ₁) + (1−t)·J(θ₂) for all t ∈ [0, 1] twice-differentiable: J convex ⟺ ∇²J ⪰ 0

Conditioning — why round bowls train fast

  • The condition number κ = λ_max / λ_min of the Hessian measures how stretched the bowl is. κ = 1 is a perfect circle; large κ is a long, thin ravine.
  • Why it hurts: one learning rate must serve all directions. It must be small enough not to diverge in the steep direction (λ_max), which makes it far too small for the flat direction (λ_min) — hence slow zig-zag.
  • Gradient-descent rate scales like (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) per step — close to 1 (slow) when κ is large.
  • Fixes: feature scaling / normalization (rounds the bowl), momentum and Adam (adapt per direction), and second-order methods (use the Hessian directly).
Condition number & GD convergence factor κ = λ_max(H) / λ_min(H) ‖θ⁽ᵗ⁾ − θ*‖ ≤ ((κ−1)/(κ+1))ᵗ ‖θ⁽⁰⁾ − θ*‖

Practical takeaway: before blaming the optimizer, scale your features. Standardizing inputs to comparable ranges shrinks κ, which is often a bigger speedup than any fancy optimizer. The eigenvalue/Hessian machinery comes from Linear Algebra, and the gradients themselves from Calculus.

08 Beyond first order & constraints

First-order methods use only the gradient. Using curvature (the Hessian) or handling constraints opens a richer toolbox — costlier, but powerful where it counts.

  • Newton's method: step θ ← θ − H⁻¹ ∇J. It rescales by curvature, so it jumps straight to the minimum of a quadratic in one step — but forming and inverting H is O(n³), infeasible for large models.
  • Quasi-Newton (L-BFGS): approximate H⁻¹ from a history of gradients. A strong choice for smaller, smooth, convex problems (e.g. logistic regression).
  • Closed-form least-squares: setting ∇J = 0 for linear regression gives the normal equations θ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy — no iteration needed when the problem is small and well-conditioned.
  • Constrained optimization: minimize subject to g(θ) ≤ 0 or h(θ) = 0. The Lagrangian folds constraints into the objective with multipliers; the KKT conditions generalize "gradient = 0" to constrained problems (the foundation of SVM duality).
  • Regularization as constraint: adding λ‖θ‖² (ridge) is equivalent to constraining ‖θ‖ ≤ t — it pulls the solution toward the origin and improves conditioning.

09 Practice & pitfalls

A field guide to what goes wrong and how to read the symptoms — the difference between a model that trains and one that doesn't is usually an optimization detail.

SymptomLikely causeFix
Loss explodes to NaN/∞η too large; exploding gradientsLower η; clip gradients; check init & scaling
Loss oscillates, won't settleη too large for the curvatureDecay η over time; use momentum/Adam
Loss barely movesη too small; plateau / saddleRaise η; add momentum; warm-up schedule
Slow, zig-zag descentHigh condition number κStandardize features; momentum; Adam
Good train, bad valOver-fit, not an optimization bugRegularize; early stopping; more data
Different runs, different minimaNon-convex landscapeExpected; fix seeds; try several inits
  • Always scale inputs first — it rounds the bowl (lowers κ) and is the cheapest speedup available.
  • Tune the learning rate before anything else. Try a log-spaced sweep (e.g. 1e-1 … 1e-5); the largest η that still trains stably minus a notch is usually right.
  • Use a schedule: warm-up then decay (step, cosine, or 1/t). Big early steps explore; small late steps refine.
  • Watch the loss curve, not just the final number — its shape diagnoses η, conditioning, and over-fitting at a glance.
  • Default stack: mini-batch + Adam + a learning-rate schedule + gradient clipping covers the vast majority of cases.
Mental model to keep: a gradient gives the direction, the learning rate gives the distance, momentum gives memory, the condition number sets the difficulty, and convexity decides whether you can trust where you land. Every optimizer is a different answer to "how far, in what direction, given what I've seen so far."
