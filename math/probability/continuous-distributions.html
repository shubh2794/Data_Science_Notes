Continuous Distributions

A latency, a weight, a voltage, the fraction of users who convert, the time until a disk fails: these quantities range over a continuum, and no single value of them has any probability at all. Probability has to be spread along the number line as a density, and read off as area. This page builds that idea from the cumulative distribution function, then works through the continuous families that keep coming back: the uniform, the exponential and its memorylessness, the gamma and chi-square, the normal, the beta, and the heavy-tailed Pareto and lognormal. It ends with the one technique that connects them all, transforming one random variable into another, and with the probability integral transform, which is how every simulation turns uniform random numbers into anything else.

01 What a continuous random variable is

A continuous random variable is a random variable whose probability is spread smoothly over an interval of the real line, so that every single value has probability zero and only intervals carry probability. It is described by a density f, a curve whose area over an interval is the probability of landing in that interval, or, more fundamentally, by its cumulative distribution function F. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that one change of viewpoint: probability is no longer something you add up value by value, it is something you integrate.

Why every single value has probability zero

  • Refining the measurement. Suppose a latency is recorded to the nearest millisecond and the value 42 ms has probability 0.03. Record it to a tenth of a millisecond instead, and that 0.03 is shared among the ten values 42.0, 42.1, …, 42.9 (strictly, among the ten values that round to 42), each getting about 0.003. Another decimal and each gets about 0.0003. Every refinement divides the probability of a single value by roughly ten, and in the limit, the true continuous latency, each exact value gets probability 0. What does not shrink is the probability of an interval such as "between 41.5 and 42.5 ms": it stays 0.03 at every precision. Intervals keep their probability; points lose theirs.
  • The squeeze argument. Let X be equally likely to land anywhere in [0, 1], so that the probability of an interval is its length. For any ε > 0, the event {X = ½} sits inside {½ − ε < X ≤ ½ + ε}, whose probability is 2ε. A probability smaller than 2ε for every positive ε is 0. The same squeeze works at any point of any distribution whose cdf is continuous.
  • Zero probability is not impossibility. The dart lands somewhere, and wherever it lands had probability 0 beforehand. In a continuous model "probability 0" means "not worth betting on any particular value", not "cannot happen". The discrete intuition "if something happened, it had positive probability" is the one that has to go.
  • No contradiction with the axioms. The interval [0, 1] is a union of its points, each with probability 0, yet it has probability 1. Additivity in the axioms is only countable: you may add up a sequence of disjoint events, but [0, 1] has uncountably many points, and no axiom says that uncountably many zeros must add to zero. This is exactly why a probability model on a continuum cannot be built by listing outcomes. It has to be built from intervals.
The density story for uncountable sample spaces. Probability Foundations noted that for a sample space like Ω = (0, ∞) probability must be assigned to intervals, and that the family of events has to be restricted to a well-behaved collection, a σ-field. Here is how the two fit. Start from probabilities of half-lines, F(x) = P(X ≤ x). Differences give every interval, P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a); complements and countable unions, applied repeatedly, give every set built from intervals: open sets, closed sets, single points, the rationals, and anything else you can describe by a formula. That collection is the Borel σ-field, and a single non-decreasing, right-continuous F with limits 0 and 1 determines the probability of every set in it consistently. There do exist subsets of [0, 1] to which no length can be assigned consistently (constructing one needs the axiom of choice), and they are left out of the σ-field (along with some other exotic sets). No set you will ever compute with is among them. Everything in Probability Foundations (complements, unions, conditioning, Bayes' rule, independence) holds verbatim for events of the form {X ∈ A}.

A density is probability per unit length

  • A random variable X is continuous (strictly, absolutely continuous) if there is a function f ≥ 0, its probability density function (pdf), such that for all a ≤ b, P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx. Taking a → −∞ and b → ∞ forces ∫ f = 1. Probability is area under the density.
  • What the height means. For a small width h and a point x where f is continuous, P(x < X ≤ x + h) ≈ f(x) · h. So f(x) is probability per unit length near x, and it has units: if X is in milliseconds, f is in "probability per millisecond". Change the units of X and the density changes by the conversion factor; the areas do not.
  • Watching the ratio converge. For an exponential waiting time with P(X > t) = e^(−t) (§04), the probability of landing in (1, 1 + h] is e⁻¹ − e^(−1−h). Divided by the width it gives 0.2325 at h = 1, 0.3501 at h = 0.1 and 0.3660 at h = 0.01, closing in on f(1) = e⁻¹ = 0.3679. The density is the limit of "probability in a small window, per unit of window".
  • A density can exceed 1. Nothing stops probability per unit length from being large, as long as the length is short. A variable uniform on (0, ½) has f(x) = 2 there: twice as much probability per unit as the uniform on (0, 1), packed into half the room, and the area is still 2 × ½ = 1. A density can even be unbounded: f(x) = 1/(2√x) on (0, 1) integrates to √1 − √0 = 1, yet f(0.0001) = 50 and f → ∞ near 0. The probability of [10⁻⁴, 10⁻²] is √0.01 − √0.0001 = 0.1 − 0.01 = 0.09, small, even though the density there is in the tens. Only areas are probabilities, and areas are at most 1.
  • Endpoints are free. Because single points carry no probability, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X < b). For the discrete variables of Random Variables & Discrete Distributions the four were different numbers; here they are one.
  • A density is not unique. Changing f at a single point, or at countably many, changes no integral, so it describes the same distribution. Statements like "f(0) = 2" are conventions; only integrals of f have meaning. That is why tables write densities on open or closed intervals interchangeably.

The cdf is the primary object

  • The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is defined for every random variable: discrete, continuous or a mixture. It has the same three properties as in the discrete case (non-decreasing, limits 0 and 1, right-continuous), and conversely any function with those properties is a cdf. For a continuous variable it is the running area, F(x) = ∫₋∞ˣ f(t) dt, and it is continuous: no jumps, because no point carries mass.
  • pdf = cdf′. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, wherever f is continuous, F′(x) = f(x). The density is the slope of the cdf: steep where probability is concentrated, flat where there is none. Going one way you integrate, going the other you differentiate, and both pictures carry the whole distribution.
  • Why the cdf comes first. It always exists; it turns every interval probability into a subtraction, P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a); and it is usually the easiest thing to derive, because "X ≤ x" is an event you can reason about directly. Densities are then obtained by differentiating. Nearly every density on this page is found that way: write down P(X ≤ x) or P(X > x) from the story, then take a derivative.
  • Worked: the dart. A dart lands uniformly at random on a disc of radius r (probability proportional to area), and X is its distance from the centre. "X ≤ b" means landing inside the disc of radius b, so F(b) = πb²/(πr²) = b²/r² for 0 ≤ b ≤ r. Differentiating, f(x) = 2x/r²: the density rises with distance even though every patch of the board is equally likely, because there is more board at larger radii. P(X ≤ r/2) = ¼ for any radius: the inner half of the radius is only a quarter of the area.
  • Not every distribution has a density. Discrete variables do not (their cdf jumps); mixed variables have a density only for their continuous part (§02). There are even continuous cdfs with no density at all (the Cantor function rises from 0 to 1 while being flat almost everywhere), but they do not arise in modelling. The cdf handles all of these in one language, which is why it is the definition and the density is a convenience.

From histograms to densities

  • A density is what a histogram becomes with unlimited data. If the bar over a bin of width w is drawn with height (fraction of observations in the bin)/w, the bar's area is the fraction of data in the bin. With more data the fractions approach the bin probabilities ∫ f over each bin; with narrower bins, height approaches f itself. Density-scaled histograms are therefore directly comparable with density curves, and that is how every simulation on this page is drawn. The data-side version, choosing bin widths and kernel estimates, is in descriptive statistics.
  • A histogram drawn with counts on the vertical axis cannot be compared with a density, and one drawn with fractions (not divided by the bin width) changes height when you change the bins. Only the density scale is stable.

The working picture for the rest of the page. Probability is a fixed amount of sand, 1 unit in total, spread along the number line. The density is the depth of the sand at each point; the cdf is how much sand lies to the left. The depth can be anything, large or infinite at a point, as long as the total is 1. Asking for the probability of a single point is asking how much sand sits on a line of zero width.

10 The family tree

The families on this page and the previous one are not separate inventions. They are connected by four kinds of relation: one is a special case of another, one is the sum of several copies of another, one is the limit of another, and one is a transformation of another. Knowing the connections halves the memorisation, explains where the formulas come from, and tells you which approximation to reach for.

Reading the tree

  • Two processes generate most of it. The Bernoulli process (independent trials) gives the binomial, geometric and negative binomial; the Poisson process (events at a constant rate in continuous time) gives the Poisson, exponential and gamma. The second is the limit of the first as trials get shorter and rarer, which is why each Bernoulli-process family has a partner in the Poisson process: geometric ↔ exponential and negative binomial ↔ gamma for the waiting times, binomial ↔ Poisson for the counts. Both processes are developed as processes in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
  • The normal sits at the bottom of every limit. Binomial, Poisson, gamma, chi-square and negative binomial all become approximately normal when they are sums of many pieces: large n, large μ, large shape. Heavy-tailed families are the exception: a sum of Pareto variables with α < 2 does not become normal at all.
  • Logs move between families. Taking logs turns the lognormal into the normal and the Pareto into the exponential; exponentiating goes back. When data look like one of these on a log scale, the other is the model on the raw scale.
  • The uniform is the root. Through the inverse transform every continuous family is a function of a uniform, and through the probability integral transform every continuous variable can be turned back into one.

11 Choosing the density

As with the discrete families, each continuous family is the answer to a question about how the quantity came to be. Start from the story and the support (can the value be negative? is it bounded? is it a proportion, a duration, an amount, an error?), then check the shape (symmetric or skewed, how heavy the tail) against data. Questions that pick the family

  1. What is the support? Whole line: normal (or a heavier-tailed t or Cauchy). Positive: exponential, gamma, lognormal, Weibull, Pareto. Bounded on (0, 1): beta. Bounded on (a, b): uniform, or a rescaled beta.
  2. Is it a waiting time? Constant hazard: exponential. Wait for several events: gamma. Hazard rising or falling with age: Weibull. The hazard plot (§04) is the best single diagnostic.
  3. Is it a sum, or a product? Many small additive effects: normal. Many multiplicative effects: lognormal. A sum of squared normal errors: chi-square.
  4. How heavy is the tail? Plot log P(X > x) against log x (§08). Bending sharply down: exponential-type. Straight: Pareto, and estimate α before trusting any mean or variance. Gently curving: lognormal.
  5. Is there a pile of exact zeros, or a cap? Then the variable is mixed (§02): model the atom and the continuous part separately.
  6. Is the quantity itself a probability you are uncertain about? Beta, with its parameters read as pseudo-counts (§07).

Moment fingerprints

  • For positive data the coefficient of variation CV = σ/μ separates families as the variance/mean ratio did for counts. Exponential: exactly 1. Gamma: 1/√α, below 1 for α > 1 (more regular than random arrivals), above 1 for α < 1. Lognormal: √(e^(σ²) − 1), which exceeds 1 once σ > 0.83. Pareto: 1/√(α(α − 2)) for α > 2, and infinite below. A sample CV well above 1 that keeps growing as data accumulate is the classic sign of a heavy tail.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Continuous distributions are written into machine learning at every level: the loss functions of regression are negative log-densities, the initial weights of every network are drawn from a uniform or a normal with a carefully chosen variance, generative models sample latent variables from a Gaussian and push them through a learned transformation, and Bayesian methods carry beta and gamma beliefs about rates. Most "tricks" in this area are a sentence from this page applied at scale.

  • Gaussian noise ⟺ least squares is the canonical case: maximising Π φ((yᵢ − ŷᵢ)/σ)/σ over the model is minimising Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)², because everything else in the log-density is constant. The normal's light tail is also why squared error is so sensitive to outliers: a residual of 10σ is "impossible" (e^(−50)) under the model, so the fit bends to avoid it. The Laplace's exponential tail makes the same residual merely unlikely, which is why absolute and Huber losses are robust.

Weight initialisation is a variance calculation

  • A unit computes Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ over n_in inputs. With independent zero-mean weights of variance σ_w² and inputs of second moment E x², the output has variance n_in · σ_w² · E x². To keep signals from shrinking or exploding through depth, choose σ_w² ≈ 1/n_in for symmetric activations, or 2/n_in for ReLU, whose output keeps only half the second moment (the E Y² = ½ computed for a ReLU in §02). The compromise 2/(n_in + n_out) balances the forward and backward passes.
  • Uniform or normal, same variance. A U(−r, r) weight has variance r²/3 (§03), so the variance 2/(n_in + n_out) needs r = √(6/(n_in + n_out)): for a 256 × 256 layer, r = 0.108 and standard deviation 0.0625. The normal version draws N(0, 2/(n_in + n_out)) directly. The choice of family barely matters; the variance is the whole content. See initialisation rules and what each assumes.

Latent variables and learned transformations

  • The reparametrisation trick is the linear change of variables of §09: a draw z ~ N(μ, σ²) is written z = μ + σε with ε ~ N(0, 1), so the randomness sits in ε and gradients can flow through μ and σ. It is how variational autoencoders are trained; see the reparametrisation trick and the latent-variable view.
  • Diffusion models corrupt data with Gaussian noise of increasing variance and learn to reverse it; every forward step uses the fact that a sum of independent normals is normal (§06). See the forward process.
  • Normalising flows are the change-of-variables formula made into an architecture: a simple density (a standard normal) is pushed through an invertible network g, and the density of the output is the base density times the stretching factor |det(dg⁻¹/dy)|, the multivariate version of §09. Each layer is designed so that factor is cheap to compute.
  • Gumbel noise and sampling. Adding independent Gumbel noise −ln(−ln Uⱼ) to logits and taking the argmax draws exactly from the softmax distribution; its relaxed version, the Gumbel-softmax, makes discrete choices differentiable. The Gumbel arises as minus the log of an exponential (§09).

Priors, conjugacy and exploration

  • Beta–binomial in production. A click-through rate with 3 clicks in 20 impressions is not 15%; with a Beta(a, b) prior fitted to all items it is (a + 3)/(a + b + 20), shrunk toward the average item. This is how rankings stay sane for new items with few impressions.
  • Thompson sampling keeps a beta posterior for each option's success rate, draws one value from each posterior, and plays the option with the largest draw. Options with few trials have wide posteriors and get explored; options known to be bad are drawn high only rarely. It is a principled alternative to the ε-greedy and UCB rules in exploration strategies.
  • Gamma priors play the same role for Poisson rates (§05) and for the precision 1/σ² of a normal; and the Dirichlet, the multi-category beta, is the conjugate prior for the categorical distributions of the discrete page. It is developed with the other joint distributions in Joint Distributions & Dependence.
  • Mixup data augmentation blends two training examples with a weight drawn from Beta(α, α); small α gives the U shape (mostly near one example or the other), α = 1 a uniform blend.

Heavy tails in training, and calibrated uncertainty

  • Gradients are heavy-tailed. Minibatch gradient noise and per-example gradient norms in deep networks are often far from normal, with occasional enormous values. A step size tuned for typical gradients is then destroyed by one rare batch, which is why gradient clipping caps the norm, and why the robust summaries of §08 (medians, quantiles) beat means when monitoring training statistics.
  • Activations and outliers. Large models develop a few activation channels with values hundreds of times the typical size; quantisation schemes that assume a normal-like range clip them and lose accuracy. Fitting the tail, not the bulk, decides the quantisation scale.
  • Calibration. A probabilistic regression model is calibrated when its predicted cdfs, evaluated at the observed outcomes, are uniform (the probability integral transform of §09); a classifier is calibrated when outcomes predicted at p happen a fraction p of the time. Both are checked with the reliability diagrams and scores in thresholds and calibration.
  • Log-uniform hyperparameter search. Learning rates and regularisation strengths are sampled as 10^U (§03, §09), which gives each decade equal probability; see search spaces.
