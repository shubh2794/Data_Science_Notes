Continuous Distributions

A latency, a weight, a voltage, the fraction of users who convert, the time until a disk fails: these quantities range over a continuum, and no single value of them has any probability at all. Probability has to be spread along the number line as a density, and read off as area. This page builds that idea from the cumulative distribution function, then works through the continuous families that keep coming back: the uniform, the exponential and its memorylessness, the gamma and chi-square, the normal, the beta, and the heavy-tailed Pareto and lognormal. It ends with the one technique that connects them all, transforming one random variable into another, and with the probability integral transform, which is how every simulation turns uniform random numbers into anything else.

01 What a continuous random variable is

A continuous random variable is a random variable whose probability is spread smoothly over an interval of the real line, so that every single value has probability zero and only intervals carry probability. It is described by a density f, a curve whose area over an interval is the probability of landing in that interval, or, more fundamentally, by its cumulative distribution function F. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that one change of viewpoint: probability is no longer something you add up value by value, it is something you integrate.

Why every single value has probability zero

  • Refining the measurement. Suppose a latency is recorded to the nearest millisecond and the value 42 ms has probability 0.03. Record it to a tenth of a millisecond instead, and that 0.03 is shared among the ten values 42.0, 42.1, …, 42.9 (strictly, among the ten values that round to 42), each getting about 0.003. Another decimal and each gets about 0.0003. Every refinement divides the probability of a single value by roughly ten, and in the limit, the true continuous latency, each exact value gets probability 0. What does not shrink is the probability of an interval such as "between 41.5 and 42.5 ms": it stays 0.03 at every precision. Intervals keep their probability; points lose theirs.
  • The squeeze argument. Let X be equally likely to land anywhere in [0, 1], so that the probability of an interval is its length. For any ε > 0, the event {X = ½} sits inside {½ − ε < X ≤ ½ + ε}, whose probability is 2ε. A probability smaller than 2ε for every positive ε is 0. The same squeeze works at any point of any distribution whose cdf is continuous.
  • Zero probability is not impossibility. The dart lands somewhere, and wherever it lands had probability 0 beforehand. In a continuous model "probability 0" means "not worth betting on any particular value", not "cannot happen". The discrete intuition "if something happened, it had positive probability" is the one that has to go.
  • No contradiction with the axioms. The interval [0, 1] is a union of its points, each with probability 0, yet it has probability 1. Additivity in the axioms is only countable: you may add up a sequence of disjoint events, but [0, 1] has uncountably many points, and no axiom says that uncountably many zeros must add to zero. This is exactly why a probability model on a continuum cannot be built by listing outcomes. It has to be built from intervals.
The density story for uncountable sample spaces. Probability Foundations noted that for a sample space like Ω = (0, ∞) probability must be assigned to intervals, and that the family of events has to be restricted to a well-behaved collection, a σ-field. Here is how the two fit. Start from probabilities of half-lines, F(x) = P(X ≤ x). Differences give every interval, P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a); complements and countable unions, applied repeatedly, give every set built from intervals: open sets, closed sets, single points, the rationals, and anything else you can describe by a formula. That collection is the Borel σ-field, and a single non-decreasing, right-continuous F with limits 0 and 1 determines the probability of every set in it consistently. There do exist subsets of [0, 1] to which no length can be assigned consistently (constructing one needs the axiom of choice), and they are left out of the σ-field (along with some other exotic sets). No set you will ever compute with is among them. Everything in Probability Foundations (complements, unions, conditioning, Bayes' rule, independence) holds verbatim for events of the form {X ∈ A}.

A density is probability per unit length

  • A random variable X is continuous (strictly, absolutely continuous) if there is a function f ≥ 0, its probability density function (pdf), such that for all a ≤ b, P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx. Taking a → −∞ and b → ∞ forces ∫ f = 1. Probability is area under the density.
  • What the height means. For a small width h and a point x where f is continuous, P(x < X ≤ x + h) ≈ f(x) · h. So f(x) is probability per unit length near x, and it has units: if X is in milliseconds, f is in "probability per millisecond". Change the units of X and the density changes by the conversion factor; the areas do not.
  • Watching the ratio converge. For an exponential waiting time with P(X > t) = e^(−t) (§04), the probability of landing in (1, 1 + h] is e⁻¹ − e^(−1−h). Divided by the width it gives 0.2325 at h = 1, 0.3501 at h = 0.1 and 0.3660 at h = 0.01, closing in on f(1) = e⁻¹ = 0.3679. The density is the limit of "probability in a small window, per unit of window".
  • A density can exceed 1. Nothing stops probability per unit length from being large, as long as the length is short. A variable uniform on (0, ½) has f(x) = 2 there: twice as much probability per unit as the uniform on (0, 1), packed into half the room, and the area is still 2 × ½ = 1. A density can even be unbounded: f(x) = 1/(2√x) on (0, 1) integrates to √1 − √0 = 1, yet f(0.0001) = 50 and f → ∞ near 0. The probability of [10⁻⁴, 10⁻²] is √0.01 − √0.0001 = 0.1 − 0.01 = 0.09, small, even though the density there is in the tens. Only areas are probabilities, and areas are at most 1.
  • Endpoints are free. Because single points carry no probability, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X < b). For the discrete variables of Random Variables & Discrete Distributions the four were different numbers; here they are one.
  • A density is not unique. Changing f at a single point, or at countably many, changes no integral, so it describes the same distribution. Statements like "f(0) = 2" are conventions; only integrals of f have meaning. That is why tables write densities on open or closed intervals interchangeably.

The cdf is the primary object

  • The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is defined for every random variable: discrete, continuous or a mixture. It has the same three properties as in the discrete case (non-decreasing, limits 0 and 1, right-continuous), and conversely any function with those properties is a cdf. For a continuous variable it is the running area, F(x) = ∫₋∞ˣ f(t) dt, and it is continuous: no jumps, because no point carries mass.
  • pdf = cdf′. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, wherever f is continuous, F′(x) = f(x). The density is the slope of the cdf: steep where probability is concentrated, flat where there is none. Going one way you integrate, going the other you differentiate, and both pictures carry the whole distribution.
  • Why the cdf comes first. It always exists; it turns every interval probability into a subtraction, P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a); and it is usually the easiest thing to derive, because "X ≤ x" is an event you can reason about directly. Densities are then obtained by differentiating. Nearly every density on this page is found that way: write down P(X ≤ x) or P(X > x) from the story, then take a derivative.
  • Worked: the dart. A dart lands uniformly at random on a disc of radius r (probability proportional to area), and X is its distance from the centre. "X ≤ b" means landing inside the disc of radius b, so F(b) = πb²/(πr²) = b²/r² for 0 ≤ b ≤ r. Differentiating, f(x) = 2x/r²: the density rises with distance even though every patch of the board is equally likely, because there is more board at larger radii. P(X ≤ r/2) = ¼ for any radius: the inner half of the radius is only a quarter of the area.
  • Not every distribution has a density. Discrete variables do not (their cdf jumps); mixed variables have a density only for their continuous part (§02). There are even continuous cdfs with no density at all (the Cantor function rises from 0 to 1 while being flat almost everywhere), but they do not arise in modelling. The cdf handles all of these in one language, which is why it is the definition and the density is a convenience.

From histograms to densities

  • A density is what a histogram becomes with unlimited data. If the bar over a bin of width w is drawn with height (fraction of observations in the bin)/w, the bar's area is the fraction of data in the bin. With more data the fractions approach the bin probabilities ∫ f over each bin; with narrower bins, height approaches f itself. Density-scaled histograms are therefore directly comparable with density curves, and that is how every simulation on this page is drawn. The data-side version, choosing bin widths and kernel estimates, is in descriptive statistics.
  • A histogram drawn with counts on the vertical axis cannot be compared with a density, and one drawn with fractions (not divided by the bin width) changes height when you change the bins. Only the density scale is stable.

The working picture for the rest of the page. Probability is a fixed amount of sand, 1 unit in total, spread along the number line. The density is the depth of the sand at each point; the cdf is how much sand lies to the left. The depth can be anything, large or infinite at a point, as long as the total is 1. Asking for the probability of a single point is asking how much sand sits on a line of zero width.

02 Working with densities

Most of the work with a continuous distribution is a short list of operations: fix the constant that makes the area 1, find the support, compute a mean and a variance by integration, read quantiles off the cdf, and recognise the shape. This section does each once, with the discrete versions from Random Variables & Discrete Distributions as the template, and then handles the case that is neither discrete nor continuous: a point mass sitting next to a density.

Normalising constants and support

  • Densities are often known only up to a constant: a model says "proportional to x² on [0, 3]" or "proportional to e^(−2x)". The constant is whatever makes the total area 1: c = 1 / ∫ (shape). Bayesian posteriors, energy-based models and Boltzmann distributions are all written this way, and the constant (the "partition function") is frequently the hard part.
  • Worked. c·x² on [0, 3]: ∫₀³ x² dx = 9, so c = 1/9. c·e^(−2x) on [0, ∞): ∫₀^∞ e^(−2x) dx = ½, so c = 2. c·x(1 − x) on [0, 1]: ∫₀¹ (x − x²) dx = ½ − ⅓ = ⅙, so c = 6 (a beta density, §07). c/(1 + x²) on the whole line: ∫ dx/(1 + x²) = arctan(∞) − arctan(−∞) = π, so c = 1/π, the Cauchy density (§08–§09).
  • Shapes that cannot be normalised. c·x on [0, ∞) has infinite area for every c > 0; so does any non-zero constant on the whole line. There is no "uniform distribution on the real line", and no density proportional to 1/x on (0, ∞). Such shapes appear as "improper priors" in Bayesian work and are legitimate only when the posterior they produce can be normalised.
  • The support is where the density is positive: (0, ∞) for waiting times, (0, 1) for proportions, the whole line for errors. Choosing a family with the right support is the first modelling decision (§11): a normal model for a waiting time puts probability on negative times, and a normal model for a proportion puts probability outside [0, 1].

Mean and variance by integration

  • Sums become integrals: E X = ∫ x f(x) dx and Var X = ∫ (x − μ)² f(x) dx = E X² − μ², with the same balance-point and spread readings as in the discrete case. The mean exists only when ∫ |x| f(x) dx < ∞. The operational rules (E(aX + b) = a E X + b, Var(aX + b) = a² Var X) carry over unchanged; the theory behind them, for every kind of random variable at once, is in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • Worked: the dart. With f(x) = 2x on [0, 1]: E X = ∫₀¹ 2x² dx = ⅔, E X² = ∫₀¹ 2x³ dx = ½, Var X = ½ − 4/9 = 1/18, σ = 0.236. The average distance from the centre is two-thirds of the radius.
  • A distribution with no mean. The Cauchy density 1/(π(1 + x²)) is symmetric about 0, so it is tempting to call 0 its mean. But ∫ |x|/(π(1 + x²)) dx = ∞ (the integrand behaves like 1/(π|x|) far out), so the mean does not exist, and averages of Cauchy draws never settle down. Symmetry gives a median of 0, not a mean. §08 is about distributions like this.
  • The tail formula. For X ≥ 0, E X = ∫₀^∞ P(X > t) dt, the continuous twin of the tail-sum formula for the geometric distribution. Proof: write x = ∫₀ˣ dt inside E X = ∫ x f(x) dx and swap the order of integration; the inner integral becomes ∫ₜ^∞ f(x) dx = P(X > t). It is the fastest route to the mean of anything whose tail is known in closed form.

The quantile function

  • The quantile function reads the cdf backwards: F⁻¹(p) = min{x : F(x) ≥ p}, the smallest value below which a fraction p of the probability lies. When F is strictly increasing on the support it is the ordinary inverse function. The median is F⁻¹(½), the quartiles are F⁻¹(¼) and F⁻¹(¾), and F⁻¹(p) is the 100p-th percentile.
  • Worked: residence time in a reactor. A particle's time in a well-mixed vessel is exponential with mean 4 minutes, so F(t) = 1 − e^(−t/4). Solving F(q) = 0.1 gives q = −4 ln 0.9 = 0.421 minutes. So 10% of particles leave within about 25 seconds, although the mean residence time is 4 minutes; the median is 4 ln 2 = 2.77 minutes and the 90th percentile is 4 ln 10 = 9.21. A single summary hides how much of the mass sits near zero.
  • Worked: the standard normal. Φ⁻¹ has no closed form but is computed to full precision: the quartiles are ±0.6745, so the interquartile range of any normal distribution is 1.349σ. That ratio is why IQR/1.349 is a robust estimate of σ for normal-looking data (spread).
  • Quantiles exist for every distribution, including those without a mean or variance, and they transform simply: for an increasing function g, the median of g(X) is g(median of X). Means do not do that (§09). The quantile function is also how a uniform random number becomes a draw from F (§09).

Symmetric and skewed shapes

  • A density is symmetric about c if f(c + x) = f(c − x) for all x. Then the median is c, and so is the mean whenever it exists. The normal is symmetric about μ; the uniform about the midpoint; the Cauchy about 0 (with no mean).
  • A right-skewed density has a long tail to the right: waiting times, incomes, file sizes, response latencies. The tail pulls the mean above the median, and the peak (the mode) usually sits below both. For the exponential with mean 1 the mode is 0, the median 0.693 and the mean 1; for the lognormal of §08 with μ = 0, σ = 1 they are 0.368, 1 and 1.649. The ordering mode < median < mean is typical of right skew but is not a theorem; there are skewed densities that break it.
  • The skewness coefficient E(X − μ)³/σ³ measures asymmetry on a scale-free basis: 0 for any symmetric distribution, 2 for every exponential, 2/√α for the gamma of §05 (so the gamma becomes symmetric as α grows). Its estimate from data is noisy, and a single far-out observation can dominate it.

Mixed distributions: a point mass plus a density

  • Many real quantities are exactly zero a good fraction of the time and continuous otherwise: daily rainfall (dry days), an insurance claim (no accident), a user's spend this week (no purchase), the output of a ReLU unit. Such a variable is mixed: a point mass p₀ = P(X = 0) plus a density carrying the remaining 1 − p₀. Its cdf jumps by p₀ at 0 and rises continuously elsewhere. It has no pmf (it is not discrete) and no density (the jump is not the integral of anything).
  • Computing with it. Expectations split into a sum over the atoms plus an integral over the continuous part: E g(X) = g(0)·p₀ + ∫ g(x) f_c(x) dx, where f_c integrates to 1 − p₀. Equivalently, condition on "zero or not" with the law of total probability.
  • Worked: rainfall. 60% of days are dry; on a wet day the amount is exponential with mean 8 mm. Then E X = 0.6 · 0 + 0.4 · 8 = 3.2 mm, E X² = 0.4 · (2 · 8²) = 51.2 (an exponential with mean m has E X² = 2m², §04), Var X = 51.2 − 3.2² = 40.96, σ = 6.4 mm. P(X > 10) = 0.4 · e^(−10/8) = 0.115. A model that ignored the dry days and fitted one exponential to all days would misstate the probability of a dry day completely (it would be 0) and get the shape of the wet days wrong.
  • Worked: a capped wait. The time between buses is uniform on 4 to 6 minutes, and a passenger who has just missed one walks if the next takes longer than 5. Her waiting time is W = min(T, 5): P(W = 5) = P(T ≥ 5) = ½, and below 5 it has density ½ on (4, 5). So P(W < 4.5) = ¼, P(W = 5) = ½, and E W = ∫₄⁵ t · ½ dt + 5 · ½ = 2.25 + 2.5 = 4.75 minutes. Capping, censoring and timeouts all create atoms like this.
  • Worked: a ReLU unit. If the input is Z ~ N(0, 1), the output Y = max(0, Z) is 0 with probability ½ and has the normal density on (0, ∞). E Y = ∫₀^∞ z φ(z) dz = φ(0) = 1/√(2π) = 0.399, E Y² = ½, Var Y = ½ − 1/(2π) = 0.341. This mean and variance, not those of Z, are what the next layer sees, and they are what weight-initialisation rules are built to preserve (§12).
  • In models. Zero-inflated and "hurdle" models handle mixed targets with two parts: a classifier for "is it zero?" and a continuous model for the amount given that it is not. Fitting one continuous density to data with a pile of exact zeros misstates both parts.

03 The uniform distribution: equal density everywhere

The simplest density is flat: every sub-interval of the same length gets the same probability. It models a value known to lie between two bounds with no reason to prefer any part of the range, such as the arrival time of a flight within its window, a rounding error, or the phase of a rotating part. Its standard version, uniform on (0, 1), is also the raw material of all simulation: computers produce uniform numbers, and every other distribution is manufactured from them.

Density, cdf, mean and variance

  • X ~ U(a, b) has density f(x) = 1/(b − a) on (a, b) and 0 elsewhere. The height is forced: a rectangle of width b − a and area 1. Integrating, F(x) = (x − a)/(b − a) on [a, b], 0 to the left and 1 to the right: a straight ramp.
  • The uniform property. For any sub-interval of length h inside (a, b), P(t < X ≤ t + h) = h/(b − a), whatever t is. Probability depends on length only, not on location. A flight due at 5:00 that lands uniformly between 4:50 and 5:10 is as likely to land before 4:55 as after 5:05 (each ¼).
  • Moments of U(0, 1): E U = ∫₀¹ u du = ½, E U² = ∫₀¹ u² du = ⅓, Var U = ⅓ − ¼ = 1/12, σ = 1/√12 = 0.2887. Every uniform is a stretched and shifted standard one, X = a + (b − a)U, so E X = (a + b)/2 (the midpoint, by symmetry) and Var X = (b − a)²/12 by the scaling rule.
  • Rounding errors. Rounding a measurement to the nearest unit leaves an error that is close to U(−½, ½) when the measurement varies over many units, with standard deviation 0.289 units. The total rounding error of 100 independent roundings has standard deviation √100 × 0.289 = 2.89 units, far below the worst case of 50, because errors of opposite sign cancel. The same arithmetic gives the noise of an 8-bit quantiser on [0, 1]: step 1/255, standard deviation 1/(255√12) = 0.00113.

Discrete uniform and continuous uniform

  • The discrete uniform on {1, …, k} has variance (k² − 1)/12. Divide its values by k to put them in (0, 1]: the variance becomes (k² − 1)/(12k²), which is 0.0810 at k = 6, 0.083325 at k = 100, and tends to 1/12 = 0.08333. The continuous uniform is the limit of finer and finer equally spaced grids, and its moments are the limits of theirs.
  • The difference is in single values. On {1/k, …, 1} each value has probability 1/k; in the limit each value has probability 0 and only lengths count. A pseudo-random generator that returns 53-bit floating-point numbers is, strictly, a discrete uniform on 2⁵³ grid points; for every question you will ask of it, it behaves as the continuous one.

The atom every simulation is built from

  • A random-number generator produces a stream of numbers that behave like independent U(0, 1) draws. Everything else is a transformation of that stream:
    • a Bernoulli(p) draw is 1{U < p}, since P(U < p) = p;
    • a categorical draw splits (0, 1) into consecutive pieces of lengths p₁, …, pₖ and reports which piece U falls in (this is how the dice of the discrete page were rolled);
    • a uniform on (a, b) is a + (b − a)U;
    • an exponential is −ln(U)/λ (§04), and any continuous distribution with cdf F is F⁻¹(U), the inverse-transform method proved in §09.
  • How generators produce such numbers, why they are only pseudo-random, and how to turn uniform draws into Monte Carlo estimates with honest error bars is the subject of Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

Uniform is not the same as "no information"

  • It is tempting to use a uniform distribution whenever nothing is known. But uniformity depends on the scale you chose. If a success probability p is uniform on (0, 1), then p² is not uniform: P(p² ≤ ¼) = P(p ≤ ½) = ½, so half the mass of p² sits in the first quarter of its range. The odds p/(1 − p) are not uniform either. "Knowing nothing about p" and "knowing nothing about p²" cannot both be expressed by a uniform, so the choice of scale is itself information.
  • Scale parameters want log-uniform. For a quantity that acts multiplicatively (a learning rate, a regularisation strength, a length scale), a uniform on (10⁻⁵, 10⁻¹) puts 90% of its mass in the top decade. Ignorance on a ratio scale is uniformity of the logarithm: log₁₀ λ ~ U(−5, −1) puts a quarter of the mass in each decade. That is the standard recipe for hyperparameter search spaces, and §09 gives its density.

Maxima and minima of uniforms: a first look at order statistics

  • Take n independent U(0, 1) draws. Their maximum is at most x exactly when all of them are, so P(max ≤ x) = xⁿ and the density is n xⁿ⁻¹. Their minimum exceeds x exactly when all of them do, so P(min > x) = (1 − x)ⁿ. The means are n/(n + 1) and 1/(n + 1): the n points split (0, 1) into n + 1 gaps of equal expected length.
  • Worked. The best of 10 random configurations, each scoring uniformly on (0, 1), falls below 0.9 with probability 0.9¹⁰ = 0.349; its expected score is 10/11 = 0.909. Random search depends on exactly this calculation. Both densities are beta densities (§07), and so is the k-th smallest of the n draws.

04 The exponential distribution: waiting without memory

Events that arrive at a constant average rate and independently of one another (requests to a server, decays of a radioactive sample, failures of a component that does not wear out) leave gaps between them that are exponential. It is the continuous twin of the geometric distribution, and like the geometric it is the only distribution of its kind that forgets how long it has already waited. This section derives it from the Poisson count, computes its moments, proves memorylessness in both directions, and carries out the limit from the geometric that the discrete page promised.

From a Poisson count to a waiting time

  • Events occur at rate λ per unit time, and the count in any window of length t is Poisson(μ = λt), as in the Poisson section. Let T be the time until the first event. "T > t" means exactly "no event in (0, t]", so P(T > t) = P(N(t) = 0) = e^(−λt). That is the whole derivation: the survival function S(t) = P(T > t) comes straight from the story, and the cdf and density follow.
  • T ~ Exp(λ) has cdf F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt) and density f(t) = F′(t) = λ e^(−λt) for t ≥ 0. The density is largest at 0 and decays by the same factor e^(−λ) over every unit of time: short gaps are the most common, long ones are rare but never impossible.
  • Rate and scale. λ is a rate (events per unit time), and 1/λ is the mean gap. If E ~ Exp(1) then E/λ ~ Exp(λ), so all exponentials are rescalings of one. Many texts and libraries parametrise by the scale θ = 1/λ instead (density (1/θ)e^(−x/θ)); check which one a function expects, because the two conventions give means that differ by a factor of λ².

Mean and variance, from the integrals

  • Mean, by parts. E T = ∫₀^∞ t · λe^(−λt) dt. With u = t, dv = λe^(−λt) dt, v = −e^(−λt): E T = [−t e^(−λt)]₀^∞ + ∫₀^∞ e^(−λt) dt = 0 + 1/λ. (The boundary term vanishes because t e^(−λt) → 0.) Or in one line with the tail formula of §02: E T = ∫₀^∞ e^(−λt) dt = 1/λ.
  • Second moment, by parts again. E T² = ∫₀^∞ t² λe^(−λt) dt = [−t² e^(−λt)]₀^∞ + ∫₀^∞ 2t e^(−λt) dt = (2/λ) · ∫₀^∞ t λe^(−λt) dt = (2/λ) E T = 2/λ². Hence Var T = 2/λ² − 1/λ² = 1/λ²: the standard deviation equals the mean. More generally E Tⁿ = n!/λⁿ, each integration by parts peeling off one factor.
  • Median and the mean. F(m) = ½ gives m = ln 2/λ = 0.693/λ, about 69% of the mean. P(T > E T) = e⁻¹ = 0.368: only about a third of waits exceed the average wait, because a few long ones pull the average up.
  • Worked: a print queue. Jobs arrive at 3 per hour. The gap between jobs is Exp(3) in hours, with mean 20 minutes. The chance the next job arrives within 5 minutes is F(1/12) = 1 − e^(−3/12) = 0.221; convert units before plugging in.
  • Worked: response time. A system's response time is exponential with rate 0.25 per second (mean 4 s). P(T > 5) = e^(−1.25) = 0.287.

Memorylessness, proved in both directions

  • The property. For all s, t ≥ 0, P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t). Having waited s already, the remaining wait has exactly the distribution of a fresh wait. A component with an exponential lifetime is "as good as new" for as long as it survives.
  • Exponential ⟹ memoryless. {T > s + t} ⊂ {T > s}, so the intersection is the smaller event and P(T > s + t | T > s) = e^(−λ(s+t))/e^(−λs) = e^(−λt). This computation appeared in the conditional-probability section of Probability Foundations, with λ = 1, s = 3, t = 1: e⁻⁴/e⁻³ = e⁻¹ = 0.368.
  • Memoryless ⟹ exponential. Let T be a positive random variable with G(t) = P(T > t) satisfying memorylessness, which rearranges to G(s + t) = G(s)G(t) for all s, t ≥ 0.
    1. Integers and fractions. Applying the equation repeatedly, G(m·t) = G(t)ᵐ for every whole number m. With t = 1/n and m = n: G(1) = G(1/n)ⁿ, so G(1/n) = G(1)^(1/n), and then G(m/n) = G(1)^(m/n). So G(q) = G(1)^q at every positive rational q.
    2. The base is strictly between 0 and 1. If G(1) = 0 then G(1/n) = 0 for every n, so P(T > 0) = 0, contradicting that T is positive. If G(1) = 1 then G(q) = 1 at every rational, so T exceeds every number with probability 1, which no random variable does. So 0 < G(1) < 1, and we can write G(1) = e^(−λ) with λ = −ln G(1) > 0.
    3. From rationals to all reals. G is non-increasing (it is a survival function). For any real t, squeeze it between rationals q₁ < t < q₂: e^(−λq₂) ≤ G(t) ≤ e^(−λq₁). Letting q₁, q₂ → t forces G(t) = e^(−λt).
    So the exponential is the only memoryless distribution on (0, ∞), exactly as the geometric is the only one on {1, 2, …}. The proof used nothing but the functional equation and monotonicity.
  • Worked. A bulb's lifetime is exponential with mean 1,000 hours. Given that it has already burned 1,000 hours, the chance it lasts another 500 is P(T > 500) = e^(−0.5) = 0.607, the same as for a new bulb. Real bulbs wear out, so real bulbs are not exponential; the model fits failures caused by external shocks (a surge, a dropped packet, a cosmic-ray bit flip), not failures caused by ageing.
  • What memorylessness rules out. "The server has been quiet for an hour, so a request is due" is the gambler's fallacy in continuous time. Equally, "this connection has been open for an hour, so it is probably a long-lived one" is a reason to doubt an exponential model: if surviving tells you something, the hazard is not constant.

The hazard rate: memory, measured

  • The hazard rate of a lifetime T is the instantaneous failure rate among survivors: h(t) = lim (Δ → 0) P(t < T ≤ t + Δ | T > t)/Δ = f(t)/S(t). It is what reliability engineers, actuaries and survival analysts estimate, and it is the most natural way to talk about how a waiting time ages.
  • It determines the distribution. Since f = −S′, h(t) = −(d/dt) ln S(t), and integrating, S(t) = exp(−∫₀ᵗ h(u) du). Any non-negative h whose integral diverges defines a lifetime distribution. A constant hazard λ gives S(t) = e^(−λt): memoryless ⟺ constant hazard.
  • Three shapes. An increasing hazard means wear-out (mechanical parts, the gamma with shape > 1, the uniform on a finite window, whose hazard 1/(b − t) explodes at the deadline). A decreasing hazard means "the longer it has lasted, the longer it will last" (software bugs that survive testing, the length of a strike, the lifetime of a startup; the Pareto of §08 has h(t) = α/t). Many populations show a bathtub: early failures, a flat middle, then wear-out.
  • Weibull: a hazard that is a power. Take h(t) = (k/s)(t/s)^(k−1). Then S(t) = exp(−(t/s)ᵏ), the Weibull distribution: k = 1 is the exponential, k > 1 wears out, k < 1 burns in. It is the standard parametric model for component lifetimes and the reason survival analysis talks in hazards rather than densities.
  • Mean residual life, E[T − s | T > s] = ∫ₛ^∞ S(t) dt / S(s), is the hazard's long-run cousin: constant 1/λ for the exponential, shrinking for wear-out, growing for decreasing hazards (for a Pareto it grows linearly, §08).

Competing exponentials

  • Two independent exponential clocks T₁ ~ Exp(λ₁), T₂ ~ Exp(λ₂) race. The first to ring, min(T₁, T₂), exceeds t exactly when both do: P(min > t) = e^(−λ₁t) e^(−λ₂t) = e^(−(λ₁+λ₂)t). The minimum of independent exponentials is exponential with the summed rate.
  • Who wins. P(T₁ < T₂) = ∫₀^∞ λ₁e^(−λ₁t) · e^(−λ₂t) dt = λ₁/(λ₁ + λ₂): condition on T₁ = t and require T₂ > t. Each clock wins in proportion to its rate, and (by memorylessness) the race restarts afresh after every ring.
  • Worked. Two servers finish jobs at rates 2 and 3 per hour. The first completion comes after an Exp(5) time, mean 12 minutes, and it is server 1's with probability 0.4. This is the engine of every continuous-time Markov chain and queueing model, developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

The geometric limit: shrinking the trials

  • The geometric distribution counts Bernoulli trials until the first success. Now let the trials be short: one every Δ units of time, each succeeding with probability p = λΔ, so that successes occur at rate λ per unit time whatever Δ is. The time of the first success is T_Δ = Δ · G with G ~ Geo(λΔ).
  • Its survival function. T_Δ > t means the first ⌊t/Δ⌋ trials all fail, so P(T_Δ > t) = (1 − λΔ)^⌊t/Δ⌋.
  • The limit. Take logarithms: ⌊t/Δ⌋ · ln(1 − λΔ). As Δ → 0, ⌊t/Δ⌋ · Δ → t (it is within Δ of t) and ln(1 − λΔ)/Δ → −λ (the derivative of ln(1 − λx) at 0). The product of the two is ⌊t/Δ⌋ ln(1 − λΔ) = (⌊t/Δ⌋Δ) · (ln(1 − λΔ)/Δ) → t · (−λ). Exponentiating, P(T_Δ > t) → e^(−λt) for every t: the geometric waiting time converges to the exponential. It is the same limit (1 − x/n)ⁿ → e^(−x) that turned the binomial into the Poisson.
  • The moments converge too, and the mean never moves. E T_Δ = Δ · 1/(λΔ) = 1/λ exactly, for every Δ. Var T_Δ = Δ² (1 − λΔ)/(λΔ)² = (1 − λΔ)/λ², which rises to 1/λ² as Δ → 0: discreteness removes a little variance, and the continuous limit puts it back.
  • How fast. The largest gap between the two cdfs is 1 − e^(−λΔ) ≈ λΔ, attained just before the first trial, where the staircase is still at 0 while the exponential has already risen. Halve the trial length and the error halves: at λ = 1 it is 0.221 at Δ = 0.25, 0.095 at Δ = 0.1 and 0.0100 at Δ = 0.01. Memorylessness survives the limit: it held for every Δ, and it holds in the limit.

05 The gamma distribution: waiting for the r-th event

The exponential waits for one event; the gamma waits for several. The time until the r-th arrival of a Poisson stream is a sum of r independent exponential gaps, and its density is a gamma density. The same family, with its shape allowed to be any positive number, is the workhorse model for positive, right-skewed quantities (amounts, durations, rates), contains the chi-square distributions of statistics as a special case, and is the mixing distribution that turns a Poisson count into a negative binomial.

The gamma function

  • For α > 0, Γ(α) = ∫₀^∞ y^(α−1) e^(−y) dy. The integral converges at 0 because α > 0 and at infinity because e^(−y) beats any power. It is the function that extends the factorial to non-integers.
  • The recurrence. Integrate by parts with u = y^α, dv = e^(−y) dy: Γ(α + 1) = [−y^α e^(−y)]₀^∞ + α ∫₀^∞ y^(α−1) e^(−y) dy = α Γ(α). With Γ(1) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−y) dy = 1, induction gives Γ(n) = (n − 1)! for every positive integer: Γ(2) = 1, Γ(3) = 2, Γ(4) = 6, Γ(5) = 24.
  • The half-integers. Substituting y = z²/2 in Γ(½) = ∫₀^∞ y^(−½) e^(−y) dy gives √2 ∫₀^∞ e^(−z²/2) dz = √2 · √(2π)/2 = √π, using the Gaussian integral ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−z²/2) dz = √(2π) proved in §06. So Γ(½) = √π = 1.7725, and by the recurrence Γ(3/2) = √π/2 = 0.8862 and Γ(5/2) = 3√π/4 = 1.3293. These are the constants that appear in the chi-square and t densities.
  • Near zero it blows up: Γ(α) = Γ(α + 1)/α ≈ 1/α for small α (Γ(0.1) = 9.514). For large arguments it grows faster than any exponential, which is why software works with ln Γ, as every figure on this page does.
  • The gamma integral. Substituting y = λx in the definition gives the identity used for every calculation below: ∫₀^∞ x^(α−1) e^(−λx) dx = Γ(α)/λ^α for all α, λ > 0.

The density, and where its shape comes from

  • X ~ Gamma(α, λ), shape α > 0 and rate λ > 0, has density f(x) = λ^α x^(α−1) e^(−λx)/Γ(α) for x > 0. It integrates to 1 by the gamma integral. The factor x^(α−1) controls the shape near 0; the factor e^(−λx) controls the tail.
  • Moments from the gamma integral. E X = (λ^α/Γ(α)) ∫ x^α e^(−λx) dx = (λ^α/Γ(α)) · Γ(α + 1)/λ^(α+1) = α/λ. Likewise E X² = Γ(α + 2)/(Γ(α)λ²) = α(α + 1)/λ², so Var X = α/λ². No integration by parts is needed, only the recurrence.
  • The shape parameter α. For α < 1 the density is infinite at 0 and decreasing (many tiny values, a few large ones); α = 1 is the exponential; for α > 1 it is zero at 0 and rises to a single peak at the mode (α − 1)/λ. The skewness is 2/√α, so as α grows the gamma becomes symmetric and, by the central limit theorem, close to normal.
  • The rate parameter λ only rescales: if X ~ Gamma(α, 1) then X/λ ~ Gamma(α, λ). Many libraries use the scale θ = 1/λ instead, with density x^(α−1)e^(−x/θ)/(Γ(α)θ^α) and mean αθ. Fitting with one convention and sampling with the other is a classic bug.
  • Matching a mean and a standard deviation. Given a mean m and standard deviation s, solve α/λ = m, α/λ² = s²: α = (m/s)², λ = m/s². Memory chips with mean lifetime 12 years and standard deviation 4 give α = 9, λ = 0.75 per year.

The waiting time for the r-th arrival

  • Events arrive at rate λ as a Poisson stream; let T_r be the time of the r-th event. "T_r > t" means "fewer than r events in (0, t]", and that count is Poisson(λt): P(T_r > t) = Σ_(k=0..r−1) e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!.
  • Differentiate, and the sum telescopes. (d/dt)[e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!] = λe^(−λt)[(λt)^(k−1)/(k − 1)! − (λt)ᵏ/k!] (the first term absent when k = 0). Summing over k = 0, …, r − 1, every term cancels against its neighbour except the last, so f(t) = −(d/dt)P(T_r > t) = λe^(−λt)(λt)^(r−1)/(r − 1)!. With (r − 1)! = Γ(r) this is the Gamma(r, λ) density. The integer-shape gamma is often called the Erlang distribution, after the engineer who used it for telephone traffic.
  • Sums of exponentials. The gaps between consecutive arrivals are independent Exp(λ) times (memorylessness restarts the clock at every arrival), so T_r = E₁ + … + E_r. Hence E T_r = r/λ and Var T_r = r/λ², agreeing with α/λ and α/λ² at α = r. More generally, independent Gamma(α₁, λ) and Gamma(α₂, λ) variables add to Gamma(α₁ + α₂, λ), provided the rates match. The proof by convolution is in Joint Distributions & Dependence and by moment generating functions in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • The gamma–Poisson identity for computing. For integer r: P(T_r ≤ t) = P(N(t) ≥ r) with N(t) ~ Poisson(λt). Gamma probabilities become Poisson sums, with no integration. It is the continuous mirror of the negative binomial–binomial duality.
  • Worked: compiling in three stages. A build runs three stages in sequence, each exponential with mean 5 minutes. The total is Gamma(3, 0.2): mean 15 minutes, variance 75, standard deviation 8.66. P(T < 12) = P(Poisson(2.4) ≥ 3) = 1 − e^(−2.4)(1 + 2.4 + 2.88) = 0.430. Integrating the density by parts twice gives the same four terms.
  • Worked: memory chips. With α = 9, λ = 0.75: P(8 < T < 10) = F(10) − F(8). F(10) = P(Poisson(7.5) ≥ 9) = 1 − 0.6620 = 0.3380 and F(8) = P(Poisson(6) ≥ 9) = 1 − 0.8472 = 0.1528, so the answer is 0.185.
  • Worked: promotions. A site gets 12 visits per minute, and every sixth visitor sees a promotion. The time between promotions is Gamma(6, 12): mean 0.5 minutes, standard deviation √6/12 = 0.204 minutes, much less variable relative to its mean than a single exponential gap.

The gamma–Poisson mixture, done honestly

  • The negative binomial section claimed that a Poisson count whose rate varies across units according to a gamma distribution is negative binomial. Here is the integral. Let the rate be Λ ~ Gamma(r, β) (shape r, rate β, mean r/β), and given Λ = μ let Y ~ Poisson(μ). By the law of total probability, now with an integral over the continuous mixing variable:
    P(Y = k) = ∫₀^∞ [e^(−μ) μᵏ/k!] · [β^r μ^(r−1) e^(−βμ)/Γ(r)] dμ = (β^r / (k! Γ(r))) · ∫₀^∞ μ^(k+r−1) e^(−(1+β)μ) dμ
  • The remaining integral is the gamma integral with shape k + r and rate 1 + β: it equals Γ(k + r)/(1 + β)^(k+r). So P(Y = k) = [Γ(k + r)/(k! Γ(r))] · (β/(1 + β))^r · (1/(1 + β))ᵏ.
  • With p = β/(1 + β), that is [Γ(k + r)/(Γ(r) k!)] pʳ (1 − p)ᵏ, exactly the failure-count negative binomial with real r. Writing the mixing mean as μ̄ = r/β gives p = r/(r + μ̄), the parametrisation the discrete page used. Its worked case, mean 4 and r = 2, is β = ½, p = ⅓, and P(Y = 0) = (⅓)² = 0.111.
  • The variance split Var Y = E Λ + Var Λ = μ̄ + μ̄²/r reads: Poisson noise within a unit, plus the spread of the rates across units. As r → ∞ the gamma concentrates at its mean and the mixture returns to a plain Poisson.
  • The same computation, read the other way, is Bayesian updating: a gamma prior on a Poisson rate, after observing a count k, gives a Gamma(r + k, β + 1) posterior. The gamma is conjugate to the Poisson, just as the beta is to the binomial (§07).

Chi-square: the gamma with rate ½

  • The chi-square distribution with k degrees of freedom is χ²_k = Gamma(k/2, ½): density x^(k/2 − 1) e^(−x/2)/(2^(k/2) Γ(k/2)), mean k, variance 2k.
  • Why it matters. If Z is standard normal, Z² ~ χ²₁ (derived by change of variables in §09, where the constant Γ(½) = √π falls out). A sum of k independent squared standard normals is χ²_k, by the gamma addition rule above. So the chi-square is the distribution of a sum of squared standardised errors, which is why it governs sample variances, goodness-of-fit statistics and likelihood-ratio tests.
  • Two checks. P(χ²₁ ≤ 3.841) = P(|Z| ≤ 1.960) = 0.95, and 1.96² = 3.8416. χ²₂ = Gamma(1, ½) = Exp(½), so its upper 5% point is the solution of e^(−x/2) = 0.05, x = 2 ln 20 = 5.991, which is the value in every chi-square table.
  • On the statistics side, the chi-square appears as the sampling distribution of a variance and in the skewed-population examples of how fast the CLT takes hold, and as the reference distribution of the chi-square goodness-of-fit test.

06 The normal distribution

The bell curve is the distribution of measurement error, of sums and averages of many small independent effects, and of a long list of physical quantities that are such sums. It is defined by two numbers, a centre μ and a spread σ, and every normal distribution is a stretched and shifted copy of one standard curve, so a single function Φ answers every normal probability question. This section takes the density apart, proves its constant, computes the 68–95–99.7 rule rather than quoting it, and says why the curve is everywhere and where it should not be used.

Anatomy of the density

  • X ~ N(μ, σ²) has density f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) · exp(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)) on the whole real line. Writing z = (x − μ)/σ, the number of standard deviations from the centre, the shape is e^(−z²/2) and everything else is bookkeeping.
  • Squared-exponential decay. Relative to its peak, the density at k standard deviations is e^(−k²/2): 0.607 at k = 1, 0.135 at 2, 0.0111 at 3, 0.000335 at 4 and 0.0000037 at 5. The exponent is quadratic in the distance, so each extra standard deviation costs more than the last. An exponential tail, by contrast, loses the same factor per unit: an exponential variable is 6 standard deviations above its mean (at 7/λ) with probability e⁻⁷ = 0.00091, a normal one with probability 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. This light tail is the normal's defining feature and its main weakness as a model (§08).
  • Landmarks. Symmetric about μ, which is the mean, median and mode. The peak height is 1/(σ√(2π)) = 0.399/σ, so for σ < 0.399 the density exceeds 1. The curve changes from concave to convex at μ ± σ (the second derivative of e^(−z²/2) is (z² − 1)e^(−z²/2), zero at z = ±1): the standard deviation is visible as the inflection points.

Standardising: one table for every normal

  • If X ~ N(μ, σ²) then Z = (X − μ)/σ ~ N(0, 1), and conversely X = μ + σZ (proved in §09: a linear function of a normal is normal). So P(X ≤ x) = Φ((x − μ)/σ), and every normal probability is a value of the standard normal cdf Φ. By symmetry Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z).
  • Φ has no formula in elementary functions. It is computed numerically to full precision (here, through the incomplete gamma function) and was tabulated for a century before that. Its inverse Φ⁻¹ turns a probability back into a z.
  • Worked: fathers' heights, mean 68.3 in, standard deviation 1.8 in. What fraction are between 67.4 and 71.9 in? Standardise: z = (67.4 − 68.3)/1.8 = −0.5 and (71.9 − 68.3)/1.8 = 2. (Subtracting table values already rounded to one decimal of a percent, 97.7% − 30.9%, gives 66.8%; the rounding happened before the subtraction.) The 30th percentile is 68.3 + 1.8 · Φ⁻¹(0.3) = 68.3 − 1.8 × 0.5244 = 67.36 in.
  • Worked: the inverse problem. Incomes are modelled as N(900, 200²). The middle class, 600 to 1,200, is Φ(1.5) − Φ(−1.5) = 0.866 of households. The threshold for the poorest 3% solves Φ((x − 900)/200) = 0.03: x = 900 + 200 · Φ⁻¹(0.03) = 900 − 200 × 1.881 = 524.   • Worked. X ~ N(3, 5) (variance 5): P(X > 1) = 1 − Φ((1 − 3)/√5) = 1 − Φ(−0.894) = 0.8145, and the 20th percentile is 3 + √5 · Φ⁻¹(0.2) = 3 − 2.236 × 0.8416 = 1.118. Sums of independent normals stay normal

  • If X ~ N(μ₁, σ₁²) and Y ~ N(μ₂, σ₂²) are independent, then X + Y ~ N(μ₁ + μ₂, σ₁² + σ₂²), and more generally any linear combination a₁X₁ + … + aₙXₙ + b of independent normals is normal with mean Σ aᵢμᵢ + b and variance Σ aᵢ²σᵢ². The means and variances follow from linearity and independence for any distribution; the remarkable part is that the shape stays normal. The cleanest proof multiplies moment generating functions, in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • Worked. Two consecutive stages take N(30, 4²) and N(45, 3²) minutes, independently. The total is N(75, 5²) (variances add, standard deviations do not: √(16 + 9) = 5, not 7), and P(total > 85) = 1 − Φ(2) = 0.0228.
  • The sample mean. The average of n independent N(μ, σ²) draws is exactly N(μ, σ²/n). For normal data this is not an approximation, which is the starting point of the sampling distribution of the mean.

Why the normal appears everywhere, and where it should not

  • The central limit theorem. A sum of many independent pieces, none of which dominates, is approximately normal, whatever the pieces' own distributions. Binomial counts and Poisson counts approach the normal for this reason (both roads lead to the normal curve), the gamma does as its shape grows, and so do measurement errors that are the accumulation of many small disturbances. The theorem, with a proof sketch and its rate, is in Limit Theorems & Convergence; its working version, standard errors for means and proportions, is in Sampling Distributions & the CLT.
  • Maximum entropy. Among all densities on the real line with a given mean and variance, the normal has the largest entropy: it assumes nothing beyond those two numbers. Choosing a normal model is, in that sense, the least committal choice once a mean and a variance are fixed.
  • Closure. Linear combinations of normals are normal, conditioning one normal on another gives a normal (the multivariate normal of Joint Distributions & Dependence), and squared distances lead to the chi-square family. Few distributions are this convenient to compute with, which is a large part of why models assume it.
  • Where it fails. Quantities that are positive and skewed (incomes, latencies, file sizes; use the gamma, lognormal or Pareto), bounded (proportions; use the beta), discrete with small counts (use the binomial or Poisson), or heavy-tailed, where the normal's e^(−z²/2) tail makes extreme events seem impossible. A 6σ market move "should" happen once in a few million years under a normal model; such moves are observed every few years. §08 is about those tails.
Six sigma, by the numbers. The manufacturing target "six sigma" does not mean the one-sided normal tail at 6, 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. It assumes the process mean may drift by 1.5σ, leaving 4.5σ to the nearer limit, and P(Z > 4.5) = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶: the familiar "3.4 defects per million". The convention is worth knowing because the two readings differ by a factor of about 3,400.

07 The beta distribution: uncertainty about a proportion

Some random quantities live on (0, 1): a click-through rate, the fraction of a document that is about a topic, a model's accuracy, the probability that a coin lands heads. The beta family is the standard set of densities on that interval. Two parameters let it be flat, U-shaped, bell-shaped or piled against one end, and its parameters have a concrete meaning: pseudo-counts of successes and failures. That reading makes it the natural way to express, and update, a belief about a probability.

The density and the beta function

  • X ~ Beta(a, b), with a, b > 0, has density f(x) = x^(a−1)(1 − x)^(b−1)/B(a, b) on (0, 1), where the normalising constant is the beta function B(a, b) = ∫₀¹ x^(a−1)(1 − x)^(b−1) dx.
  • The beta function in terms of gamma. B(a, b) = Γ(a)Γ(b)/Γ(a + b). The proof writes Γ(a)Γ(b) as a double integral and changes variables to x/(x + y) and x + y; it is the same computation that shows that if G₁ ~ Gamma(a, 1) and G₂ ~ Gamma(b, 1) are independent, then G₁/(G₁ + G₂) ~ Beta(a, b), which is how beta draws are generated on this page. Both are done in Joint Distributions & Dependence. For whole numbers, B(a, b) = (a − 1)!(b − 1)!/(a + b − 1)!; for instance B(2, 2) = 1/6, the constant 6 in 6x(1 − x) found in §02.
  • Mean and variance, by shifting the parameters. E X = B(a + 1, b)/B(a, b) = [Γ(a + 1)Γ(a + b)]/[Γ(a)Γ(a + b + 1)] = a/(a + b), using the recurrence twice. In the same way E X² = a(a + 1)/((a + b)(a + b + 1)), so Var X = ab/((a + b)²(a + b + 1)). For a, b > 1 the mode is (a − 1)/(a + b − 2).
  • The shapes. The exponent a − 1 controls the left end and b − 1 the right end.
    • a = b = 1: the uniform on (0, 1), the flat case.
    • a, b < 1: U-shaped, piling up at both ends. Beta(½, ½), the arcsine distribution, has cdf (2/π) arcsin √x: 20% of its mass lies below 0.1.
    • a, b > 1: bell-shaped with a single interior peak; Beta(2, 2) = 6x(1 − x), symmetric, variance 1/20.
    • a < 1 ≤ b or a = 1 < b: one-sided, highest at 0 and decreasing (e.g. Beta(1, 3) = 3(1 − x)²); the mirror image when the roles swap (Beta(5, 1) = 5x⁴).
    • a = b: symmetric about ½. Larger a + b: more concentrated, since the variance falls like 1/(a + b + 1).

Pseudo-counts and conjugate updating

  • The setting. An unknown success probability p is given a Beta(a, b) distribution expressing current belief (a prior). Then n independent trials with success probability p show k successes. What should the belief be now?
  • Bayes' rule, with a density. The Bayes' rule of Probability Foundations becomes "posterior density ∝ likelihood × prior density" when the unknown is continuous. The binomial likelihood is C(n, k)pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ; the prior is proportional to p^(a−1)(1 − p)^(b−1). Multiply: the posterior is proportional to p^(a+k−1)(1 − p)^(b+n−k−1), which is the shape of Beta(a + k, b + n − k). A shape on (0, 1) determines its normalising constant, so the posterior is that beta distribution. No integral needed to be done.
  • Pseudo-counts. The update just adds: successes to a, failures to b. So a Beta(a, b) prior acts like a successes and b failures already seen, and a + b is the prior's weight in "trials". (Strictly, Beta(a, b) is the posterior after a − 1 successes and b − 1 failures starting from the flat Beta(1, 1).) A family that maps to itself under updating is called conjugate; the beta is conjugate to the binomial, as the gamma was to the Poisson in §05.
  • The posterior mean is a weighted average. E[p | data] = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · a/(a + b) + (1 − w) · k/n, with w = (a + b)/(a + b + n). The prior mean and the observed proportion are blended in proportion to their counts. With little data the prior dominates; with lots of data the observed proportion takes over.
  • Worked: a flat prior. Start from Beta(1, 1) and see 7 successes in 10. The posterior is Beta(8, 4): mean 8/12 = 0.667, mode 7/10 = 0.7 (the observed proportion), 95% of its probability between 0.390 and 0.891, and P(p > ½ | data) = 0.887. Updating in two batches, 3 of 5 then 4 of 5, gives Beta(4, 3) and then Beta(8, 4), the same answer: the order in which data arrive does not matter.
  • Worked: zero successes. With no successes in 10 trials, the observed proportion is 0, but the flat-prior posterior Beta(1, 11) has mean 1/12 = 0.083 and a 95% interval of 0.002 to 0.285. The posterior mean (k + 1)/(n + 2) under a flat prior is Laplace's rule of succession, and it is the same "add-one" smoothing used for word probabilities in naive Bayes.
  • Worked: a clinical trial. Doctors believe a treatment succeeds about a third of the time, and they want 20 successes in 20 patients to raise that belief to 0.9. That fixes the prior: mean a/(a + b) = ⅓ means b = 2a, and (a + 20)/(a + b + 20) = 0.9 then gives a = 20/17 ≈ 1.18, b ≈ 2.35. The prior is weak, worth about 3.5 trials, with a 95% interval from 0.02 to 0.81. Then 22 of 40 patients succeed. The posterior is Beta(23.18, 20.35): mean 0.5325 (close to the observed 0.55, because the data's weight is 40/43.5 = 92%), 95% interval 0.385 to 0.677, and P(p > ½) = 0.668, up from 0.238 under the prior.

Order statistics: a preview

  • Sort n independent U(0, 1) draws, U₍₁₎ ≤ U₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ U₍ₙ₎. The k-th smallest has the Beta(k, n − k + 1) distribution. The heuristic: for U₍ₖ₎ to be near x, one draw must be near x, k − 1 below it (probability x^(k−1)) and n − k above it (probability (1 − x)^(n−k)), which is the beta shape; the multinomial count of which draw plays which role supplies the constant n!/((k − 1)!(n − k)!) = 1/B(k, n − k + 1).
  • The extreme cases were derived exactly in §03: the maximum is Beta(n, 1) with density n xⁿ⁻¹, and the minimum is Beta(1, n). The median of 5 uniforms is Beta(3, 3), with mean ½ and variance 1/28. Combined with the probability integral transform of §09, this gives the distribution of the k-th smallest of n draws from any continuous distribution, which is the basis of quantile confidence intervals. 08 Heavy tails: the Pareto and lognormal distributions

The exponential's tail falls by a fixed factor per unit, and the normal's falls faster still. Many real quantities do not cooperate: city sizes, incomes, file sizes, word frequencies, insurance losses, the number of followers of an account, the gradient norms of a network early in training. Their largest values are enormous compared with typical ones, and a single observation can carry a sizeable share of the total. Two families describe most such data: the Pareto, whose tail is a power law, and the lognormal, the exponential of a normal. Under heavy tails, means and standard deviations can be unstable, or not exist at all.

The Pareto distribution: a power-law tail

  • X ~ Pareto(α, xm), with shape (tail index) α > 0 and minimum xm > 0, has survival function P(X > x) = (xm/x)^α for x ≥ xm and density f(x) = α xm^α / x^(α+1). Doubling x always divides the tail probability by the same factor 2^α, no matter how far out you are. That scale invariance is the signature of a power law.
  • Which moments exist. E Xᵏ = ∫ xᵏ · α xm^α x^(−α−1) dx converges at infinity only when k − α − 1 < −1, that is, when k < α. So:
    • α ≤ 1: no finite mean. At α = 1, xm = 1: E X = ∫₁^∞ x · x⁻² dx = ∫₁^∞ dx/x = ∞. The median is still 2.
    • 1 < α ≤ 2: finite mean, infinite variance. At α = 1.5, xm = 1: E X = α xm/(α − 1) = 3, but E X² = ∫₁^∞ x² · 1.5 x^(−2.5) dx = 1.5 ∫₁^∞ x^(−0.5) dx = ∞. The median is 2^(1/1.5) = 1.587, about half the mean.
    • α > 2: finite variance α xm²/((α − 1)²(α − 2)); at α = 3, xm = 1: mean 1.5, variance 0.75.
  • The mean excess grows. Given X > x, the variable X/x is again Pareto(α, 1), so E[X − x | X > x] = x/(α − 1) for α > 1: the further out you already are, the further you expect to go. At α = 1.5 a file known to exceed 10 MB is expected to be 30 MB. Its hazard α/x decreases, the opposite of wear-out (§04).
  • The 80/20 shape. For α > 1 the top fraction q of the population holds a share q^(1 − 1/α) of the total. Proof: the top q are those above x = xm q^(−1/α), and their share of the mean is ∫ₓ^∞ t f(t) dt / E X = (xm/x)^(α−1) = q^((α−1)/α). "The top 20% hold 80%" requires 0.2^(1 − 1/α) = 0.8, so α = log 5/log 4 = 1.161. At α = 1.5 the top 20% hold 58%; at α = 2, 45%; at α = 3, 34%.
  • The Pareto is an exponential in disguise. P(ln(X/xm) > t) = P(X > xm eᵗ) = e^(−αt), so ln(X/xm) ~ Exp(α). A power law on the original scale is an exponential on the log scale. This is also how Pareto draws are made: xm · U^(−1/α).

The lognormal distribution: exp of a normal

  • Y is lognormal with parameters μ, σ if ln Y ~ N(μ, σ²), that is, Y = e^X with X normal. By the change-of-variables formula of §09 its density is f(y) = (1/(yσ√(2π))) exp(−(ln y − μ)²/(2σ²)) for y > 0. Note that μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of ln Y, not of Y.
  • Its summaries. The median is e^μ (medians pass through monotone maps). The mean is e^(μ + σ²/2), from E e^X = e^(μ + σ²/2) for normal X (a moment generating function, Expectation, Variance & Inequalities); the mode is e^(μ − σ²); the variance is (e^(σ²) − 1)e^(2μ + σ²). For μ = 0, σ = 1: mode 0.368, median 1, mean 1.649, variance 4.67. At σ = 2 the mean is e² = 7.39 times the median, and only P(Z > σ/2) = 16% of values exceed the mean.
  • Where it comes from: multiplicative processes. A quantity that changes by a random factor at each step, Yₙ = Y₀ · R₁ · R₂ ⋯ Rₙ, has ln Yₙ = ln Y₀ + Σ ln Rᵢ, a sum, which the central limit theorem makes approximately normal. So products of many independent positive factors are approximately lognormal: incomes and firm sizes under proportional growth, particle sizes after repeated breakage, latencies built from multiplicative slowdowns, the concentration of a pollutant after dilutions.
  • Worked: mean and typical outcome part ways. A stake is multiplied by 1.5 or by 0.6 with equal chance each year. The expected factor is 1.05, so the mean after 20 years is 1.05²⁰ = 2.65 times the start. But the expected log factor is (ln 1.5 + ln 0.6)/2 = −0.053, so the median outcome (10 ups, 10 downs) is 0.9¹⁰ = 0.35. The mean grows because of a thin slice of very lucky paths; the typical path shrinks. Averages of lognormal quantities are dominated by their upper tail.
  • All moments are finite, but the tail is heavy. The lognormal's tail decays faster than any power but slower than any exponential: for μ fixed and σ large, sample means converge painfully slowly even though every moment exists. Distinguishing a lognormal from a Pareto from data in the far tail is notoriously hard.

Three tails with the same mean

  • Give the Pareto (α = 1.5, xm = 1), an exponential and a lognormal (σ = 1) the same mean, 3. Near the middle they look alike: each puts between 3% and 5% of its mass above 10.   • Reading a log–log tail plot. Plot log P(X > x) against log x. A Pareto gives a straight line of slope −α, because log P(X > x) = α log xm − α log x. A lognormal gives a downward parabola, log P ≈ −(ln x − μ)²/(2σ²), which can look straight over two or three decades. A straight empirical tail is evidence of a power law; its slope estimates α. Fitting that slope by least squares on the plot is biased, which is why tail indices are estimated by maximum likelihood (the Hill estimator) instead.

Why sample means mislead under heavy tails

  • α ≤ 1. There is no mean to converge to. The running average of Pareto draws with α ≤ 1 drifts upward forever, jumping each time a new record arrives; the Cauchy distribution (§02, §09) behaves the same way without drifting, because the average of n Cauchy draws is again Cauchy, whatever n is. (The demonstration on the statistics side is in where the CLT fails.)
  • 1 < α ≤ 2. The law of large numbers holds, so the average does converge, but the variance is infinite and the usual σ/√n error bar does not exist. Convergence is slow and punctuated: long stretches of apparent stability broken by one huge draw that moves the average visibly. The sample standard deviation does not settle either; it grows with n.
  • One value dominates. For α < 1 the largest observation remains a non-vanishing fraction of the sum, however large n becomes. Totals of heavy-tailed quantities (losses, traffic, wealth) are driven by their maxima, which is why extreme-value analysis, not averages, governs risk.
  • What to do instead. Report medians and quantiles, which exist for every distribution and are not moved by one wild value; use trimmed means; analyse on the log scale; and fit the tail explicitly. The robust-statistics material in descriptive statistics (trimmed means, the breakdown point) is exactly the toolkit for this.

09 Transforming one random variable into another

If X has a known distribution and Y = g(X), what is the distribution of Y? Converting units, taking logs, squaring an error, inverting a time into a speed, pushing a score through a sigmoid: all are transformations, and several families on this page are transformations of others. For discrete variables the answer was bookkeeping, adding up the masses that land on each value. For continuous variables probability lives on intervals, so the answer goes through the cdf, and when g is monotone it collapses to one formula with a derivative in it.

The cdf method

  • Three steps. (1) For each y, find the set A_y = {x : g(x) ≤ y}. (2) F_Y(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y) = P(X ∈ A_y) = ∫ over A_y of f_X. (3) Differentiate: f_Y = F_Y′. Steps (1) and (2) always give the cdf of Y, whatever g is: monotone or not, smooth or not, one-to-one or not. Step (3) needs F_Y to be continuous and piecewise differentiable. If g is flat on a set that X lands in with positive probability, Y has an atom there and no density, as with the ReLU max(0, Z) of §02, which sends all of (−∞, 0] to 0.
  • Worked: speed from travel time. Cameras time cars over a 1 km stretch; the travel time T is uniform on 40 to 60 seconds, and the speed is V = 3600/T km/h. For 60 ≤ v ≤ 90, F_V(v) = P(3600/T ≤ v) = P(T ≥ 3600/v) = 1 − (3600/v − 40)/20 = 3 − 180/v, so f_V(v) = 180/v². A uniform time does not give a uniform speed. The share of cars above the 80 km/h limit is 1 − F_V(80) = ¼, not the ⅓ a uniform-speed model gives, and the mean speed is ∫ v · 180/v² dv = 180 ln 1.5 = 72.98, not 3600/E T = 72. Means do not pass through nonlinear functions; for convex g, E g(T) ≥ g(E T) (Jensen's inequality, in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities).
  • Worked: logarithm of an exponential. X ~ Exp(1), Y = ln X. F_Y(y) = P(X ≤ eʸ) = 1 − e^(−eʸ), so f_Y(y) = eʸ e^(−eʸ) on the whole line: a skewed, double-exponential shape. Its mirror image, −ln X, is the Gumbel distribution, which is what the "Gumbel-max" trick adds to logits to sample from a softmax.

The change-of-variables formula for monotone g

  • Statement. Suppose g is strictly monotone and differentiable with g′ ≠ 0 on the support of X (isolated zeros of g′ are harmless), with inverse x = g⁻¹(y). Then f_Y(y) = f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · |d g⁻¹(y)/dy| for y in the range of g, and 0 elsewhere.
  • Proof. If g is increasing, F_Y(y) = P(X ≤ g⁻¹(y)) = F_X(g⁻¹(y)); differentiate with the chain rule to get f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · (g⁻¹)′(y), and (g⁻¹)′ > 0. If g is decreasing, F_Y(y) = P(X ≥ g⁻¹(y)) = 1 − F_X(g⁻¹(y)); differentiating gives −f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · (g⁻¹)′(y), and now (g⁻¹)′ < 0. Both cases are the absolute-value formula.
  • The meaning of the derivative. Probability is conserved: the mass f_X(x)dx in a small interval around x lands in an interval around y = g(x) of width |g′(x)| dx. Where g stretches, the same mass is spread thinner and the density drops; where g compresses, the density rises. |dg⁻¹/dy| = 1/|g′(x)| is the stretching factor. (In several dimensions it becomes the determinant of a Jacobian: Joint Distributions & Dependence.)
  • Linear maps. Y = aX + b, a ≠ 0: f_Y(y) = f_X((y − b)/a)/|a|. Applied to N(μ, σ²), the exponent becomes −(y − aμ − b)²/(2a²σ²) and the constant 1/(|a|σ√(2π)): a linear function of a normal is normal, N(aμ + b, a²σ²). That is the standardisation of §06. A uniform maps to a uniform, an exponential to an exponential (rescaled), a gamma to a gamma when a > 0 and b = 0: the families with a scale parameter are closed under positive scaling.
  • Exponential map. Y = e^X, X ~ N(μ, σ²): g⁻¹(y) = ln y, |dg⁻¹/dy| = 1/y, so f_Y(y) = φ((ln y − μ)/σ)/(σy), the lognormal of §08. The factor 1/y is the stretching of the exponential map; it is what turns a symmetric bell into a right-skewed one. In reverse, Y = 10^U with U ~ U(log₁₀ a, log₁₀ b) has density 1/(y ln(b/a)) on (a, b): the log-uniform density of §03, proportional to 1/y, equal mass per decade.
  • Reciprocal. Y = 1/X is not monotone on ℝ ∖ {0} (it decreases on each half-line but jumps across 0), yet it is one-to-one, so each y has a single root x = 1/y and the same formula applies: f_Y(y) = f_X(1/y)/y². For the Cauchy density 1/(π(1 + x²)) this gives 1/(π y²(1 + 1/y²)) = 1/(π(1 + y²)): the reciprocal of a Cauchy variable is Cauchy again.

When g is not monotone

  • If several x map to the same y, each contributes: f_Y(y) = Σ over the roots xᵢ of g(x) = y of f_X(xᵢ)/|g′(xᵢ)|. It is the continuous version of the discrete rule "collect the mass of every x sent to y", with each contribution scaled by its own stretching factor. This needs g′(xᵢ) ≠ 0 at every root; where g′ vanishes the density of Y can blow up, and where g is flat on an interval Y has an atom. When in doubt, go back to the cdf method: its first two steps always give F_Y.
  • Worked: the square of a standard normal. Y = Z² has two roots ±√y, each with |g′| = 2√y. So f_Y(y) = 2 · φ(√y)/(2√y) = y^(−½) e^(−y/2)/√(2π) for y > 0. Compare with the Gamma(½, ½) density, (½)^½ y^(−½) e^(−y/2)/Γ(½): the two agree exactly when Γ(½) = √π. So Z² ~ χ²₁, as promised in §05, and P(Z² ≤ 1) = P(|Z| ≤ 1) = 0.683.
  • Worked: a square with an asymmetric range. X ~ U(−1, 3), density ¼, and Y = X², which ranges over (0, 9). For 0 < y < 1 both roots ±√y lie in (−1, 3): f_Y(y) = 2 · (¼)/(2√y) = 1/(4√y). For 1 < y < 9 only +√y does: f_Y(y) = 1/(8√y). The density halves abruptly at y = 1, where the negative branch runs out. By the cdf method, F_Y(2) = P(−1 < X ≤ √2) = (√2 + 1)/4 = 0.604.

The probability integral transform, both ways

  • Forward: F(X) is uniform. If X has a continuous cdf F, then U = F(X) ~ U(0, 1). Proof, for strictly increasing F: P(F(X) ≤ u) = P(X ≤ F⁻¹(u)) = F(F⁻¹(u)) = u for 0 < u < 1, which is the uniform cdf. (For a cdf with flat stretches the same holds, using the generalised inverse.) Plugging a variable into its own cdf erases its distribution: whatever X was, F(X) is flat.
  • Backward: F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. If U ~ U(0, 1) and F⁻¹(u) = min{x : F(x) ≥ u}, then X = F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. Proof: by the definition of F⁻¹ as a minimum and the right-continuity of F, F⁻¹(u) ≤ x ⟺ u ≤ F(x), so P(F⁻¹(U) ≤ x) = P(U ≤ F(x)) = F(x). This direction holds for every distribution, discrete, continuous or mixed, which is why it was the drawing method of the discrete page as well.
  • Inverse-transform sampling. The backward direction is a recipe: to draw from F, draw U and return F⁻¹(U).
    • exponential: F⁻¹(u) = −ln(1 − u)/λ, and since 1 − U is also uniform, −ln(U)/λ works;
    • Pareto: xm (1 − u)^(−1/α), or xm U^(−1/α);
    • Weibull: s(−ln U)^(1/k); Cauchy: tan(π(U − ½)); logistic: ln(U/(1 − U));
    • the normal has no closed-form Φ⁻¹, so it is drawn either by a numerical Φ⁻¹ or by a different trick (Box–Muller, which turns two uniforms into two normals using exactly the polar coordinates of §06).
    Every simulation on this page uses one of these. Efficiency, rejection sampling for the cases where F⁻¹ is expensive, and Monte Carlo error are in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
  • The forward direction as a diagnostic. If a model's predicted cdf is right, the values F̂(yᵢ) at the observed outcomes are uniform. A histogram of these PIT values that is U-shaped means the predictive distributions are too narrow (overconfident); a hump means too wide. It is the continuous counterpart of a reliability diagram for probabilistic classifiers. And because a test statistic's p-value is 1 − F(T) under the null, p-values are uniform when the null hypothesis is true (for continuous statistics), which is the fact behind the p-value and every multiple-testing correction.
  • The discrete caveat. For a discrete X, F(X) is not uniform: for a fair coin coded 0/1, F(X) takes only the values ½ and 1. The backward direction is unaffected.
  • The sigmoid is a cdf. The logistic distribution has cdf σ(x) = 1/(1 + e^(−x)), the sigmoid. So logistic regression can be read as "y = 1 exactly when a latent score wᵀx plus logistic noise exceeds 0", and probit regression is the same story with normal noise and Φ in place of σ.

Reading the tree

  • Two processes generate most of it. The Bernoulli process (independent trials) gives the binomial, geometric and negative binomial; the Poisson process (events at a constant rate in continuous time) gives the Poisson, exponential and gamma. The second is the limit of the first as trials get shorter and rarer, which is why each Bernoulli-process family has a partner in the Poisson process: geometric ↔ exponential and negative binomial ↔ gamma for the waiting times, binomial ↔ Poisson for the counts. Both processes are developed as processes in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
  • The normal sits at the bottom of every limit. Binomial, Poisson, gamma, chi-square and negative binomial all become approximately normal when they are sums of many pieces: large n, large μ, large shape. Heavy-tailed families are the exception: a sum of Pareto variables with α < 2 does not become normal at all.
  • Logs move between families. Taking logs turns the lognormal into the normal and the Pareto into the exponential; exponentiating goes back. When data look like one of these on a log scale, the other is the model on the raw scale.
  • The uniform is the root. Through the inverse transform every continuous family is a function of a uniform, and through the probability integral transform every continuous variable can be turned back into one.

Questions that pick the family

  1. What is the support? Whole line: normal (or a heavier-tailed t or Cauchy). Positive: exponential, gamma, lognormal, Weibull, Pareto. Bounded on (0, 1): beta. Bounded on (a, b): uniform, or a rescaled beta.
  2. Is it a waiting time? Constant hazard: exponential. Wait for several events: gamma. Hazard rising or falling with age: Weibull. The hazard plot (§04) is the best single diagnostic.
  3. Is it a sum, or a product? Many small additive effects: normal. Many multiplicative effects: lognormal. A sum of squared normal errors: chi-square.
  4. How heavy is the tail? Plot log P(X > x) against log x (§08). Bending sharply down: exponential-type. Straight: Pareto, and estimate α before trusting any mean or variance. Gently curving: lognormal.
  5. Is there a pile of exact zeros, or a cap? Then the variable is mixed (§02): model the atom and the continuous part separately.
  6. Is the quantity itself a probability you are uncertain about? Beta, with its parameters read as pseudo-counts (§07).

Moment fingerprints

  • For positive data the coefficient of variation CV = σ/μ separates families as the variance/mean ratio did for counts. Exponential: exactly 1. Gamma: 1/√α, below 1 for α > 1 (more regular than random arrivals), above 1 for α < 1. Lognormal: √(e^(σ²) − 1), which exceeds 1 once σ > 0.83. Pareto: 1/√(α(α − 2)) for α > 2, and infinite below. A sample CV well above 1 that keeps growing as data accumulate is the classic sign of a heavy tail.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Continuous distributions are written into machine learning at every level: the loss functions of regression are negative log-densities, the initial weights of every network are drawn from a uniform or a normal with a carefully chosen variance, generative models sample latent variables from a Gaussian and push them through a learned transformation, and Bayesian methods carry beta and gamma beliefs about rates. Most "tricks" in this area are a sentence from this page applied at scale.

Weight initialisation is a variance calculation

  • A unit computes Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ over n_in inputs. With independent zero-mean weights of variance σ_w² and inputs of second moment E x², the output has variance n_in · σ_w² · E x². To keep signals from shrinking or exploding through depth, choose σ_w² ≈ 1/n_in for symmetric activations, or 2/n_in for ReLU, whose output keeps only half the second moment (the E Y² = ½ computed for a ReLU in §02). The compromise 2/(n_in + n_out) balances the forward and backward passes.
  • Uniform or normal, same variance. It is how variational autoencoders are trained; see the reparametrisation trick and the latent-variable view.
  • Diffusion models corrupt data with Gaussian noise of increasing variance and learn to reverse it; every forward step uses the fact that a sum of independent normals is normal (§06). See the forward process.
  • Normalising flows are the change-of-variables formula made into an architecture: a simple density (a standard normal) is pushed through an invertible network g, and the density of the output is the base density times the stretching factor |det(dg⁻¹/dy)|, the multivariate version of §09. Each layer is designed so that factor is cheap to compute.
  • Gumbel noise and sampling. Adding independent Gumbel noise −ln(−ln Uⱼ) to logits and taking the argmax draws exactly from the softmax distribution; its relaxed version, the Gumbel-softmax, makes discrete choices differentiable. The Gumbel arises as minus the log of an exponential (§09).

Priors, conjugacy and exploration

  • Beta–binomial in production. A click-through rate with 3 clicks in 20 impressions is not 15%; with a Beta(a, b) prior fitted to all items it is (a + 3)/(a + b + 20), shrunk toward the average item. This is how rankings stay sane for new items with few impressions.
  • Thompson sampling keeps a beta posterior for each option's success rate, draws one value from each posterior, and plays the option with the largest draw. Options with few trials have wide posteriors and get explored; options known to be bad are drawn high only rarely. It is a principled alternative to the ε-greedy and UCB rules in exploration strategies.
  • Gamma priors play the same role for Poisson rates (§05) and for the precision 1/σ² of a normal; and the Dirichlet, the multi-category beta, is the conjugate prior for the categorical distributions of the discrete page. It is developed with the other joint distributions in Joint Distributions & Dependence.
  • Mixup data augmentation blends two training examples with a weight drawn from Beta(α, α); small α gives the U shape (mostly near one example or the other), α = 1 a uniform blend.

Heavy tails in training, and calibrated uncertainty

  • Gradients are heavy-tailed. Minibatch gradient noise and per-example gradient norms in deep networks are often far from normal, with occasional enormous values. A step size tuned for typical gradients is then destroyed by one rare batch, which is why gradient clipping caps the norm, and why the robust summaries of §08 (medians, quantiles) beat means when monitoring training statistics.
  • Activations and outliers. Large models develop a few activation channels with values hundreds of times the typical size; quantisation schemes that assume a normal-like range clip them and lose accuracy. Fitting the tail, not the bulk, decides the quantisation scale.
  • Calibration. A probabilistic regression model is calibrated when its predicted cdfs, evaluated at the observed outcomes, are uniform (the probability integral transform of §09); a classifier is calibrated when outcomes predicted at p happen a fraction p of the time. Both are checked with the reliability diagrams and scores in thresholds and calibration.
  • Log-uniform hyperparameter search. Learning rates and regularisation strengths are sampled as 10^U (§03, §09), which gives each decade equal probability; see search spaces.
