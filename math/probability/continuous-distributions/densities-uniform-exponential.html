02 Working with densities

Most of the work with a continuous distribution is a short list of operations: fix the constant that makes the area 1, find the support, compute a mean and a variance by integration, read quantiles off the cdf, and recognise the shape. This section does each once, with the discrete versions from Random Variables & Discrete Distributions as the template, and then handles the case that is neither discrete nor continuous: a point mass sitting next to a density.

Normalising constants and support

  • Densities are often known only up to a constant: a model says "proportional to x² on [0, 3]" or "proportional to e^(−2x)". The constant is whatever makes the total area 1: c = 1 / ∫ (shape). Bayesian posteriors, energy-based models and Boltzmann distributions are all written this way, and the constant (the "partition function") is frequently the hard part.
  • Worked. c·x² on [0, 3]: ∫₀³ x² dx = 9, so c = 1/9. c·e^(−2x) on [0, ∞): ∫₀^∞ e^(−2x) dx = ½, so c = 2. c·x(1 − x) on [0, 1]: ∫₀¹ (x − x²) dx = ½ − ⅓ = ⅙, so c = 6 (a beta density, §07). c/(1 + x²) on the whole line: ∫ dx/(1 + x²) = arctan(∞) − arctan(−∞) = π, so c = 1/π, the Cauchy density (§08–§09).
  • Shapes that cannot be normalised. c·x on [0, ∞) has infinite area for every c > 0; so does any non-zero constant on the whole line. There is no "uniform distribution on the real line", and no density proportional to 1/x on (0, ∞). Such shapes appear as "improper priors" in Bayesian work and are legitimate only when the posterior they produce can be normalised.
  • The support is where the density is positive: (0, ∞) for waiting times, (0, 1) for proportions, the whole line for errors. Choosing a family with the right support is the first modelling decision (§11): a normal model for a waiting time puts probability on negative times, and a normal model for a proportion puts probability outside [0, 1].

Mean and variance by integration

  • Sums become integrals: E X = ∫ x f(x) dx and Var X = ∫ (x − μ)² f(x) dx = E X² − μ², with the same balance-point and spread readings as in the discrete case. The mean exists only when ∫ |x| f(x) dx < ∞. The operational rules (E(aX + b) = a E X + b, Var(aX + b) = a² Var X) carry over unchanged; the theory behind them, for every kind of random variable at once, is in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • Worked: the dart. With f(x) = 2x on [0, 1]: E X = ∫₀¹ 2x² dx = ⅔, E X² = ∫₀¹ 2x³ dx = ½, Var X = ½ − 4/9 = 1/18, σ = 0.236. The average distance from the centre is two-thirds of the radius.
  • A distribution with no mean. The Cauchy density 1/(π(1 + x²)) is symmetric about 0, so it is tempting to call 0 its mean. But ∫ |x|/(π(1 + x²)) dx = ∞ (the integrand behaves like 1/(π|x|) far out), so the mean does not exist, and averages of Cauchy draws never settle down. Symmetry gives a median of 0, not a mean. §08 is about distributions like this.
  • The tail formula. For X ≥ 0, E X = ∫₀^∞ P(X > t) dt, the continuous twin of the tail-sum formula for the geometric distribution. Proof: write x = ∫₀ˣ dt inside E X = ∫ x f(x) dx and swap the order of integration; the inner integral becomes ∫ₜ^∞ f(x) dx = P(X > t). It is the fastest route to the mean of anything whose tail is known in closed form.

The quantile function

  • The quantile function reads the cdf backwards: F⁻¹(p) = min{x : F(x) ≥ p}, the smallest value below which a fraction p of the probability lies. When F is strictly increasing on the support it is the ordinary inverse function. The median is F⁻¹(½), the quartiles are F⁻¹(¼) and F⁻¹(¾), and F⁻¹(p) is the 100p-th percentile.
  • Worked: residence time in a reactor. A particle's time in a well-mixed vessel is exponential with mean 4 minutes, so F(t) = 1 − e^(−t/4). Solving F(q) = 0.1 gives q = −4 ln 0.9 = 0.421 minutes. So 10% of particles leave within about 25 seconds, although the mean residence time is 4 minutes; the median is 4 ln 2 = 2.77 minutes and the 90th percentile is 4 ln 10 = 9.21. A single summary hides how much of the mass sits near zero.
  • Worked: the standard normal. Φ⁻¹ has no closed form but is computed to full precision: the quartiles are ±0.6745, so the interquartile range of any normal distribution is 1.349σ. That ratio is why IQR/1.349 is a robust estimate of σ for normal-looking data (spread).
  • Quantiles exist for every distribution, including those without a mean or variance, and they transform simply: for an increasing function g, the median of g(X) is g(median of X). Means do not do that (§09). The quantile function is also how a uniform random number becomes a draw from F (§09).

Symmetric and skewed shapes

  • A density is symmetric about c if f(c + x) = f(c − x) for all x. Then the median is c, and so is the mean whenever it exists. The normal is symmetric about μ; the uniform about the midpoint; the Cauchy about 0 (with no mean).
  • A right-skewed density has a long tail to the right: waiting times, incomes, file sizes, response latencies. The tail pulls the mean above the median, and the peak (the mode) usually sits below both. For the exponential with mean 1 the mode is 0, the median 0.693 and the mean 1; for the lognormal of §08 with μ = 0, σ = 1 they are 0.368, 1 and 1.649. The ordering mode < median < mean is typical of right skew but is not a theorem; there are skewed densities that break it.
  • The skewness coefficient E(X − μ)³/σ³ measures asymmetry on a scale-free basis: 0 for any symmetric distribution, 2 for every exponential, 2/√α for the gamma of §05 (so the gamma becomes symmetric as α grows). Its estimate from data is noisy, and a single far-out observation can dominate it.

Mixed distributions: a point mass plus a density

  • Many real quantities are exactly zero a good fraction of the time and continuous otherwise: daily rainfall (dry days), an insurance claim (no accident), a user's spend this week (no purchase), the output of a ReLU unit. Such a variable is mixed: a point mass p₀ = P(X = 0) plus a density carrying the remaining 1 − p₀. Its cdf jumps by p₀ at 0 and rises continuously elsewhere. It has no pmf (it is not discrete) and no density (the jump is not the integral of anything).
  • Computing with it. Expectations split into a sum over the atoms plus an integral over the continuous part: E g(X) = g(0)·p₀ + ∫ g(x) f_c(x) dx, where f_c integrates to 1 − p₀. Equivalently, condition on "zero or not" with the law of total probability.
  • Worked: rainfall. 60% of days are dry; on a wet day the amount is exponential with mean 8 mm. Then E X = 0.6 · 0 + 0.4 · 8 = 3.2 mm, E X² = 0.4 · (2 · 8²) = 51.2 (an exponential with mean m has E X² = 2m², §04), Var X = 51.2 − 3.2² = 40.96, σ = 6.4 mm. P(X > 10) = 0.4 · e^(−10/8) = 0.115. A model that ignored the dry days and fitted one exponential to all days would misstate the probability of a dry day completely (it would be 0) and get the shape of the wet days wrong.
  • Worked: a capped wait. The time between buses is uniform on 4 to 6 minutes, and a passenger who has just missed one walks if the next takes longer than 5. Her waiting time is W = min(T, 5): P(W = 5) = P(T ≥ 5) = ½, and below 5 it has density ½ on (4, 5). So P(W < 4.5) = ¼, P(W = 5) = ½, and E W = ∫₄⁵ t · ½ dt + 5 · ½ = 2.25 + 2.5 = 4.75 minutes. Capping, censoring and timeouts all create atoms like this.
  • Worked: a ReLU unit. If the input is Z ~ N(0, 1), the output Y = max(0, Z) is 0 with probability ½ and has the normal density on (0, ∞). E Y = ∫₀^∞ z φ(z) dz = φ(0) = 1/√(2π) = 0.399, E Y² = ½, Var Y = ½ − 1/(2π) = 0.341. This mean and variance, not those of Z, are what the next layer sees, and they are what weight-initialisation rules are built to preserve (§12).
  • In models. Zero-inflated and "hurdle" models handle mixed targets with two parts: a classifier for "is it zero?" and a continuous model for the amount given that it is not. Fitting one continuous density to data with a pile of exact zeros misstates both parts.

03 The uniform distribution: equal density everywhere

The simplest density is flat: every sub-interval of the same length gets the same probability. It models a value known to lie between two bounds with no reason to prefer any part of the range, such as the arrival time of a flight within its window, a rounding error, or the phase of a rotating part. Its standard version, uniform on (0, 1), is also the raw material of all simulation: computers produce uniform numbers, and every other distribution is manufactured from them.

Density, cdf, mean and variance

  • X ~ U(a, b) has density f(x) = 1/(b − a) on (a, b) and 0 elsewhere. The height is forced: a rectangle of width b − a and area 1. Integrating, F(x) = (x − a)/(b − a) on [a, b], 0 to the left and 1 to the right: a straight ramp.
  • The uniform property. For any sub-interval of length h inside (a, b), P(t < X ≤ t + h) = h/(b − a), whatever t is. Probability depends on length only, not on location. A flight due at 5:00 that lands uniformly between 4:50 and 5:10 is as likely to land before 4:55 as after 5:05 (each ¼).
  • Moments of U(0, 1): E U = ∫₀¹ u du = ½, E U² = ∫₀¹ u² du = ⅓, Var U = ⅓ − ¼ = 1/12, σ = 1/√12 = 0.2887. Every uniform is a stretched and shifted standard one, X = a + (b − a)U, so E X = (a + b)/2 (the midpoint, by symmetry) and Var X = (b − a)²/12 by the scaling rule.
  • Rounding errors. Rounding a measurement to the nearest unit leaves an error that is close to U(−½, ½) when the measurement varies over many units, with standard deviation 0.289 units. The total rounding error of 100 independent roundings has standard deviation √100 × 0.289 = 2.89 units, far below the worst case of 50, because errors of opposite sign cancel. The same arithmetic gives the noise of an 8-bit quantiser on [0, 1]: step 1/255, standard deviation 1/(255√12) = 0.00113.

Discrete uniform and continuous uniform

  • The discrete uniform on {1, …, k} has variance (k² − 1)/12. Divide its values by k to put them in (0, 1]: the variance becomes (k² − 1)/(12k²), which is 0.0810 at k = 6, 0.083325 at k = 100, and tends to 1/12 = 0.08333. The continuous uniform is the limit of finer and finer equally spaced grids, and its moments are the limits of theirs.
  • The difference is in single values. On {1/k, …, 1} each value has probability 1/k; in the limit each value has probability 0 and only lengths count. A pseudo-random generator that returns 53-bit floating-point numbers is, strictly, a discrete uniform on 2⁵³ grid points; for every question you will ask of it, it behaves as the continuous one.

The atom every simulation is built from

  • A random-number generator produces a stream of numbers that behave like independent U(0, 1) draws. Everything else is a transformation of that stream:
    • a Bernoulli(p) draw is 1{U < p}, since P(U < p) = p;
    • a categorical draw splits (0, 1) into consecutive pieces of lengths p₁, …, pₖ and reports which piece U falls in (this is how the dice of the discrete page were rolled);
    • a uniform on (a, b) is a + (b − a)U;
    • an exponential is −ln(U)/λ (§04), and any continuous distribution with cdf F is F⁻¹(U), the inverse-transform method proved in §09.
  • How generators produce such numbers, why they are only pseudo-random, and how to turn uniform draws into Monte Carlo estimates with honest error bars is the subject of Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

Uniform is not the same as "no information"

  • It is tempting to use a uniform distribution whenever nothing is known. But uniformity depends on the scale you chose. If a success probability p is uniform on (0, 1), then p² is not uniform: P(p² ≤ ¼) = P(p ≤ ½) = ½, so half the mass of p² sits in the first quarter of its range. The odds p/(1 − p) are not uniform either. "Knowing nothing about p" and "knowing nothing about p²" cannot both be expressed by a uniform, so the choice of scale is itself information.
  • Scale parameters want log-uniform. For a quantity that acts multiplicatively (a learning rate, a regularisation strength, a length scale), a uniform on (10⁻⁵, 10⁻¹) puts 90% of its mass in the top decade. Ignorance on a ratio scale is uniformity of the logarithm: log₁₀ λ ~ U(−5, −1) puts a quarter of the mass in each decade. That is the standard recipe for hyperparameter search spaces, and §09 gives its density.

Maxima and minima of uniforms: a first look at order statistics

  • Take n independent U(0, 1) draws. Their maximum is at most x exactly when all of them are, so P(max ≤ x) = xⁿ and the density is n xⁿ⁻¹. Their minimum exceeds x exactly when all of them do, so P(min > x) = (1 − x)ⁿ. The means are n/(n + 1) and 1/(n + 1): the n points split (0, 1) into n + 1 gaps of equal expected length.
  • Worked. The best of 10 random configurations, each scoring uniformly on (0, 1), falls below 0.9 with probability 0.9¹⁰ = 0.349; its expected score is 10/11 = 0.909. Random search depends on exactly this calculation. Both densities are beta densities (§07), and so is the k-th smallest of the n draws.

04 The exponential distribution: waiting without memory

Events that arrive at a constant average rate and independently of one another (requests to a server, decays of a radioactive sample, failures of a component that does not wear out) leave gaps between them that are exponential. It is the continuous twin of the geometric distribution, and like the geometric it is the only distribution of its kind that forgets how long it has already waited. This section derives it from the Poisson count, computes its moments, proves memorylessness in both directions, and carries out the limit from the geometric that the discrete page promised.

From a Poisson count to a waiting time

  • Events occur at rate λ per unit time, and the count in any window of length t is Poisson(μ = λt), as in the Poisson section. Let T be the time until the first event. "T > t" means exactly "no event in (0, t]", so P(T > t) = P(N(t) = 0) = e^(−λt). That is the whole derivation: the survival function S(t) = P(T > t) comes straight from the story, and the cdf and density follow.
  • T ~ Exp(λ) has cdf F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt) and density f(t) = F′(t) = λ e^(−λt) for t ≥ 0. The density is largest at 0 and decays by the same factor e^(−λ) over every unit of time: short gaps are the most common, long ones are rare but never impossible.
  • Rate and scale. λ is a rate (events per unit time), and 1/λ is the mean gap. If E ~ Exp(1) then E/λ ~ Exp(λ), so all exponentials are rescalings of one. Many texts and libraries parametrise by the scale θ = 1/λ instead (density (1/θ)e^(−x/θ)); check which one a function expects, because the two conventions give means that differ by a factor of λ².

Mean and variance, from the integrals

  • Mean, by parts. E T = ∫₀^∞ t · λe^(−λt) dt. With u = t, dv = λe^(−λt) dt, v = −e^(−λt): E T = [−t e^(−λt)]₀^∞ + ∫₀^∞ e^(−λt) dt = 0 + 1/λ. (The boundary term vanishes because t e^(−λt) → 0.) Or in one line with the tail formula of §02: E T = ∫₀^∞ e^(−λt) dt = 1/λ.
  • Second moment, by parts again. E T² = ∫₀^∞ t² λe^(−λt) dt = [−t² e^(−λt)]₀^∞ + ∫₀^∞ 2t e^(−λt) dt = (2/λ) · ∫₀^∞ t λe^(−λt) dt = (2/λ) E T = 2/λ². Hence Var T = 2/λ² − 1/λ² = 1/λ²: the standard deviation equals the mean. More generally E Tⁿ = n!/λⁿ, each integration by parts peeling off one factor.
  • Median and the mean. F(m) = ½ gives m = ln 2/λ = 0.693/λ, about 69% of the mean. P(T > E T) = e⁻¹ = 0.368: only about a third of waits exceed the average wait, because a few long ones pull the average up.
  • Worked: a print queue. Jobs arrive at 3 per hour. The gap between jobs is Exp(3) in hours, with mean 20 minutes. The chance the next job arrives within 5 minutes is F(1/12) = 1 − e^(−3/12) = 0.221; convert units before plugging in.
  • Worked: response time. A system's response time is exponential with rate 0.25 per second (mean 4 s). P(T > 5) = e^(−1.25) = 0.287.

Memorylessness, proved in both directions

  • The property. For all s, t ≥ 0, P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t). Having waited s already, the remaining wait has exactly the distribution of a fresh wait. A component with an exponential lifetime is "as good as new" for as long as it survives.
  • Exponential ⟹ memoryless. {T > s + t} ⊂ {T > s}, so the intersection is the smaller event and P(T > s + t | T > s) = e^(−λ(s+t))/e^(−λs) = e^(−λt). This computation appeared in the conditional-probability section of Probability Foundations, with λ = 1, s = 3, t = 1: e⁻⁴/e⁻³ = e⁻¹ = 0.368.
  • Memoryless ⟹ exponential. Let T be a positive random variable with G(t) = P(T > t) satisfying memorylessness, which rearranges to G(s + t) = G(s)G(t) for all s, t ≥ 0.
    1. Integers and fractions. Applying the equation repeatedly, G(m·t) = G(t)ᵐ for every whole number m. With t = 1/n and m = n: G(1) = G(1/n)ⁿ, so G(1/n) = G(1)^(1/n), and then G(m/n) = G(1)^(m/n). So G(q) = G(1)^q at every positive rational q.
    2. The base is strictly between 0 and 1. If G(1) = 0 then G(1/n) = 0 for every n, so P(T > 0) = 0, contradicting that T is positive. If G(1) = 1 then G(q) = 1 at every rational, so T exceeds every number with probability 1, which no random variable does. So 0 < G(1) < 1, and we can write G(1) = e^(−λ) with λ = −ln G(1) > 0.
    3. From rationals to all reals. G is non-increasing (it is a survival function). For any real t, squeeze it between rationals q₁ < t < q₂: e^(−λq₂) ≤ G(t) ≤ e^(−λq₁). Letting q₁, q₂ → t forces G(t) = e^(−λt).
    So the exponential is the only memoryless distribution on (0, ∞), exactly as the geometric is the only one on {1, 2, …}. The proof used nothing but the functional equation and monotonicity.
  • Worked. A bulb's lifetime is exponential with mean 1,000 hours. Given that it has already burned 1,000 hours, the chance it lasts another 500 is P(T > 500) = e^(−0.5) = 0.607, the same as for a new bulb. Real bulbs wear out, so real bulbs are not exponential; the model fits failures caused by external shocks (a surge, a dropped packet, a cosmic-ray bit flip), not failures caused by ageing.
  • What memorylessness rules out. "The server has been quiet for an hour, so a request is due" is the gambler's fallacy in continuous time. Equally, "this connection has been open for an hour, so it is probably a long-lived one" is a reason to doubt an exponential model: if surviving tells you something, the hazard is not constant.

Memorylessness: the conditional remaining wait is a fresh copy family · mean · already waited s

The hazard rate: memory, measured

  • The hazard rate of a lifetime T is the instantaneous failure rate among survivors: h(t) = lim (Δ → 0) P(t < T ≤ t + Δ | T > t)/Δ = f(t)/S(t). It is what reliability engineers, actuaries and survival analysts estimate, and it is the most natural way to talk about how a waiting time ages.
  • It determines the distribution. Since f = −S′, h(t) = −(d/dt) ln S(t), and integrating, S(t) = exp(−∫₀ᵗ h(u) du). Any non-negative h whose integral diverges defines a lifetime distribution. A constant hazard λ gives S(t) = e^(−λt): memoryless ⟺ constant hazard.
  • Three shapes. An increasing hazard means wear-out (mechanical parts, the gamma with shape > 1, the uniform on a finite window, whose hazard 1/(b − t) explodes at the deadline). A decreasing hazard means "the longer it has lasted, the longer it will last" (software bugs that survive testing, the length of a strike, the lifetime of a startup; the Pareto of §08 has h(t) = α/t). Many populations show a bathtub: early failures, a flat middle, then wear-out.
  • Weibull: a hazard that is a power. Take h(t) = (k/s)(t/s)^(k−1). Then S(t) = exp(−(t/s)ᵏ), the Weibull distribution: k = 1 is the exponential, k > 1 wears out, k < 1 burns in. It is the standard parametric model for component lifetimes and the reason survival analysis talks in hazards rather than densities.
  • Mean residual life, E[T − s | T > s] = ∫ₛ^∞ S(t) dt / S(s), is the hazard's long-run cousin: constant 1/λ for the exponential, shrinking for wear-out, growing for decreasing hazards (for a Pareto it grows linearly, §08).

Competing exponentials

  • Two independent exponential clocks T₁ ~ Exp(λ₁), T₂ ~ Exp(λ₂) race. The first to ring, min(T₁, T₂), exceeds t exactly when both do: P(min > t) = e^(−λ₁t) e^(−λ₂t) = e^(−(λ₁+λ₂)t). The minimum of independent exponentials is exponential with the summed rate.
  • Who wins. P(T₁ < T₂) = ∫₀^∞ λ₁e^(−λ₁t) · e^(−λ₂t) dt = λ₁/(λ₁ + λ₂): condition on T₁ = t and require T₂ > t. Each clock wins in proportion to its rate, and (by memorylessness) the race restarts afresh after every ring.
  • Worked. Two servers finish jobs at rates 2 and 3 per hour. The first completion comes after an Exp(5) time, mean 12 minutes, and it is server 1's with probability 0.4. This is the engine of every continuous-time Markov chain and queueing model, developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

The geometric limit: shrinking the trials

  • The geometric distribution counts Bernoulli trials until the first success. Now let the trials be short: one every Δ units of time, each succeeding with probability p = λΔ, so that successes occur at rate λ per unit time whatever Δ is. The time of the first success is T_Δ = Δ · G with G ~ Geo(λΔ).
  • Its survival function. T_Δ > t means the first ⌊t/Δ⌋ trials all fail, so P(T_Δ > t) = (1 − λΔ)^⌊t/Δ⌋.
  • The limit. Take logarithms: ⌊t/Δ⌋ · ln(1 − λΔ). As Δ → 0, ⌊t/Δ⌋ · Δ → t (it is within Δ of t) and ln(1 − λΔ)/Δ → −λ (the derivative of ln(1 − λx) at 0). The product of the two is ⌊t/Δ⌋ ln(1 − λΔ) = (⌊t/Δ⌋Δ) · (ln(1 − λΔ)/Δ) → t · (−λ). Exponentiating, P(T_Δ > t) → e^(−λt) for every t: the geometric waiting time converges to the exponential. It is the same limit (1 − x/n)ⁿ → e^(−x) that turned the binomial into the Poisson.
  • The moments converge too, and the mean never moves. E T_Δ = Δ · 1/(λΔ) = 1/λ exactly, for every Δ. Var T_Δ = Δ² (1 − λΔ)/(λΔ)² = (1 − λΔ)/λ², which rises to 1/λ² as Δ → 0: discreteness removes a little variance, and the continuous limit puts it back.
  • How fast. The largest gap between the two cdfs is 1 − e^(−λΔ) ≈ λΔ, attained just before the first trial, where the staircase is still at 0 while the exponential has already risen. Halve the trial length and the error halves: at λ = 1 it is 0.221 at Δ = 0.25, 0.095 at Δ = 0.1 and 0.0100 at Δ = 0.01. Memorylessness survives the limit: it held for every Δ, and it holds in the limit.
