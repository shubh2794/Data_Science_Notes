05 The gamma distribution: waiting for the r-th event

The exponential waits for one event; the gamma waits for several. The time until the r-th arrival of a Poisson stream is a sum of r independent exponential gaps, and its density is a gamma density. The same family, with its shape allowed to be any positive number, is the workhorse model for positive, right-skewed quantities (amounts, durations, rates), contains the chi-square distributions of statistics as a special case, and is the mixing distribution that turns a Poisson count into a negative binomial.

The gamma function

  • For α > 0, Γ(α) = ∫₀^∞ y^(α−1) e^(−y) dy. The integral converges at 0 because α > 0 and at infinity because e^(−y) beats any power. It is the function that extends the factorial to non-integers.
  • The recurrence. Integrate by parts with u = y^α, dv = e^(−y) dy: Γ(α + 1) = [−y^α e^(−y)]₀^∞ + α ∫₀^∞ y^(α−1) e^(−y) dy = α Γ(α). With Γ(1) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−y) dy = 1, induction gives Γ(n) = (n − 1)! for every positive integer: Γ(2) = 1, Γ(3) = 2, Γ(4) = 6, Γ(5) = 24.
  • The half-integers. Substituting y = z²/2 in Γ(½) = ∫₀^∞ y^(−½) e^(−y) dy gives √2 ∫₀^∞ e^(−z²/2) dz = √2 · √(2π)/2 = √π, using the Gaussian integral ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−z²/2) dz = √(2π) proved in §06. So Γ(½) = √π = 1.7725, and by the recurrence Γ(3/2) = √π/2 = 0.8862 and Γ(5/2) = 3√π/4 = 1.3293. These are the constants that appear in the chi-square and t densities.
  • Near zero it blows up: Γ(α) = Γ(α + 1)/α ≈ 1/α for small α (Γ(0.1) = 9.514). For large arguments it grows faster than any exponential, which is why software works with ln Γ, as every figure on this page does.
  • The gamma integral. Substituting y = λx in the definition gives the identity used for every calculation below: ∫₀^∞ x^(α−1) e^(−λx) dx = Γ(α)/λ^α for all α, λ > 0.

The density, and where its shape comes from

  • X ~ Gamma(α, λ), shape α > 0 and rate λ > 0, has density f(x) = λ^α x^(α−1) e^(−λx)/Γ(α) for x > 0. It integrates to 1 by the gamma integral. The factor x^(α−1) controls the shape near 0; the factor e^(−λx) controls the tail.
  • Moments from the gamma integral. E X = (λ^α/Γ(α)) ∫ x^α e^(−λx) dx = (λ^α/Γ(α)) · Γ(α + 1)/λ^(α+1) = α/λ. Likewise E X² = Γ(α + 2)/(Γ(α)λ²) = α(α + 1)/λ², so Var X = α/λ². No integration by parts is needed, only the recurrence.
  • The shape parameter α. For α < 1 the density is infinite at 0 and decreasing (many tiny values, a few large ones); α = 1 is the exponential; for α > 1 it is zero at 0 and rises to a single peak at the mode (α − 1)/λ. The skewness is 2/√α, so as α grows the gamma becomes symmetric and, by the central limit theorem, close to normal.
  • The rate parameter λ only rescales: if X ~ Gamma(α, 1) then X/λ ~ Gamma(α, λ). Many libraries use the scale θ = 1/λ instead, with density x^(α−1)e^(−x/θ)/(Γ(α)θ^α) and mean αθ. Fitting with one convention and sampling with the other is a classic bug.
  • Matching a mean and a standard deviation. Given a mean m and standard deviation s, solve α/λ = m, α/λ² = s²: α = (m/s)², λ = m/s². Memory chips with mean lifetime 12 years and standard deviation 4 give α = 9, λ = 0.75 per year.

The waiting time for the r-th arrival

  • Events arrive at rate λ as a Poisson stream; let T_r be the time of the r-th event. "T_r > t" means "fewer than r events in (0, t]", and that count is Poisson(λt): P(T_r > t) = Σ_(k=0..r−1) e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!.
  • Differentiate, and the sum telescopes. (d/dt)[e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!] = λe^(−λt)[(λt)^(k−1)/(k − 1)! − (λt)ᵏ/k!] (the first term absent when k = 0). Summing over k = 0, …, r − 1, every term cancels against its neighbour except the last, so f(t) = −(d/dt)P(T_r > t) = λe^(−λt)(λt)^(r−1)/(r − 1)!. With (r − 1)! = Γ(r) this is the Gamma(r, λ) density. The integer-shape gamma is often called the Erlang distribution, after the engineer who used it for telephone traffic.
  • Sums of exponentials. The gaps between consecutive arrivals are independent Exp(λ) times (memorylessness restarts the clock at every arrival), so T_r = E₁ + … + E_r. Hence E T_r = r/λ and Var T_r = r/λ², agreeing with α/λ and α/λ² at α = r. More generally, independent Gamma(α₁, λ) and Gamma(α₂, λ) variables add to Gamma(α₁ + α₂, λ), provided the rates match. The proof by convolution is in Joint Distributions & Dependence and by moment generating functions in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • The gamma–Poisson identity for computing. For integer r: P(T_r ≤ t) = P(N(t) ≥ r) with N(t) ~ Poisson(λt). Gamma probabilities become Poisson sums, with no integration. It is the continuous mirror of the negative binomial–binomial duality.
  • Worked: compiling in three stages. A build runs three stages in sequence, each exponential with mean 5 minutes. The total is Gamma(3, 0.2): mean 15 minutes, variance 75, standard deviation 8.66. P(T < 12) = P(Poisson(2.4) ≥ 3) = 1 − e^(−2.4)(1 + 2.4 + 2.88) = 0.430. Integrating the density by parts twice gives the same four terms.
  • Worked: memory chips. With α = 9, λ = 0.75: P(8 < T < 10) = F(10) − F(8). F(10) = P(Poisson(7.5) ≥ 9) = 1 − 0.6620 = 0.3380 and F(8) = P(Poisson(6) ≥ 9) = 1 − 0.8472 = 0.1528, so the answer is 0.185.
  • Worked: promotions. A site gets 12 visits per minute, and every sixth visitor sees a promotion. The time between promotions is Gamma(6, 12): mean 0.5 minutes, standard deviation √6/12 = 0.204 minutes, much less variable relative to its mean than a single exponential gap.

The gamma–Poisson mixture, done honestly

  • The negative binomial section claimed that a Poisson count whose rate varies across units according to a gamma distribution is negative binomial. Here is the integral. Let the rate be Λ ~ Gamma(r, β) (shape r, rate β, mean r/β), and given Λ = μ let Y ~ Poisson(μ). By the law of total probability, now with an integral over the continuous mixing variable:
    P(Y = k) = ∫₀^∞ [e^(−μ) μᵏ/k!] · [β^r μ^(r−1) e^(−βμ)/Γ(r)] dμ = (β^r / (k! Γ(r))) · ∫₀^∞ μ^(k+r−1) e^(−(1+β)μ) dμ
  • The remaining integral is the gamma integral with shape k + r and rate 1 + β: it equals Γ(k + r)/(1 + β)^(k+r). So P(Y = k) = [Γ(k + r)/(k! Γ(r))] · (β/(1 + β))^r · (1/(1 + β))ᵏ.
  • With p = β/(1 + β), that is [Γ(k + r)/(Γ(r) k!)] pʳ (1 − p)ᵏ, exactly the failure-count negative binomial with real r. Writing the mixing mean as μ̄ = r/β gives p = r/(r + μ̄), the parametrisation the discrete page used. Its worked case, mean 4 and r = 2, is β = ½, p = ⅓, and P(Y = 0) = (⅓)² = 0.111.
  • The variance split Var Y = E Λ + Var Λ = μ̄ + μ̄²/r reads: Poisson noise within a unit, plus the spread of the rates across units. As r → ∞ the gamma concentrates at its mean and the mixture returns to a plain Poisson.
  • The same computation, read the other way, is Bayesian updating: a gamma prior on a Poisson rate, after observing a count k, gives a Gamma(r + k, β + 1) posterior. The gamma is conjugate to the Poisson, just as the beta is to the binomial (§07).

Chi-square: the gamma with rate ½

  • The chi-square distribution with k degrees of freedom is χ²_k = Gamma(k/2, ½): density x^(k/2 − 1) e^(−x/2)/(2^(k/2) Γ(k/2)), mean k, variance 2k.
  • Why it matters. If Z is standard normal, Z² ~ χ²₁ (derived by change of variables in §09, where the constant Γ(½) = √π falls out). A sum of k independent squared standard normals is χ²_k, by the gamma addition rule above. So the chi-square is the distribution of a sum of squared standardised errors, which is why it governs sample variances, goodness-of-fit statistics and likelihood-ratio tests.
  • Two checks. P(χ²₁ ≤ 3.841) = P(|Z| ≤ 1.960) = 0.95, and 1.96² = 3.8416. χ²₂ = Gamma(1, ½) = Exp(½), so its upper 5% point is the solution of e^(−x/2) = 0.05, x = 2 ln 20 = 5.991, which is the value in every chi-square table.
  • On the statistics side, the chi-square appears as the sampling distribution of a variance and in the skewed-population examples of how fast the CLT takes hold, and as the reference distribution of the chi-square goodness-of-fit test.

06 The normal distribution

The bell curve is the distribution of measurement error, of sums and averages of many small independent effects, and of a long list of physical quantities that are such sums. It is defined by two numbers, a centre μ and a spread σ, and every normal distribution is a stretched and shifted copy of one standard curve, so a single function Φ answers every normal probability question. This section takes the density apart, proves its constant, computes the 68–95–99.7 rule rather than quoting it, and says why the curve is everywhere and where it should not be used.

Anatomy of the density

  • X ~ N(μ, σ²) has density f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) · exp(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)) on the whole real line. Writing z = (x − μ)/σ, the number of standard deviations from the centre, the shape is e^(−z²/2) and everything else is bookkeeping.
  • Squared-exponential decay. Relative to its peak, the density at k standard deviations is e^(−k²/2): 0.607 at k = 1, 0.135 at 2, 0.0111 at 3, 0.000335 at 4 and 0.0000037 at 5. The exponent is quadratic in the distance, so each extra standard deviation costs more than the last. An exponential tail, by contrast, loses the same factor per unit: an exponential variable is 6 standard deviations above its mean (at 7/λ) with probability e⁻⁷ = 0.00091, a normal one with probability 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. This light tail is the normal's defining feature and its main weakness as a model (§08).
  • Landmarks. Symmetric about μ, which is the mean, median and mode. The peak height is 1/(σ√(2π)) = 0.399/σ, so for σ < 0.399 the density exceeds 1. The curve changes from concave to convex at μ ± σ (the second derivative of e^(−z²/2) is (z² − 1)e^(−z²/2), zero at z = ±1): the standard deviation is visible as the inflection points.

Why the constant is σ√(2π)

  • The constant must make the area 1, so we need I = ∫₋∞^∞ e^(−z²/2) dz. The integrand has no elementary antiderivative, but I² can be computed. Write it as a double integral and switch to polar coordinates z = r cos θ, w = r sin θ, where the area element dz dw becomes r dr dθ:
    I² = ∫∫ e^(−(z² + w²)/2) dz dw = ∫₀^(2π) ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²/2) r dr dθ = 2π · [−e^(−r²/2)]₀^∞ = 2π
    So I = √(2π). The extra factor r from polar coordinates is exactly what makes the inner integral elementary. (The change of variables to polar coordinates, a Jacobian, is treated in Joint Distributions & Dependence.)
  • For general σ, substitute z = (x − μ)/σ, dx = σ dz: ∫ e^(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)) dx = σ√(2π). That is the constant. The same integral gave Γ(½) = √π in §05.
  • Mean and variance. E Z = ∫ z φ(z) dz = 0 because the integrand is odd and the integral converges. For the variance, integrate by parts using φ′(z) = −zφ(z): E Z² = ∫ z · zφ(z) dz = [−zφ(z)]₋∞^∞ + ∫ φ(z) dz = 0 + 1 = 1. With X = μ + σZ, E X = μ and Var X = σ², so the two parameters are exactly the mean and the variance.

Standardising: one table for every normal

  • If X ~ N(μ, σ²) then Z = (X − μ)/σ ~ N(0, 1), and conversely X = μ + σZ (proved in §09: a linear function of a normal is normal). So P(X ≤ x) = Φ((x − μ)/σ), and every normal probability is a value of the standard normal cdf Φ. By symmetry Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z).
  • Φ has no formula in elementary functions. It is computed numerically to full precision (here, through the incomplete gamma function) and was tabulated for a century before that. Its inverse Φ⁻¹ turns a probability back into a z.
  • Worked: fathers' heights, mean 68.3 in, standard deviation 1.8 in. What fraction are between 67.4 and 71.9 in? Standardise: z = (67.4 − 68.3)/1.8 = −0.5 and (71.9 − 68.3)/1.8 = 2. The answer is Φ(2) − Φ(−0.5) = 0.97725 − 0.30854 = 0.6687, about 66.9%. (Subtracting table values already rounded to one decimal of a percent, 97.7% − 30.9%, gives 66.8%; the rounding happened before the subtraction.) The 30th percentile is 68.3 + 1.8 · Φ⁻¹(0.3) = 68.3 − 1.8 × 0.5244 = 67.36 in.
  • Worked: the inverse problem. Incomes are modelled as N(900, 200²). The middle class, 600 to 1,200, is Φ(1.5) − Φ(−1.5) = 0.866 of households. The threshold for the poorest 3% solves Φ((x − 900)/200) = 0.03: x = 900 + 200 · Φ⁻¹(0.03) = 900 − 200 × 1.881 = 524. (A real income distribution is right-skewed, so a normal model is a teaching device here; §08 has the better model.)
  • Worked. X ~ N(3, 5) (variance 5): P(X > 1) = 1 − Φ((1 − 3)/√5) = 1 − Φ(−0.894) = 0.8145, and the 20th percentile is 3 + √5 · Φ⁻¹(0.2) = 3 − 2.236 × 0.8416 = 1.118. Note the second parameter: N(3, 5) means variance 5, standard deviation 2.236. Some texts and every plotting library put the standard deviation there instead.

The 68–95–99.7 rule, computed

  • P(|Z| ≤ k) = Φ(k) − Φ(−k) = 2Φ(k) − 1. Computed: 0.682689 at k = 1, 0.954500 at k = 2, 0.997300 at k = 3, 0.999937 at k = 4. The rule "68–95–99.7" is these numbers rounded, and it holds for every normal distribution because every one is a rescaled Z.
  • The quantiles people actually use. Φ⁻¹(0.95) = 1.645 (one-sided 5%, or two-sided 90%), Φ⁻¹(0.975) = 1.960 (two-sided 95%), Φ⁻¹(0.995) = 2.576 (two-sided 99%). The "2" in "mean ± 2 standard errors" is a rounded 1.96.
  • The far tail. One-sided, P(Z > 3) = 0.00135, P(Z > 4) = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵, P(Z > 5) = 2.9 × 10⁻⁷, P(Z > 6) = 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. These numbers are only as good as the normal model in the tail, which is where real data most often depart from it.
  • A bound you can carry in your head. For z > 0, 1 − Φ(z) = ∫ᶻ^∞ φ(t) dt ≤ ∫ᶻ^∞ (t/z) φ(t) dt = φ(z)/z, because t/z ≥ 1 on the range and tφ(t) has antiderivative −φ(t). A second integration by parts gives the lower bound φ(z)(1/z − 1/z³). At z = 3: 0.001313 ≤ 0.001350 ≤ 0.001477. The tail is roughly the density divided by z, which is why it falls off like e^(−z²/2) too.

Sums of independent normals stay normal

  • If X ~ N(μ₁, σ₁²) and Y ~ N(μ₂, σ₂²) are independent, then X + Y ~ N(μ₁ + μ₂, σ₁² + σ₂²), and more generally any linear combination a₁X₁ + … + aₙXₙ + b of independent normals is normal with mean Σ aᵢμᵢ + b and variance Σ aᵢ²σᵢ². The means and variances follow from linearity and independence for any distribution; the remarkable part is that the shape stays normal. The cleanest proof multiplies moment generating functions, in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities.
  • Worked. Two consecutive stages take N(30, 4²) and N(45, 3²) minutes, independently. The total is N(75, 5²) (variances add, standard deviations do not: √(16 + 9) = 5, not 7), and P(total > 85) = 1 − Φ(2) = 0.0228.
  • The sample mean. The average of n independent N(μ, σ²) draws is exactly N(μ, σ²/n). For normal data this is not an approximation, which is the starting point of the sampling distribution of the mean.

Why the normal appears everywhere, and where it should not

  • The central limit theorem. A sum of many independent pieces, none of which dominates, is approximately normal, whatever the pieces' own distributions. Binomial counts and Poisson counts approach the normal for this reason (both roads lead to the normal curve), the gamma does as its shape grows, and so do measurement errors that are the accumulation of many small disturbances. The theorem, with a proof sketch and its rate, is in Limit Theorems & Convergence; its working version, standard errors for means and proportions, is in Sampling Distributions & the CLT.
  • Maximum entropy. Among all densities on the real line with a given mean and variance, the normal has the largest entropy: it assumes nothing beyond those two numbers. Choosing a normal model is, in that sense, the least committal choice once a mean and a variance are fixed.
  • Closure. Linear combinations of normals are normal, conditioning one normal on another gives a normal (the multivariate normal of Joint Distributions & Dependence), and squared distances lead to the chi-square family. Few distributions are this convenient to compute with, which is a large part of why models assume it.
  • Where it fails. Quantities that are positive and skewed (incomes, latencies, file sizes; use the gamma, lognormal or Pareto), bounded (proportions; use the beta), discrete with small counts (use the binomial or Poisson), or heavy-tailed, where the normal's e^(−z²/2) tail makes extreme events seem impossible. A 6σ market move "should" happen once in a few million years under a normal model; such moves are observed every few years. §08 is about those tails.
Six sigma, by the numbers. The manufacturing target "six sigma" does not mean the one-sided normal tail at 6, 9.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. It assumes the process mean may drift by 1.5σ, leaving 4.5σ to the nearer limit, and P(Z > 4.5) = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶: the familiar "3.4 defects per million". The convention is worth knowing because the two readings differ by a factor of about 3,400.

07 The beta distribution: uncertainty about a proportion

Some random quantities live on (0, 1): a click-through rate, the fraction of a document that is about a topic, a model's accuracy, the probability that a coin lands heads. The beta family is the standard set of densities on that interval. Two parameters let it be flat, U-shaped, bell-shaped or piled against one end, and its parameters have a concrete meaning: pseudo-counts of successes and failures. That reading makes it the natural way to express, and update, a belief about a probability.

The density and the beta function

  • X ~ Beta(a, b), with a, b > 0, has density f(x) = x^(a−1)(1 − x)^(b−1)/B(a, b) on (0, 1), where the normalising constant is the beta function B(a, b) = ∫₀¹ x^(a−1)(1 − x)^(b−1) dx.
  • The beta function in terms of gamma. B(a, b) = Γ(a)Γ(b)/Γ(a + b). The proof writes Γ(a)Γ(b) as a double integral and changes variables to x/(x + y) and x + y; it is the same computation that shows that if G₁ ~ Gamma(a, 1) and G₂ ~ Gamma(b, 1) are independent, then G₁/(G₁ + G₂) ~ Beta(a, b), which is how beta draws are generated on this page. Both are done in Joint Distributions & Dependence. For whole numbers, B(a, b) = (a − 1)!(b − 1)!/(a + b − 1)!; for instance B(2, 2) = 1/6, the constant 6 in 6x(1 − x) found in §02.
  • Mean and variance, by shifting the parameters. E X = B(a + 1, b)/B(a, b) = [Γ(a + 1)Γ(a + b)]/[Γ(a)Γ(a + b + 1)] = a/(a + b), using the recurrence twice. In the same way E X² = a(a + 1)/((a + b)(a + b + 1)), so Var X = ab/((a + b)²(a + b + 1)). For a, b > 1 the mode is (a − 1)/(a + b − 2).
  • The shapes. The exponent a − 1 controls the left end and b − 1 the right end.
    • a = b = 1: the uniform on (0, 1), the flat case.
    • a, b < 1: U-shaped, piling up at both ends. Beta(½, ½), the arcsine distribution, has cdf (2/π) arcsin √x: 20% of its mass lies below 0.1.
    • a, b > 1: bell-shaped with a single interior peak; Beta(2, 2) = 6x(1 − x), symmetric, variance 1/20.
    • a < 1 ≤ b or a = 1 < b: one-sided, highest at 0 and decreasing (e.g. Beta(1, 3) = 3(1 − x)²); the mirror image when the roles swap (Beta(5, 1) = 5x⁴).
    • a = b: symmetric about ½. Larger a + b: more concentrated, since the variance falls like 1/(a + b + 1).

Pseudo-counts and conjugate updating

  • The setting. An unknown success probability p is given a Beta(a, b) distribution expressing current belief (a prior). Then n independent trials with success probability p show k successes. What should the belief be now?
  • Bayes' rule, with a density. The Bayes' rule of Probability Foundations becomes "posterior density ∝ likelihood × prior density" when the unknown is continuous. The binomial likelihood is C(n, k)pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ; the prior is proportional to p^(a−1)(1 − p)^(b−1). Multiply: the posterior is proportional to p^(a+k−1)(1 − p)^(b+n−k−1), which is the shape of Beta(a + k, b + n − k). A shape on (0, 1) determines its normalising constant, so the posterior is that beta distribution. No integral needed to be done.
  • Pseudo-counts. The update just adds: successes to a, failures to b. So a Beta(a, b) prior acts like a successes and b failures already seen, and a + b is the prior's weight in "trials". (Strictly, Beta(a, b) is the posterior after a − 1 successes and b − 1 failures starting from the flat Beta(1, 1).) A family that maps to itself under updating is called conjugate; the beta is conjugate to the binomial, as the gamma was to the Poisson in §05.
  • The posterior mean is a weighted average. E[p | data] = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · a/(a + b) + (1 − w) · k/n, with w = (a + b)/(a + b + n). The prior mean and the observed proportion are blended in proportion to their counts. With little data the prior dominates; with lots of data the observed proportion takes over.
  • Worked: a flat prior. Start from Beta(1, 1) and see 7 successes in 10. The posterior is Beta(8, 4): mean 8/12 = 0.667, mode 7/10 = 0.7 (the observed proportion), 95% of its probability between 0.390 and 0.891, and P(p > ½ | data) = 0.887. Updating in two batches, 3 of 5 then 4 of 5, gives Beta(4, 3) and then Beta(8, 4), the same answer: the order in which data arrive does not matter.
  • Worked: zero successes. With no successes in 10 trials, the observed proportion is 0, but the flat-prior posterior Beta(1, 11) has mean 1/12 = 0.083 and a 95% interval of 0.002 to 0.285. The posterior mean (k + 1)/(n + 2) under a flat prior is Laplace's rule of succession, and it is the same "add-one" smoothing used for word probabilities in naive Bayes.
  • Worked: a clinical trial. Doctors believe a treatment succeeds about a third of the time, and they want 20 successes in 20 patients to raise that belief to 0.9. That fixes the prior: mean a/(a + b) = ⅓ means b = 2a, and (a + 20)/(a + b + 20) = 0.9 then gives a = 20/17 ≈ 1.18, b ≈ 2.35. The prior is weak, worth about 3.5 trials, with a 95% interval from 0.02 to 0.81. Then 22 of 40 patients succeed. The posterior is Beta(23.18, 20.35): mean 0.5325 (close to the observed 0.55, because the data's weight is 40/43.5 = 92%), 95% interval 0.385 to 0.677, and P(p > ½) = 0.668, up from 0.238 under the prior.

Order statistics: a preview

  • Sort n independent U(0, 1) draws, U₍₁₎ ≤ U₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ U₍ₙ₎. The k-th smallest has the Beta(k, n − k + 1) distribution. The heuristic: for U₍ₖ₎ to be near x, one draw must be near x, k − 1 below it (probability x^(k−1)) and n − k above it (probability (1 − x)^(n−k)), which is the beta shape; the multinomial count of which draw plays which role supplies the constant n!/((k − 1)!(n − k)!) = 1/B(k, n − k + 1).
  • The extreme cases were derived exactly in §03: the maximum is Beta(n, 1) with density n xⁿ⁻¹, and the minimum is Beta(1, n). The median of 5 uniforms is Beta(3, 3), with mean ½ and variance 1/28. Combined with the probability integral transform of §09, this gives the distribution of the k-th smallest of n draws from any continuous distribution, which is the basis of quantile confidence intervals. The full derivation is in Joint Distributions & Dependence.
