08 Heavy tails: the Pareto and lognormal distributions

The exponential's tail falls by a fixed factor per unit, and the normal's falls faster still. Many real quantities do not cooperate: city sizes, incomes, file sizes, word frequencies, insurance losses, the number of followers of an account, the gradient norms of a network early in training. Their largest values are enormous compared with typical ones, and a single observation can carry a sizeable share of the total. Two families describe most such data: the Pareto, whose tail is a power law, and the lognormal, the exponential of a normal. Under heavy tails, means and standard deviations can be unstable, or not exist at all.

The Pareto distribution: a power-law tail

  • X ~ Pareto(α, xm), with shape (tail index) α > 0 and minimum xm > 0, has survival function P(X > x) = (xm/x)^α for x ≥ xm and density f(x) = α xm^α / x^(α+1). Doubling x always divides the tail probability by the same factor 2^α, no matter how far out you are. That scale invariance is the signature of a power law.
  • Which moments exist. E Xᵏ = ∫ xᵏ · α xm^α x^(−α−1) dx converges at infinity only when k − α − 1 < −1, that is, when k < α. So:
    • α ≤ 1: no finite mean. At α = 1, xm = 1: E X = ∫₁^∞ x · x⁻² dx = ∫₁^∞ dx/x = ∞. The median is still 2.
    • 1 < α ≤ 2: finite mean, infinite variance. At α = 1.5, xm = 1: E X = α xm/(α − 1) = 3, but E X² = ∫₁^∞ x² · 1.5 x^(−2.5) dx = 1.5 ∫₁^∞ x^(−0.5) dx = ∞. The median is 2^(1/1.5) = 1.587, about half the mean.
    • α > 2: finite variance α xm²/((α − 1)²(α − 2)); at α = 3, xm = 1: mean 1.5, variance 0.75.
  • The mean excess grows. Given X > x, the variable X/x is again Pareto(α, 1), so E[X − x | X > x] = x/(α − 1) for α > 1: the further out you already are, the further you expect to go. At α = 1.5 a file known to exceed 10 MB is expected to be 30 MB. Its hazard α/x decreases, the opposite of wear-out (§04).
  • The 80/20 shape. For α > 1 the top fraction q of the population holds a share q^(1 − 1/α) of the total. Proof: the top q are those above x = xm q^(−1/α), and their share of the mean is ∫ₓ^∞ t f(t) dt / E X = (xm/x)^(α−1) = q^((α−1)/α). "The top 20% hold 80%" requires 0.2^(1 − 1/α) = 0.8, so α = log 5/log 4 = 1.161. At α = 1.5 the top 20% hold 58%; at α = 2, 45%; at α = 3, 34%.
  • The Pareto is an exponential in disguise. P(ln(X/xm) > t) = P(X > xm eᵗ) = e^(−αt), so ln(X/xm) ~ Exp(α). A power law on the original scale is an exponential on the log scale. This is also how Pareto draws are made: xm · U^(−1/α).

The lognormal distribution: exp of a normal

  • Y is lognormal with parameters μ, σ if ln Y ~ N(μ, σ²), that is, Y = e^X with X normal. By the change-of-variables formula of §09 its density is f(y) = (1/(yσ√(2π))) exp(−(ln y − μ)²/(2σ²)) for y > 0. Note that μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of ln Y, not of Y.
  • Its summaries. The median is e^μ (medians pass through monotone maps). The mean is e^(μ + σ²/2), from E e^X = e^(μ + σ²/2) for normal X (a moment generating function, Expectation, Variance & Inequalities); the mode is e^(μ − σ²); the variance is (e^(σ²) − 1)e^(2μ + σ²). For μ = 0, σ = 1: mode 0.368, median 1, mean 1.649, variance 4.67. At σ = 2 the mean is e² = 7.39 times the median, and only P(Z > σ/2) = 16% of values exceed the mean.
  • Where it comes from: multiplicative processes. A quantity that changes by a random factor at each step, Yₙ = Y₀ · R₁ · R₂ ⋯ Rₙ, has ln Yₙ = ln Y₀ + Σ ln Rᵢ, a sum, which the central limit theorem makes approximately normal. So products of many independent positive factors are approximately lognormal: incomes and firm sizes under proportional growth, particle sizes after repeated breakage, latencies built from multiplicative slowdowns, the concentration of a pollutant after dilutions.
  • Worked: mean and typical outcome part ways. A stake is multiplied by 1.5 or by 0.6 with equal chance each year. The expected factor is 1.05, so the mean after 20 years is 1.05²⁰ = 2.65 times the start. But the expected log factor is (ln 1.5 + ln 0.6)/2 = −0.053, so the median outcome (10 ups, 10 downs) is 0.9¹⁰ = 0.35. The mean grows because of a thin slice of very lucky paths; the typical path shrinks. Averages of lognormal quantities are dominated by their upper tail.
  • All moments are finite, but the tail is heavy. The lognormal's tail decays faster than any power but slower than any exponential: for μ fixed and σ large, sample means converge painfully slowly even though every moment exists. Distinguishing a lognormal from a Pareto from data in the far tail is notoriously hard.

Three tails with the same mean

  • Give the Pareto (α = 1.5, xm = 1), an exponential and a lognormal (σ = 1) the same mean, 3. Near the middle they look alike: each puts between 3% and 5% of its mass above 10. Far out they are different worlds. Above 100, the exponential has probability 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁵, the lognormal 3.1 × 10⁻⁵ and the Pareto 1.0 × 10⁻³. Above 1,000: 10⁻¹⁴⁵, 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ and 3.2 × 10⁻⁵. In a sample of a million, the Pareto will produce about 32 values above 1,000; the others essentially never.
  • Reading a log–log tail plot. Plot log P(X > x) against log x. A Pareto gives a straight line of slope −α, because log P(X > x) = α log xm − α log x. An exponential bends sharply down (on these axes its tail is −λx, exponential in log x). A lognormal gives a downward parabola, log P ≈ −(ln x − μ)²/(2σ²), which can look straight over two or three decades. A straight empirical tail is evidence of a power law; its slope estimates α. Fitting that slope by least squares on the plot is biased, which is why tail indices are estimated by maximum likelihood (the Hill estimator) instead.

Why sample means mislead under heavy tails

  • α ≤ 1. There is no mean to converge to. The running average of Pareto draws with α ≤ 1 drifts upward forever, jumping each time a new record arrives; the Cauchy distribution (§02, §09) behaves the same way without drifting, because the average of n Cauchy draws is again Cauchy, whatever n is. (The demonstration on the statistics side is in where the CLT fails.)
  • 1 < α ≤ 2. The law of large numbers holds, so the average does converge, but the variance is infinite and the usual σ/√n error bar does not exist. Convergence is slow and punctuated: long stretches of apparent stability broken by one huge draw that moves the average visibly. The sample standard deviation does not settle either; it grows with n.
  • One value dominates. For α < 1 the largest observation remains a non-vanishing fraction of the sum, however large n becomes. Totals of heavy-tailed quantities (losses, traffic, wealth) are driven by their maxima, which is why extreme-value analysis, not averages, governs risk.
  • What to do instead. Report medians and quantiles, which exist for every distribution and are not moved by one wild value; use trimmed means; analyse on the log scale; and fit the tail explicitly. The robust-statistics material in descriptive statistics (trimmed means, the breakdown point) is exactly the toolkit for this.

09 Transforming one random variable into another

If X has a known distribution and Y = g(X), what is the distribution of Y? Converting units, taking logs, squaring an error, inverting a time into a speed, pushing a score through a sigmoid: all are transformations, and several families on this page are transformations of others. For discrete variables the answer was bookkeeping, adding up the masses that land on each value. For continuous variables probability lives on intervals, so the answer goes through the cdf, and when g is monotone it collapses to one formula with a derivative in it.

The cdf method

  • Three steps. (1) For each y, find the set A_y = {x : g(x) ≤ y}. (2) F_Y(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y) = P(X ∈ A_y) = ∫ over A_y of f_X. (3) Differentiate: f_Y = F_Y′. Steps (1) and (2) always give the cdf of Y, whatever g is: monotone or not, smooth or not, one-to-one or not. Step (3) needs F_Y to be continuous and piecewise differentiable. If g is flat on a set that X lands in with positive probability, Y has an atom there and no density, as with the ReLU max(0, Z) of §02, which sends all of (−∞, 0] to 0.
  • Worked: speed from travel time. Cameras time cars over a 1 km stretch; the travel time T is uniform on 40 to 60 seconds, and the speed is V = 3600/T km/h. For 60 ≤ v ≤ 90, F_V(v) = P(3600/T ≤ v) = P(T ≥ 3600/v) = 1 − (3600/v − 40)/20 = 3 − 180/v, so f_V(v) = 180/v². A uniform time does not give a uniform speed. The share of cars above the 80 km/h limit is 1 − F_V(80) = ¼, not the ⅓ a uniform-speed model gives, and the mean speed is ∫ v · 180/v² dv = 180 ln 1.5 = 72.98, not 3600/E T = 72. Means do not pass through nonlinear functions; for convex g, E g(T) ≥ g(E T) (Jensen's inequality, in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities).
  • Worked: logarithm of an exponential. X ~ Exp(1), Y = ln X. F_Y(y) = P(X ≤ eʸ) = 1 − e^(−eʸ), so f_Y(y) = eʸ e^(−eʸ) on the whole line: a skewed, double-exponential shape. Its mirror image, −ln X, is the Gumbel distribution, which is what the "Gumbel-max" trick adds to logits to sample from a softmax.

The change-of-variables formula for monotone g

  • Statement. Suppose g is strictly monotone and differentiable with g′ ≠ 0 on the support of X (isolated zeros of g′ are harmless), with inverse x = g⁻¹(y). Then f_Y(y) = f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · |d g⁻¹(y)/dy| for y in the range of g, and 0 elsewhere.
  • Proof. If g is increasing, F_Y(y) = P(X ≤ g⁻¹(y)) = F_X(g⁻¹(y)); differentiate with the chain rule to get f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · (g⁻¹)′(y), and (g⁻¹)′ > 0. If g is decreasing, F_Y(y) = P(X ≥ g⁻¹(y)) = 1 − F_X(g⁻¹(y)); differentiating gives −f_X(g⁻¹(y)) · (g⁻¹)′(y), and now (g⁻¹)′ < 0. Both cases are the absolute-value formula.
  • The meaning of the derivative. Probability is conserved: the mass f_X(x)dx in a small interval around x lands in an interval around y = g(x) of width |g′(x)| dx. Where g stretches, the same mass is spread thinner and the density drops; where g compresses, the density rises. |dg⁻¹/dy| = 1/|g′(x)| is the stretching factor. (In several dimensions it becomes the determinant of a Jacobian: Joint Distributions & Dependence.)
  • Linear maps. Y = aX + b, a ≠ 0: f_Y(y) = f_X((y − b)/a)/|a|. Applied to N(μ, σ²), the exponent becomes −(y − aμ − b)²/(2a²σ²) and the constant 1/(|a|σ√(2π)): a linear function of a normal is normal, N(aμ + b, a²σ²). That is the standardisation of §06. A uniform maps to a uniform, an exponential to an exponential (rescaled), a gamma to a gamma when a > 0 and b = 0: the families with a scale parameter are closed under positive scaling.
  • Exponential map. Y = e^X, X ~ N(μ, σ²): g⁻¹(y) = ln y, |dg⁻¹/dy| = 1/y, so f_Y(y) = φ((ln y − μ)/σ)/(σy), the lognormal of §08. The factor 1/y is the stretching of the exponential map; it is what turns a symmetric bell into a right-skewed one. In reverse, Y = 10^U with U ~ U(log₁₀ a, log₁₀ b) has density 1/(y ln(b/a)) on (a, b): the log-uniform density of §03, proportional to 1/y, equal mass per decade.
  • Reciprocal. Y = 1/X is not monotone on ℝ ∖ {0} (it decreases on each half-line but jumps across 0), yet it is one-to-one, so each y has a single root x = 1/y and the same formula applies: f_Y(y) = f_X(1/y)/y². For the Cauchy density 1/(π(1 + x²)) this gives 1/(π y²(1 + 1/y²)) = 1/(π(1 + y²)): the reciprocal of a Cauchy variable is Cauchy again.

When g is not monotone

  • If several x map to the same y, each contributes: f_Y(y) = Σ over the roots xᵢ of g(x) = y of f_X(xᵢ)/|g′(xᵢ)|. It is the continuous version of the discrete rule "collect the mass of every x sent to y", with each contribution scaled by its own stretching factor. This needs g′(xᵢ) ≠ 0 at every root; where g′ vanishes the density of Y can blow up, and where g is flat on an interval Y has an atom. When in doubt, go back to the cdf method: its first two steps always give F_Y.
  • Worked: the square of a standard normal. Y = Z² has two roots ±√y, each with |g′| = 2√y. So f_Y(y) = 2 · φ(√y)/(2√y) = y^(−½) e^(−y/2)/√(2π) for y > 0. Compare with the Gamma(½, ½) density, (½)^½ y^(−½) e^(−y/2)/Γ(½): the two agree exactly when Γ(½) = √π. So Z² ~ χ²₁, as promised in §05, and P(Z² ≤ 1) = P(|Z| ≤ 1) = 0.683.
  • Worked: a square with an asymmetric range. X ~ U(−1, 3), density ¼, and Y = X², which ranges over (0, 9). For 0 < y < 1 both roots ±√y lie in (−1, 3): f_Y(y) = 2 · (¼)/(2√y) = 1/(4√y). For 1 < y < 9 only +√y does: f_Y(y) = 1/(8√y). The density halves abruptly at y = 1, where the negative branch runs out. By the cdf method, F_Y(2) = P(−1 < X ≤ √2) = (√2 + 1)/4 = 0.604.

The probability integral transform, both ways

  • Forward: F(X) is uniform. If X has a continuous cdf F, then U = F(X) ~ U(0, 1). Proof, for strictly increasing F: P(F(X) ≤ u) = P(X ≤ F⁻¹(u)) = F(F⁻¹(u)) = u for 0 < u < 1, which is the uniform cdf. (For a cdf with flat stretches the same holds, using the generalised inverse.) Plugging a variable into its own cdf erases its distribution: whatever X was, F(X) is flat.
  • Backward: F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. If U ~ U(0, 1) and F⁻¹(u) = min{x : F(x) ≥ u}, then X = F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. Proof: by the definition of F⁻¹ as a minimum and the right-continuity of F, F⁻¹(u) ≤ x ⟺ u ≤ F(x), so P(F⁻¹(U) ≤ x) = P(U ≤ F(x)) = F(x). This direction holds for every distribution, discrete, continuous or mixed, which is why it was the drawing method of the discrete page as well.
  • Inverse-transform sampling. The backward direction is a recipe: to draw from F, draw U and return F⁻¹(U).
    • exponential: F⁻¹(u) = −ln(1 − u)/λ, and since 1 − U is also uniform, −ln(U)/λ works;
    • Pareto: xm (1 − u)^(−1/α), or xm U^(−1/α);
    • Weibull: s(−ln U)^(1/k); Cauchy: tan(π(U − ½)); logistic: ln(U/(1 − U));
    • the normal has no closed-form Φ⁻¹, so it is drawn either by a numerical Φ⁻¹ or by a different trick (Box–Muller, which turns two uniforms into two normals using exactly the polar coordinates of §06).
    Every simulation on this page uses one of these. Efficiency, rejection sampling for the cases where F⁻¹ is expensive, and Monte Carlo error are in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
  • The forward direction as a diagnostic. If a model's predicted cdf is right, the values F̂(yᵢ) at the observed outcomes are uniform. A histogram of these PIT values that is U-shaped means the predictive distributions are too narrow (overconfident); a hump means too wide. It is the continuous counterpart of a reliability diagram for probabilistic classifiers. And because a test statistic's p-value is 1 − F(T) under the null, p-values are uniform when the null hypothesis is true (for continuous statistics), which is the fact behind the p-value and every multiple-testing correction.
  • The discrete caveat. For a discrete X, F(X) is not uniform: for a fair coin coded 0/1, F(X) takes only the values ½ and 1. The backward direction is unaffected.
  • The sigmoid is a cdf. The logistic distribution has cdf σ(x) = 1/(1 + e^(−x)), the sigmoid. So logistic regression can be read as "y = 1 exactly when a latent score wᵀx plus logistic noise exceeds 0", and probit regression is the same story with normal noise and Φ in place of σ.
