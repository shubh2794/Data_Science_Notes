09 The Poisson distribution: counting rare events

Many opportunities, each with a tiny chance: the number of typos on a page, server errors in an hour, customers arriving in a minute, mutations in a stretch of DNA. Each is a binomial count with enormous n and minute p, and in the limit all such counts follow one distribution, governed by a single number, the expected count μ. That limit is the Poisson distribution, and this section derives it rather than asserting it.

The law of rare events, derived

  • Take X ~ Bin(n, p) with p = μ/n, so that the expected count np = μ stays fixed while the number of opportunities grows and the chance of each shrinks. Fix a value k and split the pmf into four factors:
    C(n, k) (μ/n)ᵏ (1 − μ/n)ⁿ⁻ᵏ = [n(n − 1)⋯(n − k + 1)/nᵏ] · (μᵏ/k!) · (1 − μ/n)ⁿ · (1 − μ/n)⁻ᵏ
  • First factor → 1. It is a product of k terms, (1)(1 − 1/n)(1 − 2/n)⋯(1 − (k − 1)/n). With k fixed, each term tends to 1 and there are only k of them.
  • Second factor does not depend on n.
  • Third factor → e^(−μ). Take logarithms: n ln(1 − μ/n). Using ln(1 − x) = −x − x²/2 − x³/3 − … for |x| < 1, this is n(−μ/n − μ²/(2n²) − …) = −μ − μ²/(2n) − …, which tends to −μ. Exponentiate: (1 − μ/n)ⁿ → e^(−μ). This is the familiar limit (1 + x/n)ⁿ → eˣ with x = −μ.
  • Fourth factor → 1, since 1 − μ/n → 1 and the exponent −k is fixed.
  • Conclusion. P(X = k) → e^(−μ) μᵏ/k! for every fixed k = 0, 1, 2, … The argument only needs npₙ → μ, not exactly p = μ/n. The limit is a genuine pmf: Σₖ e^(−μ)μᵏ/k! = e^(−μ) · e^μ = 1, by the power series of the exponential.
  • What the limit means. No individual trial is modelled any more. The Poisson distribution says that if events are individually rare, roughly independent, and not able to happen two at once, then their count depends only on how many are expected, μ, and not on how many opportunities there were. That is why the same pmf fits radioactive decays, typos and request errors.

Mean, variance and shape

  • Mean. The k = 0 term vanishes, and k/k! = 1/(k − 1)!: E X = Σ_(k≥1) e^(−μ) μᵏ/(k − 1)! = μ Σ_(j≥0) e^(−μ) μʲ/j! = μ.
  • Variance, through the factorial moment. The same shift twice gives E X(X − 1) = μ², so E X² = μ² + μ and Var X = μ² + μ − μ² = μ. Both also follow from the binomial: np → μ and np(1 − p) → μ.
  • Mean equals variance. This is the Poisson's fingerprint and its most testable prediction. The standard deviation is √μ, so relative fluctuations shrink like 1/√μ: a count expected to be 100 typically lands within about 10 of it, a count expected to be 10,000 within about 100.
  • Shape. p(k)/p(k − 1) = μ/k, so the pmf rises while k < μ and falls after: the mode is ⌊μ⌋, with a tie between μ − 1 and μ when μ is a whole number (at μ = 3, p(2) = p(3) = 0.2240). For μ < 1 the mode is 0. The pmf is skewed right, with skewness 1/√μ, and looks increasingly normal as μ grows (§10).

Rates, windows and sums

  • Events usually come with a rate λ per unit of time, length or area, and the count in a window of size t is Poisson with mean μ = λt. Double the window, double the mean, not the count.
  • Independent Poisson counts add. If X ~ Poisson(μ₁) and Y ~ Poisson(μ₂) are independent, then X + Y ~ Poisson(μ₁ + μ₂). In the binomial picture this is just concatenating two runs of rare trials. (A direct proof is a one-line application of the binomial theorem to the convolution sum, done in Joint Distributions & Dependence.)
  • Worked: new accounts. Customers open accounts at 10 per day. Today's count is Poisson(10), and P(more than 8 today) = 1 − F(8) = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667. Over two days the count is Poisson(20), not twice a Poisson(10) count (which could only be even), and P(more than 16 in two days) = 1 − 0.221 = 0.779.
  • Worked: typos. A manuscript averages one typo per two pages, so a page's count is Poisson(0.5). P(clean page) = e^(−0.5) = 0.607, so about 303 of 500 pages are clean; P(two or more typos) = 1 − e^(−0.5)(1 + 0.5) = 0.090.
  • Worked: a classic fit. A nineteenth-century record of deaths from horse kicks in cavalry units covers 200 unit-years with 122 deaths, so μ̂ = 0.61. Poisson predicts 200 · P(k) = 108.7, 66.3, 20.2, 4.1, 0.6 unit-years with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 deaths. The record shows 109, 65, 22, 3, 1. The sample variance is 0.611, essentially equal to the mean, exactly as the Poisson model requires.
  • Worked: birthdays again. Among 23 people there are 253 pairs, each matching with probability 1/365. The number of matching pairs is approximately Poisson with μ = 253/365 = 0.693, so P(at least one match) ≈ 1 − e^(−0.693) = 0.500, against the exact 0.507. The pairs are not quite independent, which is why the Poisson answer is close rather than exact.

When Poisson fails

  • The derivation assumed three things: many opportunities with small, equal probabilities; independence between them; and no simultaneous events. Each failure leaves a signature in the data.
  • Overdispersion (variance > mean). The rate varies between units or over time (some users are heavy users, some hours are busy), or events cluster (one failure triggers others, one infection causes more). This is the most common failure by far, and the negative binomial is the standard replacement.
  • Underdispersion (variance < mean). Events are more regular than chance: scheduled buses, a machine that produces exactly one part per cycle, counts with a hard cap. A binomial with a moderate p often fits better.
  • Excess zeros. Some units can never produce an event (non-users never click), so zeros exceed e^(−μ) even when the non-zero counts look Poisson.
  • A changing rate. If the rate drifts across the observation window, the count over the whole window is a mixture and is overdispersed. Model the rate as a function of time instead.
  • Quick check. Compare Var/mean on the data with 1. For the horse-kick data it is 1.00; for the overdispersed example of §07 it is 3.
Where the Poisson shows up in machine learning. Count features and count targets (Poisson regression uses a log link, log E[y | x] = wᵀx, and its loss is the negative Poisson log-likelihood); request and event rates for capacity planning; anomaly detection on counts, where an observed count is flagged if its Poisson tail probability P(X ≥ x) is tiny; and the arrival process behind queues and streaming systems, the Poisson process, whose inter-arrival times are exponential. The process itself is developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

10 The Poisson approximation in practice

The limit in §09 says the binomial becomes Poisson eventually. In practice you have one particular n and p and want to know whether the Poisson answer is good enough. That needs a way to measure the distance between two distributions and a bound on it. The answer turns out to depend almost entirely on p, not on n.

Measuring the gap: total-variation distance

  • The total-variation distance between two distributions on the same values is d_TV(P, Q) = ½ Σₖ |p(k) − q(k)|. It has a direct meaning: it is the largest difference in probability that the two can assign to any event, d_TV = max_A |P(A) − Q(A)|. If d_TV = 0.01, then every probability you compute with the approximation, for any event whatever, is within 0.01 of the truth.
  • Why the two definitions agree. Let B = {k : p(k) > q(k)}. For any event A, dropping the values outside B can only increase P(A) − Q(A), and adding the values of B not yet in A also increases it. So P(A) − Q(A) ≤ P(B) − Q(B). Because both pmfs sum to 1, P(B) − Q(B) = Q(Bᶜ) − P(Bᶜ), and adding these two equal quantities gives Σ |p(k) − q(k)|. So the maximum is half the sum, attained at A = B.

A bound that depends on p

  • For a sum of independent Bernoulli trials with success probabilities p₁, …, pₙ and mean μ = Σpᵢ, the distance to Poisson(μ) satisfies d_TV ≤ ((1 − e^(−μ))/μ) · Σ pᵢ². For the binomial, with all pᵢ = p, this is d_TV ≤ (1 − e^(−μ)) · p ≤ p. A simpler and older bound, d_TV ≤ Σpᵢ² = np², is weaker by a factor μ/(1 − e^(−μ)): about 1.6 at μ = 1 (0.100 against 0.063 in the table below), approaching μ once μ is a few units.
  • Three consequences. (1) The quality of the approximation is controlled by the size of the individual probabilities, not by n: at fixed μ, halving p halves the error. (2) The trials do not need equal probabilities. Typos on pages with different lengths, errors on requests of different sizes and failures of components with different reliabilities all give Poisson counts as long as every pᵢ is small. That is what makes the "law of rare events" a law. (3) The bound is honest in its order of magnitude: the true distance is never smaller than about Σpᵢ²/32 times min(1, 1/μ). When μ is of order 1 or less, that factor is 1, and a few trials with a large p ruin the approximation. At large μ the factor 1/μ makes the lower bound tiny: one Bernoulli(0.9) trial among trials totalling μ ≈ 100 still leaves the count close to Poisson(100), with a lower bound of about 0.00025.
  • In the table below the actual distance is about a quarter to a third of p in every row, under the bound each time. A practical rule: the Poisson approximation is good to within about 1.5 percentage points when p ≤ 0.05 (the usual textbook rule is n ≥ 20 and p ≤ 0.05), and very good when p ≤ 0.01, whatever n is.

Worked approximations

  • A batch of lamps. 1,000 lamps, each defective with probability 0.001. Exactly: P(0) = 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ = 0.3677, P(more than 2) = 0.0802. Poisson(1): e⁻¹ = 0.3679 and 0.0803. The distance is 0.00028, smaller than the rounding in any practical answer.
  • When p is close to 1, flip it. 97% of messages arrive without error; what is the chance that at least 195 of 200 do? The successes are not rare, but the failures are: Y = 200 − X ~ Bin(200, 0.03) with μ = 6. P(X ≥ 195) = P(Y ≤ 5), which is 0.4457 under Poisson(6) and 0.4432 exactly.
  • When neither is rare. At n = 10, p = 0.5, the distance is 0.17, and no Poisson answer should be trusted. Moderate p with large n is the territory of the normal approximation instead.

Both roads lead to the normal curve

  • As μ grows, the Poisson pmf becomes symmetric and bell-shaped around μ with standard deviation √μ; as n grows with p fixed, the binomial does the same around np with standard deviation √(np(1 − p)). Both are instances of the central limit theorem, because both are sums of many small independent pieces: Bernoulli trials for the binomial, and unit-sized Poisson windows for the Poisson.
  • Worked. X ~ Poisson(100): exactly, P(X ≤ 110) = 0.8529. The normal approximation N(100, 10²) with continuity correction gives Φ(1.05) = 0.8531; without it, Φ(1.0) = 0.8413.
  • Which approximation when. Small p (or 1 − p) and moderate np: Poisson. np(1 − p) above roughly 10: normal with continuity correction. Otherwise, or whenever in doubt: compute the exact sum, which any computer does instantly.
  • The theorem itself, with a proof sketch and the rate at which the normal curve takes over, is in Limit Theorems & Convergence. The applied side, standard errors for proportions and counts, is in Sampling Distributions & the CLT.

11 Categorical and multinomial: more than two outcomes

A Bernoulli trial has two outcomes. A die has six, a classifier chooses among ten classes, and a language model chooses among tens of thousands of tokens. One such trial is categorical; the counts from n independent trials are multinomial. If machine learning has a native distribution, this is it: every softmax output is a categorical distribution, and every cross-entropy loss is its negative log-likelihood.

The categorical distribution: one roll of a k-sided die

  • A categorical random variable takes one of k values, labelled 1, …, k, with probabilities p₁, …, pₖ that are non-negative and sum to 1. Its pmf is just the list: P(X = j) = pⱼ. The labels are names, not quantities: "the mean class label" is meaningless when classes are cat, dog and car.
  • The one-hot form. Encode the outcome as a vector x = (x₁, …, xₖ) with a single 1 in position j. Then the pmf can be written p(x) = p₁^(x₁) p₂^(x₂) ⋯ pₖ^(xₖ): the exponents pick out the right factor, exactly as in the Bernoulli formula pˣ(1 − p)¹⁻ˣ, which is the case k = 2.
  • Mean and variance, coordinate by coordinate. Each coordinate of the one-hot vector is an indicator, xⱼ = 1{X = j}, so E xⱼ = pⱼ and Var xⱼ = pⱼ(1 − pⱼ). The mean of the one-hot vector is the probability vector itself, which is why a model's predicted probabilities can be read as its expected one-hot label.
  • Uncertainty. The spread of a categorical distribution is measured by its entropy, H = −Σ pⱼ log pⱼ: 0 when one outcome is certain, and log k, the maximum, when all k outcomes are equally likely (ln 6 = 1.792 nats for a fair die).

The multinomial distribution: counts from n rolls

  • Roll the k-sided die n times independently and let Nⱼ be the number of times face j appears, so N₁ + … + Nₖ = n. The vector (N₁, …, Nₖ) is multinomial(n; p₁, …, pₖ).
  • Deriving the pmf exactly as for the binomial. Any particular sequence of rolls with counts n₁, …, nₖ has probability p₁^(n₁) ⋯ pₖ^(nₖ) by independence. The number of such sequences is the number of ways to split n positions into labelled groups of sizes n₁, …, nₖ, the multinomial coefficient n!/(n₁! ⋯ nₖ!) of counting. Multiply. The probabilities sum to 1 by the multinomial theorem, (p₁ + … + pₖ)ⁿ = 1.
  • Each count on its own is binomial. Face j versus "anything else" is a Bernoulli trial with success probability pⱼ, so Nⱼ ~ Bin(n, pⱼ), with E Nⱼ = npⱼ and Var Nⱼ = npⱼ(1 − pⱼ). Merging categories keeps the multinomial form: the counts of "faces 1–2", "faces 3–4" and "faces 5–6" are multinomial with the summed probabilities.
  • The counts are negatively related. They must add to n, so if one count is high the others must, on balance, be lower. The covariance of two different counts is −n pᵢ pⱼ; it is derived in Joint Distributions & Dependence. This is the reason the multinomial is a joint distribution of a vector rather than k separate binomials.

The distribution machine learning runs on

  • Class labels. A multiclass classifier models y | x as categorical. Its last layer produces a vector of real-valued scores z (logits), and the softmax turns them into a valid probability vector: pⱼ = e^(zⱼ) / Σᵢ e^(zᵢ). Every entry is positive and they sum to 1, whatever the scores are. Adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, which is why implementations subtract the largest logit before exponentiating. See multiclass logistic regression.
  • Cross-entropy is the negative categorical log-likelihood. In one-hot form, log p(y) = Σⱼ yⱼ log pⱼ = log p_(true class). Training with cross-entropy maximises the probability the model assigns to the observed labels, exactly as binary cross-entropy did for the Bernoulli (§04).
  • Temperature. Dividing the logits by T before the softmax sharpens (T < 1) or flattens (T > 1) the distribution without changing the ranking. As T → 0 all mass goes to the largest logit (greedy choice); as T → ∞ the distribution becomes uniform. For logits (2, 1, 0.5, 0, −0.5, −1) the top probability is 0.825 at T = 0.5, 0.538 at T = 1 and 0.338 at T = 2, and the entropy rises from 0.64 to 1.33 to 1.67 nats.
  • Next-token prediction. A language model's output at each step is a categorical distribution over its vocabulary, and generating text means drawing a token from it, often after reshaping it with a temperature or by truncating to the most likely tokens; see decoding. A model that knew nothing would put 1/V on each of V tokens; with V = 50,000 its cross-entropy would be ln 50,000 = 10.8 nats per token, which is the baseline a trained model's loss is measured against.
  • Bag-of-words. Treating a document of n words as n independent draws from a topic-specific word distribution makes its word counts multinomial. That model, with Bayes' rule on top, is multinomial naive Bayes; scoring each vocabulary word as present or absent instead gives Bernoulli naive Bayes. See naive Bayes variants and the classical text-classification pipeline. Testing whether observed counts fit a multinomial is the chi-square goodness-of-fit test of categorical data analysis.
