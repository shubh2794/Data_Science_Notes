Expectation, Variance & Inequalities

A distribution is a whole curve or table of probabilities; most questions need far less. What does this quantity do on average, how far does it typically stray, and how unlikely is it to stray a lot? This page answers those three questions in general, for every kind of random variable at once. It defines the expectation and the conditions for it to exist, computes expectations of functions without finding their distributions, proves that expectation is linear with no independence assumption, builds variance and higher moments on top, and packs all the moments into one function, the moment generating function. Then it turns those summaries into probability bounds: Markov, Chebyshev, Cantelli, Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz, and the exponential bounds of Chernoff and Hoeffding, which are the reason a model evaluated on ten thousand examples can be trusted. It ends with conditional expectation: the tower rule, the law of total variance, and the fact that the conditional mean is the best possible predictor under squared loss, which is what every regression model is trying to estimate.

01 What expectation is

The expectation (expected value, mean) of a random variable X is the probability-weighted average of its values: E X = Σ x · P(X = x) when X is discrete, E X = ∫ x f(x) dx when it has a density f. It is the centre of mass of the distribution, the point where the probability balances, and it is the number that the average of many independent repetitions settles on. It is defined only when the sum or integral of |x| is finite; when that fails, the mean is either +∞ (the St. Petersburg game) or undefined (the Cauchy distribution); either way there is no finite mean to estimate, and averages of data never settle down.

The definition, discrete and continuous

  • Discrete. If X takes the values x₁, x₂, … with pmf p(xᵢ) = P(X = xᵢ), then E X = Σᵢ xᵢ p(xᵢ). Each possible value is weighted by how likely it is. This is the definition used throughout Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.
  • Worked: drill bits. A drill bit lasts 2, 3 or 4 hours (to the nearest hour) with probabilities 0.1, 0.7 and 0.2. Then E X = 0.1 × 2 + 0.7 × 3 + 0.2 × 4 = 3.1 hours. If a project reserves 10 bits, 31 hours is the natural planning figure: the total could be anything from 20 to 40 hours, but for many bits the total running time is close to 3.1 times their number with high probability. That last statement is the law of large numbers, and it is why the weighted average deserves the name "expected".
  • Continuous. If X has density f, chop the line into short intervals of width h. The interval around x carries probability about f(x)·h, so the discrete average Σ x · f(x)h approximates the mean of X, and as h → 0 that Riemann sum becomes the integral E X = ∫ x f(x) dx. Every rule on this page has a discrete version with a sum and a continuous version with an integral, and the proofs are the same with Σ replaced by ∫.
  • Mixed. A variable with atoms and a continuous part (rainfall with dry days, a capped wait, a ReLU output) has E X = Σ (atoms) x·P(X = x) + ∫ x f_c(x) dx, the sum over the point masses plus the integral over the continuous part. The worked cases are in Continuous Distributions; the one notation that covers every case at once is E X = ∫ x dF(x), an integral against the cdf.

The centre of mass

  • Put a mass p(x) at each point x of a weightless rod. A pivot at c feels a net turning force (torque) proportional to Σ (x − c) p(x) = E X − c. The rod balances exactly when the torque is zero, at c = E X. That is not an analogy; it is the same formula. Physicists call Σ x p(x)/Σ p(x) the centre of mass, and in probability the denominator is 1.
  • The balance identity. Equivalently, E(X − μ) = 0: the deviations from the mean, weighted by probability, cancel exactly. Values far from the mean have more leverage than values close to it, so a small amount of probability far out can move the mean a long way. A long right tail drags the mean to the right of the median.
  • The mean need not be a possible value, or even near one. A fair die has E X = 3.5. A variable that is 1 or 9 with probability ½ each has E X = 5, four units from any value it can take. The mean is a balance point, not a typical value. When a summary of a "typical" outcome is wanted, the median or mode may be the honest answer (the mean is not the typical value).
  • The frequency reading. If the experiment is repeated independently, the value x turns up in a fraction close to p(x) of the runs, so the average of the observed values is close to Σ x p(x). Precisely: the sample mean converges to E X whenever E|X| < ∞. A weak form of this, with an explicit error probability, is proved in §06 from Chebyshev's inequality; the full law of large numbers is in Limit Theorems & Convergence.

Existence: E|X| must be finite

  • A sum or integral over an infinite range can fail to converge, and an alternating one can converge to different values depending on the order of the terms. To make E X a single well-defined number regardless of how the values are listed, the definition demands absolute convergence: E X exists (and is finite) when E|X| = Σ |x| p(x) < ∞, or ∫ |x| f(x) dx < ∞. Then any rearrangement of the sum gives the same answer, which the proofs below need.
  • Positive and negative parts. Write X = X⁺ − X⁻ with X⁺ = max(X, 0) and X⁻ = max(−X, 0), both non-negative. For a non-negative variable the sum or integral always makes sense, possibly as +∞. Then E X = E X⁺ − E X⁻: finite when both parts are finite; +∞ when only the positive part is infinite (a Pareto with α ≤ 1); and undefined when both are infinite (the Cauchy), because ∞ − ∞ has no value.
  • A finite random variable with an infinite mean. Every value of X can be finite while E X = ∞: the values are finite, but the probability-weighted sum of them is not. Nothing is wrong with the variable; it simply has no mean, and its sample averages carry no stable information about a centre.

The St. Petersburg game

  • The game. Toss a fair coin until the first head. If it arrives on toss K, you win 2ᴷ units. K is geometric with P(K = k) = 2⁻ᵏ, so the prize is 2 with probability ½, 4 with probability ¼, 8 with probability ⅛, and so on. How much would you pay to play?
  • The expected prize is infinite. E(2ᴷ) = Σₖ 2ᵏ · 2⁻ᵏ = 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯ = ∞. Every possible toss contributes exactly one unit to the mean: the prizes double exactly as fast as their probabilities halve. Yet the prize is at most 8 with probability ⅞ and at most 1,024 with probability 1 − 2⁻¹⁰ = 0.999. Nobody would pay more than a few tens of units, and that refusal to pay "the expected value" is the paradox.
  • A finite bank resolves it. If the casino can pay at most 2ᵐ, the prize is min(2ᴷ, 2ᵐ) and its mean is m + 1: the first m tosses contribute one unit each, and all longer games together contribute 2ᵐ · P(K > m) = 2ᵐ · 2⁻ᵐ = 1. A bank of about a billion (2³⁰) makes the fair price 31 units. The infinite mean is entirely produced by prizes nobody could pay.
  • What averages do. The mean prize over n independent games keeps growing, roughly like log₂ n: about 10 after a thousand games, 20 after a million. It does not converge to anything, because there is nothing to converge to; each time a new record game arrives, the average jumps. The figure below shows this, against a well-behaved control.
  • A close cousin. The "doubling strategy" of Random Variables & Discrete Distributions (double the stake after every loss) wins a fixed amount with certainty, but the stake needed has expectation Σₖ 100·2ᵏ⁻¹·2⁻ᵏ = ∞ in a fair game. Both are the same series: values doubling while probabilities halve.

The Cauchy distribution has no mean

  • The Cauchy density f(x) = 1/(π(1 + x²)) is symmetric about 0, so 0 is its median and its centre of symmetry. But E X⁺ = ∫₀^∞ x/(π(1 + x²)) dx = [ln(1 + x²)/(2π)]₀^∞ = ∞, and by symmetry E X⁻ = ∞ too. Both halves are infinite, so E X is undefined, not 0.
  • Why "it's symmetric, so the mean is 0" fails. The symmetric truncation ∫₋ₐᵃ x f(x) dx is 0 for every a, but the lopsided truncation ∫₋ₐ^(2a) x f(x) dx = ln((1 + 4a²)/(1 + a²))/(2π) → ln 4/(2π) = 0.221. Different ways of letting the limits go to infinity give different answers, which is precisely what absolute convergence rules out. A mean must not depend on the order in which you add things up.
  • The consequence for data. The average of n independent Cauchy draws is itself Cauchy, with the same spread as one draw (shown by characteristic functions in §05). Averaging buys nothing. The same failure, in a one-sided form, is the Pareto distribution with tail index α ≤ 1 in heavy tails, whose mean is +∞ and whose running average climbs forever.

An indicator's expectation is a probability

  • For an event A, the indicator 1_A is 1 if A happens and 0 otherwise (indicators). Its expectation is E 1_A = 1 · P(A) + 0 · (1 − P(A)) = P(A). So probability is a special case of expectation: every probability statement in Probability Foundations is a statement about the mean of an indicator.
  • Why that matters. Everything proved about expectations (linearity, the inequalities, conditioning) instantly applies to probabilities. The union bound of the axioms section, P(A ∪ B) ≤ P(A) + P(B), is the pointwise inequality 1_(A∪B) ≤ 1_A + 1_B averaged. Inclusion–exclusion is the identity 1_(A∪B) = 1_A + 1_B − 1_A 1_B averaged. The law of total probability is the tower rule of §10 applied to an indicator.
  • The frequency reading again. The fraction of runs in which A happens is the sample mean of 1_A. That an observed frequency estimates a probability is the special case of "a sample mean estimates an expectation", which is why concentration bounds for averages (§08) are also bounds on how far an observed error rate can be from the true one.

Expectation from the tail

  • For a variable with values in {0, 1, 2, …}, E X = Σ_(k≥0) P(X > k); for any non-negative variable, E X = ∫₀^∞ P(X > t) dt. Both were proved earlier (the tail-sum formula and the tail integral). They show that the mean is the area under the survival curve: a heavy tail is a large area. For a general X, E X = ∫₀^∞ P(X > t) dt − ∫₀^∞ P(X < −t) dt, the two areas being E X⁺ and E X⁻.
  • The St. Petersburg tail is P(2ᴷ > t) ≈ 1/t, and ∫ dt/t diverges logarithmically: the same reason the running average grows like log₂ n. Any tail that falls like 1/t or slower has an infinite mean; any tail that falls like 1/t^(1+ε) has a finite one.
The working rule for the rest of the page. Before using a mean, ask whether it exists. For bounded variables (indicators, proportions, losses in [0, 1]) it always does. For counts and waiting times with exponential-type tails it does. For anything with a power-law tail, check the tail index first: the mean needs E|X| < ∞, the variance needs E X² < ∞, and every theorem below that mentions a mean or a variance is silently assuming the corresponding moment is finite.

02 Expectations of functions: the law of the unconscious statistician

Often the quantity of interest is not X but a function of it: the area X² of a square plot, the speed 3600/T from a travel time, a loss ℓ(X), a payoff e^X. The direct route to E g(X) is to find the distribution of g(X) first, which can be hard. The shortcut is to average g(x) against the distribution of X itself: E g(X) = Σ g(x) p(x) or ∫ g(x) f(x) dx. It is called the law of the unconscious statistician (LOTUS), because people use it without noticing that it needs proof. It does, and the proof is a regrouping of terms.

The statement

  • For any function g, E g(X) = Σₓ g(x) p(x) if X is discrete, and E g(X) = ∫ g(x) f(x) dx if X has density f, whenever the sum or integral of |g(x)| is finite. You never need the pmf or density of Y = g(X).
  • The betting reading. Draw X, and I pay you g(X). Your average income is g(x) times the chance that X = x, summed over x. Nothing about the payoff's own distribution is needed, only the payoff for each outcome.
  • Functions of several variables. The same holds for a pair: E r(X, Y) = Σₓ Σᵧ r(x, y) P(X = x, Y = y), summed over the joint pmf (and with a joint density in the continuous case). The joint distribution of a pair is developed in Joint Distributions & Dependence; here it is only needed as the table of probabilities P(X = x, Y = y), which is exactly what §03 uses to prove linearity.

The proof, by regrouping

  • Let X be discrete and Y = g(X). By definition E Y = Σᵧ y · P(Y = y), summed over the values y of Y. The event {Y = y} is the disjoint union of the events {X = x} over all x with g(x) = y, so by additivity P(Y = y) = Σ_(x: g(x)=y) p(x).
  • Substitute: E Y = Σᵧ y Σ_(x: g(x)=y) p(x) = Σᵧ Σ_(x: g(x)=y) g(x) p(x), because inside the inner sum y = g(x). The double sum runs over every x exactly once, grouped by the value of g(x); ungrouping gives Σₓ g(x) p(x). That is the whole proof.
  • Where absolute convergence enters. The step "ungroup the double sum" reorders a possibly infinite series. That is legitimate when Σ |g(x)| p(x) < ∞, which is why it is part of the statement. Without it neither route is well defined: the sums can depend on the order of their terms.
  • The continuous case. For an increasing differentiable g, the change-of-variables formula of Continuous Distributions gives the density of Y as f_Y(y) = f(x)/g′(x) at x = g⁻¹(y); substituting y = g(x), dy = g′(x) dx in ∫ y f_Y(y) dy turns it into ∫ g(x) f(x) dx. A decreasing g flips the limits and the sign of g′ together; a non-monotone g is split into monotone pieces. The general proof, for every g and every distribution, goes through approximation by step functions, and is the same regrouping argument applied to each step.

E g(X) is not g(E X)

  • The one exception: affine maps. For g(x) = ax + b, LOTUS and the linearity of sums give E(aX + b) = Σ (ax + b) p(x) = a Σ x p(x) + b Σ p(x) = a E X + b. Changing units (Celsius to Fahrenheit, seconds to minutes) commutes with taking means. For every other function, E g(X) and g(E X) are in general different numbers, and the difference is not a rounding issue.
  • Squares. E X² ≥ (E X)², with the difference being exactly the variance (§04): E X² − (E X)² = Var X. The building plots: 33⅓ − 25 = 8⅓ = 10²/12, the variance of U(0, 10).
  • Reciprocals. A plant targets 240 chips a day and makes 40, 60 and 80 on three days. Average production is 60, so "on average production must rise 4-fold". But the shortfall factors are 240/40 = 6, 240/60 = 4 and 240/80 = 3, whose average is 13/3 = 4.33. With X the fraction of target achieved, E(1/X) = 4.33 > 1/E X = 4. For X uniform on (1, 3): E(1/X) = ln 3/2 = 0.549 against 1/E X = 0.5.
  • A reciprocal can blow up. For an exponential X, E(1/X) = ∫₀^∞ (1/x) λe^(−λx) dx = ∞, because the integrand behaves like λ/x near 0. A perfectly well-behaved variable can have a function with no mean. Any ratio estimator with a denominator that can come close to zero (a speed from a short time, a rate from a tiny exposure) inherits this problem.
  • The general direction. For convex g (curving up, like x², 1/x on the positives, eˣ), E g(X) ≥ g(E X); for concave g (like ln x, √x) the inequality reverses. That is Jensen's inequality, proved in §07, and it is the reason "the log of an average" and "the average of logs" differ in a predictable direction.

Why the name. Applying E g(X) = Σ g(x) p(x) feels like a definition, so it is used "unconsciously". It is not one: the definition of E g(X) refers to the distribution of g(X), and the formula is a theorem connecting the two. The theorem is what lets every loss function in machine learning be written as E ℓ(f(X), Y) = Σ ℓ(f(x), y) P(x, y) without ever deriving the distribution of the loss.

03 Linearity of expectation, proved

For any two random variables with finite means, defined on the same sample space, E(X + Y) = E X + E Y, and more generally E(a₁X₁ + ⋯ + aₙXₙ + b) = a₁E X₁ + ⋯ + aₙE Xₙ + b. There is no independence assumption, and that is what makes it the most useful identity in probability: the mean of a complicated count can be found by splitting it into simple pieces, however tangled the dependence between the pieces. Random Variables & Discrete Distributions stated it and used it; here is the proof, then its sharpest applications, and the place where the analogous rule for products breaks.

The proof, discrete case

  • Route 1: sum over outcomes. Let the sample space Ω be countable, with outcome probabilities P({ω}). A random variable is a function on Ω, and grouping the outcomes by the value X(ω) takes, which is the LOTUS regrouping of §02, gives E X = Σ_ω X(ω) P({ω}). Now
    E(X + Y) = Σ_ω (X(ω) + Y(ω)) P({ω}) = Σ_ω X(ω) P({ω}) + Σ_ω Y(ω) P({ω}) = E X + E Y
    The middle step splits one absolutely convergent series into two, which is legitimate because E|X| and E|Y| are finite. No joint distribution, no independence: X and Y are added outcome by outcome, and summation is linear.
  • Route 2: through the joint pmf. Write p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y). By LOTUS for pairs, E(X + Y) = Σₓ Σᵧ (x + y) p(x, y) = Σₓ x Σᵧ p(x, y) + Σᵧ y Σₓ p(x, y). The inner sums are marginals: Σᵧ p(x, y) = P(X = x) by the law of total probability (the events {Y = y} partition Ω), and likewise for Y. So E(X + Y) = Σₓ x P(X = x) + Σᵧ y P(Y = y) = E X + E Y.
  • Where independence would have entered, and does not. In route 2 the joint pmf p(x, y) can be anything. If X and Y were independent it would factor as P(X = x)P(Y = y), but the proof never uses a factorisation: it only sums the joint pmf over one coordinate, and that gives the marginal for every joint distribution. That is the precise sense in which linearity "needs no independence".
  • Constants and scaling. E(aX) = Σ ax p(x) = a E X and E b = b. Combining with additivity and induction on n: E(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ + b) = Σᵢ aᵢ E Xᵢ + b for any finite collection.
  • Continuous and general variables. The same two routes work with integrals: ∫∫ (x + y) f(x, y) dx dy splits, and integrating a joint density over one variable gives the marginal density. For a pair with no joint density at all (one discrete, one continuous, or Y = X), route 1 still applies, because the expectation is an integral over Ω and integrals are linear. The general proof approximates each variable by step functions, for which route 1 is a finite sum, and passes to the limit.
  • Countably many terms. For non-negative Xᵢ, E Σ_(i=1..∞) Xᵢ = Σᵢ E Xᵢ, with both sides possibly infinite (monotone convergence). That is what licenses the infinite indicator sums in the tail-sum formula of §01.

A small check you can do by hand

  • Roll two fair dice; let M be the maximum and N the minimum. M and N are strongly dependent (N ≤ M always). Yet M + N is just the sum of the two dice, so E M + E N = 7. With E M = 161/36 = 4.472 from the discrete page, linearity forces E N = 7 − 161/36 = 91/36 = 2.528, which the direct sum Σ k(13 − 2k)/36 = 91/36 confirms. A dependent pair, and the means still add.

The killer application: indicators

  • The method. To find the mean of a count N, write it as a sum of indicators, N = 1_(A₁) + ⋯ + 1_(Aₙ), one per thing that might be counted. Then E N = P(A₁) + ⋯ + P(Aₙ), since each indicator's mean is its probability (§01). The events can be as dependent as they like; only their individual probabilities are needed.
  • Hat matching. n people throw their hats in a pile and each takes one at random. Let N be the number who get their own. Person i gets their own hat with probability 1/n (all hats are equally likely to end up with them), so E N = n · 1/n = 1, for every n. The events are dependent (if the first n − 1 people all match, so must the last), and the distribution of N is a complicated alternating sum (inclusion–exclusion, where P(N = 0) → 1/e). Its mean is one line.
  • Hat matching, second moment. Linearity also handles E N², because N² = Σᵢ 1_(Aᵢ) + Σ_(i≠j) 1_(Aᵢ)1_(Aⱼ) and 1_(Aᵢ)1_(Aⱼ) = 1_(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ). Persons i and j both match with probability 1/(n(n − 1)), and there are n(n − 1) ordered pairs, so E N² = 1 + 1 = 2 and Var N = 2 − 1² = 1 (for n ≥ 2). Mean 1 and variance 1, exactly the Poisson(1) values, which is the distribution N approaches as n grows.
  • The coupon collector. The wait to see all n types is a sum of stage waits, stage j being geometric with success probability (n − j)/n; linearity gives E T = n(1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n) = n Hₙ: 14.7 rolls of a die, about 225 draws for 50 types (the coupon collector). Here the stages happen to be independent, but the mean would be the same if they were not.
  • Shared birthdays. Among 23 people, let N be the number of pairs with the same birthday. There are C(23, 2) = 253 pairs, each matching with probability 1/365, so E N = 253/365 = 0.693. The pairs are dependent (A–B and B–C matching forces A–C), yet the expected count needs none of that. Compare the birthday problem, where P(N ≥ 1) = 0.507 needed a careful complement calculation.
  • Without replacement. A sample of n from N items, K of them marked: draw i is marked with probability K/N by symmetry, although the draws are dependent, so the hypergeometric mean is nK/N (the hypergeometric), the same as with replacement. The variance is where the dependence shows (§04).
  • Elevator stops and runs. m people pick one of 21 floors at random: the expected number of floors with a stop is 21(1 − (20/21)ᵐ), 8.11 for m = 10 (the elevator example). In n fair coin tosses the number of runs is 1 + Σ_(i=2..n) 1{toss i ≠ toss i−1}, with mean 1 + (n − 1)/2.

Products are different: E XY needs independence

  • The product rule. If X and Y are independent, E XY = E X · E Y. Proof, discrete case: independence makes the joint pmf factor, p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y), so by LOTUS E XY = Σₓ Σᵧ xy p_X(x) p_Y(y) = (Σₓ x p_X(x))(Σᵧ y p_Y(y)). The factorisation, which linearity never used, is the whole proof here. The same holds for functions: E[g(X)h(Y)] = E g(X) · E h(Y) for independent X, Y, because g(X) and h(Y) are again independent.
  • It fails without independence. Take Y = X with X a fair 0/1 coin: E XY = E X² = ½, but E X · E Y = ¼. The gap E XY − E X E Y is the covariance, the measure of exactly how much the product rule fails. Covariance and correlation are developed in Joint Distributions & Dependence.
  • The converse is false. Let X be −1, 0, 1 with probability ⅓ each, and Y = X². Then E XY = E X³ = 0 = E X · E Y, so the product rule holds, yet Y is a function of X, as dependent as a pair can be. E XY = E X E Y ("uncorrelated") is strictly weaker than independence.
  • Variances of sums. Expanding a square and applying linearity, Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2(E XY − E X E Y). Under independence the bracket vanishes and variances add; in general the covariance term stays. This is the formula behind every "variances add" statement on the earlier pages, and §04 develops it.

Sums are easy, products are hard. Linearity is a property of the averaging operation, so it holds for every joint distribution. The product rule is a property of the joint distribution (that it factors), so it holds only for some. Almost every "surprisingly simple" expectation calculation is a sum in disguise, and almost every hard one has a product, a ratio or a maximum at its core.

04 Variance and the higher moments

The mean says where the distribution balances; the variance Var X = E(X − μ)² says how far from the balance point the mass typically sits, in squared units, and the standard deviation σ = √Var X says it in the units of X. With linearity proved, the familiar rules for variance follow for every kind of random variable in two lines each. Beyond the variance, the third and fourth standardised moments, skewness and kurtosis, describe asymmetry and the weight of the tails, and this section computes them for the families of the series.

The rules, now proved in general

  • The shortcut. Expand the square and apply linearity (§03): E(X − μ)² = E(X² − 2μX + μ²) = E X² − 2μ E X + μ² = E X² − μ². So Var X = E X² − (E X)², the mean of the square minus the square of the mean. The discrete page did this with sums; the argument now covers densities and mixed variables too. For the drill bits of §01: E X² = 0.4 + 6.3 + 3.2 = 9.9, so Var X = 9.9 − 3.1² = 0.29, the same as 0.1(−1.1)² + 0.7(−0.1)² + 0.2(0.9)².
  • Shift and scale. aX + b has mean aμ + b, so its deviation from its mean is a(X − μ), and Var(aX + b) = E a²(X − μ)² = a² Var X. Adding a constant moves the distribution without spreading it; multiplying by a multiplies every deviation by a and the variance by a². Standard deviations scale by |a|.
  • Zero variance means no randomness. Var X ≥ 0 always, and Var X = 0 exactly when X = μ with probability 1. (If P(|X − μ| ≥ ε) > 0 for some ε > 0, then E(X − μ)² ≥ ε² P(|X − μ| ≥ ε) > 0; this is Chebyshev's inequality of §06 in disguise.)
  • Standardising. Z = (X − μ)/σ has E Z = 0 and Var Z = 1 for every X with finite non-zero variance, by the two rules above. A z-score is "how many standard deviations from the mean", a scale-free number that can be compared across variables with different units. For the normal family standardising produces a single distribution (standardising); for other families it only fixes the first two moments.

The mean is the best constant guess

  • Guess a constant c for X and pay the squared error. The expected cost is E(X − c)² = Var X + (μ − c)². Proof: write X − c = (X − μ) + (μ − c) and square; the cross term is 2(μ − c) E(X − μ) = 0 by the balance identity of §01. The cost is a parabola in c with its minimum, Var X, at c = μ.
  • So the mean is the best constant predictor under squared loss, and the variance is the irreducible cost of predicting with a constant. Every "error = variance + bias²" decomposition in statistics and machine learning (§12, bias, variance and MSE of estimators) is this one-line identity applied to some random quantity. §10 extends it from constants to functions of another variable, where it makes the conditional mean the best predictor.
  • The median is the best constant under absolute loss. E|X − c| is minimised at any median of X, not at the mean (proved in §12). Each summary is the optimal guess for a particular way of scoring guesses.

Variances of sums

  • Let X and Y have means μ_X, μ_Y. Then (X + Y) − (μ_X + μ_Y) = (X − μ_X) + (Y − μ_Y), and squaring and taking expectations, Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)]. The last term is twice the covariance, which is E XY − E X E Y (§03). For independent X, Y it is zero and variances add. For n variables, Var(Σ Xᵢ) = Σ Var Xᵢ + Σ_(i≠j) Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ), which reduces to Σ Var Xᵢ when they are pairwise uncorrelated.
  • The square-root law. For independent X₁, …, Xₙ with common variance σ², Var(X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ) = nσ² and the sample mean has Var X̄ = σ²/n, standard deviation σ/√n. Averaging 100 measurements cuts the noise tenfold, not a hundredfold. This is the standard error of the mean, and the reason every additional digit of precision costs a hundred times more data.
  • Variances subtract nothing. Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var Y for independent X, Y, because Var(−Y) = Var Y. The difference of two noisy measurements is noisier than either. That is why a paired design, which makes X and Y positively correlated so that the covariance term subtracts, is worth so much.
  • Dependence changes the answer. The hypergeometric count (sampling without replacement) has variance n(K/N)(1 − K/N)·(N − n)/(N − 1), smaller than the binomial's by the finite-population factor, because successive draws are negatively correlated (the hypergeometric). Hat matching (§03) has Var N = 1 rather than the 1 − 1/n that independent indicators with probability 1/n would give; there the pairwise correlations are positive.

Why variance, and not the mean absolute deviation?

  • The obvious measure of spread is E|X − μ|, the average distance from the mean. It is in the right units and is less sensitive to outliers. The variance won anyway, for four reasons.
  • 1. It is differentiable. E(X − c)² is a smooth parabola in c with a unique minimum at the mean, found by setting a derivative to zero. E|X − c| is a polygon with corners at the data, minimised anywhere on the interval of medians. Calculus, least squares and gradient descent all run on the squared version.
  • 2. It adds under independence. Two independent fair ±1 coins X, Y each have E|X| = 1 and Var X = 1. Their sum is −2, 0, 2 with probabilities ¼, ½, ¼: Var(X + Y) = 2 = 1 + 1, but E|X + Y| = 1, which is neither 1 + 1 nor √2. There is no rule for the mean absolute deviation of a sum. The variance's additivity is what makes the square-root law, standard errors and the central limit theorem possible; the statement is proved above from E XY = E X E Y, and its general form with covariances is the subject of Joint Distributions & Dependence.
  • 3. It is a parameter. The normal distribution is indexed by its mean and variance, the central limit theorem's limiting normal has the variance of the summands, and the maximum-entropy distribution with a given variance is normal. Mean absolute deviation plays no such structural role.
  • 4. It is geometry. E(X − μ)² is a squared length: in the space of random variables with E X² < ∞, E XY is an inner product, uncorrelated means orthogonal, and "variances add" is Pythagoras. The law of total variance (§11) and the ANOVA split are the same Pythagoras with a different right angle.
  • The price. Squaring makes large deviations dominate. One outlier 10σ out contributes 100 to a sum of squared deviations and only 10 to a sum of absolute ones, and under heavy tails the variance may not exist at all (a Pareto with α ≤ 2). The robust measures of spread exist for this reason. The two are always related by E|X − μ| ≤ σ (a consequence of Jensen's inequality, §07); for the normal, E|X − μ| = σ√(2/π) = 0.798σ.

Higher moments

  • The k-th moment is E Xᵏ and the k-th central moment is μₖ = E(X − μ)ᵏ, defined when E|X|ᵏ < ∞. The mean is the first moment and the variance is the second central moment.
  • Lower moments come free. If E|X|ᵏ < ∞ and j < k, then E|X|ʲ < ∞. Proof: |x|ʲ ≤ 1 when |x| ≤ 1 and |x|ʲ ≤ |x|ᵏ when |x| > 1, so |x|ʲ ≤ 1 + |x|ᵏ everywhere, and E|X|ʲ ≤ 1 + E|X|ᵏ. A finite variance therefore guarantees a finite mean, and the Pareto's rule "E Xᵏ < ∞ ⟺ k < α" is a ladder that is climbed from the bottom.
  • Central from raw. By the binomial theorem and linearity, μ₃ = E X³ − 3μ E X² + 2μ³ and μ₄ = E X⁴ − 4μ E X³ + 6μ² E X² − 3μ⁴. Raw moments are often the easy ones to compute (the exponential's are n!/λⁿ), and these formulas convert them.
  • Skewness γ₁ = μ₃/σ³ is the standardised third moment: 0 for every symmetric distribution, positive for a long right tail. Kurtosis κ = μ₄/σ⁴ is the standardised fourth moment, E Z⁴ for the z-score: 3 for every normal distribution, so excess kurtosis κ − 3 measures tail weight relative to the normal. Both are invariant to shifting and to rescaling by a positive constant, so they describe shape, not location or size.
  • What kurtosis measures. the region |Z| ≤ 1 can contribute at most 1 to E Z⁴, so any kurtosis well above 1 must come from |Z| > 1: it is a measure of the tails, not of how "peaked" the centre looks. It is never below 1 + γ₁²: since E Z = 0 and E Z² = 1, γ₁ = E Z³ = E[Z(Z² − 1)], and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of §07 gives γ₁² ≤ E Z² · E(Z² − 1)² = κ − 1. Equality holds only for two-point distributions, and the smallest possible kurtosis, 1, belongs to a symmetric two-point one: a fair coin has kurtosis exactly 1.

05 Moment generating functions

The moment generating function (mgf) of X is M(t) = E e^(tX), a function of a real variable t. It packs every moment of X into one function (they are its derivatives at t = 0), it turns sums of independent variables into products, and, when it exists near 0, it determines the distribution completely. Those three properties make it the fastest way to prove that a family is closed under addition, and this section uses it to prove two results promised earlier in the series: that sums of independent normals are normal, and that the mean of a lognormal is e^(μ + σ²/2).

Definition, and moments as derivatives

  • M(t) = E e^(tX) = Σ e^(tx) p(x) or ∫ e^(tx) f(x) dx, by LOTUS. Always M(0) = 1. The mgf is useful only when it is finite on an open interval around 0, (−t₀, t₀) for some t₀ > 0; "the mgf exists" means exactly that.
  • Why it generates moments. Expand the exponential: e^(tX) = 1 + tX + t²X²/2! + t³X³/3! + ⋯. Taking expectations term by term (legitimate inside the interval where M is finite), M(t) = 1 + t E X + t² E X²/2! + t³ E X³/3! + ⋯. So E Xᵏ is k! times the k-th Taylor coefficient of M at 0, that is, M⁽ᵏ⁾(0) = E Xᵏ.
  • Equivalently, differentiate under the expectation: M′(t) = E[X e^(tX)], so M′(0) = E X; M″(t) = E[X² e^(tX)], so M″(0) = E X². Then Var X = M″(0) − M′(0)².
  • Worked: exponential. M(t) = ∫₀^∞ e^(tx) λe^(−λx) dx = λ ∫₀^∞ e^(−(λ−t)x) dx = λ/(λ − t) for t < λ, and infinite for t ≥ λ. Then M′(t) = λ/(λ − t)² and M″(t) = 2λ/(λ − t)³, so E X = 1/λ, E X² = 2/λ², Var X = 1/λ², with no integration by parts. Expanding λ/(λ − t) = Σ (t/λ)ᵏ reads off E Xᵏ = k!/λᵏ in one step.
  • Worked: Poisson. M(t) = Σₖ e^(tk) e^(−μ) μᵏ/k! = e^(−μ) Σₖ (μeᵗ)ᵏ/k! = e^(μ(eᵗ − 1)), finite for all t. M′(t) = μeᵗ M(t), so M′(0) = μ; M″(t) = (μeᵗ + μ²e^(2t))M(t), so M″(0) = μ + μ² and Var X = μ.
  • Worked: normal. For Z ~ N(0, 1), complete the square: tz − z²/2 = −(z − t)²/2 + t²/2, so E e^(tZ) = e^(t²/2) ∫ φ(z − t) dz = e^(t²/2), the remaining integral being the area under a shifted normal density. For X = μ + σZ, E e^(tX) = e^(μt) E e^((σt)Z) = e^(μt + σ²t²/2).

Sums of independent variables: mgfs multiply

  • If X and Y are independent, so are e^(tX) and e^(tY), and by the product rule of §03, M_(X+Y)(t) = E[e^(tX) e^(tY)] = E e^(tX) · E e^(tY) = M_X(t) M_Y(t). For n independent copies of X, M_(X₁+⋯+Xₙ)(t) = M(t)ⁿ, and the sample mean has M_X̄(t) = M(t/n)ⁿ. Convolutions of distributions, which are hard, become products of functions, which are easy.
  • Uniqueness. If two random variables have mgfs that are finite and equal on an open interval around 0, they have the same distribution. (The proof is an inversion theorem for Laplace transforms and is not given here.) So to identify the distribution of a sum, compute the product of mgfs and recognise it in the table.
  • The caveat is not a technicality. Uniqueness needs the mgf to exist near 0; knowing all the moments is not enough when it does not. The standard lognormal has every moment finite, E Yᵏ = e^(k²/2), but no mgf for t > 0, and the density f(y)(1 + sin(2π ln y)), with f the lognormal density, has exactly the same moments (substituting y = eᶻ, each extra term is e^(k²/2) E sin(2π(Z + k)) = 0). Two different distributions, one moment sequence.

Delivered: sums of independent normals are normal

  • The normal section stated that if X ~ N(μ₁, σ₁²) and Y ~ N(μ₂, σ₂²) are independent, then X + Y ~ N(μ₁ + μ₂, σ₁² + σ₂²), and deferred the proof here. Multiply the mgfs:
    M_(X+Y)(t) = e^(μ₁t + σ₁²t²/2) · e^(μ₂t + σ₂²t²/2) = e^((μ₁ + μ₂)t + (σ₁² + σ₂²)t²/2)

  • Any linear combination. For independent Xᵢ ~ N(μᵢ, σᵢ²) and constants aᵢ, b, M(t) = e^(bt) Πᵢ e^(aᵢμᵢt + aᵢ²σᵢ²t²/2), so Σ aᵢXᵢ + b ~ N(Σ aᵢμᵢ + b, Σ aᵢ²σᵢ²). More closure results, each in one line

  • Gamma. Independent Gamma(α₁, β) and Gamma(α₂, β): (β/(β − t))^α₁ (β/(β − t))^α₂ = (β/(β − t))^(α₁+α₂), so the sum is Gamma(α₁ + α₂, β), the addition rule used for the r-th arrival. With α = 1: a sum of n independent Exp(λ) gaps has mgf (λ/(λ − t))ⁿ and is Gamma(n, λ). The rates must match; with different rates the product is not in the table and the sum is not gamma.
  • Chi-square. Z² ~ χ²₁ has mgf (1 − 2t)^(−½), so a sum of k independent squared standard normals has (1 − 2t)^(−k/2): it is χ²_k.
  • Poisson. e^(μ₁(eᵗ − 1)) e^(μ₂(eᵗ − 1)) = e^((μ₁ + μ₂)(eᵗ − 1)): independent Poisson counts add to a Poisson count with the summed mean (Poisson sums).
  • Binomial and negative binomial. (1 − p + peᵗ)^n₁ (1 − p + peᵗ)^n₂ is Bin(n₁ + n₂, p), and r independent geometric waits multiply to the negative binomial mgf. Again the success probability must be shared.
  • Not closed. The sum of two independent U(0, 1) has mgf ((eᵗ − 1)/t)², which is not of the uniform form: the sum is triangular on (0, 2), not uniform. Closure under addition is special.

Cumulants: the logarithm of the mgf

  • The cumulant generating function K(t) = ln M(t) has derivatives at 0 called the cumulants κ₁, κ₂, …: κ₁ = μ, κ₂ = σ², κ₃ = μ₃, κ₄ = μ₄ − 3σ⁴. Skewness is κ₃/κ₂^(3/2) and excess kurtosis is κ₄/κ₂².
  • Cumulants add. Because mgfs of independent variables multiply, their logarithms add: every cumulant of a sum of independent variables is the sum of their cumulants. For n iid copies, κⱼ becomes nκⱼ, so the skewness of the sum is nκ₃/(nκ₂)^(3/2) = γ₁/√n and its excess kurtosis is nκ₄/(nκ₂)² = γ₂/n, writing γ₂ = κ₄/κ₂² for the excess kurtosis of one term. Shape fades at a known rate as terms are added. That is the quantitative heart of the central limit theorem, developed in Limit Theorems & Convergence; it is why the gamma's skewness is 2/√α (a sum of α exponentials, each with skewness 2) and the binomial's is (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)).
  • Two exact cases. Poisson: K(t) = μ(eᵗ − 1), so every cumulant equals μ: μ₃ = μ, μ₄ = κ₄ + 3κ₂² = μ + 3μ², which are the values used in §04. Normal: K(t) = μt + σ²t²/2, a quadratic, so every cumulant beyond the second is zero. The normal is the only distribution with this property, which is one way to say why it is the limit of sums.

06 From moments to probabilities: Markov, Chebyshev and Cantelli

A mean and a variance do not determine a distribution, but they do limit it. A non-negative quantity with a small mean cannot often be large; a quantity with a small variance cannot often be far from its mean. The three inequalities in this section turn those sentences into numbers that hold for every distribution with the given moments. They are usually loose, because they must cover the worst case, but they need no model at all, and they are the first rung of a ladder that ends, in §08, with bounds that shrink exponentially.

Markov's inequality

  • Statement. If X ≥ 0 and a > 0, then P(X ≥ a) ≤ E X / a.
  • Proof in one line. For every outcome, a · 1{X ≥ a} ≤ X: when X ≥ a the left side is a ≤ X, and otherwise it is 0 ≤ X. Take expectations of both sides (expectation preserves inequalities, by linearity applied to the non-negative difference): a · P(X ≥ a) ≤ E X.
  • Reading it. No more than a fraction 1/k of a non-negative population can exceed k times its average. At most a fifth of households can earn five times the mean income; if a service's mean response time is 200 ms, at most 20% of responses can take a second or more.
  • It is tight. The two-point variable equal to a with probability E X / a and to 0 otherwise has P(X ≥ a) = E X / a exactly. So without further information, Markov's bound cannot be improved. With further information it usually can: if the response times are exponential with mean 200 ms, P(X ≥ 1000) = e⁻⁵ = 0.0067, thirty times below the bound.
  • Non-negativity is essential. X = ±100 with probability ½ each has E X = 0, and "P(X ≥ 100) ≤ 0/100 = 0" is false (it is ½). Negative values can cancel large positive ones in the mean without making the large values any rarer.
  • The general form. For any non-decreasing φ ≥ 0 with φ(a) > 0, {X ≥ a} ⊂ {φ(X) ≥ φ(a)}, so P(X ≥ a) ≤ E φ(X)/φ(a). Choosing φ well is the whole art: φ(x) = x² applied to |X − μ| gives Chebyshev below, φ(x) = |x|ᵏ gives the moment bounds P(|X| ≥ a) ≤ E|X|ᵏ/aᵏ, and φ(x) = e^(tx) gives the Chernoff bound of §08.

Chebyshev's inequality

  • Statement. If X has mean μ and finite variance σ², then for every ε > 0, P(|X − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/ε². With ε = kσ: P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k².
  • Proof from Markov. (X − μ)² is non-negative, and {|X − μ| ≥ ε} = {(X − μ)² ≥ ε²}. Markov gives P((X − μ)² ≥ ε²) ≤ E(X − μ)²/ε² = σ²/ε².
  • Reading it. At least 75% of any distribution lies within 2 standard deviations of its mean, and at least 88.9% within 3. For the normal the true figures are 95.45% and 99.73%; for an exponential, P(|X − 1/λ| ≥ 2/λ) = P(X ≥ 3/λ) = e⁻³ = 0.0498. The "68–95–99.7" rule is a fact about one family; Chebyshev's "0–75–88.9" is a fact about all of them.
  • It is tight too. The three-point variable at μ − kσ, μ, μ + kσ with probabilities 1/(2k²), 1 − 1/k², 1/(2k²) has mean μ, variance σ², and P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) = 1/k². To be far from the mean as often as possible on a fixed variance budget, put the far mass exactly at distance kσ and the rest exactly at the mean.
  • Worked: software errors. A release has an expected 20 defects with standard deviation 2. Then P(more than 30) ≤ P(|X − 20| ≥ 10) ≤ 4/100 = 0.04. With standard deviation 5 the bound is only 25/100 = 0.25: the same mean, but much more room for a bad surprise. Chebyshev's inequality is the reason variance is used as a measure of risk.
  • Worked: a test error rate. A classifier with unknown error rate p is evaluated on n independent test cases. The observed error rate p̂ has variance p(1 − p)/n ≤ 1/(4n), so P(|p̂ − p| ≥ ε) ≤ 1/(4nε²). For n = 100 and ε = 0.2 that is 1/16 = 0.0625. §08 improves this number by a factor of almost 100.

The weak law of large numbers, in one line

  • Let X₁, …, Xₙ be independent with mean μ and variance σ². The sample mean X̄ has mean μ and variance σ²/n (§04), so Chebyshev gives P(|X̄ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), which tends to 0 as n → ∞ for every fixed ε. The sample mean converges in probability to the expectation: the frequency reading of §01 is a theorem. (It is called "weak" because it bounds the probability of a large deviation at each n, rather than following one infinite sequence; the strong law, and versions that need only E|X| < ∞, are in Limit Theorems & Convergence. The simulation view is in the law of large numbers.)
  • A sample size with no model. How large must a poll be so that its proportion is within 5 points of the truth with probability at least 95%? Chebyshev: 1/(4n · 0.05²) ≤ 0.05 needs n ≥ 2,000. The normal approximation says n ≈ (1.96/(2 × 0.05))² = 385, and Hoeffding's inequality (§08), which is still distribution-free, says n = 738. Guarantees that hold for every distribution cost data; how much depends on which inequality is used.

Cantelli's one-sided inequality

  • Statement. For a > 0, P(X − μ ≥ a) ≤ σ²/(σ² + a²), and symmetrically P(X − μ ≤ −a) ≤ σ²/(σ² + a²).
  • Proof: shift before squaring. For any u ≥ 0, {X − μ ≥ a} ⊂ {(X − μ + u)² ≥ (a + u)²}, so by Markov P(X − μ ≥ a) ≤ E(X − μ + u)²/(a + u)² = (σ² + u²)/(a + u)². The free shift u lets the parabola sit off-centre, which Chebyshev's u = 0 does not. Minimising over u (set the derivative to zero) gives u = σ²/a, and substituting, (σ² + σ⁴/a²)/(a + σ²/a)² = σ²/(σ² + a²).
  • When it beats Chebyshev. For a one-sided question, Chebyshev can only say P(X − μ ≥ kσ) ≤ P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k². Cantelli says 1/(1 + k²), which is always smaller: ½ against 1 at k = 1 (where Chebyshev says nothing at all), 0.2 against 0.25 at k = 2, 0.1 against 0.111 at k = 3. The gain is largest close to the mean; far out the two agree to leading order. Use Cantelli whenever only one tail matters: an upper latency limit, a minimum yield, a worst-case loss.
  • It is tight. The two-point variable at μ + a with probability σ²/(σ² + a²) and at μ − σ²/a otherwise has mean μ, variance σ² and exactly that upper-tail probability.
  • A corollary: the median is within one standard deviation of the mean. If a median m had m > μ + σ, then P(X ≥ m) ≥ ½, but Cantelli gives P(X − μ ≥ m − μ) ≤ σ²/(σ² + (m − μ)²) < ½. The same on the other side. So |μ − m| ≤ σ for every distribution with a finite variance; skewness can separate the mean and median, but never by more than a standard deviation.
  • Worked: an exponential with mean 1 (so σ = 1), upper tail at 4, that is 3 standard deviations above the mean. True value e⁻⁴ = 0.0183; Markov 1/4 = 0.25; Chebyshev 1/9 = 0.111; Cantelli 1/10 = 0.100. Close to the mean the order changes: at 1.5, Markov gives 0.667 and Cantelli 0.8, because Markov uses the non-negativity that the variance-based bounds ignore. Why the bounds are loose, and why that is their point. Each bound is attained by some distribution, always a lumpy one with two or three atoms. Real distributions are smoother, and their tails are far thinner than the worst case. A bound that holds for every distribution with a given mean and variance must pay for the worst one. That is the right trade when the distribution is genuinely unknown (a new system, an adversarial input, an arbitrary hypothesis class); when it is known, compute the probability instead.

07 Inequalities for expectations: Jensen and Cauchy–Schwarz

The bounds of §06 turn moments into probabilities. Two more inequalities relate expectations to each other. Jensen's inequality says which way E g(X) and g(E X) differ when g curves: for convex g, the average of the function is at least the function of the average. It is behind the AM–GM inequality, the non-negativity of KL divergence, the evidence lower bound of variational inference, and the fact that a sample standard deviation underestimates σ. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds the mean of a product by the second moments of its factors, and is why a correlation can never exceed 1.

Convexity

  • A function g on an interval is convex if every chord lies on or above the graph: g(λx + (1 − λ)y) ≤ λg(x) + (1 − λ)g(y) for all x, y and λ ∈ [0, 1]. It is concave if −g is convex. A twice-differentiable g is convex exactly when g″ ≥ 0, and strictly convex when g″ > 0.
  • Convex: x², |x|, eˣ, 1/x and −ln x on the positives, x ln x, any norm, the log-sum-exp function. Concave: ln x, √x, −x², min(x, c).
  • The supporting line. A convex function lies above each of its tangent lines: for every point m in the interior of the interval there is a slope c (the derivative g′(m) if it exists, any slope between the one-sided derivatives at a corner) with g(x) ≥ g(m) + c(x − m) for all x. This is the property Jensen's proof uses.

Jensen's inequality

  • Statement. If g is convex and E|X|, E|g(X)| are finite, then E g(X) ≥ g(E X). If g is concave, E g(X) ≤ g(E X). If g is strictly convex, equality holds only when X is constant with probability 1.
  • Proof. Take the supporting line at m = E X: g(X) ≥ g(E X) + c(X − E X) for every outcome. Take expectations; by linearity the last term has mean c(E X − E X) = 0, leaving E g(X) ≥ g(E X). For strict convexity the supporting line touches the graph only at m, so equality forces X = m almost surely.
  • The picture, for a two-point variable. Let X = a with probability 1 − p and b with probability p. The point (E X, E g(X)) is the weighted average of (a, g(a)) and (b, g(b)), so it lies on the chord between them, at the fraction p of the way along. The point (E X, g(E X)) lies on the curve directly below. Convexity means "chord above curve", which is exactly Jensen. For a general distribution the point (E X, E g(X)) is a weighted average of points on the graph, so it lies in their convex hull, which for a convex function sits above the graph.
  • The size of the gap. For g(x) = x² the gap E X² − (E X)² is exactly Var X. In general, a second-order Taylor expansion around μ gives E g(X) − g(μ) ≈ ½ g″(μ) Var X: the gap grows with the curvature of g and the spread of X. For the lognormal, ln E e^X − E X = σ²/2 exactly, the curvature of eˣ in log terms times the variance.

Consequences worth knowing by heart

  • Squares and absolute values. E X² ≥ (E X)² (variance is non-negative) and E|X| ≥ |E X|. Applied to the concave √x and the variable (X − μ)²: E|X − μ| = E√((X − μ)²) ≤ √(E(X − μ)²) = σ, the bound quoted in §04. More generally (E|X|ᵖ)^(1/p) increases with p (Lyapunov's inequality), which is a second proof that a finite k-th moment implies finite lower ones.
  • Reciprocals. For positive X, E(1/X) ≥ 1/E X, strictly unless X is constant: the chip example of §02 (4.33 against 4) and every "average of ratios" against "ratio of averages" comparison. Average speed over a route is not the average of the speeds.
  • Logs: E ln X ≤ ln E X. Since ln is concave, the average of logs is at most the log of the average. Exponentiating, the geometric mean e^(E ln X) is at most the arithmetic mean E X: the AM–GM inequality, with equality only for a constant. For the stake multiplied by 1.5 or 0.6 each year (mean and typical outcome part ways), E ln(factor) = −0.0527 while ln E(factor) = ln 1.05 = +0.0488: the arithmetic mean grows 5% a year and the typical path shrinks 5% a year. Anyone compounding returns should maximise E ln, not E.
  • Exponentials. E e^X ≥ e^(E X): the lognormal mean e^(μ + σ²/2) exceeds the median e^μ (§05). Jensen predicted the direction; the mgf computed the size.
  • The sample standard deviation is biased low. The sample variance S² (dividing by n − 1) has E S² = σ² exactly (proved in §12). But √ is strictly concave, so E S = E√(S²) < √(E S²) = σ. Unbiasedness does not survive a nonlinear transformation; see bias, variance and MSE.
  • The median–mean bound, again. |μ − m| = |E(X − m)| ≤ E|X − m| ≤ E|X − μ| ≤ σ, using Jensen twice and the fact (§12) that the median minimises E|X − c|. Two different routes, Cantelli in §06 and Jensen here, to the same fact.

Jensen in machine learning: E log ≤ log E

  • KL divergence is non-negative. For distributions p and q on the same outcomes, KL(p ‖ q) = E_p ln(p(X)/q(X)). Then −KL(p ‖ q) = E_p ln(q(X)/p(X)) ≤ ln E_p(q(X)/p(X)) = ln Σ p(x) · q(x)/p(x) = ln Σ q(x) ≤ ln 1 = 0. So KL ≥ 0, with equality only when q = p (Gibbs' inequality), and the cross-entropy −E_p ln q is always at least the entropy −E_p ln p: no model can score better on average than the true distribution. That is why minimising cross-entropy loss pulls predicted probabilities toward the true ones.
  • The evidence lower bound. A latent-variable model has likelihood p(x) = Σ_z p(x, z), a sum that is usually intractable. For any distribution q(z), write ln p(x) = ln E_q[p(x, Z)/q(Z)] ≥ E_q ln[p(x, Z)/q(Z)] by Jensen. The right side is the ELBO, computable by sampling from q, and the gap is exactly KL(q(z) ‖ p(z | x)). Variational autoencoders and EM both maximise this bound; see the evidence lower bound.
  • Ensembles never hurt log-loss. If an ensemble predicts the average q̄ = (q₁ + ⋯ + q_K)/K of its members' probabilities for the true class, then by convexity of −ln, −ln q̄ ≤ (1/K) Σ (−ln qₖ): the ensemble's log-loss is at most the average member's, on every single example. The same holds for squared loss, where the gap is exactly the spread of the members' predictions (the "ambiguity").
  • Log of an average versus average of logs. Perplexity is exp of the average negative log-probability, a geometric-mean quantity. Reporting the average probability instead would be the arithmetic mean, which by AM–GM is larger and flatters the model. Any metric computed on a log scale and then exponentiated differs from the metric computed on the raw scale in Jensen's direction.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

  • Statement. If E X² and E Y² are finite, then (E XY)² ≤ E X² · E Y², and in particular E|XY| ≤ √(E X² · E Y²). Equality holds exactly when one variable is a constant multiple of the other with probability 1.
  • Proof: a quadratic that cannot go negative. For every real t, 0 ≤ E(X − tY)² = E X² − 2t E XY + t² E Y². A quadratic in t that is never negative has at most one real root, so its discriminant is not positive: 4(E XY)² − 4 E X² E Y² ≤ 0. Equality means the quadratic touches zero at some t, that is, E(X − tY)² = 0, so X = tY almost surely (§04: zero variance means constant). (If E Y² = 0 then Y = 0 almost surely and both sides are 0.)
  • Correlation is bounded by 1. Apply it to the centred variables X − μ_X and Y − μ_Y: |Cov(X, Y)| ≤ σ_X σ_Y. So the correlation ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) lies in [−1, 1], with |ρ| = 1 exactly when Y is an exact linear function of X. Correlation and what it does and does not measure are developed in Joint Distributions & Dependence and, on the data side, in the correlation coefficient.
  • Other uses. With Y = 1: (E X)² ≤ E X², Jensen's first consequence again. With Y = 1_A: (E[X; A])² ≤ E X² · P(A), which bounds how much of a mean can come from a rare event. It also gave the kurtosis bound κ ≥ 1 + γ₁² in §04. Geometrically, E XY is an inner product and Cauchy–Schwarz says the cosine of the angle between two random variables is at most 1: that cosine, for centred variables, is the correlation.

08 Exponential bounds: the Chernoff method and Hoeffding's inequality

Chebyshev's bound on the deviation of an average falls like 1/n. The truth, for well-behaved variables, falls like e^(−cn): exponentially. The way to capture that is to apply Markov's inequality not to X or X² but to e^(tX), and then choose the best t. That is the Chernoff method. For a sum of independent bounded variables it gives Hoeffding's inequality, a bound that needs nothing but the range of the variables and is strong enough to support every finite-sample guarantee in learning theory.

The Chernoff method

  • The step. For any t > 0, x ↦ e^(tx) is increasing and positive, so Markov's general form gives P(X ≥ a) = P(e^(tX) ≥ e^(ta)) ≤ e^(−ta) E e^(tX) = e^(−ta) M(t). This holds for every t > 0 at once, so take the best one: P(X ≥ a) ≤ inf_(t>0) e^(−ta) M(t).
  • Why it is so much stronger. Markov uses only E X and Chebyshev only E X². The mgf contains every moment, so optimising over t is in effect choosing the best moment bound for the threshold in question. In log form, for a ≥ E X, the bound is e^(−I(a)) with I(a) = sup_t (ta − ln M(t)), the rate function, which is the Legendre transform of the cumulant generating function of §05.
  • Worked: the normal. e^(−ta) e^(t²/2) is smallest at t = a, giving P(Z ≥ a) ≤ e^(−a²/2). At a = 3: 0.0111, against the true 0.00135 and Cantelli's 1/(1 + 9) = 0.1. Chernoff loses the factor 1/(a√(2π)) of the true tail (the tail bound φ(z)/z) but captures its e^(−a²/2) decay, which no polynomial bound can. It beats Cantelli for every a > 1.585.
  • Worked: the exponential with rate 1. e^(−ta)/(1 − t) for 0 < t < 1 is smallest at t = 1 − 1/a, giving P(X ≥ a) ≤ a e^(1−a) for a > 1. At a = 8 that is 0.0073 against the true 0.00034, Cantelli's 0.020 and Markov's 0.125. But near the mean it is weaker than Cantelli: the two cross at a ≈ 6.11, which is the comparison the figure below draws.
  • Worked: the Poisson. e^(−ta) e^(μ(eᵗ − 1)) is smallest at eᵗ = a/μ, giving P(X ≥ a) ≤ e^(−μ)(eμ/a)^a for a > μ. For μ = 10, a = 20: 0.0210, against the true P(X ≥ 20) = 0.0035 and Chebyshev's 10/100 = 0.1.
  • Sums of independent variables. If Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ with the Xᵢ independent and identically distributed, M_S(t) = M(t)ⁿ (§05), so P(Sₙ ≥ na) ≤ inf_t [e^(−ta) M(t)]ⁿ = e^(−n I(a)). The bound on the sample mean being at least a > μ decays exponentially in n, at the rate I(a). This is the upper half of Cramér's large-deviations theorem: the exponent I(a) is not just a bound but the exact rate.
  • Worked: a coin. For the mean of n fair coin flips, I(a) = a ln(2a) + (1 − a) ln(2(1 − a)), the KL divergence between Bernoulli(a) and Bernoulli(½). Hoeffding's lemma and the sub-Gaussian idea

  • Chernoff's bound needs the mgf, which needs the distribution. For bounded variables there is a universal upper bound on the mgf. Hoeffding's lemma: if a ≤ Y ≤ b and E Y = 0, then E e^(tY) ≤ e^(t²(b − a)²/8) for every real t.
  • Proof sketch. On [a, b] the convex function e^(ty) lies below its chord: e^(tY) ≤ ((b − Y)/(b − a)) e^(ta) + ((Y − a)/(b − a)) e^(tb). Taking expectations with E Y = 0 gives E e^(tY) ≤ e^(L(u)) for an explicit function L of u = t(b − a) with L(0) = L′(0) = 0 and L″ ≤ ¼ everywhere (it is the variance of a Bernoulli variable, at most ¼). Taylor's theorem then gives L(u) ≤ u²/8. The extreme case is a variable sitting at a and b only: bounded variables are at their most spread out when they live at their endpoints.
  • Sub-Gaussian variables, in one paragraph. A zero-mean variable is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy s² if E e^(tY) ≤ e^(s²t²/2) for all t: its mgf is dominated by that of N(0, s²). Chernoff then gives the normal-type tail P(Y ≥ ε) ≤ e^(−ε²/(2s²)), and for independent sub-Gaussians the proxies add, because the mgfs multiply. Hoeffding's lemma says a variable confined to [a, b] is sub-Gaussian with s² = (b − a)²/4, whatever its actual distribution. Normals, bounded variables and many others are in this class; exponentials, Poissons and anything heavy-tailed are not (their mgfs grow faster), and need sub-exponential versions such as Bernstein's inequality below.

Hoeffding's inequality

  • Statement. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be independent with aᵢ ≤ Xᵢ ≤ bᵢ, and Sₙ = Σ Xᵢ. For every ε > 0, P(Sₙ − E Sₙ ≥ ε) ≤ exp(−2ε²/Σ(bᵢ − aᵢ)²), and the same bound holds for the lower tail.
  • For averages of [0, 1] variables (indicators, losses, accuracies, any score in a fixed range): P(X̄ − μ ≥ ε) ≤ e^(−2nε²) and P(|X̄ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ 2e^(−2nε²). The Xᵢ need not be identically distributed; only independence and the range are used.
  • Proof. Chernoff plus the lemma: with Yᵢ = Xᵢ − E Xᵢ, P(Σ Yᵢ ≥ ε) ≤ e^(−tε) Πᵢ E e^(tYᵢ) ≤ e^(−tε + t² Σ(bᵢ − aᵢ)²/8). The exponent is a parabola in t, minimised at t = 4ε/Σ(bᵢ − aᵢ)², where it equals −2ε²/Σ(bᵢ − aᵢ)². For the mean of n variables in [0, 1] this is t = 4nε and the bound e^(−2nε²).
  • Worked: the error rate again. n = 100 test cases, ε = 0.2: Chebyshev gave 0.0625; Hoeffding gives 2e^(−8) = 0.00067, about 93 times smaller. With n = 1,000 and ε = 0.05: 2e^(−5) = 0.0135.
  • Worked: an interval with a guarantee. Setting 2e^(−2nε²) = δ and solving, ε = √(ln(2/δ)/(2n)): the interval p̂ ± ε contains the true rate with probability at least 1 − δ, for every distribution on [0, 1]. At n = 1,000 and δ = 0.05, ε = √(ln 40/2000) = 0.0429. The normal-approximation interval for a rate near ½ is ±1.96 × 0.5/√1000 = ±0.031: narrower, but only approximate. Hoeffding's interval is wider, but valid (conservative) for every n.
  • Sample size. To guarantee ±ε with probability 1 − δ: n ≥ ln(2/δ)/(2ε²). For ε = 0.05, δ = 0.05: 738, against Chebyshev's 2,000 (§06). The dependence on δ is only logarithmic: asking for 99.9% instead of 95% multiplies n by ln 2000/ln 40 = 2.06, where Chebyshev would multiply it by 50.
  • When Hoeffding is loose. It uses the range, not the variance. For a rare event (p = 0.01) the variance p(1 − p) = 0.0099 is far below the worst case ¼, and Bernstein's inequality, P(X̄ − μ ≥ ε) ≤ exp(−nε²/(2σ² + 2bε/3)) for |Xᵢ − μ| ≤ b, is much tighter. Use Hoeffding when nothing is known but the range, Bernstein when the variance is known to be small.

09 Concentration in action: the generalisation guarantee

Probability Foundations described a generalisation guarantee as "an independence assumption, a concentration bound, and a union bound over a finite set of hypotheses". With Hoeffding's inequality in hand, all three pieces are available, and this section assembles them into a complete, numerical statement: how far a model's error on n examples can be from its true error, uniformly over every model that was considered, with a stated probability. The result is the simplest theorem of statistical learning theory, and its caveats are as instructive as its conclusion.

The setting

  • Examples (X, Y) come from an unknown distribution. A hypothesis h (a classifier, a regressor) incurs a loss ℓ(h(X), Y) taking values in [0, 1]: the 0/1 misclassification loss, or any loss clipped to that range.
  • The true risk is the expected loss, R(h) = E ℓ(h(X), Y), an expectation over the unknown distribution. The empirical risk on a sample of n examples is the average loss, R̂(h) = (1/n) Σᵢ ℓ(h(xᵢ), yᵢ). By linearity E R̂(h) = R(h): the empirical risk is an unbiased estimate of the true risk, for a hypothesis fixed before the sample was drawn.
  • A learning algorithm chooses among a finite class H = {h₁, …, h_k} of k hypotheses, all fixed before looking at the data, usually by minimising R̂ (empirical risk minimisation, ERM).

Three steps

  • 1. Independence. Assume the n examples are independent draws from the distribution that will be met in deployment (iid). Then for a fixed h, the losses ℓ(h(xᵢ), yᵢ) are independent [0, 1] variables with mean R(h).
  • 2. Concentration. Hoeffding's inequality (§08) for that one hypothesis: P(|R̂(h) − R(h)| > ε) ≤ 2e^(−2nε²).
  • 3. Union bound. The learner looks at all k hypotheses, and the chosen one is whichever looked best, so a guarantee for one fixed h is not enough. Let Bⱼ be the bad event |R̂(hⱼ) − R(hⱼ)| > ε. By the union bound of the axioms, P(B₁ ∪ ⋯ ∪ B_k) ≤ Σⱼ P(Bⱼ) ≤ 2k e^(−2nε²), whatever the dependence between the events (and the hypotheses' errors, measured on the same examples, are generally dependent).
  • Solve for ε. Setting 2k e^(−2nε²) = δ: with probability at least 1 − δ over the draw of the sample, every hypothesis in the class satisfies |R̂(h) − R(h)| ≤ ε with ε = √(ln(2k/δ)/(2n)). Uniform convergence: the empirical risks are simultaneously close to the true ones.
  • What it says about the chosen model. Let ĥ minimise R̂ and h* minimise R. On the good event, R(ĥ) ≤ R̂(ĥ) + ε ≤ R̂(h*) + ε ≤ R(h*) + 2ε. The first and last steps are the uniform bound; the middle is the definition of ERM. So the model picked by ERM is within 2ε of the best in the class, and its training error underestimates its true error by at most ε.

Honest caveats

  • The iid assumption carries all the weight. If deployment data differ from training data (a new population, a new season, a changed product), R̂ estimates the risk on the old distribution, and no amount of n fixes that. Duplicates between training and test sets, leakage of test labels into features, and time-ordered data split at random all break independence in ways the bound cannot see.
  • The class must be fixed before the data are seen. The union bound covers the k hypotheses that could have been chosen. Every time a test set is used to make another decision (a new feature, a new architecture, a new threshold), the effective k grows, and a test set reused for hundreds of adaptive decisions is no longer a clean k = 1 estimate. That is the formal reason for keeping a final test set untouched.
  • The class must be finite. A linear classifier has infinitely many settings. The finite-class argument extends to infinite classes by replacing ln k with a measure of the class's richness (its VC dimension, or its Rademacher complexity), but those tools are beyond this page. For models with far more parameters than examples the classical versions of these bounds are loose to the point of vacuity, and why such models generalise anyway is an active research question.
  • It is a worst-case bound. It holds for every distribution and every loss in [0, 1], so it is pessimistic for any particular one. It answers "can this be trusted at all?", not "how accurate is this, exactly?".
  • It says nothing about the best in the class. If every hypothesis in H is poor, ERM reliably finds a poor one. The bound controls the estimation error (variance, in the language of the bias–variance decomposition), not the approximation error of the class itself. A richer class reduces the second and, through ln k, increases the first.

10 Conditional expectation and the best predictor

If Y has been observed, the right average of X is the average over the outcomes consistent with what was seen. That is the conditional expectation E[X | Y = y]: an ordinary expectation, computed with the conditional distribution of conditional probability. Read as a function of the random Y, E[X | Y] is itself a random variable, and two facts about it carry most of applied probability: averaging it gives back E X (the tower rule), and among all functions of Y it is the one closest to X in mean squared error (the best predictor), which is what regression estimates.

E[X | Y = y], a number; E[X | Y], a random variable

  • Definition. For discrete variables, E[X | Y = y] = Σₓ x P(X = x | Y = y), where P(X = x | Y = y) = P(X = x, Y = y)/P(Y = y) for every y with P(Y = y) > 0. With densities, E[X | Y = y] = ∫ x f(x | y) dx with the conditional density f(x | y) = f(x, y)/f_Y(y). Every rule for expectations (LOTUS, linearity, the inequalities) holds for conditional expectations, because a conditional distribution is a distribution.
  • The random variable. Write h(y) = E[X | Y = y]. Then E[X | Y] means h(Y): before Y is observed it is random, and once Y = y is seen it becomes the number h(y). It is a function of Y only, a compression of everything Y says about the average of X.
  • Worked: two dice. With D₁ the first die and S the sum, E[S | D₁ = d] = d + 3.5, so E[S | D₁] = D₁ + 3.5. The other way round, the outcomes with S = 4 are (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), so E[D₁ | S = 4] = (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 2. By symmetry and linearity in general, E[D₁ | S] + E[D₂ | S] = E[S | S] = S and the two terms are equal, so E[D₁ | S] = S/2.
  • Worked: a two-stage draw. X is uniform on (0, 1); given X = x, Y is uniform on (x, 1). Then E[Y | X = x] = (1 + x)/2, the midpoint of (x, 1), so E[Y | X] = (1 + X)/2, a random variable uniform on (½, 1).

The tower rule (law of total expectation)

  • Statement. E[E[X | Y]] = E X. To average X, first average within each value of Y, then average those averages with the weights P(Y = y).
  • Proof, discrete case.
    E[E[X | Y]] = Σᵧ E[X | Y = y]·P(Y = y) = Σᵧ Σₓ x·P(X = x | Y = y)·P(Y = y) = Σₓ x·Σᵧ P(X = x, Y = y) = Σₓ x·P(X = x) = E X
    The third step swaps the order of summation (absolute convergence, from E|X| < ∞) and the fourth is the law of total probability. With X = 1_A the tower rule is the law of total probability, P(A) = Σ P(A | Y = y) P(Y = y), of Probability Foundations.
  • Worked. In the two-stage draw, E Y = E[(1 + X)/2] = (1 + ½)/2 = ¾, with no need for the density of Y (which is −ln(1 − y) on (0, 1), and gives the same ¾). Two dice: E S = E[D₁ + 3.5] = 7.
  • First-step analysis is the tower rule. The geometric mean 1/p was found in the geometric section by conditioning on the first trial: E X = p · 1 + (1 − p)(1 + E X). That equation is E X = E[E[X | first trial]] with the two conditional means written out. Every "condition on what happens first, then solve" calculation is this rule.
  • Random sums. A shop has N customers in an hour and the i-th spends Xᵢ, with the Xᵢ iid with mean μ and independent of N. Given N = n the total T = X₁ + ⋯ + X_N has mean nμ, so E[T | N] = Nμ and E T = μ E N (Wald's identity). Ten customers an hour on average, spending 20 each on average, gives 200 an hour, even though T is a sum of a random number of terms.

Rules for conditional expectations

  • Linearity: E[aX + bZ | Y] = a E[X | Y] + b E[Z | Y].
  • Taking out what is known: E[g(Y) X | Y] = g(Y) E[X | Y]. Given Y = y, g(Y) is the constant g(y), and constants come out of expectations.
  • Independence: if X and Y are independent, E[X | Y] = E X. Observing Y changes nothing about X.
  • The tower with more information: E[E[X | Y, Z] | Y] = E[X | Y]. Averaging out the finer information leaves the coarser average.
  • Orthogonality: if E X² < ∞, then for every function g with E g(Y)² < ∞, E[(X − E[X | Y]) g(Y)] = 0. Proof: by the tower rule and taking out what is known, E[X g(Y)] = E[E[X | Y] g(Y)]. The prediction error X − E[X | Y] is uncorrelated with every function of Y: it contains nothing that Y could still predict.

The conditional mean is the best predictor

  • The question. Let E Y² < ∞. Predict Y by some function g(X) of an observed X with E g(X)² < ∞, and pay the squared error. Which g minimises the mean squared error E(Y − g(X))²?
  • The answer. g(X) = E[Y | X], the regression function. Proof: write m(X) = E[Y | X] and split Y − g(X) = (Y − m(X)) + (m(X) − g(X)). Squaring and taking expectations,
    E(Y − g(X))² = E(Y − m(X))² + E(m(X) − g(X))² + 2·E[(Y − m(X))·(m(X) − g(X))]
    The cross term is zero by orthogonality, since m(X) − g(X) is a function of X. The first term does not depend on g and the second is non-negative and zero exactly when g(X) = m(X) almost surely. So m wins, and every other predictor pays exactly E(m(X) − g(X))² extra. This is §04's "the mean is the best constant" with the constant replaced by a function of X.
  • The irreducible error. The minimum is E(Y − m(X))² = E[Var(Y | X)], the average conditional variance: the noise that no function of X can remove. In machine learning it is the Bayes error for squared loss, and it is the floor under every learning curve.
  • The best line is a different question. Restricted to straight lines a + bX, the minimiser is b = Cov(X, Y)/Var X, a = E Y − b E X, the population version of least squares (the regression line, why least squares). It coincides with E[Y | X] exactly when the regression function is linear, as it is for a bivariate normal pair; otherwise the best line pays the extra E(m(X) − a − bX)², the part of the signal a line cannot follow.
  • Worked. Let X be uniform on (−1, 2) and Y = X² + ε, with ε ~ N(0, 0.5²) independent of X. The moments of X are E X = 0.5, E X² = 1, E X³ = 1.25, E X⁴ = 2.2. The regression function is m(x) = x², with mean squared error 0.25. 11 The law of total variance

The tower rule splits a mean into an average of conditional means. The law of total variance splits a variance into two parts: the average of the conditional variances (within groups) and the variance of the conditional means (between groups). Var Y = E[Var(Y | X)] + Var(E[Y | X]). It is the identity behind overdispersed counts, hierarchical models, random sums, the analysis of variance and the R² of a regression, and this section uses it to deliver the variance split of the negative binomial promised in Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.

Statement and proof

  • Conditional variance. Var(Y | X = x) is the variance of the conditional distribution of Y given X = x: E[(Y − m(x))² | X = x] with m(x) = E[Y | X = x]. As with the mean, Var(Y | X) is that function evaluated at the random X. The shortcut holds conditionally: Var(Y | X) = E[Y² | X] − m(X)².
  • Proof by the tower rule. Take expectations of the shortcut: E[Var(Y | X)] = E[E[Y² | X]] − E[m(X)²] = E Y² − E[m(X)²]. The variance of the conditional mean is Var(m(X)) = E[m(X)²] − (E m(X))² = E[m(X)²] − (E Y)², using E m(X) = E Y. Adding the two, the E[m(X)²] terms cancel and what is left is E Y² − (E Y)² = Var Y.
  • Proof by Pythagoras. Y − E Y = (Y − m(X)) + (m(X) − E Y). The second piece is a function of X, so by orthogonality (§10) it is uncorrelated with the first, and squared lengths add: E(Y − E Y)² = E(Y − m(X))² + E(m(X) − E Y)². The first term is E[Var(Y | X)] and the second is Var(m(X)). Same identity, seen as a right angle.
  • Reading it. Total uncertainty about Y = uncertainty that remains after learning X (on average) + uncertainty that learning X resolves. The fraction Var(E[Y | X])/Var Y is the share of variance explained by X, the correlation ratio η². For the worked predictor of §10, η² = 1.20/1.45 = 0.83, while the best line explains only 0.75/1.45 = 0.52: the R² of a linear fit is the share explained by the best line, which is at most η².

Delivered: why the negative binomial has variance μ + μ²/r

  • The overdispersion section explained the negative binomial's excess variance as a mixture: each unit has its own Poisson rate Λ, and the rates vary across units following a gamma distribution with shape r. Continuous Distributions did the integral showing the mixture is negative binomial. Here is the variance split.
  • The model. Λ ~ Gamma(r, β) with shape r and rate β, so E Λ = r/β = μ and Var Λ = r/β² = μ²/r. Given Λ, the count is Y | Λ ~ Poisson(Λ), so E[Y | Λ] = Λ and Var(Y | Λ) = Λ: the Poisson's mean equals its variance.
  • The mean. By the tower rule, E Y = E[E[Y | Λ]] = E Λ = μ.
  • The variance. By the law of total variance, Var Y = E[Var(Y | Λ)] + Var(E[Y | Λ]) = E Λ + Var Λ = μ + μ²/r. The first term is the Poisson noise within a unit, averaged over units; the second is the spread of the rates between units. A plain Poisson model has only the first.
  • Worked: mean 4, r = 2 (the case worked on the discrete page, with β = ½ and p = ⅓). Within-unit variance E Λ = 4, between-unit variance Var Λ = 16/2 = 8, total 12. Two-thirds of the variability in the counts is heterogeneity between units, not chance within them. As r → ∞ the between term μ²/r vanishes and the count is Poisson again; at r = 0.5 the between term is 32 and dwarfs the Poisson noise.
  • The distribution too, by the tower rule. Conditioning works for mgfs as well: E e^(tY) = E[E[e^(tY) | Λ]] = E[e^(Λ(eᵗ − 1))] = M_Λ(eᵗ − 1) = (β/(β − (eᵗ − 1)))^r, using the Poisson and gamma mgfs of §05. With p = β/(1 + β) this is (p/(1 − (1 − p)eᵗ))^r, the mgf of the failure-count negative binomial, so by uniqueness the mixture is negative binomial: the integral of Continuous Distributions, replaced by two lines of algebra.

More hierarchical models

  • Counties and a disease. Draw a region at random; its disease prevalence Q varies across regions, uniform on (0, 1) say. Sample n people from it and count the cases: X | Q ~ Bin(n, Q). Then E X = E[nQ] = n/2, and Var X = E[nQ(1 − Q)] + Var(nQ) = n/6 + n²/12. For n = 10: 1.67 + 8.33 = 10, four times the binomial variance 10 × ¼ = 2.5. (Check: with a uniform prevalence, X turns out to be uniform on {0, 1, …, 10}, whose variance is (11² − 1)/12 = 10.) The between-region part grows like n², so for large samples it dominates completely: more people per region pins down each region's prevalence but does nothing about the spread between regions.
  • Random sums. For T = X₁ + ⋯ + X_N with iid Xᵢ (mean μ, variance σ²) independent of N: Var(T | N) = Nσ² and E[T | N] = Nμ, so Var T = σ² E N + μ² Var N. Customers per hour Poisson(10), spend mean 20 and standard deviation 5: Var T = 10 × 25 + 400 × 10 = 4,250, standard deviation 65.2. With exactly 10 customers it would be √250 = 15.8: the uncertainty about how many customers dwarfs the uncertainty about how much each spends.
  • Mixtures. A mixture of distributions with weights wⱼ, means μⱼ and variances σⱼ² has variance Σ wⱼσⱼ² + Σ wⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)², with μ̄ = Σ wⱼμⱼ: within-component plus between-component. A model that ignores a latent grouping underestimates neither the mean nor the variance, but it misattributes the variance, all of it to noise.

The bridge to the analysis of variance

  • Data in k groups, yᵢⱼ for observation i in group j, N observations in all. Pick one observation at random, each with probability 1/N: let G be its group and Y its value. Then E[Y | G = j] is the group mean ȳⱼ, E Y is the grand mean ȳ, and the law of total variance, multiplied by N, reads Σ(yᵢⱼ − ȳ)² = Σ(yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ)² + Σ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ)²: SS_total = SS_within + SS_between. The decomposition that one-way ANOVA is built on (splitting the variation) is the law of total variance applied to the empirical distribution of the data.
  • Worked. Three groups: 2, 4, 6; 5, 7, 9; 8, 10, 12. Group means 4, 7, 10; grand mean 7. Within: each group's squared deviations sum to 4 + 0 + 4 = 8, so SS_within = 24. Between: 3 × (9 + 0 + 9) = 54. Total: (25 + 9 + 1) + (4 + 0 + 4) + (1 + 9 + 25) = 78 = 24 + 54. The group explains 54/78 = 69% of the variation. Dividing by N = 9 gives the probabilistic version: 8.67 = 2.67 + 6.
  • What ANOVA adds. The identity is pure algebra. The statistical question, whether the between part is larger than chance would produce if the groups had equal means, needs the degrees of freedom and the F distribution that the ANOVA page develops; this page supplies the decomposition it starts from.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Machine learning is, to a first approximation, the estimation and minimisation of expectations. The objective is an expected loss; the data give an average that estimates it; the gradient that drives training is another average; and the choice of loss decides which property of the conditional distribution the model learns. Each sentence is a result from this page.

The loss surface is an expectation

  • The quantity a model is meant to minimise is the risk R(θ) = E ℓ(f(X; θ), Y), an expectation over the unknown data distribution. It cannot be computed. What can be computed is the empirical risk R̂(θ) = (1/n) Σ ℓ(f(xᵢ; θ), yᵢ), the average over the training set, which by linearity is an unbiased estimate of R(θ) for any θ fixed in advance and, by the law of large numbers, close to it for large n. Training minimises R̂ hoping to minimise R; the objective and the surrogate treats the distinction from the optimiser's side.
  • The catch is the word "fixed". For the trained θ̂, chosen because it makes R̂ small, R̂(θ̂) is biased downward: the training error flatters the model. The size of that optimism is what the uniform bound of §09 controls for finite classes, and what a held-out set measures directly.

Which loss estimates which summary

  • Squared loss ⇒ the conditional mean. Proved in §10: E(Y − g(X))² is minimised by g(x) = E[Y | X = x]. A regression network trained with mean squared error is estimating the conditional mean, whatever the noise distribution.
  • Absolute loss ⇒ the conditional median. For a constant c, E|Y − c| = ∫_(−∞)^c P(Y ≤ t) dt + ∫_c^∞ P(Y > t) dt (the tail formula of §01 applied to each side). Differentiating, (d/dc) E|Y − c| = P(Y ≤ c) − P(Y > c) = 2F(c) − 1, which is negative below the median and positive above it, so the minimum is at any median. Applying this given each X = x, absolute loss is minimised by the conditional median. For exponential noise the gap is visible: E|E − c| for E ~ Exp(1) is 0.693 at the median ln 2 and 0.736 at the mean 1, while the squared error is 1 at the mean and 1 + (1 − ln 2)² = 1.094 at the median (the absolute-loss setting of the §10 figure).
  • Pinball loss ⇒ a conditional quantile. The loss ρ_τ(u) = u(τ − 1{u < 0}) on u = y − c has derivative P(Y ≤ c) − τ in c, so it is minimised at the τ-quantile. Quantile regression and prediction intervals from a single network use exactly this.
  • Log loss ⇒ the conditional probability. For a binary Y with P(Y = 1 | x) = p, the expected cross-entropy of a prediction q is −p ln q − (1 − p) ln(1 − q), whose derivative −p/q + (1 − p)/(1 − q) vanishes only at q = p. In general, the expected cross-entropy exceeds its minimum by KL(p ‖ q) ≥ 0 (§07). Losses with this property are proper scoring rules: they reward honest probabilities, which is why a classifier trained with log loss can be calibrated.
  • 0/1 loss ⇒ the conditional mode. The expected misclassification 1 − P(Y = ŷ | x) is minimised by predicting the most probable class: the Bayes classifier of Probability Foundations.

Minibatch gradients are Monte Carlo estimates

  • The full gradient is an expectation. Under mild conditions the derivative passes inside the expectation, as it did for the mgf: ∇R(θ) = E ∇ℓ(f(X; θ), Y). Over a training set it is the average of the per-example gradients.
  • A minibatch average is unbiased, by linearity alone. Draw B examples and average their gradients: g_B = (1/B) Σ ∇ℓᵢ. Each drawn index is marginally uniform over the training set, whether drawn with or without replacement, so each term has expectation equal to the full gradient and E g_B = ∇R̂. No independence between the draws is needed for unbiasedness; it is §03's indicator argument again.
  • Its noise falls like 1/√B. With independent draws the covariance of g_B is Σ/B, where Σ is the covariance of a single example's gradient (§04's square-root law, coordinate by coordinate). Doubling the batch halves the variance and cuts the noise by √2; sampling without replacement from N examples multiplies the variance by the finite-population factor (N − B)/(N − 1), as for the hypergeometric. That trade between batch size, noise and step size is the subject of batch size and the learning-rate coupling; the minibatch itself is introduced in gradient descent and the minibatch.
  • Concentration is why SGD works at all. If each per-example gradient coordinate lies in a range of width w, Hoeffding's inequality (§08) gives P(|g_B − ∇R̂| ≥ ε) ≤ 2e^(−2Bε²/w²) for that coordinate. A few hundred examples already pin the gradient direction down well enough to make progress: a minibatch estimate is often accurate enough that the extra precision of a full pass over the data is not worth its cost. When gradients are heavy-tailed the range assumption fails, single batches can produce enormous steps, and gradient clipping restores boundedness by force. Monte Carlo estimation, its error bars and its variance-reduction tricks are developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

Expectations hiding in familiar tricks

  • Inverted dropout. A unit's output x is kept with probability 1 − p and scaled by 1/(1 − p): E[m x/(1 − p)] = x for m ~ Bernoulli(1 − p), by linearity, so at test time the network can run without dropout and see the same expected activations (dropout).
  • Bias correction in Adam. An exponential moving average mₜ = (1 − β) Σ_(i≤t) β^(t−i) gᵢ started at zero has E mₜ = (1 − βᵗ) E g when the gradients have a stable mean: linearity and a geometric sum. Dividing by 1 − βᵗ removes the bias (moving averages and bias correction).
  • Importance weighting. By LOTUS, E_p f(X) = Σ f(x) p(x) = Σ f(x) (p(x)/q(x)) q(x) = E_q[f(X) p(X)/q(X)] whenever q > 0 where p > 0. Data from one distribution can estimate expectations under another: off-policy evaluation in reinforcement learning, correction for covariate shift, and rare-event simulation all use it. The weights can have enormous variance when q is small where p is not, and then the Chebyshev guarantee for the estimate becomes too weak to be useful.
  • The score-function gradient. ∇_θ E_(p_θ) f(X) = E_(p_θ)[f(X) ∇_θ ln p_θ(X)], by differentiating Σ f(x) p_θ(x) and writing ∇p = p ∇ln p. It is the identity behind policy gradients; the alternative for continuous variables, writing the draw as a deterministic function of noise, is the reparametrisation trick.
  • Jensen in variational inference (§07): the ELBO is a lower bound on ln p(x) because ln is concave, and the gap is a KL divergence.

Bias and variance of estimators

  • Mean squared error splits. For an estimator θ̂ of a fixed θ, apply §04's identity E(X − c)² = Var X + (E X − c)² with X = θ̂ and c = θ: MSE(θ̂) = Var θ̂ + (E θ̂ − θ)² = variance + bias². An unbiased estimator is not automatically best; trading a little bias for a large cut in variance can lower the MSE (bias, variance and MSE, when bias wins).
  • Why the sample variance divides by n − 1. Expand around the true mean: Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)² = Σ(Xᵢ − μ)² − n(X̄ − μ)². Take expectations with linearity: nσ² − n · σ²/n = (n − 1)σ². So S² = Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)²/(n − 1) has E S² = σ²; dividing by n underestimates by the factor (n − 1)/n, because the data are closer to their own mean than to the true one. (And by Jensen, §07, S itself is still biased low.)
  • The model-level decomposition. For a model f̂ trained on a random training set and evaluated at a point x where Y = m(x) + ε, the same identity applied twice gives E(Y − f̂(x))² = Var ε + (m(x) − E f̂(x))² + Var f̂(x): noise, plus squared bias, plus variance across training sets. The derivation and its consequences for model complexity are in the bias–variance decomposition.
