10 Conditional expectation and the best predictor

If Y has been observed, the right average of X is the average over the outcomes consistent with what was seen. That is the conditional expectation E[X | Y = y]: an ordinary expectation, computed with the conditional distribution of conditional probability. Read as a function of the random Y, E[X | Y] is itself a random variable, and two facts about it carry most of applied probability: averaging it gives back E X (the tower rule), and among all functions of Y it is the one closest to X in mean squared error (the best predictor), which is what regression estimates.

E[X | Y = y], a number; E[X | Y], a random variable

  • Definition. For discrete variables, E[X | Y = y] = Σₓ x P(X = x | Y = y), where P(X = x | Y = y) = P(X = x, Y = y)/P(Y = y) for every y with P(Y = y) > 0. With densities, E[X | Y = y] = ∫ x f(x | y) dx with the conditional density f(x | y) = f(x, y)/f_Y(y). Every rule for expectations (LOTUS, linearity, the inequalities) holds for conditional expectations, because a conditional distribution is a distribution.
  • The random variable. Write h(y) = E[X | Y = y]. Then E[X | Y] means h(Y): before Y is observed it is random, and once Y = y is seen it becomes the number h(y). It is a function of Y only, a compression of everything Y says about the average of X.
  • Worked: two dice. With D₁ the first die and S the sum, E[S | D₁ = d] = d + 3.5, so E[S | D₁] = D₁ + 3.5. The other way round, the outcomes with S = 4 are (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1), so E[D₁ | S = 4] = (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 2. By symmetry and linearity in general, E[D₁ | S] + E[D₂ | S] = E[S | S] = S and the two terms are equal, so E[D₁ | S] = S/2.
  • Worked: a two-stage draw. X is uniform on (0, 1); given X = x, Y is uniform on (x, 1). Then E[Y | X = x] = (1 + x)/2, the midpoint of (x, 1), so E[Y | X] = (1 + X)/2, a random variable uniform on (½, 1).

The tower rule (law of total expectation)

  • Statement. E[E[X | Y]] = E X. To average X, first average within each value of Y, then average those averages with the weights P(Y = y).
  • Proof, discrete case.
    E[E[X | Y]] = Σᵧ E[X | Y = y]·P(Y = y) = Σᵧ Σₓ x·P(X = x | Y = y)·P(Y = y) = Σₓ x·Σᵧ P(X = x, Y = y) = Σₓ x·P(X = x) = E X
    The third step swaps the order of summation (absolute convergence, from E|X| < ∞) and the fourth is the law of total probability. With X = 1_A the tower rule is the law of total probability, P(A) = Σ P(A | Y = y) P(Y = y), of Probability Foundations.
  • Worked. In the two-stage draw, E Y = E[(1 + X)/2] = (1 + ½)/2 = ¾, with no need for the density of Y (which is −ln(1 − y) on (0, 1), and gives the same ¾). Two dice: E S = E[D₁ + 3.5] = 7.
  • First-step analysis is the tower rule. The geometric mean 1/p was found in the geometric section by conditioning on the first trial: E X = p · 1 + (1 − p)(1 + E X). That equation is E X = E[E[X | first trial]] with the two conditional means written out. Every "condition on what happens first, then solve" calculation is this rule.
  • Random sums. A shop has N customers in an hour and the i-th spends Xᵢ, with the Xᵢ iid with mean μ and independent of N. Given N = n the total T = X₁ + ⋯ + X_N has mean nμ, so E[T | N] = Nμ and E T = μ E N (Wald's identity). Ten customers an hour on average, spending 20 each on average, gives 200 an hour, even though T is a sum of a random number of terms.

Rules for conditional expectations

  • Linearity: E[aX + bZ | Y] = a E[X | Y] + b E[Z | Y].
  • Taking out what is known: E[g(Y) X | Y] = g(Y) E[X | Y]. Given Y = y, g(Y) is the constant g(y), and constants come out of expectations.
  • Independence: if X and Y are independent, E[X | Y] = E X. Observing Y changes nothing about X.
  • The tower with more information: E[E[X | Y, Z] | Y] = E[X | Y]. Averaging out the finer information leaves the coarser average.
  • Orthogonality: if E X² < ∞, then for every function g with E g(Y)² < ∞, E[(X − E[X | Y]) g(Y)] = 0. Proof: by the tower rule and taking out what is known, E[X g(Y)] = E[E[X | Y] g(Y)]. The prediction error X − E[X | Y] is uncorrelated with every function of Y: it contains nothing that Y could still predict.

The conditional mean is the best predictor

  • The question. Let E Y² < ∞. Predict Y by some function g(X) of an observed X with E g(X)² < ∞, and pay the squared error. Which g minimises the mean squared error E(Y − g(X))²?
  • The answer. g(X) = E[Y | X], the regression function. Proof: write m(X) = E[Y | X] and split Y − g(X) = (Y − m(X)) + (m(X) − g(X)). Squaring and taking expectations,
    E(Y − g(X))² = E(Y − m(X))² + E(m(X) − g(X))² + 2·E[(Y − m(X))·(m(X) − g(X))]
    The cross term is zero by orthogonality, since m(X) − g(X) is a function of X. The first term does not depend on g and the second is non-negative and zero exactly when g(X) = m(X) almost surely. So m wins, and every other predictor pays exactly E(m(X) − g(X))² extra. This is §04's "the mean is the best constant" with the constant replaced by a function of X.
  • The irreducible error. The minimum is E(Y − m(X))² = E[Var(Y | X)], the average conditional variance: the noise that no function of X can remove. In machine learning it is the Bayes error for squared loss, and it is the floor under every learning curve.
  • The best line is a different question. Restricted to straight lines a + bX, the minimiser is b = Cov(X, Y)/Var X, a = E Y − b E X, the population version of least squares (the regression line, why least squares). It coincides with E[Y | X] exactly when the regression function is linear, as it is for a bivariate normal pair; otherwise the best line pays the extra E(m(X) − a − bX)², the part of the signal a line cannot follow.
  • Worked. Let X be uniform on (−1, 2) and Y = X² + ε, with ε ~ N(0, 0.5²) independent of X. The moments of X are E X = 0.5, E X² = 1, E X³ = 1.25, E X⁴ = 2.2. The regression function is m(x) = x², with mean squared error 0.25. The best line has slope Cov(X, X²)/Var X = (1.25 − 0.5)/0.75 = 1 and intercept 1 − 0.5 = 0.5, so it is 0.5 + X; its error is 0.25 + (Var X² − b² Var X) = 0.25 + (1.2 − 0.75) = 0.70. The best constant, E Y = 1, has error Var Y = 1.45. Curve, line, constant: 0.25, 0.70, 1.45.

11 The law of total variance

The tower rule splits a mean into an average of conditional means. The law of total variance splits a variance into two parts: the average of the conditional variances (within groups) and the variance of the conditional means (between groups). Var Y = E[Var(Y | X)] + Var(E[Y | X]). It is the identity behind overdispersed counts, hierarchical models, random sums, the analysis of variance and the R² of a regression, and this section uses it to deliver the variance split of the negative binomial promised in Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.

Statement and proof

  • Conditional variance. Var(Y | X = x) is the variance of the conditional distribution of Y given X = x: E[(Y − m(x))² | X = x] with m(x) = E[Y | X = x]. As with the mean, Var(Y | X) is that function evaluated at the random X. The shortcut holds conditionally: Var(Y | X) = E[Y² | X] − m(X)².
  • Proof by the tower rule. Take expectations of the shortcut: E[Var(Y | X)] = E[E[Y² | X]] − E[m(X)²] = E Y² − E[m(X)²]. The variance of the conditional mean is Var(m(X)) = E[m(X)²] − (E m(X))² = E[m(X)²] − (E Y)², using E m(X) = E Y. Adding the two, the E[m(X)²] terms cancel and what is left is E Y² − (E Y)² = Var Y.
  • Proof by Pythagoras. Y − E Y = (Y − m(X)) + (m(X) − E Y). The second piece is a function of X, so by orthogonality (§10) it is uncorrelated with the first, and squared lengths add: E(Y − E Y)² = E(Y − m(X))² + E(m(X) − E Y)². The first term is E[Var(Y | X)] and the second is Var(m(X)). Same identity, seen as a right angle.
  • Reading it. Total uncertainty about Y = uncertainty that remains after learning X (on average) + uncertainty that learning X resolves. The fraction Var(E[Y | X])/Var Y is the share of variance explained by X, the correlation ratio η². For the worked predictor of §10, η² = 1.20/1.45 = 0.83, while the best line explains only 0.75/1.45 = 0.52: the R² of a linear fit is the share explained by the best line, which is at most η².

Delivered: why the negative binomial has variance μ + μ²/r

  • The overdispersion section explained the negative binomial's excess variance as a mixture: each unit has its own Poisson rate Λ, and the rates vary across units following a gamma distribution with shape r. Continuous Distributions did the integral showing the mixture is negative binomial. Here is the variance split.
  • The model. Λ ~ Gamma(r, β) with shape r and rate β, so E Λ = r/β = μ and Var Λ = r/β² = μ²/r. Given Λ, the count is Y | Λ ~ Poisson(Λ), so E[Y | Λ] = Λ and Var(Y | Λ) = Λ: the Poisson's mean equals its variance.
  • The mean. By the tower rule, E Y = E[E[Y | Λ]] = E Λ = μ.
  • The variance. By the law of total variance, Var Y = E[Var(Y | Λ)] + Var(E[Y | Λ]) = E Λ + Var Λ = μ + μ²/r. The first term is the Poisson noise within a unit, averaged over units; the second is the spread of the rates between units. A plain Poisson model has only the first.
  • Worked: mean 4, r = 2 (the case worked on the discrete page, with β = ½ and p = ⅓). Within-unit variance E Λ = 4, between-unit variance Var Λ = 16/2 = 8, total 12. Two-thirds of the variability in the counts is heterogeneity between units, not chance within them. As r → ∞ the between term μ²/r vanishes and the count is Poisson again; at r = 0.5 the between term is 32 and dwarfs the Poisson noise.
  • The distribution too, by the tower rule. Conditioning works for mgfs as well: E e^(tY) = E[E[e^(tY) | Λ]] = E[e^(Λ(eᵗ − 1))] = M_Λ(eᵗ − 1) = (β/(β − (eᵗ − 1)))^r, using the Poisson and gamma mgfs of §05. With p = β/(1 + β) this is (p/(1 − (1 − p)eᵗ))^r, the mgf of the failure-count negative binomial, so by uniqueness the mixture is negative binomial: the integral of Continuous Distributions, replaced by two lines of algebra.

More hierarchical models

  • Counties and a disease. Draw a region at random; its disease prevalence Q varies across regions, uniform on (0, 1) say. Sample n people from it and count the cases: X | Q ~ Bin(n, Q). Then E X = E[nQ] = n/2, and Var X = E[nQ(1 − Q)] + Var(nQ) = n/6 + n²/12. For n = 10: 1.67 + 8.33 = 10, four times the binomial variance 10 × ¼ = 2.5. (Check: with a uniform prevalence, X turns out to be uniform on {0, 1, …, 10}, whose variance is (11² − 1)/12 = 10.) The between-region part grows like n², so for large samples it dominates completely: more people per region pins down each region's prevalence but does nothing about the spread between regions.
  • Random sums. For T = X₁ + ⋯ + X_N with iid Xᵢ (mean μ, variance σ²) independent of N: Var(T | N) = Nσ² and E[T | N] = Nμ, so Var T = σ² E N + μ² Var N. Customers per hour Poisson(10), spend mean 20 and standard deviation 5: Var T = 10 × 25 + 400 × 10 = 4,250, standard deviation 65.2. With exactly 10 customers it would be √250 = 15.8: the uncertainty about how many customers dwarfs the uncertainty about how much each spends.
  • Mixtures. A mixture of distributions with weights wⱼ, means μⱼ and variances σⱼ² has variance Σ wⱼσⱼ² + Σ wⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)², with μ̄ = Σ wⱼμⱼ: within-component plus between-component. A model that ignores a latent grouping underestimates neither the mean nor the variance, but it misattributes the variance, all of it to noise.

The bridge to the analysis of variance

  • Data in k groups, yᵢⱼ for observation i in group j, N observations in all. Pick one observation at random, each with probability 1/N: let G be its group and Y its value. Then E[Y | G = j] is the group mean ȳⱼ, E Y is the grand mean ȳ, and the law of total variance, multiplied by N, reads Σ(yᵢⱼ − ȳ)² = Σ(yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ)² + Σ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ)²: SS_total = SS_within + SS_between. The decomposition that one-way ANOVA is built on (splitting the variation) is the law of total variance applied to the empirical distribution of the data.
  • Worked. Three groups: 2, 4, 6; 5, 7, 9; 8, 10, 12. Group means 4, 7, 10; grand mean 7. Within: each group's squared deviations sum to 4 + 0 + 4 = 8, so SS_within = 24. Between: 3 × (9 + 0 + 9) = 54. Total: (25 + 9 + 1) + (4 + 0 + 4) + (1 + 9 + 25) = 78 = 24 + 54. The group explains 54/78 = 69% of the variation. Dividing by N = 9 gives the probabilistic version: 8.67 = 2.67 + 6.
  • What ANOVA adds. The identity is pure algebra. The statistical question, whether the between part is larger than chance would produce if the groups had equal means, needs the degrees of freedom and the F distribution that the ANOVA page develops; this page supplies the decomposition it starts from.
