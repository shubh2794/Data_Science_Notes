Probability Foundations

The rules for reasoning about chance: describe the experiment as a sample space, name the things you care about as events, and assign them numbers that obey three axioms. Everything else on this page follows from those three rules: the complement and union rules, counting, conditional probability, the law of total probability, Bayes' rule and independence. Together they cover almost every probability calculation you will ever do, and nearly every classifier, filter and diagnostic test in machine learning is one of them running at scale.

01 What probability is

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 attached to a statement about an uncertain outcome: 0 means "cannot happen", 1 means "certain", and everything in between is graded. The mathematics is the same however you interpret the number. The interpretation decides what the number means and which questions you are allowed to ask.

Chance experiments

  • A chance experiment (also called a random experiment) is any process whose outcome is not determined in advance but whose set of possible outcomes is known. Tossing a coin, rolling a die, the next customer's basket, tomorrow's server latency, whether a new email is spam, the label a model assigns to an image: each of these is an experiment in this sense.
  • "Not determined in advance" is about what you know, not about physics. A coin toss follows Newton's laws: given the exact launch velocity, spin and height, the outcome is computable. We call it random because those initial conditions are unknown and the outcome is extremely sensitive to them. Probability is the calculus for situations where the relevant information is missing, whatever the reason.
  • An experiment must be specified precisely enough that every run ends in exactly one of the listed outcomes. "Toss a coin" is ambiguous until you say whether an edge landing counts and whether you record the order of two tosses. Most paradoxes in elementary probability are experiments that were never fully specified (§11 has three of them).

Interpretation 1: long-run relative frequency

  • Repeat the experiment n times under the same conditions and let n_A be the number of runs in which the event A happened. The relative frequency n_A / n wanders when n is small and settles down as n grows. The frequentist reading defines P(A) as the value it settles to.
  • The canonical demonstration: a mathematician interned during the Second World War tossed a coin 10,000 times and recorded 5,067 heads, a relative frequency of 0.5067. That is 67 more heads than "half", and 134 more heads than tails. The section's figure shows that this is what convergence looks like.
  • Proportions converge; counts do not. If heads has probability ½, the gap #heads − #tails does not shrink toward 0. Its typical size grows like √n (about 80 after 10,000 fair tosses; the record above shows 134, an ordinary value). The proportion still converges, because a gap of size √n divided by n goes to zero like 1/√n. Confusing these two statements is the gambler's fallacy (§11).
  • The frequency reading has two limits. It needs an experiment that can be repeated under stable conditions, and it is a statement about a limit you can never observe. "The probability that this particular bridge collapses this decade" or "the probability that a specific historical claim is true" have no natural repetition.

Interpretation 2: degree of belief

  • The subjective (or Bayesian) reading treats P(A) as a degree of belief: how strongly a particular reasoner, holding particular information, expects A to be true. "There is a 30% chance my friend calls today" is a statement of this kind. No repetition is implied.
  • Two people with different information can rationally hold different probabilities for the same event, so probability is relative to an information state. It is still not arbitrary. Beliefs must obey the same axioms (§03), or the believer can be offered a set of bets each of which looks fair to them but which together lose money whatever happens (a Dutch book). That coherence argument is why degrees of belief must add up the way frequencies do.
  • Beliefs move with evidence by a fixed rule: Bayes' rule (§08) turns a prior belief plus data into a posterior belief. Over many observations, reasoners who started with different priors are driven toward the same answer, provided neither prior ruled the truth out in advance. This is the bridge between the two readings.

  • The point to keep: the calculus is the same. The axioms, conditioning, Bayes' rule and independence on this page are valid under every interpretation. Interpretation only starts to matter when you go from data back to a model, which is the subject of the Statistics series.

Why machine learning is built on this

  • A classifier's output is a conditional probability. A calibrated model that says "0.83 spam" is claiming P(spam | features) = 0.83. Logistic regression, softmax outputs and naive Bayes are all estimating such a quantity (§06, §12).
  • A loss function is a log-probability. Cross-entropy is the negative log-probability the model assigned to the observed label, and squared error is the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian noise. Training means making the observed data probable.
  • Evaluation is conditional probability under other names. Precision is P(actually positive | flagged), recall is P(flagged | actually positive), and they differ by exactly the Bayes'-rule inversion of §08. See model evaluation.
  • Generalisation is a probability statement. "The test error estimates the error on new data" assumes training and test examples are draws from the same distribution, independent of each other (§09). When that assumption fails (leakage, distribution shift), the guarantee goes with it.
The working definition for the rest of this page. A probability model is a triple: a set of outcomes, a collection of events, and a function assigning each event a number, subject to three axioms. Every claim below is a theorem about that triple, true whatever the numbers "mean".

02 Sample spaces and events

Before you can assign probabilities you need a list of what can happen. The sample space is that list; an event is any question about the outcome that has a yes/no answer, written as the set of outcomes for which the answer is yes. From this point on, logic becomes set algebra.

Outcomes and the sample space

  • The sample space Ω is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment. Each element ω ∈ Ω is one complete, indivisible result. Exactly one outcome occurs per run.
  • Examples, from small to large:
    • One coin: Ω = {H, T}.
    • One die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
    • The month of a stranger's birthday: Ω = {Jan, Feb, …, Dec}, twelve outcomes.
    • The order in which three envelopes, numbered 1, 2, 3, lie stacked on a doormat, read from the top down (so 213 means envelope 2 on top, then 1, then 3): Ω = {123, 132, 213, 231, 312, 321}, the 3! = 6 permutations.
    • Number of emails until the first spam: Ω = {1, 2, 3, …}, countably infinite.
    • A latency in milliseconds, or the load at which a beam fails: Ω = (0, ∞), uncountable.
  • Choosing Ω is a modelling decision. A sample space can be bigger than reality requires (latency in (0, ∞) although no request takes a year) and that is harmless: the extra outcomes simply get probability zero. It must never be too coarse, though. If the event you care about cannot be written as a set of outcomes, the sample space is wrong. Recording only "number of heads in two tosses", {0, 1, 2}, makes "the first toss was heads" impossible to express.
  • A good sample space also makes the probabilities easy. For two coins, {HH, HT, TH, TT} has four equally likely outcomes; {0 heads, 1 head, 2 heads} is a valid sample space whose outcomes are not equally likely (¼, ½, ¼). Treating the second as uniform is the oldest mistake in the subject (§04).

Events are subsets

  • An event is a subset A ⊂ Ω. We say A occurs if the realised outcome lies in A. "The die shows an even number" is A = {2, 4, 6}; "the birthday falls in a 31-day month" is L = {Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec}.
  • Two events are always available: Ω itself (the sure event, which always occurs) and ∅ (the impossible event, which never does). A one-outcome set {ω} is an elementary event. Strictly, probabilities attach to events, so you write P({H}), though everyone writes P(H).
  • Every logical connective becomes a set operation, and every set operation is a statement about the outcome.

De Morgan's laws

  • "It is not true that neither the cache nor the database failed" means "the cache or the database (or both) failed". Double negations like this are where verbal reasoning goes wrong, and De Morgan's laws do the bookkeeping: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and vice versa.
  • Proof of the first, by chasing an outcome: ω ∈ (A ∪ B)ᶜ ⇔ ω is in neither A nor B ⇔ ω ∈ Aᶜ and ω ∈ Bᶜ ⇔ ω ∈ Aᶜ ∩ Bᶜ. The second follows by applying the first to Aᶜ and Bᶜ and complementing.
  • Their main use in probability is converting "at least one" (a union, awkward) into "none" (an intersection, often a product). That conversion is the complement move in §03 and §06.

Products of sample spaces: repeating an experiment

  • Running two experiments, or one experiment twice, gives the product space Ω₁ × Ω₂, the set of ordered pairs. Two coins: {H,T} × {H,T} = {(H,H), (H,T), (T,H), (T,T)}. Two dice: 36 ordered pairs. If Ω₁ has r outcomes and Ω₂ has s, the product has r·s, which is the multiplication principle of §05.
  • n repetitions give Ω = Ω₁ × … × Ωₙ. For binary trials that is {0,1}ⁿ, with 2ⁿ sequences, and the event "exactly one success in five trials" is the five-element set {00001, 00010, 00100, 01000, 10000}.
  • The product space says nothing yet about probabilities. The usual assignment multiplies, P((ω₁, ω₂)) = P(ω₁)·P(ω₂). That is a modelling choice, and it means the runs do not influence each other. Its formal name is independence (§09). Other assignments are perfectly legal: "the second die always copies the first" puts probability ⅙ on each of the six diagonal pairs and 0 elsewhere, and is still a valid probability model on the same 36-point space.

An infinite sample space: toss until the first head

  • Toss a coin with P(H) = p until heads appears and record how many tosses that took. The outcomes are Ω = {1, 2, 3, …}. The outcome n means n − 1 tails followed by a head, so with independent tosses P(n) = (1 − p)ⁿ⁻¹ p.
  • With infinitely many outcomes, "probabilities add up to one" becomes a statement about an infinite series, and finite additivity is no longer enough. The countable version of the third axiom (§03) is what makes P(Ω) equal the infinite series Σₙ P(n), and the geometric series shows that series equals 1.
  • That one sample space answers several natural questions. The probability the first head comes on an odd toss, with a fair coin, is ½ + ⅛ + 1/32 + … = 2/3: the player who tosses first in an alternating game wins two times in three. There is also the event "heads never appears", the all-tails infinite sequence. It is not an outcome in {1, 2, 3, …} at all; in the richer space of infinite sequences it is a legitimate event with probability lim (1 − p)ⁿ = 0 (for any p > 0). Possible, in that the sequence exists, and of probability zero.

Uncountable spaces need one more idea. For Ω = (0, ∞) you cannot list outcomes and add their probabilities: each single latency value has probability 0, and summing zeros never gives 1. Probability has to be assigned to intervals through a density. For technical reasons, not every subset of the real line can be given a consistent probability, so the collection of events is restricted to a well-behaved family (a σ-field) that contains every set you will ever meet in practice. 03 The three axioms, and everything that follows

A probability is any assignment of numbers to events that obeys three rules: non-negativity, total mass one, and additivity over disjoint events. Nothing else is assumed. The complement rule, the union rule, the union bound and inclusion–exclusion are all theorems, each a line or two of algebra away from the axioms.

The axioms

  • A probability measure on a sample space Ω is a function P from events to real numbers satisfying:
    1. Non-negativity. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A.
    2. Normalisation. P(Ω) = 1. Something in the list happens.
    3. Countable additivity. If A₁, A₂, A₃, … are pairwise disjoint, then P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ …) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …
  • Think of probability as a fixed amount of mass, one unit, spread over Ω. An event's probability is the mass sitting on it. Mass is never negative, there is exactly one unit of it, and the mass of a region made of non-overlapping pieces is the sum of the pieces. Every rule below is a statement about how that mass is distributed, which is why Venn diagrams with area as probability are a faithful picture.
  • Why "countable" and not just "finite". Finite additivity (for two, three or any finite number of disjoint events) cannot handle the toss-until-heads space of §02, where the total is an infinite sum, or limits such as "the probability of heads never appearing". Countable additivity is the minimum needed for limits to behave, and it buys continuity: if events grow, A₁ ⊂ A₂ ⊂ …, then P(Aₙ) → P(∪Aₙ).
  • On a finite or countable Ω, the axioms reduce to one recipe: give each outcome a weight pᵢ ≥ 0 with Σpᵢ = 1, and set P(A) = Σ_(ωᵢ ∈ A) pᵢ. Every such assignment is a valid probability, and, taking every subset as an event, every valid probability is of this form. Whether a particular assignment is the right one is a modelling question that the axioms cannot answer.

Consequences, each with its proof

  • The empty set has probability zero. Take A₁ = Ω and A₂ = A₃ = … = ∅. These are pairwise disjoint and their union is Ω, so axiom 3 gives P(Ω) = P(Ω) + P(∅) + P(∅) + …. A finite number can only equal itself plus an infinite sum of copies of a non-negative constant if that constant is 0.
  • Finite additivity. Pad any finite disjoint list with infinitely many copies of ∅ and apply axiom 3, using P(∅) = 0.
  • Complement rule. A and Aᶜ are disjoint with union Ω, so P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = 1.
  • Bounded by one. P(A) = 1 − P(Aᶜ) ≤ 1, since P(Aᶜ) ≥ 0. The axioms never had to say this explicitly.
  • Difference rule and monotonicity. If A ⊂ B, split B = A ∪ (B \ A), disjointly. So P(B) = P(A) + P(B \ A), which gives P(B \ A) = P(B) − P(A) ≥ 0 and hence P(A) ≤ P(B). A smaller event cannot be more probable. (The conjunction fallacy, where people judge "a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement" more likely than "a bank teller", is a violation of exactly this rule.)
  • General difference. Without containment, P(A \ B) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B), from the disjoint split A = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A \ B).
  • Union of two events (inclusion–exclusion, n = 2). Split the union into three disjoint pieces, A ∪ B = (A \ B) ∪ (A ∩ B) ∪ (B \ A). Then P(A ∪ B) = [P(A) − P(A∩B)] + P(A∩B) + [P(B) − P(A∩B)] = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice, so it is subtracted once.
  • Union bound (Boole's inequality). Since P(A ∩ B) ≥ 0, P(A ∪ B) ≤ P(A) + P(B). By induction P(A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ) ≤ ΣP(Aᵢ) for finitely many events, and letting n → ∞ (continuity of probability, which follows from countable additivity) extends it to any countable collection, with no assumption about how the events overlap. It is crude and it always holds. That is why it underpins multiple-testing corrections (the Bonferroni correction is this inequality) and the simplest generalisation bounds in learning theory, where the bad events are "this particular hypothesis looks good on the training set but is not".
  • Lower bound (Bonferroni). P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1. Two events that each have probability 0.9 must overlap in at least 0.8.

Inclusion–exclusion for three events, and in general

  • For three events, adding the three probabilities counts every pairwise overlap twice, so you subtract the three pairwise intersections. That removes the triple overlap three times after adding it three times, leaving it counted zero times, so you add it back once. Each region of the Venn diagram ends up counted exactly once.
  • Check by counting a single outcome. Suppose ω lies in exactly m ≥ 1 of the events. It is counted C(m,1) times in the singles, C(m,2) in the pairs, C(m,3) in the triples, and so on, giving a net count of C(m,1) − C(m,2) + C(m,3) − … = 1 − (1 − 1)ᵐ = 1 by the binomial theorem. So every outcome in the union is counted exactly once, which is the whole proof for any number of events.
  • Truncating the alternating sum gives bounds that alternate in direction (the Bonferroni inequalities): stopping after the singles over-counts, stopping after the pairs under-counts, and so on.

A classic application: nobody gets their own hat

  • n people throw their hats in a pile and each takes one at random. What is the probability that at least one person gets their own hat back? Let Aᵢ = "person i gets their own hat". The axioms are not a definition of chance. They say nothing about which events are likely. They only say how probabilities must fit together. Any assignment that obeys them, sensible or absurd, is a probability. That is the strength of the approach: the rules are fixed, and all the modelling goes into choosing the numbers, which is where the next section starts.

04 Equally likely outcomes

When symmetry gives you no reason to prefer one outcome over another, the natural model gives every outcome the same weight. Probability then reduces to counting: favourable outcomes over total outcomes. This is the oldest and most useful special case. It is also an assumption about the world, and it is often false.

Classical probability

  • If Ω is finite with N outcomes and all are equally likely, normalisation forces each to have probability 1/N. Additivity then gives P(A) = |A| / N, where |A| is the number of outcomes in A. This is called the uniform distribution on Ω.
  • All the difficulty moves into counting |A| and |Ω|, which can be very hard for large spaces: card hands, passwords, seatings, lottery draws. §05 supplies the tools.
  • Two dice: Ω has 36 ordered pairs. "Sum is 7" = {(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)}, so P = 6/36 = 1/6. "Sum is 11" = {(5,6), (6,5)}, P = 2/36. "Sum is 12" = {(6,6)}, P = 1/36. Sums are not equally likely even though the pairs are. Uniformity on one sample space does not carry over to a coarser description of it.

When "equally likely" fails

  • The wrong sample space. "Two children: 0, 1 or 2 girls, so P(two girls) = 1/3." The three family types are not equally likely, because "one of each" arises two ways (GB, BG). On the ordered space {GG, GB, BG, BB} the answer is ¼, and "one of each" has probability ½. The same error with coins has a long history: {0, 1, 2} heads is a legitimate sample space, but not a uniform one.
  • The world is not symmetric. About 48.8% of newborns are girls, not 50%. Months have 28 to 31 days, so P(born in January) ≈ 31/365 rather than 1/12, and accounting for leap years in a four-year cycle of 1,461 days gives 124/1461 for each 31-day month and 113/1461 for February. Birthdays also cluster seasonally. Real dice have hollowed pips and slightly uneven faces. Each of these is a small departure, and each one matters if you multiply it across enough trials.
  • "Equally likely" depends on the description. Pick a square "at random" with side length between 0 and 1 cm. If the side is uniform, P(side < ½) = ½. If instead the area is uniform on [0, 1], then "side < ½" is "area < ¼", with probability ¼. Same words, two answers. The principle of indifference cannot choose between them, and the experiment has to say how the randomness enters.
  • Human "randomness" is not uniform. Ask people for a random number from 1 to 10 and 7 comes up far more often than one time in ten. Passwords, PINs and lottery picks chosen by people cluster on birthdays and patterns, and attackers exploit that.

Where uniformity is real: because someone built it

  • Uniformity is reliable when a randomisation device was deliberately used: a well-shuffled deck, a lottery machine, a cryptographic random generator, random assignment in an experiment, simple random sampling from a list. In those cases the uniform model describes the process.
  • That is why the design of experiments and surveys insists on randomisation: it is the only way to make the equally-likely model true by construction rather than by hope. The same idea underlies shuffling a dataset before splitting it into training and test sets.
A habit worth forming. Before dividing two counts, ask: is every outcome I am counting equally likely, and why? If the answer is "symmetry of a physical device" or "it was randomised", go ahead. If the answer is "I could not think of a reason for them to differ", you have an assumption, not a fact, and it should be stated.

05 Counting: the engine behind equally likely outcomes

Once outcomes are equally likely, every probability is a ratio of two counts, and sample spaces get large very quickly. A five-card hand has about 2.6 million possibilities and an 8-character password about 218 trillion. Combinatorics is the set of shortcuts that count these sets without listing them. One principle, multiplication, generates all of it.

The multiplication principle

  • If a procedure has k stages, stage 1 can be done in n₁ ways, and for each way of doing the earlier stages stage j can be done in nⱼ ways, then the whole procedure can be done in n₁ · n₂ · … · nₖ ways. Picture a tree: n₁ branches, each splitting into n₂, and so on. The leaves are the outcomes.
  • The condition is that the number of choices at each stage is fixed. The choices themselves can depend on earlier stages. In "choose a president, then a different vice-president, from 10 people" the vice-president options change with the president, but there are always 9 of them, so the count is 10 · 9 = 90.
  • This is the product-space count of §02: |Ω₁ × Ω₂| = |Ω₁|·|Ω₂|.

The four basic counts

  • Almost every counting problem is "choose k items from n", classified by two questions. Does order matter? (Is (a, b) a different outcome from (b, a)?) Is replacement allowed? (Can an item be chosen twice?)

  • Ordered, without replacement: n, then n − 1, …, then n − k + 1 choices. This is the falling factorial n!/(n − k)!, the number of permutations of k out of n. With k = n it is n!, the number of orderings of all n items (and 0! = 1, since there is one way to arrange nothing).
  • Unordered, without replacement: each unordered set of k distinct items appears exactly k! times among the ordered selections, so divide: C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!), read "n choose k". These are combinations: committees, card hands, lottery tickets, random samples.

Warning: these multisets are not equally likely when items are drawn at random (rolling two dice gives 21 multisets, but {3, 4} has probability 2/36 and {3, 3} only 1/36). Binomial coefficients and Pascal's triangle

  • Symmetry: C(n, k) = C(n, n − k). Choosing the k who are in is the same as choosing the n − k who are out.
  • Pascal's rule: C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k). Proof by a story: fix one particular item. A k-subset either contains it (choose the other k − 1 from the remaining n − 1) or does not (choose all k from the remaining n − 1). These two cases are disjoint and cover everything. This is exactly how each entry of Pascal's triangle is the sum of the two above it.
  • Row sums: Σₖ C(n, k) = 2ⁿ. Both sides count all subsets of an n-set: the right side decides in or out for each item, the left side groups subsets by size.
  • Binomial theorem: (x + y)ⁿ = Σₖ C(n, k) xᵏ yⁿ⁻ᵏ. Expanding the product, each term picks x from k of the n factors. Put x = p, y = 1 − p and the binomial probabilities of §02 sum to 1ⁿ = 1. The same identity proved inclusion–exclusion in §03.
  • Vandermonde: C(m + n, k) = Σⱼ C(m, j) C(n, k − j). Choose k from a group of m men and n women, and split by how many men were chosen. Check: C(10, 4) = 210 = Σⱼ C(5, j)C(5, 4 − j). This is the identity behind the hypergeometric distribution in Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.

Multinomial coefficients: splitting into more than two groups

  • C(n, k) splits n items into two labelled groups of sizes k and n − k. To split into r labelled groups of sizes n₁, …, nᵣ (with Σnᵢ = n), choose the first group, then the second from what is left, and so on. The product telescopes to the multinomial coefficient.
  • Equivalent story: the number of distinct arrangements of a word with repeated letters. All n! orderings, divided by the nᵢ! rearrangements of each repeated letter that leave the word unchanged.
  • A subtlety: if the groups are unlabelled and equal-sized, you over-count by the number of ways to permute the groups. Splitting 4 people into two teams of 2 is C(4,2)/2! = 3, not 6, because "team 1 = {a,b}, team 2 = {c,d}" and the reverse are the same split. Most counting mistakes are of this kind: the numerator and denominator were counted under different conventions about order.

The birthday problem, fully worked

  • Question. In a group of n people, what is the probability that at least two share a birthday? Model: 365 equally likely days (ignore 29 February and seasonality; both effects are small and the second makes matches slightly more likely), and birthdays independent.
  • Complement first. "At least one shared" is a messy union over all pairs. Its complement, "all different", is a clean ordered count. The sample space of birthday sequences has 365ⁿ equally likely points. All-distinct sequences are ordered selections without replacement: 365 · 364 · … · (365 − n + 1).
  • The same product, by the multiplication rule of §06. Person 2 avoids person 1's day with probability 364/365. Given that, person 3 avoids both with probability 363/365, and so on.
  • The approximation. Take logs and use ln(1 − x) ≈ −x for small x: ln P(all distinct) ≈ −(1 + 2 + … + (n−1))/365 = −n(n−1)/730. This also explains the surprise. What matters is the number of pairs, C(n, 2) = n(n−1)/2, and each pair matches with probability 1/365. 23 people make C(23, 2) = 253 pairs, so about 253/365 ≈ 0.69 expected matching pairs, and e^(−0.69) ≈ ½.
  • The general rule (the birthday bound). With N equally likely values, collisions become likely at about √N draws. The 50% point is n ≈ 1.18 √N, from n²/(2N) = ln 2. For N = 365 that predicts 22.5, and the exact answer is 23.

06 Conditional probability

Information shrinks the sample space. Once you learn that B happened, every outcome outside B is ruled out, and the probability mass left inside B is scaled up so that it totals 1 again. That rescaling is the whole definition of conditional probability. Most of the rest of probability theory consists of applying it carefully.

The definition, as renormalisation

  • For an event B with P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Read it as: of the probability mass sitting on B, what fraction also sits on A?
  • Two steps. (1) Restrict: outcomes outside B are impossible now, so the only part of A that matters is A ∩ B. (2) Renormalise: the surviving mass is P(B) rather than 1, so divide by it. Relative odds inside B are unchanged. Two outcomes in B that were equally likely before are still equally likely after.
  • Frequency reading. Among many repetitions, keep only those in which B occurred. P(A | B) is the long-run fraction of those kept runs in which A also occurred: n_(A∩B) / n_B = (n_(A∩B)/n) / (n_B/n).
  • Worked, with months. A stranger's birth month is uniform over 12. L = 31-day months = {Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec}. R = months with an "r" = {Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec}. Told the month is long, 7 possibilities remain, and 4 of them (Jan, Mar, Oct, Dec) contain an r. So P(R | L) = 4/7 ≈ 0.571, against an unconditional P(R) = 8/12 = 0.667, and against P(R ∩ L) = 4/12 = 1/3, which is a different question.
  • Worked, with a die. Told the roll is even, P(6 | even) = (1/6)/(1/2) = 1/3, and P(5 | even) = 0.

A conditional probability is a probability

  • Fix B. The function Q(A) = P(A | B) satisfies all three axioms: Q(A) ≥ 0; Q(Ω) = P(B)/P(B) = 1; and for disjoint Aᵢ the sets Aᵢ ∩ B are disjoint too, so additivity carries over. Every theorem of §03 therefore holds to the left of the bar: P(Aᶜ | B) = 1 − P(A | B), P(A ∪ C | B) = P(A | B) + P(C | B) − P(A ∩ C | B), and so on.
  • Nothing of the kind holds to the right of the bar. In general P(A | B) + P(A | Bᶜ) ≠ 1, and P(A | B ∪ C) is not P(A | B) + P(A | C). The conditioning event is the new sample space, not something you add up.
  • The bar is not symmetric. P(spots | measles) is close to 1, while P(measles | spots) is small, because many things cause spots. The two are linked only through Bayes' rule (§08). Confusing them is called confusion of the inverse (or the transposed conditional), and its best-known instance is the prosecutor's fallacy of §08. It is probably the most consequential error in applied probability.

The multiplication (chain) rule

  • Multiply the definition through by P(B): P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) · P(B). A joint probability is built as "how likely is the first thing, times how likely is the second given the first". This is often far easier than computing the intersection directly.
  • Applied repeatedly, it gives the chain rule: P(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ) = P(A₁) · P(A₂ | A₁) · P(A₃ | A₁ ∩ A₂) ⋯ P(Aₙ | A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ₋₁). It holds for every ordering of the events.
  • Choose the order that makes the factors easy. For birthdays, "person n avoids the first n − 1 birthdays, given those are distinct" is 1 − (n−1)/365, which is immediate. The reverse, "the first n − 1 are distinct given person n avoided them all", is correct but much harder to evaluate. If conditioning does not make the pieces simpler, condition the other way round.
  • The chain rule is how sequence models assign probability. A language model scores a sentence as P(w₁)·P(w₂ | w₁)·P(w₃ | w₁, w₂)⋯, which is exactly this identity, with each factor predicted by a network. It is exact and needs no independence assumption. The modelling happens in estimating the factors.

The two basic moves

  • Almost every hand calculation is a composition of two moves. Complement: if "at least one" is hard, compute "none" and subtract from 1. Condition: if an event is hard, split it into cases where it is easy (§07), or build it up one stage at a time (the chain rule).
  • Worked: at least one six in three rolls. Directly, "six on roll 1 or roll 2 or roll 3" is a union of overlapping events. 07 The law of total probability

When an event is hard to assess directly but easy to assess case by case, split the world into cases, work out the event's probability within each case, and average the answers, weighted by how likely each case is. That is the law of total probability: conditioning in the "divide and conquer" direction.

Statement and proof

  • Let B₁, …, Bₘ be a partition of Ω: pairwise disjoint, together covering everything, each with positive probability. Then for any event A, P(A) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ).
  • Proof. A = (A ∩ B₁) ∪ … ∪ (A ∩ Bₘ), and these pieces are disjoint because the Bᵢ are. Additivity gives P(A) = Σ P(A ∩ Bᵢ), and the multiplication rule rewrites each term as P(A | Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ). The same works for a countably infinite partition.
  • It is a weighted average. The weights P(Bᵢ) are non-negative and sum to 1, so P(A) lies between the smallest and the largest of the P(A | Bᵢ), closer to the cases that are more common. The overall defect rate of a factory cannot be worse than its worst machine or better than its best.
  • The simplest partition is {B, Bᶜ}: P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | Bᶜ)(1 − P(B)). On a tree, this is "add up the leaves where A happened".
  • It holds conditionally too: P(A | C) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ ∩ C) P(Bᵢ | C). Note that the weights become P(Bᵢ | C); forgetting to update the weights is how Simpson's paradox arises (a trend that holds within every group can reverse in the aggregate, because the groups are weighted differently). See Simpson's paradox.

Worked backwards: asking a sensitive question privately

  • To estimate how many students have cheated on an exam without anyone admitting anything, use a randomised response survey. Each student privately tosses a coin.   • In one survey 27 of 57 students answered yes, so P̂(yes) = 27/57 = 0.474 and π̂ = (0.474 − 0.25)/0.5 = 0.447. This trade-off between privacy and accuracy is the core idea of differential privacy, which formalises randomised response and is used by large-scale telemetry systems. The "at most a factor of 3" bound is exactly its privacy parameter: ε = ln 3 ≈ 1.1.

08 Bayes' rule

The data usually come as P(evidence | cause): how often a test fires on sick patients, how often a word appears in spam. The question is usually P(cause | evidence): is this patient sick, is this email spam? Bayes' rule converts one into the other. It is one line of algebra, and the number it produces usually surprises people.

Derivation

  • The multiplication rule can be written two ways: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) = P(B | A)P(A). Divide by P(A). That is all of Bayes' rule.
  • Usually P(A) is not given directly. Expand it by total probability over the possible causes, and the denominator becomes the sum of the numerators over every cause. The posterior probabilities of the causes therefore always sum to 1.
  • Vocabulary, for causes Hᵢ (hypotheses) and evidence E:
    • Prior P(Hᵢ): how plausible the cause was before the evidence.
    • Likelihood P(E | Hᵢ): how well that cause predicts the evidence.
    • Evidence or marginal likelihood P(E) = Σⱼ P(E | Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ): the normaliser.
    • Posterior P(Hᵢ | E): the plausibility after the evidence.
  • In words: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. A cause ends up probable if it explains the evidence well and was plausible to begin with. Neither factor alone decides.

The diagnostic test, worked in counts

  • A disease has prevalence 1%. The test's sensitivity (true-positive rate) P(+ | D) = 0.90, and its specificity (true-negative rate) P(− | Dᶜ) = 0.91, so the false-positive rate is P(+ | Dᶜ) = 0.09. A patient tests positive. What is the chance they have the disease?
  • Think in people, not percentages. Picture 10,000 people. 100 have the disease: 90 test positive (true positives) and 10 negative (false negatives). 9,900 do not: 9% of them, 891 people, test positive (false positives) and 9,009 negative (true negatives).
  • There are 90 + 891 = 981 positives, and 90 of them are sick. P(D | +) = 90/981 = 0.0917, about 9.2%. A positive result from a "90% accurate" test means the patient is still about ten times more likely to be healthy than sick.
  • Why. The test is good, but the healthy population is 99 times larger than the sick one. A 9% false-positive rate applied to 9,900 people produces ten times as many false alarms as the 90% true-positive rate produces from 100. The prior (prevalence) dominates.
  • The negative result is very informative. P(Dᶜ | −) = 9,009/9,019 = 0.9989. A rare-disease test is good at ruling out and bad at ruling in, which is exactly why screening programmes confirm positives with a second, different test.
  • Prevalence changes everything. The same test gives PPV = 1.0% at prevalence 0.1%, 9.2% at 1%, 52.6% at 10%, and 90.9% at 50%. A test does not have one "chance of being right"; it has a sensitivity and a specificity, and the PPV also depends on who is being tested. To reach PPV = ½ at 1% prevalence with this sensitivity, the specificity would have to be 99.1%.

The odds form, and the Bayes factor

  • Odds are often a more natural scale than probability: odds(H) = P(H)/P(Hᶜ), and p = odds/(1 + odds). Write Bayes' rule for H and for Hᶜ and divide. The awkward denominator P(E) cancels, leaving a single multiplication.
  • Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. The likelihood ratio, also called the Bayes factor when H and Hᶜ are competing models, is P(E | H)/P(E | Hᶜ). It measures how much more probable the evidence is under one hypothesis than the other, and it is the only thing the evidence contributes. The prior is whatever you brought with you.
  • For a diagnostic test, a positive result has LR⁺ = sensitivity/(1 − specificity) = 0.90/0.09 = 10, and a negative result has LR⁻ = (1 − sensitivity)/specificity = 0.10/0.91 = 0.110. These are properties of the test alone, independent of prevalence, which is why clinicians and model-evaluation specialists quote them.
  • On the log scale, evidence adds. log posterior odds = log prior odds + Σ log LRᵢ for pieces of evidence that are conditionally independent given each hypothesis, H and Hᶜ alike. Each piece of evidence contributes a fixed amount, which can be positive or negative. This additive form is how naive Bayes and logistic regression combine features (§12).

  • The independence caveat is real. Multiplying likelihood ratios assumes the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given each hypothesis, under both H and Hᶜ (§09). Base-rate neglect and the prosecutor's fallacy

  • Base-rate neglect is reporting P(E | H), or a sensitivity, as if it were P(H | E), ignoring the prior. Asked about the test above, most people, including many clinicians in published surveys, answer something near 90%. The right answer is 9%.
  • The prosecutor's fallacy is the courtroom version. "The chance of a random person matching this DNA profile is one in a million, so the chance the defendant is innocent is one in a million." That swaps P(match | innocent) for P(innocent | match). If 5 million people could have been the source, about 5 innocent people are expected to match as well. With nothing else pointing at the defendant, P(guilty | match) ≈ 1/6.
  • The defence attorney's fallacy is the mirror-image error: "so the match is nearly worthless". It is not. It multiplied the odds of guilt by a million, from 1 in 5 million to about 1 in 6. Combined with any independent evidence, such as motive, opportunity or a witness, it can be decisive. The correct statement is the likelihood ratio, followed by an honest prior.
  • The same inversion error is behind misreading a p-value as "the probability the null hypothesis is true". A p-value is a probability of data given a hypothesis, not of a hypothesis given data. See the base-rate problem in significance testing.

09 Independence

Two events are independent when learning that one happened tells you nothing about the other: conditioning on it leaves the probability where it was. Independence is what turns hard joint probabilities into easy products, and almost every clean formula in probability assumes it somewhere. It is also more subtle than it looks.

Definition and equivalent forms

  • A and B are independent (written A ⊥ B) if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B).
  • When P(B) > 0 this is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A), "B carries no information about A", by dividing both sides by P(B). When P(A) > 0 it is equivalent to P(B | A) = P(B). So independence is symmetric: if A tells you nothing about B, then B tells you nothing about A. The product form is taken as the definition because it is symmetric on sight and still makes sense when a probability is zero.
  • Independence passes to complements. If A ⊥ B then P(A ∩ Bᶜ) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 − P(B)) = P(A)P(Bᶜ), so A ⊥ Bᶜ. The same argument gives Aᶜ ⊥ B and Aᶜ ⊥ Bᶜ. Knowing that B did not happen is just as uninformative as knowing that it did. Any one of these twelve statements (three forms, four complement patterns) implies all the others, wherever the conditional forms are defined: the event conditioned on, or its complement, must have probability strictly between 0 and 1.
  • An event with probability 0 or 1 is independent of every event, including itself. It carries no information because its outcome is known in advance.

  • Independence arises in two ways. Usually it is assumed, as a modelling statement about physically separate mechanisms such as separate tosses, separate machines or separately sampled customers. Occasionally it is derived: it turns out the product rule holds, as with the die and month examples above, even though both events are defined from the same outcome. Independence is not disjointness

  • A common confusion is to treat "independent" as meaning "have nothing to do with each other", and therefore as "do not overlap". The opposite is true. If A and B are disjoint and both have positive probability, then P(A ∩ B) = 0 < P(A)P(B), so they are dependent, and strongly so. Learning that A happened tells you for certain that B did not.
  • Disjoint events are about as dependent as events can be. Independent events with positive probabilities must overlap, and by exactly the right amount: A ∩ B has to take up the same fraction of B as A takes up of Ω.
  • This is why you cannot see independence in a Venn diagram. It is not a question of whether regions overlap but of proportions. In the mosaic of §07 it is visible: A is independent of B exactly when the shaded heights in the two columns are equal, P(A | B) = P(A | Bᶜ). More than two events: pairwise is not enough

  • Events A₁, …, Aₙ are mutually independent if the product rule holds for every sub-collection: P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ) = P(Aᵢ)P(Aⱼ) for every pair, P(Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ) = P(Aᵢ)P(Aⱼ)P(Aₖ) for every triple, and so on up to all n. That is 2ⁿ − n − 1 conditions: 4 for three events, 11 for four, 1,013 for ten.
  • Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence. Toss two fair coins. Let A = "first is heads", B = "second is heads", and C = "exactly one is heads" (the XOR of the two). Conditional independence

  • A and B are conditionally independent given C, written A ⊥ B | C, if P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C)·P(B | C). Equivalently, once you know C, learning B tells you nothing more about A. This is just independence under the renormalised measure P(· | C).
  • Conditional independence does not imply independence. Pick one of two coins at random, a fair one or one that lands heads 90% of the time, and toss it twice. Given the coin, the tosses are independent. Unconditionally they are not. P(H₁) = 0.7, but P(H₂ | H₁) = 0.53/0.7 = 0.757, because a first head is evidence that you have the biased coin. A shared hidden cause makes its effects dependent.
  • Independence does not imply conditional independence. Two fair coins are independent, but given C = "exactly one head", they become perfectly dependent: if the first is heads the second must be tails, P(H₁ ∩ H₂ | C) = 0 ≠ ¼. The same thing happens in an alarm that is set off by either a burglary or an earthquake. The two causes are independent, but once the alarm has sounded, learning there was an earthquake makes a burglary less likely. This is explaining away: conditioning on a common effect creates dependence between its causes, which is the mechanism behind many selection biases.

The training/test split relies on a different independence: that examples are independent and identically distributed, so that the test set behaves like fresh draws. 10 Reliability: systems in series and in parallel

Reliability engineering applies independence, complements and De Morgan's laws to one question: if each component of a system works with a known probability, how likely is the whole system to work? The same arithmetic covers backups, replicated databases, microservice chains, majority-vote ensembles and multi-factor authentication.

Series: every component must work

  • Components in series form a chain: the system works only if all of them work. With independent components the system reliability is the product R = p₁ p₂ ⋯ pₙ. This is always at most the weakest component's reliability, and usually much less.
  • Long chains decay geometrically. Ten components each 99% reliable give 90.4%; a hundred give 36.6%. This is why a request that passes through many services must have each of them far more reliable than the target for the whole, and why "five nines" per component is not a vanity metric.
  • Worked. A launch needs three independent devices, which fail with probabilities 0.01, 0.02 and 0.02. P(on time) = 0.99 · 0.98 · 0.98 = 0.9508.

Parallel: one working component is enough

  • Components in parallel are redundant: the system works if at least one works. Use the complement. The system fails only if every component fails, so R = 1 − (1 − p₁)(1 − p₂)⋯(1 − pₙ). This is De Morgan's law with the product rule for independent failures.
  • Redundancy compounds failure probabilities multiplicatively. One disk that fails 1% of the time, backed up by two copies that each fail 2% of the time, loses data with probability 0.01 · 0.02 · 0.02 = 4 × 10⁻⁶. Reliability goes from 0.99 to 0.999996.
  • Duality. A parallel system of working events is a series system of failure events, and vice versa. Every formula has a mirror image obtained by swapping p ↔ 1 − p.

k-of-n: majority votes and quorums

  • A k-of-n system works if at least k of its n components work. Series is n-of-n and parallel is 1-of-n. With equal, independent component reliabilities p, the number that work is binomial, so R = Σ_(j=k..n) C(n, j) pʲ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ʲ.
  • The 2-of-3 majority vote (triple modular redundancy, and the quorum rule of replicated databases) gives R = 3p²(1 − p) + p³. At p = 0.9 that is 0.972, better than one component. At p = 0.6 it is 0.648, still better. At p = 0.4 it is 0.352, worse than a single component, and the curves cross exactly at p = ½. Majority voting amplifies whatever tendency the components already have.
  • That is the arithmetic behind ensembles of classifiers. Majority-voting n independent classifiers that are each right with probability 0.6 gives 0.733 at n = 9 and 0.826 at n = 21, approaching 1 as n grows. The essential word is independent. Real ensemble members make correlated errors, and the gain is far smaller. Methods like bagging and random feature subsets exist to reduce that correlation.

11 Classic puzzles and fallacies

The famous probability puzzles are not tricks. Each one is a case where intuition skips a step the rules require: specifying how the information was produced, conditioning on exactly what was observed, or keeping independence separate from compensation. Working them carefully is the best way to calibrate your intuition.

Monty Hall

  • The protocol, stated fully. A car is behind one of three doors, placed uniformly at random, with goats behind the other two. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, then opens one of the other two doors, always revealing a goat. If both other doors hide goats he picks between them at random. He always offers you the chance to switch to the remaining closed door. Should you?
  • The intuition that fails. "Two doors are left, so it is 50/50." That treats the host's action as carrying no information beyond "this door has a goat". But the host is constrained: he can never open your door and never open the car's door, and those constraints carry information.
  • By conditioning. Say you picked door 1 and the host opened door 3. Let Cᵢ = "car behind door i", each with prior ⅓. The likelihoods of "host opens 3" are: given C₁, ½ (he could open 2 or 3); given C₂, 1 (door 3 is his only legal choice); given C₃, 0 (he never reveals the car). Bayes' rule gives P(C₁ | opens 3) = (½·⅓)/(½·⅓ + 1·⅓ + 0) = ⅓ and P(C₂ | opens 3) = ⅔. Switch.
  • By a one-line argument. Switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong, which happens with probability ⅔. The host's action never changes whether your first pick was right. It only removes the wrong alternative so that switching takes you to the car.
  • With 100 doors. You pick one, and the host opens 98 of the remaining 99, all goats. Staying wins with probability 1/100, and switching to the one door he conspicuously skipped wins with probability 99/100.
  • The protocol is everything. If the host does not know where the car is, opens one of the other doors at random, and happens to reveal a goat, then the likelihoods of "door 3 opened and showed a goat" are ½ under C₁ and ½ under C₂. The posterior is ½ / ½, and switching neither helps nor hurts. The same observation leads to different conclusions under different processes that could have produced it. That is the general lesson: condition on how the data were generated, not just on what you saw.

The gambler's fallacy and the law of large numbers

  • The fallacy. After five heads in a row, tails is "due". For a fair coin with independent tosses, P(tails on toss 6 | five heads) = P(tails) = ½. That is the definition of independence: the coin has no memory and no ledger.
  • Where it comes from. People know the long-run proportion of heads goes to ½, the law of large numbers, and conclude that a surplus of heads must be paid back. It is not paid back. As §01's figure shows, the count gap #H − #T wanders on a √n scale and does not shrink. The proportion converges because the early surplus is diluted by an ever-growing number of tosses, not because it is cancelled. The precise statement, and the difference between the weak and strong versions, is the subject of Limit Theorems & Convergence. The applied version is in Sampling Distributions & the CLT.
  • The opposite error. The "hot hand" belief says a streak makes another success more likely. For a mechanism that really is independent, that is equally wrong.
  • When a streak should change your mind. If you are not sure the coin is fair, a long run of heads is evidence that it is biased toward heads, and the rational update is toward more heads, not fewer. Ten heads in a row has probability 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 0.001 under a fair coin and 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.35 under a coin with P(H) = 0.9. That is a likelihood ratio of about 357 against fairness. The gambler's fallacy is the one conclusion that neither "fair" nor "biased" supports.

The two-children problem, and why it is ambiguous

  • "A family has two children, and at least one is a boy. What is the probability both are boys?" The answer depends on how you learned that at least one is a boy, and the question as usually stated does not say. Model: the four ordered families BB, BG, GB, GG, equally likely.
  • Protocol 1, a filter. From all two-child families, keep those with at least one boy. The surviving families are BB, BG and GB, equally likely, so P(BB | at least one boy) = 1/3. This is the answer to "the family answered yes to: do you have at least one boy?"
  • Protocol 2, a sample. You meet one of the two children, chosen at random, and he is a boy. The observation "the child I met is a boy" has probability 1 under BB, ½ under BG and GB, and 0 under GG. Bayes' rule gives P(BB | met a boy) = (1·¼)/(1·¼ + ½·¼ + ½·¼) = ½. Meeting a particular boy tells you the sex of one specific child, and the other child is an independent ½.
  • The honest answer is "it depends on the protocol". Both 1/3 and 1/2 are correct answers to two different, fully specified questions, and the everyday wording does not choose between them. Adding seemingly irrelevant details moves the answer between the two. "At least one is a boy born on a Tuesday", under the filter protocol, gives 13/27 ≈ 0.481, because the more specific the detail, the closer the filter comes to identifying one particular child. This is Monty Hall's lesson again: the evidence is the event that was observed, including how it came to be observed.

The pattern behind every puzzle on this page. (1) Write the sample space so that its outcomes really are equally likely. (2) Write down the event that was actually observed, including the mechanism that revealed it. (3) Apply the definition of conditional probability, or Bayes' rule, without shortcuts. Each famous paradox breaks at step 1 or step 2. Step 3 never fails.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

A classifier is Bayes' rule with learned ingredients. A training objective is a probability of the data. An evaluation metric is a conditional probability. A generalisation guarantee is an independence assumption, a concentration bound, and a union bound over a finite set of hypotheses. This page is the grammar these statements are written in.

Bayes' rule as a classifier

  • Given features x, predict the class c with the largest posterior P(c | x). By Bayes' rule, P(c | x) = P(x | c)P(c)/P(x). The denominator is the same for every class, so the decision only needs likelihood × prior: ĉ = argmax_c P(x | c)·P(c).
  • If the true P(c | x) were known, this rule would minimise the misclassification probability. No classifier can do better, and the error it still makes is the Bayes error, the irreducible overlap between classes. Every real classifier is an approximation to this rule, built from estimated probabilities.
  • With 0/1 loss the rule is "pick the most probable class". With unequal costs (missing a fraud costs far more than a false alarm), the optimal rule thresholds the posterior at a cost-dependent level instead of ½. The probability model and the decision are separate steps, which is why threshold choice is its own topic.

Generative vs discriminative

  • Generative models learn how each class produces data, P(x | c), together with the class frequencies P(c), and get P(c | x) from Bayes' rule. Naive Bayes, linear and quadratic discriminant analysis, Gaussian mixture classifiers and hidden Markov models work this way. They can generate synthetic examples, handle missing features naturally by summing them out, and need little data if their model of P(x | c) is roughly right.
  • Discriminative models learn P(c | x), or just the decision boundary, directly, without modelling how x is distributed. Logistic regression, SVMs, and most neural classifiers are discriminative. They spend all their capacity on the question asked, and usually win when data are plentiful and P(x | c) is complicated.
  • The two meet in the middle. Naive Bayes with independent features gives log posterior odds that are a sum of per-feature terms plus a prior term, which is the same functional form logistic regression fits directly. That holds for binary (Bernoulli) features, and for Gaussian features whose variance is shared across classes. Gaussian naive Bayes with class-specific variances adds quadratic terms, so its boundary is not linear. Naive Bayes estimates the terms from class-conditional counts, while logistic regression fits them to maximise P(c | x). Generative is often better with small samples; discriminative is usually better asymptotically.

A spam filter, by hand

  • A mail corpus is 20% spam. The word "money" appears in 8% of spam and 1% of legitimate mail. The word "winner" appears in 5% of spam and 0.2% of legitimate mail. Assume the two words occur independently given the class, which is the naive Bayes assumption (§09).
  • "money" alone. Total probability gives P(money) = 0.08·0.2 + 0.01·0.8 = 0.024, and Bayes' rule gives P(spam | money) = 0.016/0.024 = ⅔. In odds: the prior odds are 0.2/0.8 = ¼, the likelihood ratio is 0.08/0.01 = 8, and the posterior odds are 2 : 1.
  • "money" and "winner". Conditional independence lets the likelihood ratios multiply: ¼ × 8 × 25 = 50, so P(spam) = 50/51 = 0.980. On the log scale the evidence adds: −1.386 + 2.079 + 3.219 = 3.912 = ln 50. Each word contributes a fixed weight, and a positive total means spam.
  • The absence of a word is evidence too. A message with neither word gets likelihood ratios 0.92/0.99 and 0.95/0.998, both slightly below 1. The posterior drops a little below the prior, to 0.181. This is the Bernoulli variant of naive Bayes, which scores every vocabulary word as present or absent. The multinomial variant, the usual choice for text, models word counts and simply ignores absent words.
  • Prior shift. The same message scored ⅔ spam by a filter calibrated on 20% spam would be only 0.296 spam in a mailbox where 5% of mail is spam. The likelihood ratio (8) is a property of the words. The posterior also depends on the base rate, so a model deployed into a population with a different class balance must have its prior odds rescaled. This is §08's diagnostic-test lesson in a different setting.

The other direction: statistics

  • Everything on this page runs forward: given the probability model (fair coin, 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity), what will we observe? Real problems usually run backward: given what we observed, what is the model? How biased is this coin? What is this test's sensitivity? How much better is version B than version A?
  • That inverse problem is Statistics. The bridge between the two directions is the likelihood, P(data | model), a forward probability read as a function of the model. Frequentist methods ask which models make the data unsurprising. Bayesian methods put a prior on the model and apply Bayes' rule, exactly as in §08, with "hypothesis" now ranging over parameter values.
  • Before that bridge can carry any weight, outcomes need to become numbers, with distributions, averages and spreads. That step is the subject of Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.
