06 Conditional probability

Information shrinks the sample space. Once you learn that B happened, every outcome outside B is ruled out, and the probability mass left inside B is scaled up so that it totals 1 again. That rescaling is the whole definition of conditional probability. Most of the rest of probability theory consists of applying it carefully.

The definition, as renormalisation

  • For an event B with P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Read it as: of the probability mass sitting on B, what fraction also sits on A?
  • Two steps. (1) Restrict: outcomes outside B are impossible now, so the only part of A that matters is A ∩ B. (2) Renormalise: the surviving mass is P(B) rather than 1, so divide by it. Relative odds inside B are unchanged. Two outcomes in B that were equally likely before are still equally likely after.
  • Frequency reading. Among many repetitions, keep only those in which B occurred. P(A | B) is the long-run fraction of those kept runs in which A also occurred: n_(A∩B) / n_B = (n_(A∩B)/n) / (n_B/n).
  • Worked, with months. A stranger's birth month is uniform over 12. L = 31-day months = {Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec}. R = months with an "r" = {Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec}. Told the month is long, 7 possibilities remain, and 4 of them (Jan, Mar, Oct, Dec) contain an r. So P(R | L) = 4/7 ≈ 0.571, against an unconditional P(R) = 8/12 = 0.667, and against P(R ∩ L) = 4/12 = 1/3, which is a different question.
  • Worked, with a die. Told the roll is even, P(6 | even) = (1/6)/(1/2) = 1/3, and P(5 | even) = 0.
definitionP(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), P(B) > 0 equally likely outcomes: just count inside BP(A | B) = |A ∩ B| / |B| workedP(R | L) = (4/12)/(7/12) = 4/7 ≈ 0.571 P(L | R) = (4/12)/(8/12) = 1/2 — not the same thing

A conditional probability is a probability

  • Fix B. The function Q(A) = P(A | B) satisfies all three axioms: Q(A) ≥ 0; Q(Ω) = P(B)/P(B) = 1; and for disjoint Aᵢ the sets Aᵢ ∩ B are disjoint too, so additivity carries over. Every theorem of §03 therefore holds to the left of the bar: P(Aᶜ | B) = 1 − P(A | B), P(A ∪ C | B) = P(A | B) + P(C | B) − P(A ∩ C | B), and so on.
  • Nothing of the kind holds to the right of the bar. In general P(A | B) + P(A | Bᶜ) ≠ 1, and P(A | B ∪ C) is not P(A | B) + P(A | C). The conditioning event is the new sample space, not something you add up.
  • The bar is not symmetric. P(spots | measles) is close to 1, while P(measles | spots) is small, because many things cause spots. The two are linked only through Bayes' rule (§08). Confusing them is called confusion of the inverse (or the transposed conditional), and its best-known instance is the prosecutor's fallacy of §08. It is probably the most consequential error in applied probability.

The multiplication (chain) rule

  • Multiply the definition through by P(B): P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) · P(B). A joint probability is built as "how likely is the first thing, times how likely is the second given the first". This is often far easier than computing the intersection directly.
  • Applied repeatedly, it gives the chain rule: P(A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ) = P(A₁) · P(A₂ | A₁) · P(A₃ | A₁ ∩ A₂) ⋯ P(Aₙ | A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ₋₁). It holds for every ordering of the events.
  • Choose the order that makes the factors easy. For birthdays, "person n avoids the first n − 1 birthdays, given those are distinct" is 1 − (n−1)/365, which is immediate. The reverse, "the first n − 1 are distinct given person n avoided them all", is correct but much harder to evaluate. If conditioning does not make the pieces simpler, condition the other way round.
  • The chain rule is how sequence models assign probability. A language model scores a sentence as P(w₁)·P(w₂ | w₁)·P(w₃ | w₁, w₂)⋯, which is exactly this identity, with each factor predicted by a network. It is exact and needs no independence assumption. The modelling happens in estimating the factors.

That last line is the defining property of the exponential distribution, developed in Continuous Distributions. Here it is just the definition of conditional probability applied to two nested events, using {wait > 4} ⊂ {wait > 3} so that the intersection is the smaller event.

Tree diagrams

  • A tree lays out a multi-stage experiment. Each level is a stage, each branch is labelled with a conditional probability given the path so far, and the branches leaving any node sum to 1. Multiply along a path to get the probability of that leaf (the chain rule). Add across leaves to get the probability of any event made of several leaves (additivity).
  • Worked. An urn holds 3 red and 2 blue balls. Draw two without replacement.
    • Red then red: (3/5)(2/4) = 6/20. Red then blue: (3/5)(2/4) = 6/20. Blue then red: (2/5)(3/4) = 6/20. Blue then blue: (2/5)(1/4) = 2/20. The four leaves sum to 20/20 ✓.
    • P(second is red) = 6/20 + 6/20 = 12/20 = 3/5, exactly the same as P(first is red). Unconditionally, every draw position is symmetric. Only once you know what came out first does the second draw change. This is why dealing order does not matter in card games.
    • P(first red | second red) = (6/20)/(12/20) = 1/2. You can condition on a later event; conditional probability is about information, not about time.

The two basic moves

  • Almost every hand calculation is a composition of two moves. Complement: if "at least one" is hard, compute "none" and subtract from 1. Condition: if an event is hard, split it into cases where it is easy (§07), or build it up one stage at a time (the chain rule).
  • Worked: at least one six in three rolls. Directly, "six on roll 1 or roll 2 or roll 3" is a union of overlapping events. Complement instead: "no six in any roll" has probability (5/6)³ = 125/216 because the rolls are independent (§09). So P(at least one six) = 1 − 125/216 = 91/216 ≈ 0.421. The naive sum 3 × 1/6 = 0.5 over-counts the overlaps, as inclusion–exclusion predicts.
  • An old gambling puzzle, settled the same way. Is "at least one six in 4 rolls of one die" or "at least one double-six in 24 rolls of two dice" the better bet? The naive proportional argument (4/6 = 24/36) says they are equal. The complement says 1 − (5/6)⁴ = 0.5177 against 1 − (35/36)²⁴ = 0.4914. The first is a winning bet and the second a losing one. A gambler of the period reportedly raised the discrepancy, but play at the table could not have settled it: detecting a probability 0.0086 below ½ at two standard deviations takes roughly 13,500 bets. Only the calculation could.
complement + independenceP(at least one success in n independent tries) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ one die, 3 rolls: 1 − (5/6)³ = 91/216 = 0.4213 one die, 4 rolls: 1 − (5/6)⁴ = 0.5177 two dice, 24 rolls: 1 − (35/36)²⁴ = 0.4914 10 fair coins, at least one head: 1 − (½)¹⁰ = 1023/1024 ≈ 0.999
Where this is in ML. Every supervised model estimates a conditional quantity, P(y | x) or E[y | x]: the distribution of the label once the features are known. The restrict and renormalise picture is literally what a decision-tree leaf does. It keeps the training examples that satisfy the path's conditions and reports the label frequencies among them. 07 The law of total probability

When an event is hard to assess directly but easy to assess case by case, split the world into cases, work out the event's probability within each case, and average the answers, weighted by how likely each case is. That is the law of total probability: conditioning in the "divide and conquer" direction.

Statement and proof

  • Let B₁, …, Bₘ be a partition of Ω: pairwise disjoint, together covering everything, each with positive probability. Then for any event A, P(A) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ).
  • Proof. A = (A ∩ B₁) ∪ … ∪ (A ∩ Bₘ), and these pieces are disjoint because the Bᵢ are. Additivity gives P(A) = Σ P(A ∩ Bᵢ), and the multiplication rule rewrites each term as P(A | Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ). The same works for a countably infinite partition.
  • It is a weighted average. The weights P(Bᵢ) are non-negative and sum to 1, so P(A) lies between the smallest and the largest of the P(A | Bᵢ), closer to the cases that are more common. The overall defect rate of a factory cannot be worse than its worst machine or better than its best.
  • The simplest partition is {B, Bᶜ}: P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) + P(A | Bᶜ)(1 − P(B)). On a tree, this is "add up the leaves where A happened".
  • It holds conditionally too: P(A | C) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ ∩ C) P(Bᵢ | C). Note that the weights become P(Bᵢ | C); forgetting to update the weights is how Simpson's paradox arises (a trend that holds within every group can reverse in the aggregate, because the groups are weighted differently). See Simpson's paradox.
law of total probabilityP(A) = Σ_(i=1..m) P(A | Bᵢ) · P(Bᵢ) {B₁, …, Bₘ} a partition of Ω P(A) = P(A | B)·P(B) + P(A | Bᶜ)·P(Bᶜ) the two-case version min_i P(A | Bᵢ) ≤ P(A) ≤ max_i P(A | Bᵢ)

Worked: a two-stage experiment

  • A plant has three machines. Machine 1 makes 50% of the output with a 1% defect rate, machine 2 makes 30% with 2%, and machine 3 makes 20% with 3%. Stage one picks a machine according to its share; stage two decides whether the item is defective given the machine. What fraction of all items is defective?
  • Nobody measured "defective" directly, but each case is easy: P(D) = 0.01·0.50 + 0.02·0.30 + 0.03·0.20 = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.006 = 0.017. As a weighted average it sits between 1% and 3%, pulled toward 1% because machine 1 dominates the output.
  • The natural follow-up runs the other way: this item is defective, which machine made it? That needs Bayes' rule, and §08 answers it with these numbers.

Worked backwards: asking a sensitive question privately

  • To estimate how many students have cheated on an exam without anyone admitting anything, use a randomised response survey. Each student privately tosses a coin. On tails they answer the real question, "have you cheated?". On heads they toss again and answer "did the second toss land tails?". The interviewer sees only "yes" or "no" and cannot tell which question was answered. That gives plausible deniability, not zero information. A cheater says yes with probability ½ + ½·½ = 0.75 and a non-cheater with probability 0.25, so a single "yes" multiplies the odds of having cheated by 3. At the estimated rate below (0.447), a "yes" raises the posterior to 0.708. What the design guarantees is that no single answer can move the odds by more than a factor of 3 in either direction.
  • In aggregate, the law of total probability still links the observable to the unknown: P(yes) = P(yes | real Q)·½ + P(yes | coin Q)·½ = π·½ + ½·½, where π is the cheating rate. Solve for π.
  • In one survey 27 of 57 students answered yes, so P̂(yes) = 27/57 = 0.474 and π̂ = (0.474 − 0.25)/0.5 = 0.447. The individual-level noise is the price of privacy: the estimate is much less precise than direct questioning would be, if direct questioning worked. This trade-off between privacy and accuracy is the core idea of differential privacy, which formalises randomised response and is used by large-scale telemetry systems. The "at most a factor of 3" bound is exactly its privacy parameter: ε = ln 3 ≈ 1.1.
randomised responseP(yes) = ½·π + ½·½ ⟹ π = (P(yes) − ¼) / ½ = 2·P(yes) − ½ 27 of 57 say yes: P̂(yes) = 0.4737 π̂ = 2(0.4737) − 0.5 = 0.447

08 Bayes' rule

The data usually come as P(evidence | cause): how often a test fires on sick patients, how often a word appears in spam. The question is usually P(cause | evidence): is this patient sick, is this email spam? Bayes' rule converts one into the other. It is one line of algebra, and the number it produces usually surprises people.

Derivation

  • The multiplication rule can be written two ways: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) = P(B | A)P(A). Divide by P(A). That is all of Bayes' rule.
  • Usually P(A) is not given directly. Expand it by total probability over the possible causes, and the denominator becomes the sum of the numerators over every cause. The posterior probabilities of the causes therefore always sum to 1.
  • Vocabulary, for causes Hᵢ (hypotheses) and evidence E:
    • Prior P(Hᵢ): how plausible the cause was before the evidence.
    • Likelihood P(E | Hᵢ): how well that cause predicts the evidence.
    • Evidence or marginal likelihood P(E) = Σⱼ P(E | Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ): the normaliser.
    • Posterior P(Hᵢ | E): the plausibility after the evidence.
  • In words: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. A cause ends up probable if it explains the evidence well and was plausible to begin with. Neither factor alone decides.
Bayes' ruleP(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(B) / P(A) P(B | A) = P(A | B)·P(B) / [ P(A | B)·P(B) + P(A | Bᶜ)·P(Bᶜ) ] over a partition of causes H₁, …, HₘP(Hᵢ | E) = P(E | Hᵢ)·P(Hᵢ) / Σⱼ P(E | Hⱼ)·P(Hⱼ) posterior ∝ likelihood × prior worked: the three machines of §07, given that an item is defectiveP(M₁ | D) = 0.005/0.017 = 0.294 P(M₂ | D) = 0.006/0.017 = 0.353 P(M₃ | D) = 0.006/0.017 = 0.353 machine 3 makes 20% of the items but 35% of the defects; machine 1 makes 50% of the items but only 29% of the defects

The diagnostic test, worked in counts

  • A disease has prevalence 1%. The test's sensitivity (true-positive rate) P(+ | D) = 0.90, and its specificity (true-negative rate) P(− | Dᶜ) = 0.91, so the false-positive rate is P(+ | Dᶜ) = 0.09. A patient tests positive. What is the chance they have the disease?
  • Think in people, not percentages. Picture 10,000 people. 100 have the disease: 90 test positive (true positives) and 10 negative (false negatives). 9,900 do not: 9% of them, 891 people, test positive (false positives) and 9,009 negative (true negatives).
  • There are 90 + 891 = 981 positives, and 90 of them are sick. P(D | +) = 90/981 = 0.0917, about 9.2%. A positive result from a "90% accurate" test means the patient is still about ten times more likely to be healthy than sick.
  • Why. The test is good, but the healthy population is 99 times larger than the sick one. A 9% false-positive rate applied to 9,900 people produces ten times as many false alarms as the 90% true-positive rate produces from 100. The prior (prevalence) dominates.
  • The negative result is very informative. P(Dᶜ | −) = 9,009/9,019 = 0.9989. A rare-disease test is good at ruling out and bad at ruling in, which is exactly why screening programmes confirm positives with a second, different test.
  • Prevalence changes everything. The same test gives PPV = 1.0% at prevalence 0.1%, 9.2% at 1%, 52.6% at 10%, and 90.9% at 50%. A test does not have one "chance of being right"; it has a sensitivity and a specificity, and the PPV also depends on who is being tested. To reach PPV = ½ at 1% prevalence with this sensitivity, the specificity would have to be 99.1%.

The odds form, and the Bayes factor

  • Odds are often a more natural scale than probability: odds(H) = P(H)/P(Hᶜ), and p = odds/(1 + odds). Write Bayes' rule for H and for Hᶜ and divide. The awkward denominator P(E) cancels, leaving a single multiplication.
  • Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. The likelihood ratio, also called the Bayes factor when H and Hᶜ are competing models, is P(E | H)/P(E | Hᶜ). It measures how much more probable the evidence is under one hypothesis than the other, and it is the only thing the evidence contributes. The prior is whatever you brought with you.
  • For a diagnostic test, a positive result has LR⁺ = sensitivity/(1 − specificity) = 0.90/0.09 = 10, and a negative result has LR⁻ = (1 − sensitivity)/specificity = 0.10/0.91 = 0.110. These are properties of the test alone, independent of prevalence, which is why clinicians and model-evaluation specialists quote them.
  • On the log scale, evidence adds. log posterior odds = log prior odds + Σ log LRᵢ for pieces of evidence that are conditionally independent given each hypothesis, H and Hᶜ alike. Each piece of evidence contributes a fixed amount, which can be positive or negative. This additive form is how naive Bayes and logistic regression combine features (§12).
odds formP(H | E) / P(Hᶜ | E) = [ P(E | H) / P(E | Hᶜ) ] × [ P(H) / P(Hᶜ) ] posterior odds = likelihood ratio (Bayes factor) × prior odds the test again, in oddsprior odds = 1 : 99 LR⁺ = 0.90/0.09 = 10 posterior odds = 10 : 99 P = 10/109 = 0.0917 ✓ sequential updating: the posterior becomes the next priorsecond independent positive: 10 × (10 : 99) = 100 : 99 ⟹ P = 0.503 third independent positive: 1000 : 99 ⟹ P = 0.910 a positive then a negative: (1 : 99) × 10 × 0.110 = 1.10 : 99 ⟹ P = 0.011 — back near the prior
  • Order does not matter. Updating on E₁ and then on E₂ gives the same posterior as updating on both at once, because multiplication commutes. Yesterday's posterior is today's prior, which is what makes Bayesian updating suitable for streaming data.
  • The independence caveat is real. Multiplying likelihood ratios assumes the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given each hypothesis, under both H and Hᶜ (§09). Re-running the same assay on the same sample repeats the same error. A second positive from that is not a second piece of evidence, and multiplying by 10 again overstates the case. The same assumption, made deliberately, is what makes naive Bayes "naive".

Base-rate neglect and the prosecutor's fallacy

  • Base-rate neglect is reporting P(E | H), or a sensitivity, as if it were P(H | E), ignoring the prior. Asked about the test above, most people, including many clinicians in published surveys, answer something near 90%. The right answer is 9%.
  • The prosecutor's fallacy is the courtroom version. "The chance of a random person matching this DNA profile is one in a million, so the chance the defendant is innocent is one in a million." That swaps P(match | innocent) for P(innocent | match). If 5 million people could have been the source, about 5 innocent people are expected to match as well. With nothing else pointing at the defendant, P(guilty | match) ≈ 1/6.
  • The defence attorney's fallacy is the mirror-image error: "so the match is nearly worthless". It is not. It multiplied the odds of guilt by a million, from 1 in 5 million to about 1 in 6. Combined with any independent evidence, such as motive, opportunity or a witness, it can be decisive. The correct statement is the likelihood ratio, followed by an honest prior.
  • The same inversion error is behind misreading a p-value as "the probability the null hypothesis is true". A p-value is a probability of data given a hypothesis, not of a hypothesis given data. See the base-rate problem in significance testing.
the DNA matchP(match | innocent) = 10⁻⁶ plausible sources N = 5,000,000 one of them guilty prior odds of guilt = 1 : 4,999,999 LR = 1 / 10⁻⁶ = 10⁶ posterior odds = 10⁶ : 4,999,999 ≈ 1 : 5 P(guilty | match) ≈ 0.167 prosecutor's claim: P(innocent | match) = 10⁻⁶ actual: P(innocent | match) ≈ 0.833
Rules of thumb that survive any example. (1) Never report P(E | H) when the question was P(H | E). (2) When the hypothesis is rare, even strong evidence leaves it unlikely; when it is common, weak evidence is enough. (3) Write the 2 × 2 table of counts per 10,000. It is the fastest way to get Bayes' rule right, and the easiest way to explain the result to someone else.
