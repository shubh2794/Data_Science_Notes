02 Sample spaces and events

Before you can assign probabilities you need a list of what can happen. The sample space is that list; an event is any question about the outcome that has a yes/no answer, written as the set of outcomes for which the answer is yes. From this point on, logic becomes set algebra.

Outcomes and the sample space

  • The sample space Ω is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment. Each element ω ∈ Ω is one complete, indivisible result. Exactly one outcome occurs per run.
  • Examples, from small to large:
    • One coin: Ω = {H, T}.
    • One die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
    • The month of a stranger's birthday: Ω = {Jan, Feb, …, Dec}, twelve outcomes.
    • The order in which three envelopes, numbered 1, 2, 3, lie stacked on a doormat, read from the top down (so 213 means envelope 2 on top, then 1, then 3): Ω = {123, 132, 213, 231, 312, 321}, the 3! = 6 permutations.
    • Number of emails until the first spam: Ω = {1, 2, 3, …}, countably infinite.
    • A latency in milliseconds, or the load at which a beam fails: Ω = (0, ∞), uncountable.
  • Choosing Ω is a modelling decision. A sample space can be bigger than reality requires (latency in (0, ∞) although no request takes a year) and that is harmless: the extra outcomes simply get probability zero. It must never be too coarse, though. If the event you care about cannot be written as a set of outcomes, the sample space is wrong. Recording only "number of heads in two tosses", {0, 1, 2}, makes "the first toss was heads" impossible to express.
  • A good sample space also makes the probabilities easy. For two coins, {HH, HT, TH, TT} has four equally likely outcomes; {0 heads, 1 head, 2 heads} is a valid sample space whose outcomes are not equally likely (¼, ½, ¼). Treating the second as uniform is the oldest mistake in the subject (§04).

Events are subsets

  • An event is a subset A ⊂ Ω. We say A occurs if the realised outcome lies in A. "The die shows an even number" is A = {2, 4, 6}; "the birthday falls in a 31-day month" is L = {Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec}.
  • Two events are always available: Ω itself (the sure event, which always occurs) and ∅ (the impossible event, which never does). A one-outcome set {ω} is an elementary event. Strictly, probabilities attach to events, so you write P({H}), though everyone writes P(H).
  • Every logical connective becomes a set operation, and every set operation is a statement about the outcome.

  • A collection A₁, A₂, … is pairwise disjoint if Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ = ∅ whenever i ≠ j. It is a partition of Ω if it is pairwise disjoint and its union is all of Ω: every outcome lands in exactly one piece. Partitions are what the law of total probability (§07) and Bayes' rule (§08) run on.
  • The indicator of an event, 1_A(ω) = 1 if ω ∈ A and 0 otherwise, turns set algebra into arithmetic: 1_(A∩B) = 1_A · 1_B, 1_(Aᶜ) = 1 − 1_A, and 1_(A∪B) = 1_A + 1_B − 1_A·1_B. The last identity is inclusion–exclusion (§03) in a single line, and indicators return as the simplest random variables in Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.

De Morgan's laws

  • "It is not true that neither the cache nor the database failed" means "the cache or the database (or both) failed". Double negations like this are where verbal reasoning goes wrong, and De Morgan's laws do the bookkeeping: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and vice versa.
  • Proof of the first, by chasing an outcome: ω ∈ (A ∪ B)ᶜ ⇔ ω is in neither A nor B ⇔ ω ∈ Aᶜ and ω ∈ Bᶜ ⇔ ω ∈ Aᶜ ∩ Bᶜ. The second follows by applying the first to Aᶜ and Bᶜ and complementing.
  • Their main use in probability is converting "at least one" (a union, awkward) into "none" (an intersection, often a product). That conversion is the complement move in §03 and §06.

Products of sample spaces: repeating an experiment

  • Running two experiments, or one experiment twice, gives the product space Ω₁ × Ω₂, the set of ordered pairs. Two coins: {H,T} × {H,T} = {(H,H), (H,T), (T,H), (T,T)}. Two dice: 36 ordered pairs. If Ω₁ has r outcomes and Ω₂ has s, the product has r·s, which is the multiplication principle of §05.
  • n repetitions give Ω = Ω₁ × … × Ωₙ. For binary trials that is {0,1}ⁿ, with 2ⁿ sequences, and the event "exactly one success in five trials" is the five-element set {00001, 00010, 00100, 01000, 10000}.
  • The product space says nothing yet about probabilities. The usual assignment multiplies, P((ω₁, ω₂)) = P(ω₁)·P(ω₂). That is a modelling choice, and it means the runs do not influence each other. Its formal name is independence (§09). Other assignments are perfectly legal: "the second die always copies the first" puts probability ⅙ on each of the six diagonal pairs and 0 elsewhere, and is still a valid probability model on the same 36-point space.

An infinite sample space: toss until the first head

  • Toss a coin with P(H) = p until heads appears and record how many tosses that took. The outcomes are Ω = {1, 2, 3, …}. The outcome n means n − 1 tails followed by a head, so with independent tosses P(n) = (1 − p)ⁿ⁻¹ p.
  • With infinitely many outcomes, "probabilities add up to one" becomes a statement about an infinite series, and finite additivity is no longer enough. The countable version of the third axiom (§03) is what makes P(Ω) equal the infinite series Σₙ P(n), and the geometric series shows that series equals 1.
  • That one sample space answers several natural questions. The probability the first head comes on an odd toss, with a fair coin, is ½ + ⅛ + 1/32 + … = 2/3: the player who tosses first in an alternating game wins two times in three. There is also the event "heads never appears", the all-tails infinite sequence. It is not an outcome in {1, 2, 3, …} at all; in the richer space of infinite sequences it is a legitimate event with probability lim (1 − p)ⁿ = 0 (for any p > 0). Possible, in that the sequence exists, and of probability zero.
the geometric sample spaceP(n) = (1 − p)ⁿ⁻¹ · p, n = 1, 2, 3, … Σₙ P(n) = p · Σₙ (1 − p)ⁿ⁻¹ = p · 1/(1 − (1 − p)) = p/p = 1 ✓ worked, fair coinP(first head within 3 tosses) = ½ + ¼ + ⅛ = 7/8 = 1 − (½)³ P(first head on an odd toss) = Σₖ (½)²ᵏ⁻¹ = (½)/(1 − ¼) = 2/3 P(never a head) = lim (n → ∞) (½)ⁿ = 0 — "probability zero" is not "impossible"
Uncountable spaces need one more idea. For Ω = (0, ∞) you cannot list outcomes and add their probabilities: each single latency value has probability 0, and summing zeros never gives 1. Probability has to be assigned to intervals through a density. For technical reasons, not every subset of the real line can be given a consistent probability, so the collection of events is restricted to a well-behaved family (a σ-field) that contains every set you will ever meet in practice. The density story belongs to Continuous Distributions. Everything on this page holds verbatim in that setting.

03 The three axioms, and everything that follows

A probability is any assignment of numbers to events that obeys three rules: non-negativity, total mass one, and additivity over disjoint events. Nothing else is assumed. The complement rule, the union rule, the union bound and inclusion–exclusion are all theorems, each a line or two of algebra away from the axioms.

The axioms

  • A probability measure on a sample space Ω is a function P from events to real numbers satisfying:
    1. Non-negativity. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A.
    2. Normalisation. P(Ω) = 1. Something in the list happens.
    3. Countable additivity. If A₁, A₂, A₃, … are pairwise disjoint, then P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ …) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …
  • Think of probability as a fixed amount of mass, one unit, spread over Ω. An event's probability is the mass sitting on it. Mass is never negative, there is exactly one unit of it, and the mass of a region made of non-overlapping pieces is the sum of the pieces. Every rule below is a statement about how that mass is distributed, which is why Venn diagrams with area as probability are a faithful picture.
  • Why "countable" and not just "finite". Finite additivity (for two, three or any finite number of disjoint events) cannot handle the toss-until-heads space of §02, where the total is an infinite sum, or limits such as "the probability of heads never appearing". Countable additivity is the minimum needed for limits to behave, and it buys continuity: if events grow, A₁ ⊂ A₂ ⊂ …, then P(Aₙ) → P(∪Aₙ).
  • On a finite or countable Ω, the axioms reduce to one recipe: give each outcome a weight pᵢ ≥ 0 with Σpᵢ = 1, and set P(A) = Σ_(ωᵢ ∈ A) pᵢ. Every such assignment is a valid probability, and, taking every subset as an event, every valid probability is of this form. Whether a particular assignment is the right one is a modelling question that the axioms cannot answer.

Consequences, each with its proof

  • The empty set has probability zero. Take A₁ = Ω and A₂ = A₃ = … = ∅. These are pairwise disjoint and their union is Ω, so axiom 3 gives P(Ω) = P(Ω) + P(∅) + P(∅) + …. A finite number can only equal itself plus an infinite sum of copies of a non-negative constant if that constant is 0.
  • Finite additivity. Pad any finite disjoint list with infinitely many copies of ∅ and apply axiom 3, using P(∅) = 0.
  • Complement rule. A and Aᶜ are disjoint with union Ω, so P(A) + P(Aᶜ) = 1.
  • Bounded by one. P(A) = 1 − P(Aᶜ) ≤ 1, since P(Aᶜ) ≥ 0. The axioms never had to say this explicitly.
  • Difference rule and monotonicity. If A ⊂ B, split B = A ∪ (B \ A), disjointly. So P(B) = P(A) + P(B \ A), which gives P(B \ A) = P(B) − P(A) ≥ 0 and hence P(A) ≤ P(B). A smaller event cannot be more probable. (The conjunction fallacy, where people judge "a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement" more likely than "a bank teller", is a violation of exactly this rule.)
  • General difference. Without containment, P(A \ B) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B), from the disjoint split A = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A \ B).
  • Union of two events (inclusion–exclusion, n = 2). Split the union into three disjoint pieces, A ∪ B = (A \ B) ∪ (A ∩ B) ∪ (B \ A). Then P(A ∪ B) = [P(A) − P(A∩B)] + P(A∩B) + [P(B) − P(A∩B)] = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice, so it is subtracted once.
  • Union bound (Boole's inequality). Since P(A ∩ B) ≥ 0, P(A ∪ B) ≤ P(A) + P(B). By induction P(A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ) ≤ ΣP(Aᵢ) for finitely many events, and letting n → ∞ (continuity of probability, which follows from countable additivity) extends it to any countable collection, with no assumption about how the events overlap. It is crude and it always holds. That is why it underpins multiple-testing corrections (the Bonferroni correction is this inequality) and the simplest generalisation bounds in learning theory, where the bad events are "this particular hypothesis looks good on the training set but is not".
  • Lower bound (Bonferroni). P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1. Two events that each have probability 0.9 must overlap in at least 0.8.

Inclusion–exclusion for three events, and in general

  • For three events, adding the three probabilities counts every pairwise overlap twice, so you subtract the three pairwise intersections. That removes the triple overlap three times after adding it three times, leaving it counted zero times, so you add it back once. Each region of the Venn diagram ends up counted exactly once.
  • Check by counting a single outcome. Suppose ω lies in exactly m ≥ 1 of the events. It is counted C(m,1) times in the singles, C(m,2) in the pairs, C(m,3) in the triples, and so on, giving a net count of C(m,1) − C(m,2) + C(m,3) − … = 1 − (1 − 1)ᵐ = 1 by the binomial theorem. So every outcome in the union is counted exactly once, which is the whole proof for any number of events.
  • Truncating the alternating sum gives bounds that alternate in direction (the Bonferroni inequalities): stopping after the singles over-counts, stopping after the pairs under-counts, and so on.

A classic application: nobody gets their own hat

  • n people throw their hats in a pile and each takes one at random. What is the probability that at least one person gets their own hat back? Let Aᵢ = "person i gets their own hat". Any k specified people all get their own hats with probability (n − k)!/n! (the other n − k hats are permuted freely), and there are C(n, k) ways to choose them, so the k-th inclusion–exclusion term is C(n,k)·(n−k)!/n! = 1/k!.
  • The answer is the alternating series 1 − 1/2! + 1/3! − …, which converges extremely fast to 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632. For 3 people it is 0.667, for 4 it is 0.625, and for 10 people or 10 million it is 0.632 to three decimals. The number of people barely matters, which is hard to believe until you have seen the derivation.

The axioms are not a definition of chance. They say nothing about which events are likely. They only say how probabilities must fit together. Any assignment that obeys them, sensible or absurd, is a probability. That is the strength of the approach: the rules are fixed, and all the modelling goes into choosing the numbers, which is where the next section starts.

04 Equally likely outcomes

When symmetry gives you no reason to prefer one outcome over another, the natural model gives every outcome the same weight. Probability then reduces to counting: favourable outcomes over total outcomes. This is the oldest and most useful special case. It is also an assumption about the world, and it is often false.

Classical probability

  • If Ω is finite with N outcomes and all are equally likely, normalisation forces each to have probability 1/N. Additivity then gives P(A) = |A| / N, where |A| is the number of outcomes in A. This is called the uniform distribution on Ω.
  • All the difficulty moves into counting |A| and |Ω|, which can be very hard for large spaces: card hands, passwords, seatings, lottery draws. §05 supplies the tools.
  • Two dice: Ω has 36 ordered pairs. "Sum is 7" = {(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)}, so P = 6/36 = 1/6. "Sum is 11" = {(5,6), (6,5)}, P = 2/36. "Sum is 12" = {(6,6)}, P = 1/36. Sums are not equally likely even though the pairs are. Uniformity on one sample space does not carry over to a coarser description of it.

When "equally likely" fails

  • The wrong sample space. "Two children: 0, 1 or 2 girls, so P(two girls) = 1/3." The three family types are not equally likely, because "one of each" arises two ways (GB, BG). On the ordered space {GG, GB, BG, BB} the answer is ¼, and "one of each" has probability ½. The same error with coins has a long history: {0, 1, 2} heads is a legitimate sample space, but not a uniform one.
  • The world is not symmetric. About 48.8% of newborns are girls, not 50%. Months have 28 to 31 days, so P(born in January) ≈ 31/365 rather than 1/12, and accounting for leap years in a four-year cycle of 1,461 days gives 124/1461 for each 31-day month and 113/1461 for February. Birthdays also cluster seasonally. Real dice have hollowed pips and slightly uneven faces. Each of these is a small departure, and each one matters if you multiply it across enough trials.
  • "Equally likely" depends on the description. Pick a square "at random" with side length between 0 and 1 cm. If the side is uniform, P(side < ½) = ½. If instead the area is uniform on [0, 1], then "side < ½" is "area < ¼", with probability ¼. Same words, two answers. The principle of indifference cannot choose between them, and the experiment has to say how the randomness enters.
  • Human "randomness" is not uniform. Ask people for a random number from 1 to 10 and 7 comes up far more often than one time in ten. Passwords, PINs and lottery picks chosen by people cluster on birthdays and patterns, and attackers exploit that.

Where uniformity is real: because someone built it

  • Uniformity is reliable when a randomisation device was deliberately used: a well-shuffled deck, a lottery machine, a cryptographic random generator, random assignment in an experiment, simple random sampling from a list. In those cases the uniform model describes the process.
  • That is why the design of experiments and surveys insists on randomisation: it is the only way to make the equally-likely model true by construction rather than by hope. The same idea underlies shuffling a dataset before splitting it into training and test sets.
A habit worth forming. Before dividing two counts, ask: is every outcome I am counting equally likely, and why? If the answer is "symmetry of a physical device" or "it was randomised", go ahead. If the answer is "I could not think of a reason for them to differ", you have an assumption, not a fact, and it should be stated.

05 Counting: the engine behind equally likely outcomes

Once outcomes are equally likely, every probability is a ratio of two counts, and sample spaces get large very quickly. A five-card hand has about 2.6 million possibilities and an 8-character password about 218 trillion. Combinatorics is the set of shortcuts that count these sets without listing them. One principle, multiplication, generates all of it.

The multiplication principle

  • If a procedure has k stages, stage 1 can be done in n₁ ways, and for each way of doing the earlier stages stage j can be done in nⱼ ways, then the whole procedure can be done in n₁ · n₂ · … · nₖ ways. Picture a tree: n₁ branches, each splitting into n₂, and so on. The leaves are the outcomes.
  • The condition is that the number of choices at each stage is fixed. The choices themselves can depend on earlier stages. In "choose a president, then a different vice-president, from 10 people" the vice-president options change with the president, but there are always 9 of them, so the count is 10 · 9 = 90.
  • This is the product-space count of §02: |Ω₁ × Ω₂| = |Ω₁|·|Ω₂|.

The four basic counts

  • Almost every counting problem is "choose k items from n", classified by two questions. Does order matter? (Is (a, b) a different outcome from (b, a)?) Is replacement allowed? (Can an item be chosen twice?)
  • Ordered, with replacement: n choices every time, so nᵏ. PINs, passwords, sequences of die rolls.
  • Ordered, without replacement: n, then n − 1, …, then n − k + 1 choices. This is the falling factorial n!/(n − k)!, the number of permutations of k out of n. With k = n it is n!, the number of orderings of all n items (and 0! = 1, since there is one way to arrange nothing).
  • Unordered, without replacement: each unordered set of k distinct items appears exactly k! times among the ordered selections, so divide: C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!), read "n choose k". These are combinations: committees, card hands, lottery tickets, random samples.
  • Unordered, with replacement: a multiset of size k from n types. Encode it as k stars separated into n bins by n − 1 bars. Any arrangement of the k + n − 1 symbols is one multiset, so the count is C(n + k − 1, k). Warning: these multisets are not equally likely when items are drawn at random (rolling two dice gives 21 multisets, but {3, 4} has probability 2/36 and {3, 3} only 1/36). Use this count for counting, not as a denominator for probabilities.

Binomial coefficients and Pascal's triangle

  • Symmetry: C(n, k) = C(n, n − k). Choosing the k who are in is the same as choosing the n − k who are out.
  • Pascal's rule: C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k). Proof by a story: fix one particular item. A k-subset either contains it (choose the other k − 1 from the remaining n − 1) or does not (choose all k from the remaining n − 1). These two cases are disjoint and cover everything. This is exactly how each entry of Pascal's triangle is the sum of the two above it.
  • Row sums: Σₖ C(n, k) = 2ⁿ. Both sides count all subsets of an n-set: the right side decides in or out for each item, the left side groups subsets by size.
  • Binomial theorem: (x + y)ⁿ = Σₖ C(n, k) xᵏ yⁿ⁻ᵏ. Expanding the product, each term picks x from k of the n factors. Put x = p, y = 1 − p and the binomial probabilities of §02 sum to 1ⁿ = 1. The same identity proved inclusion–exclusion in §03.
  • Vandermonde: C(m + n, k) = Σⱼ C(m, j) C(n, k − j). Choose k from a group of m men and n women, and split by how many men were chosen. Check: C(10, 4) = 210 = Σⱼ C(5, j)C(5, 4 − j). This is the identity behind the hypergeometric distribution in Random Variables & Discrete Distributions.

Multinomial coefficients: splitting into more than two groups

  • C(n, k) splits n items into two labelled groups of sizes k and n − k. To split into r labelled groups of sizes n₁, …, nᵣ (with Σnᵢ = n), choose the first group, then the second from what is left, and so on. The product telescopes to the multinomial coefficient.
  • Equivalent story: the number of distinct arrangements of a word with repeated letters. All n! orderings, divided by the nᵢ! rearrangements of each repeated letter that leave the word unchanged.
  • A subtlety: if the groups are unlabelled and equal-sized, you over-count by the number of ways to permute the groups. Splitting 4 people into two teams of 2 is C(4,2)/2! = 3, not 6, because "team 1 = {a,b}, team 2 = {c,d}" and the reverse are the same split. Most counting mistakes are of this kind: the numerator and denominator were counted under different conventions about order.

The birthday problem, fully worked

  • Question. In a group of n people, what is the probability that at least two share a birthday? Model: 365 equally likely days (ignore 29 February and seasonality; both effects are small and the second makes matches slightly more likely), and birthdays independent.
  • Complement first. "At least one shared" is a messy union over all pairs. Its complement, "all different", is a clean ordered count. The sample space of birthday sequences has 365ⁿ equally likely points. All-distinct sequences are ordered selections without replacement: 365 · 364 · … · (365 − n + 1).
  • The same product, by the multiplication rule of §06. Person 2 avoids person 1's day with probability 364/365. Given that, person 3 avoids both with probability 363/365, and so on.
  • The approximation. Take logs and use ln(1 − x) ≈ −x for small x: ln P(all distinct) ≈ −(1 + 2 + … + (n−1))/365 = −n(n−1)/730. This also explains the surprise. What matters is the number of pairs, C(n, 2) = n(n−1)/2, and each pair matches with probability 1/365. 23 people make C(23, 2) = 253 pairs, so about 253/365 ≈ 0.69 expected matching pairs, and e^(−0.69) ≈ ½.
  • The general rule (the birthday bound). With N equally likely values, collisions become likely at about √N draws. The 50% point is n ≈ 1.18 √N, from n²/(2N) = ln 2. For N = 365 that predicts 22.5, and the exact answer is 23.

  • Why it matters outside parties. The birthday bound sets the size at which hash tables start colliding, the length random identifiers need before duplicates become a risk, and the strength of a hash against collision attacks, which is half its bit length. It also explains why a "surprising coincidence" in a large dataset, such as two records with the same fingerprint or two users with the same 4-digit PIN, is usually not surprising at all. Count the pairs, not the people.
  • The question that is not surprising. "Does someone share my birthday?" is a different event: 1 − (364/365)ⁿ⁻¹, which needs n = 254 people to pass ½. The famous answer of 23 is for "any two people", which can be any of the 253 pairs. Getting the event right is half the problem.

Cards and lotteries

  • Poker hands. A hand is an unordered set of 5 cards from 52: C(52, 5) = 2,598,960 equally likely hands. To count a hand type, build it with the multiplication principle, choosing ranks first, then suits, and be careful never to order things that are not ordered.
  • Full house (three of one rank, two of another). Choose the rank of the triple (13), its 3 suits (C(4,3) = 4), the rank of the pair from the remaining 12 (12, ordered, because "triple of kings with a pair of 2s" differs from "triple of 2s with a pair of kings"), and its 2 suits (C(4,2) = 6).
  • Two pair. Here the two pair ranks are interchangeable, so choose them as an unordered set, C(13,2) = 78, not 13·12. Then suits for each pair (6 · 6) and a fifth card from the 44 of other ranks. Writing 13·12 here double-counts every hand, which is the standard error.
  • Lottery, 6 numbers from 49. C(49, 6) = 13,983,816 tickets. "Exactly 3 of your 6 numbers drawn" means choosing 3 of the 6 winners and 3 of the 43 losers. This is the hypergeometric count: sampling without replacement, split by type.

The one rule that prevents most counting errors. Count the numerator and the denominator in the same sample space, ordered in both or unordered in both. Any consistent choice gives the right ratio: P(two aces in a 2-card hand) is C(4,2)/C(52,2) = 6/1326 unordered and (4·3)/(52·51) = 12/2652 ordered, both 1/221. Mixing an ordered numerator with an unordered denominator is wrong by a factor of k!.
