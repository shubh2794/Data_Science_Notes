Probability

Statistics reasons from a sample back to the population; probability runs the other way: given a model of a chance experiment, what will the data look like? This is the overview — the map of the subject, the thread that connects the parts, and a one-screen cheat sheet. The notes themselves live on seven sub-pages, in the order the ideas depend on each other.

why The thread that runs through it

Every probability computation is the same move performed at a different scale: describe the experiment precisely enough that the uncertainty itself becomes something you can calculate with.

an experiment→a sample space→a random variable→a distribution→expectation & variance→behaviour as n → ∞
  • Two moves do most of the work. Conditioning (split the world into cases, weight each case) and complementing (compute the probability of the opposite). Bayes' rule, the law of total probability, and half the exercises in the subject are these two moves composed.
  • Expectation is linear, always. E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y) needs no independence, no distributional assumption, nothing. Variance only adds under independence — the cross term is covariance, and that asymmetry is where dependence lives.
  • Distributions are stories, not formulas. Each named family is the answer to "what does this experiment produce?" — count the successes (binomial), wait for the first one (geometric), count arrivals in a window (Poisson), wait for the next arrival (exponential). Recognise the story and the formula is free.
  • The normal is an attractor, not an assumption. Sums of many small independent contributions end up normal whatever the pieces looked like — that is the central limit theorem, and it is why the bell curve appears in places nobody put it.
  • Independence is an assumption about the world, not a property of data. It can be checked within the model, never observed directly — and nearly every clean formula in the subject quietly charges it as the price.
  • Simulation is the universal fallback. Any probability you can describe as an experiment, you can estimate by running the experiment in code — with an error bar that shrinks like 1/√n, courtesy of the limit theorems.
