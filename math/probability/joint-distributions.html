Joint Distributions & Dependence

One random variable at a time is enough to describe a die, a waiting time or a measurement error. Almost every interesting question involves several: a feature and a label, the height and weight of the same person, the errors of five models on the same test case, the returns of two stocks on the same day. This page treats random variables together. It defines the joint distribution of a pair (and of a vector), recovers the single-variable marginals from it, and makes precise what it means for variables to be independent. It measures linear dependence with covariance and correlation, proves the general variance-of-a-sum formula, and shows why averaging correlated predictors hits a floor. It conditions one variable on another by slicing and renormalising the joint density, finds the distribution of a sum by convolution and of any smooth transformation by a Jacobian, and derives the distribution of sorted samples. It ends with the multivariate normal, whose conditional means are exactly linear, with mixtures and the Dirichlet, and with the places all of this surfaces in machine learning.

01 What a joint distribution is

The joint distribution of two random variables X and Y, defined on the same sample space, says how probability is spread over the pairs of values (x, y) they can take together. For discrete variables it is a table of probabilities p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y); for continuous ones it is a surface f(x, y) whose volume over a region is the probability that the pair lands in that region. The single-variable distributions of X and of Y, called the marginals, are recovered from it by summing or integrating out the other variable. The reverse is impossible: the marginals do not determine the joint, and everything this page calls "dependence" lives in the difference.

Two variables on one sample space

  • Where pairs come from. A random variable is a function on the sample space Ω (Random Variables & Discrete Distributions). Two random variables are two functions on the same Ω, and each outcome ω produces a pair (X(ω), Y(ω)). Throw two dice: Ω is the 36-point product space of ordered pairs of faces, and from one outcome we can read off the sum S, the maximum M, the first face D₁, all at once. Because they are computed from the same outcome, learning one of them can change what we believe about another.
  • The pair is a random point. (X, Y) is a random point in the plane, and its distribution is a distribution on the plane: for every region A ⊂ ℝ², a probability P((X, Y) ∈ A). The joint pmf, joint density and joint cdf below are three ways of writing that one object down.
  • More than two. For n variables the pair becomes a random vector X = (X₁, …, Xₙ), a random point in ℝⁿ, and everything on this page generalises with sums and integrals over n coordinates. Most statements are made for pairs because the pictures are planar; the vector versions are stated where they matter (§03, §09, §10).

The discrete case: a two-way table

  • Definition. The joint probability mass function of discrete X and Y is p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y), the probability that both happen. It satisfies p(x, y) ≥ 0 and Σₓ Σᵧ p(x, y) = 1, and for any set A of pairs, P((X, Y) ∈ A) = Σ_((x, y)∈A) p(x, y), by additivity over the disjoint events {X = x, Y = y}.

The marginals do not determine the joint

  • Take two 0/1 variables with the table p(0, 0) = ¼ − ε, p(0, 1) = ¼ + ε, p(1, 0) = ¼ + ε, p(1, 1) = ¼ − ε, for any ε between −¼ and ¼. Every row and every column sums to ½, so every member of this family has the same two marginals: two fair coins. At ε = 0 the coins are independent; at ε = ¼ they always disagree; at ε = −¼ they always agree. Looking at each coin separately cannot tell these situations apart.
  • What this means. Two marginal distributions are two lists of numbers; the joint is a table, with one more degree of freedom per extra cell. The dependence between the variables is exactly the information in the table that is not in its margins. Covariance (§03) summarises that information in one number; the copula (§11) captures it in full when the marginals are continuous (for discrete variables like these coins the copula is not unique, and the table itself is the honest description).
  • A practical consequence. Two separately collected datasets, one recording X for a population and another recording Y, can never reveal how X and Y are related, however large they are. To learn a joint distribution you must observe the pairs together. That is why a model of P(label | features) needs labelled examples, not a pile of features and a separate pile of labels.

The joint cdf

  • Definition. The joint distribution function is F(x, y) = P(X ≤ x, Y ≤ y), the probability that the random point falls in the quadrant to the south-west of (x, y). Unlike the pmf it is defined in the same way for discrete, continuous and mixed pairs, and it determines the joint distribution completely.
  • Rectangles by inclusion–exclusion. P(a < X ≤ b, c < Y ≤ d) = F(b, d) − F(a, d) − F(b, c) + F(a, c). The quadrant at (b, d) contains the rectangle plus two strips; subtracting the quadrants at (a, d) and (b, c) removes the strips but removes the quadrant at (a, c) twice, so it is added back. That this combination is never negative is the two-dimensional version of "a cdf is non-decreasing", and it is a genuine extra condition: a function can increase in each coordinate separately and still fail it.
  • Marginal cdfs are limits. F_X(x) = P(X ≤ x) = lim_(y→∞) F(x, y), because the events {X ≤ x, Y ≤ y} increase to {X ≤ x} as y grows. Likewise F_Y(y) = F(∞, y). For the dice, F(5, 3) = P(S ≤ 5, M ≤ 3) = 8/36, while F(5, 6) = P(S ≤ 5) = 10/36.

The continuous case: a density surface

  • Definition. X and Y have a joint density f(x, y) ≥ 0 if, for every region A of the plane, P((X, Y) ∈ A) = ∬_A f(x, y) dx dy. In particular ∬ f = 1 over the whole plane. The probability of a region is the volume under the surface above it, exactly as a one-dimensional probability is an area under a curve (densities).
  • Reading a density. For a small rectangle of sides dx and dy at (x, y), P(X ∈ [x, x + dx], Y ∈ [y, y + dy]) ≈ f(x, y) dx dy. So f is probability per unit area; it is not a probability, can exceed 1, and every single point and every curve in the plane has probability zero. Where the cdf is smooth, f(x, y) = ∂²F/∂x∂y.
  • Marginal densities by integrating out. f_X(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy and f_Y(y) = ∫ f(x, y) dx: the continuous version of row and column sums. Proof: P(X ≤ a) = ∫_(−∞)^a [∫ f(x, y) dy] dx, so the bracket is the density of X.
  • Worked: a density on a rectangle. Let f(x, y) = (2/75)(2x²y + xy²) for 0 ≤ x ≤ 3, 1 ≤ y ≤ 2, and 0 elsewhere. It integrates to 1. Then P(1 ≤ X ≤ 2, 4/3 ≤ Y ≤ 5/3) = 187/2025 = 0.0923; the marginals are f_X(x) = (2/225)(9x² + 7x) on [0, 3] and f_Y(y) = (3y² + 12y)/25 on [1, 2]. Section §03 computes its covariance.
  • Worked: a density on a curved region. Let f(x, y) = c x²y on the region x² ≤ y ≤ 1 (so −1 ≤ x ≤ 1). The constant: ∫₋₁¹ ∫_(x²)^1 x²y dy dx = ∫₋₁¹ x²(1 − x⁴)/2 dx = 4/21, so c = 21/4. The region does the work: for each x, y runs from the parabola to 1. Then P(X ≥ Y) = (21/4) ∫₀¹ ∫_(x²)^x x²y dy dx = 3/20, and the marginal of X is f_X(x) = (21/8) x²(1 − x⁴). On a non-rectangular region, the limits of integration of the inner integral depend on the outer variable, and getting them right is most of the work.

Random vectors, and a marginal that surprises

  • n variables. The joint pmf p(x₁, …, xₙ) = P(X₁ = x₁, …, Xₙ = xₙ) or joint density f(x₁, …, xₙ) describes the whole vector; the marginal of any sub-collection is obtained by summing or integrating out all the others. The joint distribution of a vector carries every marginal of every subset, and all the dependence between them.
  • Worked: drawing without replacement. Draw n balls one by one, without replacement, from N balls numbered 1 to N, and let Xᵢ be the number on the i-th ball. Every ordered sequence of distinct numbers has probability 1/(N(N − 1)⋯(N − n + 1)), so the draws are clearly dependent (no number can repeat). Yet summing out all coordinates but the i-th leaves (N − 1)(N − 2)⋯(N − n + 1) equal terms, and P(Xᵢ = k) = 1/N: the third ball, or the last, is exactly as likely to be any given number as the first. The draws are exchangeable, each one marginally uniform, which is the fact behind the hypergeometric mean nK/N (linearity) and the dependence that §03 measures.
  • Mixed pairs. One coordinate can be discrete and the other continuous: a class label C and a measured feature X, a diagnosis and a blood test, a count and an exposure time. The joint is then described by P(C = c, X ∈ B) = ∫_B p(c) f(x | c) dx, a pmf in one coordinate times a density in the other. This is the joint distribution behind every classifier, and §05 conditions it in both directions.
The joint is the whole story; the marginals are its shadows. Every probability statement about X alone, or Y alone, can be read off the marginals. Every statement about how they move together (whether a high X comes with a high Y, what Y is likely to be once X is seen, how variable X + Y is) needs the joint. The rest of this page is a set of tools for extracting those statements.

02 Independence of random variables

Two random variables are independent when every event about one is independent of every event about the other: P(X ∈ A, Y ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A) P(Y ∈ B) for all sets A and B. In terms of distributions, the joint is the product of the marginals: the table is an outer product of its margins, the density surface factorises. Independence is the assumption that turns joint distributions back into single-variable ones, it is inherited by functions of the variables, and the "independent and identically distributed" sample built from it is the starting point of almost all of statistics and machine learning.

Three equivalent definitions

  • Events. X and Y are independent (X ⊥ Y) if P(X ∈ A, Y ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A) · P(Y ∈ B) for all (measurable) sets A, B. That is the independence of events from Probability Foundations, required for every event determined by X and every event determined by Y at once.
  • The cdf factorises. It is enough to check the quadrants: F(x, y) = F_X(x) F_Y(y) for all x, y. The rectangle formula of §01 then factorises too, and every set can be built from rectangles. This version covers discrete, continuous and mixed variables at once.
  • The pmf or density factorises. For discrete variables, p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y) for every pair. For continuous ones, f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y) (up to sets of zero area): differentiate F(x, y) = F_X(x)F_Y(y) in x and y, or integrate the product to get the cdf back. A joint table is independent exactly when it is the outer product of its margins: every row is proportional to every other row.
  • n variables. X₁, …, Xₙ are (mutually) independent if F(x₁, …, xₙ) = Π F_(Xᵢ)(xᵢ), equivalently the joint pmf or density is Π fᵢ(xᵢ). As with events, pairwise independence is weaker: let X and Y be independent fair bits and Z = X ⊕ Y (1 when exactly one of them is 1). Each pair is independent, but Z is a function of the other two, so the three are not mutually independent (pairwise is not enough).

Checking a table, and the factorisation shortcut

  • A table that factorises. The two-bit table p(0, 0) = 1/9, p(0, 1) = 2/9, p(1, 0) = 2/9, p(1, 1) = 4/9 has margins (⅓, ⅔) for both variables, and every cell equals the product of its margins (⅓ · ⅓ = 1/9, ⅓ · ⅔ = 2/9, ⅔ · ⅔ = 4/9): independent. The second row is twice the first, which is the "rows proportional" test.
  • One zero is enough to break it. If some cell is 0 while both its margins are positive, the product p_X(x)p_Y(y) is positive and the table cannot factorise. The dice table of §01 has many such zeros (P(S = 12, M = 1) = 0 while P(S = 12) P(M = 1) > 0), so S and M are dependent at a glance. More generally, if the set of possible pairs is not a rectangle (a product of the possible x values and the possible y values), the variables are dependent.
  • The factorisation shortcut. If f(x, y) = g(x) h(y) for some non-negative functions g and h, and the region where f > 0 is a rectangle (possibly infinite), then X ⊥ Y; g and h need not be densities, since the normalising constants can be shuffled between them. Example: f(x, y) = 2e^(−(x + 2y)) on x, y > 0 is e^(−x) · 2e^(−2y), so X ~ Exp(1) and Y ~ Exp(2) are independent.
  • The support trap. The density (21/4)x²y of §01 is a product of a function of x and a function of y, but its support x² ≤ y ≤ 1 is not a rectangle: learning X = 0.9 forces Y ≥ 0.81. So X and Y are dependent. Likewise f(x, y) = 8xy on 0 < x < y < 1 looks like a product and is not one. The shape of the region where the density lives is part of the formula.

Computing with independence

  • Worked: a sum below a threshold. X and Y are independent with the same density 2x on [0, 1]. The joint density is 4xy on the unit square, and P(X + Y ≤ 1) = ∫₀¹ ∫₀^(1−x) 4xy dy dx = ∫₀¹ 2x(1 − x)² dx = 1/6.
  • Worked: a race of exponentials. X ~ Exp(λ) and Y ~ Exp(μ) are independent (two components failing, two customers arriving). Then P(X < Y) = ∫₀^∞ λe^(−λx) e^(−μx) dx = λ/(λ + μ): the chance that X wins is its share of the total rate. And P(min(X, Y) > t) = P(X > t) P(Y > t) = e^(−(λ + μ)t), so the first failure is Exp(λ + μ). A unit with rate 1 racing one with rate 2 wins with probability ⅓, and the first of them fails after a mean time of ⅓.
  • Expectations of products. If X ⊥ Y then E[g(X) h(Y)] = E g(X) · E h(Y) for all functions with finite means, because the joint pmf or density factorises inside the double sum or integral; this is the product rule of Expectation, Variance & Inequalities. The converse also holds if it is required for all bounded g and h (take indicators of sets), but not if it is checked only for g(x) = x, h(y) = y: that weaker condition is "uncorrelated" (§03).

Functions of independent variables are independent

  • Statement. If X₁, …, Xₙ are independent and Yᵢ = hᵢ(Xᵢ), then Y₁, …, Yₙ are independent. Proof for two: P(h₁(X₁) ∈ A, h₂(X₂) ∈ B) = P(X₁ ∈ h₁⁻¹(A), X₂ ∈ h₂⁻¹(B)), which factorises because {X₁ ∈ h₁⁻¹(A)} is an event about X₁ alone.
  • Blocks. More generally, functions of disjoint groups of mutually independent variables are independent: X₁ + X₂ is independent of X₃ X₄, and the first half of an iid sample is independent of any summary of the second half. That is what licenses "split the data, compute a statistic on each half, treat them as independent".
  • Why it matters in simulation. To generate independent waiting times T and service times S from different distributions, feed separate uniform draws into separate inverse cdfs: T = F⁻¹(U₁), S = G⁻¹(U₂). Independence of the uniforms is inherited by the outputs. Sharing a uniform between them, by contrast, makes them perfectly dependent, the trick behind common random numbers.

iid: the modelling atom

  • Definition. X₁, …, Xₙ are independent and identically distributed (iid) with distribution F when they are mutually independent and each has marginal F. Their joint density is then Π f(xᵢ), one factor per observation. A "random sample of size n from F" means exactly this.
  • The likelihood is this product. If f depends on a parameter θ, the joint density of the observed data, read as a function of θ, is the likelihood L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ; θ), and its logarithm is a sum, Σ ln f(xᵢ; θ). Every average training loss of the form (1/n) Σ ℓ(xᵢ) is a negative log-likelihood of this shape, and the sum is only justified by the product, which is only justified by independence.
  • Identically distributed but dependent. The draws without replacement of §01 all have the same marginal but are not independent: they are exchangeable (the joint distribution is unchanged by permuting the draws) rather than iid. Exchangeability is weaker than iid and is often all that a method actually needs: the rank-based guarantees of §08 and the de Finetti view of §10 both run on it.

Checking independence versus assuming it

  • It is almost always an assumption. Independence is a property of the joint distribution, which is not observed; what is observed is a finite sample of pairs. With categorical data, independence can be tested by comparing observed cell counts with the products of the margins (the chi-square test of independence in a two-way table), but a test can only fail to find dependence, never establish independence.
  • Zero correlation is not a check. A sample correlation near zero rules out only linear association. The variables X and X² with X symmetric about 0 are uncorrelated and completely dependent (§03).
  • Where it fails in practice. Repeated measurements on the same patient, pixels of the same image, sentences from the same document, consecutive days of a time series, users who influence each other: all violate independence, and a method that assumes it will treat n correlated observations as if they were n independent pieces of evidence, overstating its certainty. §04 quantifies the damage: n observations with pairwise correlation ρ are worth only n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) independent ones. Random train/test splits of grouped or time-ordered data, which leak information between the halves, are the machine-learning form of the same mistake (leakage).
  • Independence given something. Often variables are dependent overall but independent once a common cause is known: two symptoms of the same disease, two features of the same class. That is conditional independence, defined in §05, and it is the assumption behind naive Bayes (§11).
Independence is the cheapest joint distribution. Specifying a general joint pmf on k values for each of n variables takes kⁿ − 1 numbers; under independence it takes n(k − 1). Ten binary features need 1,023 numbers in general and 10 under independence. That economy is why independence is assumed so often, and why it is the first assumption to question when a model's probabilities turn out to be badly calibrated.

03 Covariance: the gap in the product rule

For independent variables E XY = E X · E Y; for dependent ones the two sides differ, and the difference is the covariance, Cov(X, Y) = E XY − E X · E Y = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)]. Its sign says whether large values of one tend to come with large or small values of the other; its size enters every variance of a sum. This section develops it in full: the definition and its two forms, the rules that make it behave like a product, the general variance formula for sums of dependent variables, the counterexamples that separate "uncorrelated" from "independent", and the covariances that produce the finite-population correction of the hypergeometric and the negative dependence of multinomial counts.

Definition, and what the sign means

  • Definition. For variables with finite variances, Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)], the average product of the deviations from the means. It is computed from the joint distribution by LOTUS for pairs: Σₓ Σᵧ (x − μ_X)(y − μ_Y) p(x, y) or the corresponding double integral.
  • The shortcut. Expanding the product and using linearity, E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)] = E XY − μ_X E Y − μ_Y E X + μ_X μ_Y = E XY − E X · E Y. This is the "gap in the product rule" that Expectation, Variance & Inequalities left open: the product rule E XY = E X E Y holds exactly when the covariance is zero.
  • The four quadrants. Draw axes through the point of means (μ_X, μ_Y). A pair (x, y) in the north-east or south-west quadrant has deviations of the same sign and contributes a positive product; a pair in the north-west or south-east contributes a negative one. Each product is the signed area of the rectangle between the point and the centre. The covariance is the probability-weighted average of those areas: positive when the mass sits mostly in the agreeing quadrants (large with large, small with small), negative when it sits in the disagreeing ones, near zero when the two kinds balance.
  • Units. Cov(X, Y) is in units of X times units of Y: heights in centimetres and weights in kilograms give a covariance in cm·kg, and changing to inches divides it by 2.54. Its sign is meaningful, its size on its own is not; correlation (§04) is the scale-free version.

Worked covariances

  • The sum and the maximum of two dice. E S = 7 and E M = 161/36. Rather than summing 36 products, use symmetry. Let N be the minimum, so S = M + N. Replacing each face d by 7 − d leaves the distribution of the two dice unchanged but turns M into 7 − N and S into 14 − S, so Cov(S, M) = Cov(14 − S, 7 − N) = Cov(S, N). The two covariances add to Cov(S, M + N) = Var S = 35/6, so each is 35/12 = 2.917: a large maximum comes with a large sum, as it must.
  • A density on a rectangle. For f(x, y) = (2/75)(2x²y + xy²) on [0, 3] × [1, 2] of §01, the double integrals give E X = 109/50, E Y = 157/100 and E XY = 171/50, so Cov(X, Y) = 171/50 − (109/50)(157/100) = −13/5000 = −0.0026. The variables are very slightly negatively related, which is not visible in the formula for f; it takes the computation to see it.
  • Indicators. For events A and B, Cov(1_A, 1_B) = E[1_A 1_B] − P(A)P(B) = P(A ∩ B) − P(A)P(B). It is positive exactly when P(B | A) > P(B), when A makes B more likely. So the covariance of indicators is the "how far from independent" gap of event independence, and the two hat-matching events of the hat problem have covariance 1/(n(n − 1)) − 1/n² = 1/(n²(n − 1)) > 0: one person's match makes another's slightly more likely.

The rules: covariance behaves like a product

  • Symmetry and the diagonal. Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X), and Cov(X, X) = E(X − μ_X)² = Var X: variance is the covariance of a variable with itself.
  • Constants drop out. Cov(X, c) = 0 for a constant c, and Cov(X + a, Y + b) = Cov(X, Y): shifting either variable changes nothing, since covariance only sees deviations from the mean.
  • Bilinearity. Cov(aX + bY, Z) = a Cov(X, Z) + b Cov(Y, Z), and the same in the second argument. Proof: the deviation of aX + bY from its mean is a(X − μ_X) + b(Y − μ_Y); multiply by (Z − μ_Z) and use linearity of expectation. Iterating, Cov(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ, Σⱼ bⱼYⱼ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢbⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Yⱼ): expand like a product of sums, and every pair of terms contributes. In particular Cov(rX + s, tY + u) = rt Cov(X, Y).
  • Worked. With Cov(X, Y) = −13/5000 above, Cov(−2X + 7, 5Y − 3) = (−2)(5)(−13/5000) = 13/500: the sign flips once for the −2, and the scale grows by 10.

Delivered: the variance of a sum, with every covariance term

  • Two variables. Bilinearity with X + Y in both slots: Var(X + Y) = Cov(X + Y, X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y). This is the general formula that Expectation, Variance & Inequalities stated for the uncorrelated case.
  • With weights. Var(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢaⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). Collect the covariances in the covariance matrix Σ with entries Σᵢⱼ = Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ); then the formula is the quadratic form Var(aᵀX) = aᵀΣa.

The covariance matrix

  • Definition. For a random vector X = (X₁, …, Xₙ) with mean vector μ, the covariance matrix is Σ = E[(X − μ)(X − μ)ᵀ], the n × n matrix of all pairwise covariances, with the variances on the diagonal. It is symmetric.
  • It is positive semi-definite. For every vector a, aᵀΣa = Var(aᵀX) ≥ 0, because a variance cannot be negative. So Σ has non-negative eigenvalues, and it is singular exactly when some linear combination aᵀX is constant, a linear dependence among the coordinates. Not every symmetric table of numbers with a positive diagonal is a covariance matrix; it must pass this test. The covariance matrix as a Gram matrix, and its eigenvectors as principal directions, are developed in the covariance matrix of the linear-algebra notes.
  • Linear maps. For a fixed matrix A and vector b, Y = AX + b has mean Aμ + b and covariance AΣAᵀ: bilinearity in matrix form. With A a single row aᵀ this is aᵀΣa again.

Uncorrelated is not independent

  • Independent ⟹ uncorrelated. If X ⊥ Y, the product rule gives E XY = E X E Y, so Cov(X, Y) = 0. Variables with zero covariance are called uncorrelated.
  • The converse fails: the classic counterexample. Let X be symmetric about 0 with E X³ finite, and Y = X². Then Cov(X, X²) = E X³ − E X · E X² = 0 − 0 = 0, because the odd moments of a symmetric variable vanish. Yet Y is a function of X: knowing X tells you Y exactly. With X uniform on {−1, 0, 1}: P(X = 0, Y = 0) = ⅓ while P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = ⅓ · ⅓ = 1/9. With X ~ N(0, 1) or X ~ U(−1, 1) the scatter of (X, X²) is a perfect parabola with correlation exactly zero. The positive products on the right arm cancel the negative products on the left arm.
  • The diamond. X and Y take values 0, 1, 2, with probability ¼ on each of the four points (1, 0), (0, 1), (2, 1), (1, 2). Both have mean 1, and E XY = (0 + 0 + 2 + 2)/4 = 1 = E X E Y: uncorrelated. But P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0 ≠ P(X = 0)P(Y = 0) = 1/16: dependent. Given X = 1, Y is 0 or 2; given X = 0, Y must be 1. The dependence is real but has no linear direction.
  • The circle. A point uniform on the unit circle, (cos Θ, sin Θ) with Θ ~ U(0, 2π): E cos Θ sin Θ = ½ E sin 2Θ = 0, so the coordinates are uncorrelated, and yet X² + Y² = 1 exactly. Likewise the sum and difference of two iid uniforms: Cov(U + V, U − V) = Var U − Var V = 0, but the pair lives on a tilted square, so learning U + V = 1.9 forces |U − V| ≤ 0.1.
  • When the converse does hold. For two indicators, uncorrelated does mean independent (P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) gives all four cells). And for a pair with a joint normal distribution, zero covariance implies independence (§09). Outside those cases, "uncorrelated" says only that there is no linear trend.

04 Correlation: covariance without units

The correlation coefficient ρ(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) is the covariance of the standardised variables: it does not change when either variable is shifted or rescaled, it always lies between −1 and 1, and it reaches ±1 exactly when one variable is a linear function of the other. Those properties make it the standard summary of how strongly two quantities move together, and they also mark its limits: it measures linear association and nothing else. Its most consequential use is in averages: the variance of a mean of correlated quantities has a floor set by their correlation, which is why diversification works only up to a point and why an ensemble of similar models is worth fewer models than it contains.

Definition and invariance

  • Definition. For variables with positive finite variances, ρ(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) = E[Z_X Z_Y], where Z_X = (X − μ_X)/σ_X and Z_Y are the z-scores. It is the average product of standardised deviations, a pure number.
  • Affine maps. ρ(aX + b, cY + d) = sign(ac) · ρ(X, Y) for a, c ≠ 0: the covariance picks up ac and the standard deviations pick up |a||c|. Converting temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit or heights from inches to centimetres leaves every correlation unchanged; reversing the direction of one scale flips its sign. For the rectangle density of §01 and §03, ρ(X, Y) = −0.0026/√(0.3956 × 0.0791) = −0.0147, and ρ(−2X + 7, 5Y − 3) = +0.0147.
  • Worked: two dice. ρ(S, M) = (35/12)/√((35/6)(2555/1296)) = 0.860: knowing the maximum says a lot about the sum, but not everything.

Delivered: |ρ| ≤ 1, with equality only for a straight line

  • From Cauchy–Schwarz. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the centred variables gives Cov(X, Y)² ≤ Var X · Var Y, hence |ρ| ≤ 1. Here is a second proof, using only the variance of a sum, that also identifies the equality case.
  • The variance proof. For the z-scores, 0 ≤ Var(Z_X + Z_Y) = 1 + 1 + 2ρ = 2(1 + ρ), so ρ ≥ −1; and 0 ≤ Var(Z_X − Z_Y) = 2(1 − ρ), so ρ ≤ 1.
  • The equality case. ρ = 1 means Var(Z_X − Z_Y) = 0, so Z_Y = Z_X with probability 1 (a zero-variance variable is constant, and this one has mean 0). Unwinding the standardisation: Y = μ_Y + (σ_Y/σ_X)(X − μ_X), a straight line with positive slope. Likewise ρ = −1 forces a line with negative slope. Conversely, if Y = a + bX with b ≠ 0, then ρ = sign(b). So |ρ| = 1 exactly when all the probability lies on a line.
  • The geometry. For centred variables, E XY is an inner product and σ is a length, so ρ is the cosine of the angle between X − μ_X and Y − μ_Y. Uncorrelated means orthogonal; ρ = ±1 means parallel. The same picture, with data vectors in place of random variables, gives the sample correlation as the cosine between centred columns.

What ρ² measures: the best line

  • The best linear predictor. Among all lines a + bX, the one minimising E(Y − a − bX)² has b = Cov(X, Y)/Var X = ρσ_Y/σ_X and a = μ_Y − bμ_X (set the two partial derivatives to zero; this is the population version of least squares, and it appeared in the best-predictor discussion).
  • Its error. Substituting, E(Y − a − bX)² = Var(Y − bX) = σ_Y² − 2bCov(X, Y) + b²σ_X² = σ_Y²(1 − ρ²). So ρ² is the fraction of the variance of Y that the best straight line removes, and 1 − ρ² the fraction left. A correlation of 0.5 removes only 25% of the variance; 0.9 removes 81%; even 0.99 leaves 2%. A correlation that "looks high" can leave most of the variance unexplained.
  • Correlation is symmetric, prediction is not. The best line for predicting Y from X has slope ρσ_Y/σ_X; the best line for predicting X from Y, drawn in the same plane, has slope σ_Y/(ρσ_X). They coincide only if |ρ| = 1. The two regression lines and the regression to the mean between them are drawn in §09.

Correlation measures linear association, and only that

  • Zero correlation, perfect dependence. Y = X² with X symmetric has ρ = 0 (§03). The dependence is identical in kind; the correlation changes from 0 to 0.97 because on (0, 1) the parabola happens to be nearly straight.
  • The same ρ, different pictures. Four small datasets can share their means, variances and a correlation of 0.82 while one is a clean linear cloud, one a smooth curve, one a perfect line with one outlier, and one a vertical stack with a single far point that creates the whole correlation. A correlation is a summary of a scatterplot; it is never a substitute for looking at one. The data-side treatment, with the plots, is in the correlation coefficient, and describing two variables covers the scatterplot itself.
  • One point can make or break it. Because correlation averages products of deviations, a single observation far from the centre in both coordinates contributes a huge product. Add one point at (6, 6) to 99 independent standard normal pairs: it contributes 36 to a sum of products that otherwise averages zero, against total sums of squares of about 99 + 36 in each coordinate, so the correlation jumps from about 0 to about 36/135 = 0.27. Rank correlations (Spearman's, which correlates the ranks instead of the values) are the robust alternative; they measure monotone rather than linear association.
  • Correlations constrain each other. The correlation matrix of three variables must be positive semi-definite, which bounds ρ_XZ given ρ_XY and ρ_YZ: ρ_XZ ≥ ρ_XY ρ_YZ − √((1 − ρ_XY²)(1 − ρ_YZ²)). Correlation is not transitive, unless the two links are strong enough (ρ_XY² + ρ_YZ² > 1).
  • Correlation is not causation. A correlation between X and Y can arise because X affects Y, because Y affects X, or because a third variable drives both (shoe size and reading ability in schoolchildren, both driven by age). Nothing in the joint distribution alone distinguishes these; see association versus causation.

Averages of correlated quantities: why diversification has a floor

  • The formula. Let X₁, …, Xₙ each have variance σ² and every pair have correlation ρ. By §03, Var(Σ Xᵢ) = nσ² + n(n − 1)ρσ², so the average has
    Var X̄ = σ²/n + ((n − 1)/n)·ρσ² = σ²·(ρ + (1 − ρ)/n) → ρσ² as n → ∞
    The independent part (1 − ρ)σ²/n averages away; the shared part ρσ² does not. However many correlated quantities are averaged, the variance never falls below ρσ².
  • Effective sample size. Setting σ²(ρ + (1 − ρ)/n) = σ²/n_eff: n_eff = n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ), the number of independent quantities the average is worth. With ρ = 0.2, ten quantities are worth 3.6 independent ones, and infinitely many are worth at most 1/ρ = 5. With ρ = 0.05, a hundred are worth 16.8. This is the same "design effect" that makes clustered survey samples less informative than their size suggests.
  • A portfolio. Two assets with return standard deviations 0.20 and 0.30. A 50/50 mix has standard deviation √(0.25 × 0.04 + 0.25 × 0.09 + 2 × 0.25 × ρ × 0.06): 0.250 if ρ = 1 (no diversification, just the average risk), 0.218 at ρ = 0.5, 0.180 at ρ = 0, 0.132 at ρ = −0.5. The minimum-variance weight on the first asset is w* = (σ₂² − ρσ₁σ₂)/(σ₁² + σ₂² − 2ρσ₁σ₂); at ρ = 0.2 it is 0.736, with standard deviation 0.181, below that of either asset alone. Combining risky things reduces risk only to the extent that they are not perfectly correlated.
  • Why correlated errors ruin ensembles. An ensemble that averages B models' predictions has error variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B, where σ² is one model's variance and ρ the correlation between models' errors: the formula above, unchanged. Training the same architecture on the same data with different seeds gives highly correlated errors, and the ensemble gains little; bagging, random feature subsets and different model families exist to lower ρ. A random forest's per-split feature sampling is a decorrelation device (ensembles of trees, bagging). The same arithmetic explains why a "wisdom of crowds" average fails when the crowd shares its information.
  • Negative correlation helps. With ρ < 0 the floor disappears, and for a given n the variance is below σ²/n (a common negative ρ is only possible for n ≤ 1 + 1/|ρ|); the smallest possible common correlation among n variables is −1/(n − 1), at which the average is constant. Deliberately pairing each simulated draw with a negatively correlated twin (antithetic variates) is a standard variance-reduction trick, developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

05 Conditional distributions: slicing and renormalising

Once X = x is observed, the only pairs still possible are those on the line {X = x}, and the right distribution for Y is the joint distribution restricted to that line and rescaled to total 1. That is the conditional distribution of Y given X = x: a row or column of the joint table divided by its margin, or a slice of the joint density divided by the marginal density. Read the other way, it says that every joint distribution factors as a marginal times a conditional, which is how hierarchical models and two-stage experiments are built, and it gives Bayes' rule for densities, the engine of every posterior.

Discrete: a row of the table, renormalised

  • Definition. For x with p_X(x) > 0, the conditional pmf of Y given X = x is p_(Y|X)(y | x) = P(Y = y | X = x) = p(x, y)/p_X(x). It is conditional probability from Probability Foundations, applied to the events {Y = y} and {X = x}. For each fixed x it is a pmf in y: non-negative, and summing to p_X(x)/p_X(x) = 1.
  • Worked: the sum given the maximum. In the dice table of §01, the column M = 4 holds 2/36, 2/36, 2/36, 1/36 at S = 5, 6, 7, 8, with total P(M = 4) = 7/36. So p_(Y|X)(y | x) ∝ p(x, y) as a function of y: to find a conditional distribution, write down the joint, treat x as a constant, and normalise. This "proportional to" reasoning is the working form of Bayes' rule below.
  • Conditional expectation is the mean of the slice. E[Y | X = x] = Σᵧ y p_(Y|X)(y | x), and Var(Y | X = x) its variance. The tower rule, the best-predictor property and the law of total variance of conditional expectation are all statements about these slice means and slice variances.

Continuous: a slice of the density, renormalised

  • Definition. For x with f_X(x) > 0, the conditional density of Y given X = x is f_(Y|X)(y | x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x), and P(Y ∈ B | X = x) = ∫_B f_(Y|X)(y | x) dy. Geometrically: cut the density surface with the vertical plane X = x, take the curve that the cut traces, and rescale it to have area 1. The area of the raw slice is exactly ∫ f(x, y) dy = f_X(x), which is why dividing by the marginal is the right rescaling.

In practice, conditioning on X = x for a named variable X, as here, is unambiguous.

Joint = marginal × conditional: the two-stage view

  • The multiplication rule for distributions. Rearranging the definition, f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_(Y|X)(y | x) = f_Y(y) f_(X|Y)(x | y), and the same with pmfs. A joint distribution can therefore be built in two stages: draw X from its marginal, then draw Y from the conditional given the value drawn. This is the form in which most models are written: a cause, then an effect given the cause.
  • Worked: a two-stage draw. X ~ U(0, 1); given X = x, Y ~ U(x, 1). Then f(x, y) = 1 · 1/(1 − x) on 0 < x < y < 1, and integrating out x, f_Y(y) = ∫₀^y dx/(1 − x) = −ln(1 − y) on (0, 1). Its mean is ¾, as the tower rule found in one line in conditional expectation; the density is what the two-stage view adds.
  • The law of total probability for densities. f_Y(y) = ∫ f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x) dx: the marginal of Y is an average of the conditional densities, weighted by the distribution of X. It is the continuous form of total probability, and when X is discrete it is a mixture (§10).
  • Independence in this language. X ⊥ Y exactly when the conditional distribution of Y given X = x is the same for every x (and then equals the marginal of Y). In the figure, independence would mean every slice has the same normalised shape.
  • Conditional independence. X and Y are conditionally independent given Z if f(x, y | z) = f(x | z) f(y | z) for every z. Two noisy thermometers in the same room are dependent (both track the room temperature) but independent given that temperature; this is the structure that naive Bayes assumes for features given the class, and that graphical models encode in their missing edges. Conditional independence neither implies nor is implied by independence: two independent coins become dependent once you are told whether they agreed.

Bayes' rule for densities

  • The rule. Equating the two factorisations, f_(X|Y)(x | y) = f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x)/f_Y(y), with f_Y(y) = ∫ f_(Y|X)(y | x′) f_X(x′) dx′. As a function of x, f_(X|Y)(x | y) ∝ f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x): posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. It is Bayes' rule with densities in place of probabilities, and it holds just as well when one variable is discrete and the other continuous.
  • A discrete cause, a continuous observation. A class C with prior P(C = c) produces a feature with density f(x | c). Then P(C = c | X = x) = P(C = c) f(x | c)/Σ_c′ P(C = c′) f(x | c′): a probability computed from densities. Every generative classifier, from naive Bayes to a Gaussian mixture's "responsibilities" (§10), evaluates this ratio.
  • A continuous parameter, discrete data: the beta–binomial model. Let the unknown success probability be P ~ Beta(a, b), and given P = p, let K | P = p ~ Bin(n, p). The joint "density" (a density in p, a pmf in k) is
    f(p, k) = [p^(a−1)(1 − p)^(b−1)/B(a, b)] · C(n, k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ 0 < p < 1, k = 0, 1, …, n
    Treating k as fixed, the posterior is proportional to p^(a+k−1)(1 − p)^(b+n−k−1), the shape of Beta(a + k, b + n − k), the conjugate update of the beta distribution. What the joint adds is the other factorisation: integrating out p with the beta integral gives the marginal (prior predictive) distribution of the count, P(K = k) = C(n, k) B(a + k, b + n − k)/B(a, b), the beta-binomial distribution.
  • Worked: prior Beta(2, 2), 7 successes in 10. The posterior is Beta(9, 5): mean 9/14 = 0.643, central 95% interval 0.386 to 0.861, and P(p > ½ | data) = 0.867, up from ½ under the symmetric prior. Before the data, the beta-binomial gave P(K = 7) = 120 · B(9, 5)/B(2, 2) = 0.1119; its mean is 5 and its variance n·ab(a + b + n)/((a + b)²(a + b + 1)) = 7, far above the binomial 10 × ¼ = 2.5, because uncertainty about p adds the between-p variance of the law of total variance.
  • A flat prior gives a flat count. With a = b = 1, P(K = k) = C(n, k)·k!(n − k)!/(n + 1)! = 1/(n + 1) for every k: if the success probability is uniform, the number of successes is uniform on {0, …, n}. That is the uniform count found for the hierarchical binomial in the law of total variance, now derived from the joint distribution.

06 Sums of random variables: convolution

The mean and variance of a sum need only the means, variances and covariances of the terms. The distribution of a sum needs more: Z = X + Y equals z along the whole line x + y = z, so its pmf or density is the total joint probability along that line. For independent terms that total is the convolution of the two distributions: flip one, slide it past the other, and integrate the product. This section computes it, proves with it that Poisson counts and gamma times are closed under addition, shows why two uniforms add to a triangle, and cross-checks every result against the moment generating function.

Discrete convolution

  • The line. {X + Y = z} is the disjoint union of {X = z − y, Y = y} over the possible y, so p_Z(z) = Σᵧ p(z − y, y) for any joint pmf. If X ⊥ Y the joint factorises and p_Z(z) = Σᵧ p_X(z − y) p_Y(y), the convolution p_X * p_Y.
  • Worked: two dice. P(S = s) = Σ_(j=1)^6 P(D₁ = s − j)·(1/6), and P(D₁ = s − j) = 1/6 exactly when 1 ≤ s − j ≤ 6. The number of such j is 6 − |s − 7|, so P(S = s) = (6 − |s − 7|)/36: the right margin of the table in §01, now as a convolution. The triangle is the overlap length of two shifted boxes.
  • Worked: a spare radio. A solo sailor has two radios, each failing independently with probability p = 1/75 each day it is switched on. The days until each fails are geometric, P(X = k) = (1 − p)^(k−1)p. Their sum has P(X + Y = k) = Σ_(j=1)^(k−1) (1 − p)^(k−j−1)p (1 − p)^(j−1)p = (k − 1)p²(1 − p)^(k−2) for k ≥ 2, the negative binomial with r = 2, not a geometric. The expected number of days of radio contact is E(X − 1) + E(Y − 1) = 148, comfortably over a 100-day crossing, yet P(X + Y − 2 ≤ 99) = Σ_(k=2)^(101) (k − 1)p²(1 − p)^(k−2) = 0.390. A mean that looks safe hides a 39% chance of losing contact.

Delivered: Poisson counts add, by convolution and the binomial theorem

  • The Poisson section stated that independent X ~ Poisson(μ₁) and Y ~ Poisson(μ₂) add to Poisson(μ₁ + μ₂), with a promise of a direct proof. Convolve, for n ≥ 0:
    P(X + Y = n) = Σ_(k=0)^n e^(−μ₁) μ₁ᵏ/k! · e^(−μ₂) μ₂ⁿ⁻ᵏ/(n − k)! = (e^(−(μ₁+μ₂))/n!) · Σ_(k=0)^n C(n, k) μ₁ᵏ μ₂ⁿ⁻ᵏ = e^(−(μ₁+μ₂)) (μ₁ + μ₂)ⁿ / n!

  • A bonus from the same algebra.   • Binomials too. For independent Bin(n, p) and Bin(m, p), the convolution Σₖ C(n, k)C(m, j − k)pʲ(1 − p)^(n+m−j) collapses by Vandermonde's identity Σₖ C(n, k)C(m, j − k) = C(n + m, j) to Bin(n + m, p). The success probability must be shared; with different p the sum is not binomial.

Continuous convolution

  • Derivation. For independent X, Y with densities, F_Z(z) = P(X + Y ≤ z) = ∫ [∫_(−∞)^(z−y) f_X(x) dx] f_Y(y) dy = ∫ F_X(z − y) f_Y(y) dy. Differentiating under the integral, f_Z(z) = ∫ f_X(z − y) f_Y(y) dy. Without independence, f_Z(z) = ∫ f(z − y, y) dy, the joint density integrated along the line x + y = z (with a factor that §07 explains is 1).
  • Flip and slide. As a function of y, f_X(z − y) is f_X reflected and shifted so that its origin sits at z. The convolution at z is the area under the product of this flipped copy and f_Y. Sliding z from left to right traces out f_Z. The figure below does exactly this.
  • Worked: two uniforms make a triangle. 07 Change of variables in two dimensions: the Jacobian

In one dimension, a smooth one-to-one transformation Y = g(X) changes a density by the factor |dx/dy|, the local stretching of the line (transformations). In two dimensions the line becomes the plane, lengths become areas, and the stretching factor becomes the absolute value of a Jacobian determinant. With that one formula, sums, differences, ratios, polar coordinates and the beta–gamma connection all become routine, and two classic results fall out: the normal constant √(2π), by passing to polar coordinates, and the Box–Muller method for turning uniform random numbers into normal ones.

The theorem

  • Statement. Let (X, Y) have joint density f_(X,Y), positive on an open set S. Let (U, V) = T(X, Y), where T is one-to-one from S onto a set T(S), and its inverse x = x(u, v), y = y(u, v) has continuous partial derivatives. Define the Jacobian
    J(u, v) = det [ ∂x/∂u ∂x/∂v ; ∂y/∂u ∂y/∂v ] = (∂x/∂u)(∂y/∂v) − (∂x/∂v)(∂y/∂u)
    and assume it is non-zero on T(S), and that f_(X,Y) vanishes outside S, so that P((X, Y) ∈ S) = 1. Then (U, V) has density f_(U,V)(u, v) = f_(X,Y)(x(u, v), y(u, v)) · |J(u, v)| on T(S), and 0 elsewhere.
  • Why the determinant. Near a point, a smooth map acts like its linear approximation, and a linear map multiplies every area by the absolute value of its determinant. A small rectangle of area du dv in the (u, v) plane comes from a small parallelogram of area |J| du dv in the (x, y) plane. Probability is conserved, f_(U,V) du dv = f_(X,Y) |J| du dv, and that is the formula. It is the same bookkeeping as the substitution rule for double integrals.
  • Forward or inverse. The Jacobian of the inverse map is the reciprocal of the Jacobian of the forward map: |J| = 1/|∂(u, v)/∂(x, y)|, evaluated at the matching point. Use whichever derivatives are easier.
  • Linear maps. For (U, V)ᵀ = A(X, Y)ᵀ + b with A invertible, f_(U,V)(w) = f_(X,Y)(A⁻¹(w − b))/|det A|. In n dimensions the statement is identical, with an n × n determinant.
  • When T is not one-to-one. If several points map to the same (u, v), split S into pieces on which T is one-to-one and add the contributions from each piece. Sorting a sample (§08) is the main example: n! pieces, one per ordering.
  • One output, not two. To find the density of a single function U = g(X, Y), invent a convenient second coordinate V (often V = Y or V = X), apply the theorem, and integrate V out. With U = X + Y and V = Y: x = u − v, y = v, J = 1, so f_U(u) = ∫ f(u − v, v) dv. The convolution formula of §06 is a corollary, and the "factor 1" it mentioned is this Jacobian.

Worked: sums and differences

  • The map. U = X + Y, V = X − Y inverts to x = (u + v)/2, y = (u − v)/2, with J = det[½ ½; ½ −½] = −½ and |J| = ½: the map doubles areas (its matrix [1 1; 1 −1] has determinant −2: a reflection combined with a scaling by √2 in each direction).
  • Two iid normals. For X, Y ~ N(0, 1) independent, x² + y² = (u² + v²)/2, so f_(U,V)(u, v) = (1/2π) e^(−(u² + v²)/4) · ½ = [e^(−u²/4)/√(4π)] · [e^(−v²/4)/√(4π)]. The density factorises: U and V are independent, each N(0, 2). The sum and the difference of two iid normals carry separate information. (The spherical symmetry of the standard normal is all that is used, and remarkably the normal is the only distribution for which the sum and difference of iid copies are independent.)
  • Two iid uniforms. The same map sends the unit square to a diamond with corners (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 0), (1, −1) in the (u, v) plane, with density ½ inside. Cov(U, V) = Var X − Var Y = 0, so U and V are uncorrelated, but the diamond is not a rectangle, so they are dependent: at u = 1.9, v is confined to [−0.1, 0.1]. Integrating v out over the diamond's cross-section of length 2·min(u, 2 − u) gives f_U(u) = min(u, 2 − u), the triangle of §06 again.

Products and ratios, briefly

  • Ratio. For Z = X/Y, take W = Y: x = zw, y = w, J = det[w z; 0 1] = w, so f_Z(z) = ∫ f(zw, w) |w| dw.   • Product. For Z = XY, take W = X: y = z/w, |J| = 1/|w|, so f_Z(z) = ∫ f(w, z/w) dw/|w|. 08 Order statistics

Sort a sample X₁, …, Xₙ and call the results X₍₁₎ ≤ X₍₂₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ X₍ₙ₎: the minimum, the second smallest, and so on up to the maximum. These order statistics are what a sample median, a percentile, a range, a record, a series system's first failure or a parallel system's last are made of. Even when the Xᵢ are iid, the sorted values are dependent (each is at least the one before), and their joint distribution is one of the cleanest applications of the change-of-variables theorem. For uniform samples every order statistic is a beta variable, and because every continuous distribution is a transformed uniform, that one fact explains why sample quantiles estimate population quantiles.

The extremes: minimum and maximum

  • Maximum. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be iid with cdf F and density f. The maximum is at most x exactly when every observation is, so by independence P(X₍ₙ₎ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ, and differentiating, f₍ₙ₎(x) = n F(x)ⁿ⁻¹ f(x).
  • Minimum. The minimum exceeds x exactly when every observation does: P(X₍₁₎ > x) = (1 − F(x))ⁿ and f₍₁₎(x) = n(1 − F(x))ⁿ⁻¹ f(x).
  • Worked: exponential lifetimes. Five components with independent Exp lifetimes of mean 10 years. In series (the system fails at the first failure) the lifetime is the minimum, P(min > t) = e^(−5t/10): exponential with mean 2 years. Five components make the system five times less durable. In parallel (the system works until the last fails) the lifetime is the maximum, with mean 10(1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + ⅕) = 22.83 years. The harmonic sum comes from the gaps: after the j-th failure, n − j components remain, and by memorylessness the wait for the next is Exp with rate (n − j)/10. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent exponentials.
  • Uniforms. For U(0, 1) samples, f₍ₙ₎(x) = nxⁿ⁻¹ (the Beta(n, 1) density) and f₍₁₎(x) = n(1 − x)ⁿ⁻¹ (Beta(1, n)): the extremes of a uniform sample sit about 1/(n + 1) from the ends.

The joint density of all n order statistics

  • Statement. For an iid sample from a density f, the vector (X₍₁₎, …, X₍ₙ₎) has joint density n! · f(y₁) f(y₂) ⋯ f(yₙ) on the region y₁ < y₂ < ⋯ < yₙ, and 0 elsewhere.
  • Proof by the change-of-variables theorem. Ties have probability zero, so with probability 1 the sample is in one of n! regions, one for each ordering of the coordinates. On each region, sorting is a permutation of the coordinates: one-to-one, linear, with Jacobian determinant ±1. So each region contributes f(y₁)⋯f(yₙ) · 1 to the density at a sorted point (y₁, …, yₙ), and the n! regions contribute n! equal terms: the "not one-to-one, sum over the pieces" rule of §07.
  • A check. For n = 2 uniforms the joint density of (min, max) is 2 on the triangle 0 < y₁ < y₂ < 1, whose area is ½. Integrating out y₂ gives 2(1 − y₁), the Beta(1, 2) density of the minimum, as above.
  • Two order statistics. Integrating out the others gives, for j < k and x < y:
    f₍ⱼ,ₖ₎(x, y) = n!/((j − 1)!(k − j − 1)!(n − k)!) · F(x)^(j−1) · [F(y) − F(x)]^(k−j−1) · [1 − F(y)]^(n−k) · f(x) f(y)
    Read it as a multinomial: j − 1 observations below x, one at x, k − j − 1 between, one at y, n − k above.

The k-th order statistic

  • Its density. Integrating out everything except the k-th coordinate gives
    f₍ₖ₎(x) = n!/((k − 1)!(n − k)!) · F(x)^(k−1) · [1 − F(x)]^(n−k) · f(x)
    The multinomial reading makes it memorable: for the k-th smallest to be near x, one observation must be in [x, x + dx] (probability f(x)dx), k − 1 below (each with probability F(x)) and n − k above (each 1 − F(x)), and the coefficient counts the ways to assign the n labelled observations to those three roles. The beta section gave this as a heuristic; the joint density makes it a proof.
  • Its cdf, by counting. X₍ₖ₎ ≤ x exactly when at least k of the n observations are ≤ x, and the number that are is Bin(n, F(x)). So P(X₍ₖ₎ ≤ x) = Σ_(j=k)^n C(n, j) F(x)ʲ (1 − F(x))ⁿ⁻ʲ. Differentiating this sum, consecutive terms telescope and only the density above survives: a second proof.

Why sample quantiles work

  • The sample quantile concentrates. Take k ≈ np. Then F(X₍ₖ₎) ~ Beta(k, n − k + 1) has mean about p and variance about p(1 − p)/n, which shrinks to 0. So the population fraction below the sample p-quantile converges to p, and when F is continuous and increasing at its p-quantile q_p, X₍ₖ₎ → q_p. That is the precise sense in which sample percentiles and quartiles estimate their population counterparts.
  • How precisely. A first-order expansion of F⁻¹ around p gives Var X₍ₖ₎ ≈ p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²). For the median of a normal sample (p = ½, f(q_p) = 1/(σ√(2π))) this is πσ²/(2n), against σ²/n for the mean: the median uses the data with efficiency 2/π = 0.637 under normality, and wins under heavy tails, as tabulated in efficiency of estimators. Where the density at the quantile is low, the quantile is hard to pin down: extreme percentiles need far more data than the median. The limit theorem behind the approximation is in Limit Theorems & Convergence.
  • Confidence intervals with no model. The population median m lies between X₍ⱼ₎ and X₍ₖ₎ exactly when between j and k − 1 observations fall below it, and the number below m is Bin(n, ½) for any continuous distribution. So P(X₍ⱼ₎ ≤ m < X₍ₖ₎) = Σ_(i=j)^(k−1) C(n, i)/2ⁿ. For n = 10, the interval from the 2nd to the 9th smallest observation covers the median with probability 1 − 2(1 + 10)/1024 = 0.979, whatever the distribution.
  • Conformal prediction runs on the same fact. If n calibration scores and a new score are exchangeable, the new score's rank among all n + 1 is uniform on {1, …, n + 1}. Taking the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest calibration score as a threshold therefore covers the new case with probability at least 1 − α (provided ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉ ≤ n and there are no ties, or ties are broken at random), for any model and any data distribution. Every distribution-free prediction interval is an order-statistic argument of this kind.

09 The bivariate and multivariate normal

The multivariate normal is the joint distribution of linear combinations of independent standard normals. It is described completely by a mean vector and a covariance matrix; its density is the exponential of a quadratic form, so its contours are ellipses; every linear combination of its coordinates is normal; zero covariance between its coordinates already means independence; and, the property that makes it the backbone of regression and of Gaussian models in machine learning, every conditional distribution is again normal, with a mean that is exactly linear in the conditioning value and a variance that does not depend on it at all.

Construction and density

  • Built from independent normals. Let Z₁, Z₂ be iid N(0, 1), and for σ_X, σ_Y > 0 and −1 < ρ < 1 set
    X = μ_X + σ_X·Z₁, Y = μ_Y + σ_Y·(ρZ₁ + √(1 − ρ²)·Z₂)
    Each coordinate is normal (a linear combination of independent normals, proved by mgfs), with means μ_X, μ_Y and variances σ_X², σ_Y² (since ρ² + (1 − ρ²) = 1), and by bilinearity Cov(X, Y) = σ_X σ_Y ρ: ρ is the correlation.
  • The density, by the Jacobian. The map from (z₁, z₂) to (x, y) is linear with determinant σ_X σ_Y √(1 − ρ²), and inverting it, z₁ = z_x, z₂ = (z_y − ρz_x)/√(1 − ρ²) in terms of the z-scores z_x = (x − μ_X)/σ_X, z_y = (y − μ_Y)/σ_Y. Since z₁² + z₂² = (z_x² − 2ρz_x z_y + z_y²)/(1 − ρ²), §07 gives
    f(x, y) = 1/(2π σ_X σ_Y √(1 − ρ²)) · exp( −(z_x² − 2ρ·z_x·z_y + z_y²) / (2(1 − ρ²)) )
  • Matrix form. With Σ = [σ_X² ρσ_Xσ_Y ; ρσ_Xσ_Y σ_Y²], det Σ = σ_X²σ_Y²(1 − ρ²), and the exponent is −½(x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ). In k dimensions, X ~ N(μ, Σ) has density (2π)^(−k/2) (det Σ)^(−1/2) exp(−½(x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ)) for a symmetric positive-definite Σ, and it is generated as X = μ + LZ for any L with LLᵀ = Σ (the Cholesky factor, whose two-dimensional form is exactly the construction above).
  • The ellipses. The density is constant where the quadratic form Q = (x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ) is constant: ellipses centred at μ, with axes along the eigenvectors of Σ and half-lengths proportional to the square roots of its eigenvalues (quadratic forms). With ρ = 0 and equal variances they are circles; as |ρ| → 1 they collapse onto a line. In two dimensions Q ~ χ²₂ = Exp(½) (the polar fact of §07 after whitening), so the ellipse Q ≤ c² holds probability 1 − e^(−c²/2): 39.3% for c = 1, 86.5% for c = 2, 98.9% for c = 3, and 95% at c = 2.448. The one-dimensional "68–95–99.7" does not carry over to ellipses.

Linear combinations stay normal

  • Statement. If X ~ N(μ, Σ), then AX + b ~ N(Aμ + b, AΣAᵀ) for any matrix A and vector b: AX + b = (Aμ + b) + (AL)Z is again a linear function of independent normals, and its mean and covariance follow from §03. For a pair, aX + bY ~ N(aμ_X + bμ_Y, a²σ_X² + b²σ_Y² + 2abρσ_Xσ_Y), with the covariance term in the variance. In particular each marginal is normal, and any sub-vector is multivariate normal with the corresponding block of Σ.
  • A characterisation. X is multivariate normal if and only if every linear combination aᵀX is univariate normal. Normal marginals are not enough (below); normality of every one-dimensional projection is.
  • Worked: heights of couples. Wives' heights N(66.8, 2²) inches, husbands' N(70, 2²), correlation 0.68, jointly normal. The difference W − H is normal with mean −3.2 and variance 4 + 4 − 2(0.68)(2)(2) = 2.56, standard deviation 1.6, so P(wife taller) = P(Z > 2) = 1 − Φ(2) = 0.0228. Ignoring the correlation would give variance 8 and probability 1 − Φ(1.13) = 0.129, more than five times too high: married couples are positively correlated in height, which makes their difference less variable.

Zero covariance means independence, inside the family

  • Proof. With ρ = 0 the cross term in the exponent vanishes, and the density factorises into N(μ_X, σ_X²) times N(μ_Y, σ_Y²). In k dimensions, a block-diagonal Σ makes the quadratic form a sum over the blocks and the density a product: jointly normal sub-vectors with zero cross-covariance are independent. For jointly normal variables, uncorrelated and independent are the same thing; §03 showed that outside this family they are not.
  • The famous caveat: normal marginals, not jointly normal. Let X ~ N(0, 1) and S = ±1 a fair sign independent of X, and set Y = SX. By symmetry Y ~ N(0, 1), and Cov(X, Y) = E S · E X² = 0. Both marginals are normal and the correlation is zero, yet |Y| = |X|: completely dependent. The pair is not jointly normal (X + Y = 0 with probability ½, which no normal variable does), so the theorem does not apply. A second version: Y = X when |X| ≤ c and Y = −X otherwise has Y ~ N(0, 1) for every c, and choosing c = 1.538 makes E[X²; |X| ≤ c] = ½ and the correlation exactly 0.
  • The lesson. "Each variable is normal, and they are uncorrelated" does not license treating them as independent; joint normality is a property of the pair. Models that assume a joint normal (or a Gaussian copula, §11) for variables that are only marginally normal can badly misjudge how often they are extreme together.

Delivered: conditional distributions are normal, with exactly linear means

  • From the construction. Conditioning on X = x fixes Z₁ = z_x = (x − μ_X)/σ_X. In Y = μ_Y + σ_Y ρ z_x + σ_Y √(1 − ρ²) Z₂, the only random part left is Z₂, independent of Z₁. So
    Y | X = x ~ N( μ_Y + ρ·(σ_Y/σ_X)·(x − μ_X), σ_Y²·(1 − ρ²) )
  • From the density, by completing the square. Divide the joint density by f_X(x) = e^(−z_x²/2)/(σ_X√(2π)). The exponents subtract: (z_x² − 2ρz_x z_y + z_y²)/(1 − ρ²) − z_x² = (z_y² − 2ρz_x z_y + ρ²z_x²)/(1 − ρ²) = (z_y − ρz_x)²/(1 − ρ²), a perfect square. So f(y | x) = exp(−(z_y − ρz_x)²/(2(1 − ρ²)))/(σ_Y√(2π(1 − ρ²))), a normal density in y centred where z_y = ρz_x, with standard deviation σ_Y√(1 − ρ²). Same answer, as it must be.
  • What is special. Three things that are false for joint distributions in general hold here exactly. The conditional mean E[Y | X = x] is a straight line in x; the conditional variance σ_Y²(1 − ρ²) is the same for every x (homoscedastic); and the conditional shape is normal. These are the promises made for the multivariate normal in conditional expectation: its conditional means are exactly linear.
  • The general form. Partition X = (X_a, X_b) with blocks μ_a, μ_b and Σ_aa, Σ_ab, Σ_ba, Σ_bb. Then X_b | X_a = x_a ~ N(μ_b + Σ_baΣ_aa⁻¹(x_a − μ_a), Σ_bb − Σ_baΣ_aa⁻¹Σ_ab). The mean is linear in x_a, and the conditional covariance (a Schur complement) does not depend on x_a and is never larger than Σ_bb: observing more can only shrink the uncertainty. This one formula is the Kalman filter's update and the Gaussian-process prediction of §11.

The regression line, the SD line and regression to the mean

  • The regression line is the conditional mean. The slope ρσ_Y/σ_X = Cov(X, Y)/Var X is the slope of the best straight line of §04. For a bivariate normal, the best line is the best predictor among all functions: least squares loses nothing by being linear, and its error variance σ_Y²(1 − ρ²) is the irreducible error, so R² = ρ². This is why the line fitted in regression is interpreted as an average of y at each x, with a vertical spread that is the same everywhere, and why a Gaussian noise model leads to the squared loss of linear regression.
  • Regression to the mean. In standard units the prediction is ẑ_Y = ρ z_X: an observation two standard deviations above average in X predicts only 2ρ standard deviations above average in Y. With equal spreads, test–retest data with ρ = 0.6 predict that a student two SDs above the mean on the first test will be 1.2 SDs above on the second, on average. Nothing causes this: the top scorers on the first test are a mixture of the strong and the lucky, and the luck does not repeat. The SD line (slope σ_Y/σ_X when ρ > 0), which is the ellipse's major axis in standard units, is steeper than the regression line, and the gap between them is the regression effect (regression to the mean).
  • Two regression lines. Predicting X from Y regresses toward the mean the other way: E[X | Y = y] = μ_X + ρ(σ_X/σ_Y)(y − μ_Y), which, drawn in the (x, y) plane, has slope σ_Y/(ρσ_X), steeper than the SD line. Worked with exam scores (midterm mean 49.5, sd 10.2; final mean 69.1, sd 11.8; ρ = 0.67): a midterm of 41 predicts a final of 69.1 + 0.67·(11.8/10.2)·(41 − 49.5) = 62.5, with conditional sd 11.8·√(1 − 0.67²) = 8.76; a final of 89 predicts a midterm of 49.5 + 0.67·(10.2/11.8)·(89 − 69.1) = 61.0, not the value obtained by solving the first line for x.

Mahalanobis distance

  • The quadratic form d²(x) = (x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ) is the Mahalanobis distance of a point from the centre: the Euclidean distance after whitening, z = L⁻¹(x − μ), which turns the ellipses into circles. It measures distance in units of the spread in that direction: with ρ = 0.6 and unit variances, the point (2, 2) lies along the ellipse's long axis and has d² = (4 − 2·0.6·4 + 4)/(1 − 0.36) = 5, d = 2.24, while (2, −2), at the same Euclidean distance 2.83, has d² = 20, d = 4.47: a much stranger point. Under the model d² ~ χ²_k, which turns it into an outlier test, and it is the distance used by Gaussian classifiers and by the covariance-as-Gram-matrix view of the covariance matrix.

10 Mixtures, hierarchies and the Dirichlet

A joint distribution built as "marginal × conditional" with the first stage hidden is a hierarchical model, and its marginal for the observed variable is a mixture. Mixtures are often confused with sums, though they behave completely differently. This section separates the two, chains conditionals into hierarchies, and introduces the Dirichlet distribution, the multivariate generalisation of the beta: the natural distribution of a probability vector, built from normalised gammas, and the conjugate prior for multinomial counts.

A mixture is not a sum

  • Mixture. Choose a component C = j with probability wⱼ, then draw X from component density fⱼ. The density of X is f(x) = Σⱼ wⱼ fⱼ(x), a weighted average of densities, by the law of total probability of §05. The value is drawn from one component.
  • Sum. Draw from every component independently and add: the density is the convolution of the component densities (§06). A different operation with a different result.
  • Worked. Components N(−2, 1) and N(2, 1). The 50/50 mixture is bimodal, with peaks near ±2, mean 0 and variance 1 + 4 = 5 (within-component plus between-component, the law of total variance). The sum of independent draws from each is N(0, 2): unimodal, variance 2. Even the "50/50 average" (X₁ + X₂)/2 is N(0, ½), nothing like the mixture. Writing a mixture as wX₁ + (1 − w)X₂ is the classic error; the correct expression is B·X₁ + (1 − B)·X₂ with B ~ Bernoulli(w) independent of both.
  • Responsibilities. Bayes' rule for densities (§05) gives the probability that an observed x came from component j: P(C = j | X = x) = wⱼfⱼ(x)/Σₖ wₖfₖ(x). In the worked mixture the density ratio is φ(x − 2)/φ(x + 2) = e^(((x + 2)² − (x − 2)²)/2) = e^(4x), so P(C = 2 | x) = 1/(1 + e^(−4x)), which is 0.881 at x = 0.5. That is a logistic function of x: two Gaussian classes with equal variances produce exactly the posterior that logistic regression models. Computing these responsibilities is the E-step of the EM algorithm for Gaussian mixture models, which soft-assigns each point to clusters.

Hierarchies: chains of conditionals

  • The general form. f(θ, x) = f(θ) f(x | θ), and with more levels, f(η, θ, x) = f(η) f(θ | η) f(x | θ): hyperparameters, then parameters, then data. The marginal of the data integrates out every hidden level. Every model of this page's §05 and of the law of total variance is one: beta–binomial, gamma–Poisson (the negative binomial), uniform-prevalence binomial.
  • Continuous mixtures make heavy tails. If X | V ~ N(0, 1/V) and the precision V ~ Gamma(ν/2, ν/2), then X has Student's t distribution with ν degrees of freedom. Averaging normals of different widths fattens the tails: occasional draws from a wide component make extremes far likelier than any single normal would. Gamma-mixed Poissons overdisperse counts in the same way.
  • Exchangeability and de Finetti. Given p, the trials of the beta–binomial model are iid; unconditionally they are exchangeable but dependent (seeing successes raises the chance of the next one). De Finetti's theorem says the converse: every infinite exchangeable sequence of 0/1 variables is a mixture of iid sequences, with some distribution on p. A Pólya urn (draw a ball, return it with another of the same colour) generates exactly the beta–binomial sequence. Hierarchies are not an arbitrary modelling choice; they are what exchangeability forces.

Delivered: the Dirichlet distribution

  • Definition. For α = (α₁, …, α_K) with all αᵢ > 0 and α₀ = Σ αᵢ, the Dirichlet distribution is a distribution on the simplex {p : pᵢ ≥ 0, Σ pᵢ = 1}, with density (in the first K − 1 coordinates)
    f(p₁, …, p_K) = Γ(α₀)/(Γ(α₁)⋯Γ(α_K)) · p₁^(α₁−1) ⋯ p_K^(α_K−1)
    With K = 2, p₂ = 1 − p₁ and it is Beta(α₁, α₂): the Dirichlet is the beta for probability vectors with more than two entries (the beta distribution).
  • As normalised gammas. If G₁, …, G_K are independent with Gᵢ ~ Gamma(αᵢ, β) and S = Σ Gᵢ, then (G₁/S, …, G_K/S) ~ Dirichlet(α), independent of S ~ Gamma(α₀, β). The proof is §07's with more coordinates: change from (g₁, …, g_K) to (s, p₁, …, p_(K−1)) with gᵢ = s pᵢ and g_K = s(1 − p₁ − ⋯ − p_(K−1)); the Jacobian is s^(K−1), the integrand separates into s^(α₀−1)e^(−βs) times Π pᵢ^(αᵢ−1), and integrating out s produces the constant Γ(α₀)/Π Γ(αᵢ). This is also how Dirichlet vectors are sampled, in the figure below and in practice.
  • Moments. E pᵢ = αᵢ/α₀, Var pᵢ = αᵢ(α₀ − αᵢ)/(α₀²(α₀ + 1)), and Cov(pᵢ, pⱼ) = −αᵢαⱼ/(α₀²(α₀ + 1)) for i ≠ j: negative, because the entries share a fixed total, exactly as multinomial counts did in §03. Each marginal is pᵢ ~ Beta(αᵢ, α₀ − αᵢ), and merging categories adds their αs (p₁ + p₂ ~ Beta(α₁ + α₂, α₀ − α₁ − α₂)).
  • The simplex picture. α₀ controls concentration and α/α₀ the centre. All αᵢ = 1: uniform over the simplex. All αᵢ large: tightly concentrated around the centre. All αᵢ < 1: mass piled into the corners and edges, so most draws are sparse, with a few large entries and the rest near 0. That last regime is the prior used for topic mixtures in documents, where each document should use only a few topics.
  • Conjugate to the multinomial. With prior p ~ Dirichlet(α) and counts n = (n₁, …, n_K) from n multinomial trials (the multinomial), the likelihood is ∝ Π pᵢ^(nᵢ), so the posterior is ∝ Π pᵢ^(αᵢ + nᵢ − 1): Dirichlet(α + n). Prior parameters are pseudo-counts, and the posterior mean (αᵢ + nᵢ)/(α₀ + n) is additive smoothing: with every αᵢ = 1 it is Laplace's add-one rule used for word probabilities in naive Bayes smoothing.
  • Worked. Dirichlet(2, 3, 5): means 0.2, 0.3, 0.5; Var p₁ = 2·8/(100·11) = 0.0145; Cov(p₁, p₂) = −6/1100 = −0.0055. After observing counts (3, 1, 6), the posterior is Dirichlet(5, 4, 11) with means 0.25, 0.20, 0.55.

11 Where this feeds machine learning

A supervised learning problem is a joint distribution of features and a label; an unsupervised one is a joint distribution of features alone. The tools of this page are therefore the tools of the field: covariance matrices summarise feature dependence and drive PCA, correlated features destabilise linear models, conditional independence is the bet naive Bayes makes, the multivariate normal is conditioned to make Gaussian-process predictions, and mixtures and Dirichlets describe clusters and topics.

Covariance matrices and PCA

  • The variance in a direction. For a unit vector u, the projection uᵀX has variance uᵀΣu (§03). The direction of greatest variance maximises this quadratic form, and the maximiser is the top eigenvector of Σ, with the variance equal to its eigenvalue. Principal component analysis is exactly this: eigendecompose the (sample) covariance matrix Σ = VΛVᵀ and keep the leading directions (PCA from the quadratic form, PCA in practice).
  • Decorrelation and whitening. In the eigenbasis the coordinates Vᵀ(X − μ) are uncorrelated with variances λᵢ; dividing by √λᵢ whitens them to identity covariance. For a multivariate normal, uncorrelated means independent (§09), so PCA of Gaussian data yields independent components; for other data it only removes linear dependence.

Correlated features and multicollinearity

  • Unstable coefficients. In least squares, Var β̂ = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹. When features are correlated, XᵀX is close to singular and the coefficient variances inflate. For feature j, the inflation factor is VIF = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ), with R²ⱼ from regressing feature j on the others; for two features with correlation ρ it is 1/(1 − ρ²): 1.33 at ρ = 0.5, 5.26 at 0.9, 50.3 at 0.99. The data cannot tell how to split credit between nearly identical inputs (multicollinearity).
  • Predictions are fine, interpretations are not. The sum of the two coefficients is well determined; their difference is not. Ridge regularisation stabilises the split by adding a multiple of the identity to XᵀX (weight decay), and PCA removes the collinearity outright.

Naive Bayes: a bet on conditional independence

  • The assumption. Naive Bayes models P(x₁, …, x_d | c) = Π P(xⱼ | c): features conditionally independent given the class (§05), which cuts the parameters from exponential in d to linear (§02). Features are typically dependent overall, because they share the class as a common cause; the model only assumes the dependence vanishes once the class is known (the naive assumption).
  • What breaks when the bet fails. Correlated evidence is double-counted. Duplicate a feature whose likelihood ratio is 3: with prior odds 1, the posterior probability goes from 3/4 = 0.75 to 9/10 = 0.9 although no new information arrived. Naive Bayes rankings are often still good while its probabilities are overconfident, which is why its scores are usually recalibrated before being read as probabilities.
  • The Gaussian versions. With multivariate normal class-conditionals sharing one covariance matrix, the posterior log-odds are linear in x (linear discriminant analysis); with separate covariances they are quadratic. The one-dimensional case is the logistic responsibility of §10. See linear discriminant analysis.

Gaussian processes, in one paragraph

  • A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions f such that for any finite set of inputs x₁, …, xₙ, the vector (f(x₁), …, f(xₙ)) is multivariate normal, with covariances Cov(f(xᵢ), f(xⱼ)) = k(xᵢ, xⱼ) given by a kernel that makes nearby inputs strongly correlated. Prediction at a new input is the conditional distribution of §09, whose exactly linear conditional mean was first met in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities: with a zero-mean prior and K the kernel matrix of the observed inputs plus the noise term σ²I (drop it for noise-free observations), f(x*) | data is normal with mean k*ᵀK⁻¹y and variance k(x*, x*) − k*ᵀK⁻¹k*, a linear function of the observations and a Schur-complement variance that shrinks near observed points. That calibrated uncertainty is why Gaussian processes are the classic surrogate model in Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters.

More joint distributions in models

  • The reparametrisation trick. A variational autoencoder samples its latent code as z = μ + σ ⊙ ε (or μ + Lε with a Cholesky factor for full covariance): the construction X = μ + LZ of §09, used so that gradients can flow through μ and L (reparametrisation).
  • Mixtures. Gaussian mixture models, mixture density networks and mixture-of-experts layers all compute Σ wⱼ(x) fⱼ with input-dependent weights; a gating network is a learned responsibility (routing in mixtures of experts).
  • Co-occurrence and PMI. Word embeddings are learned from the joint distribution of words and their contexts. The pointwise mutual information ln[p(w, c)/(p(w)p(c))] measures how far a pair's joint probability is from the independence product, and count-based methods fit vectors whose dot products match log co-occurrences (global co-occurrence embeddings).
  • Correlated examples. The effective sample size of §04 applies to data: n examples with within-group correlation ρ are worth about n/(1 + (m − 1)ρ) for groups of size m, and splits must keep groups together, or validation scores will be optimistic.

Copulas: marginals plus dependence, in one honest paragraph

  • Sklar's theorem says every joint cdf can be written F(x, y) = C(F_X(x), F_Y(y)), where C, the copula, is a joint cdf on the unit square with uniform marginals (unique when the marginals are continuous). For continuous marginals it splits a joint distribution into its marginals and a pure dependence structure, which can be modelled separately: fit each marginal however suits it, then choose a copula. Rank correlations (Spearman's, Kendall's) then depend only on the copula. The honest caveat: the convenient Gaussian copula has no tail dependence, so joint extremes become asymptotically independent however high ρ is; models that used it for pooled credit risk badly underestimated how often many loans would fail together. Copulas make dependence modelling flexible, not automatic: the choice of C decides how extremes cluster, and that is exactly the part data are thinnest on.
