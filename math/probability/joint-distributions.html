Joint Distributions & Dependence

One random variable at a time is enough to describe a die, a waiting time or a measurement error. Almost every interesting question involves several: a feature and a label, the height and weight of the same person, the errors of five models on the same test case, the returns of two stocks on the same day. This page treats random variables together. It defines the joint distribution of a pair (and of a vector), recovers the single-variable marginals from it, and makes precise what it means for variables to be independent. It measures linear dependence with covariance and correlation, proves the general variance-of-a-sum formula, and shows why averaging correlated predictors hits a floor. It conditions one variable on another by slicing and renormalising the joint density, finds the distribution of a sum by convolution and of any smooth transformation by a Jacobian, and derives the distribution of sorted samples. It ends with the multivariate normal, whose conditional means are exactly linear, with mixtures and the Dirichlet, and with the places all of this surfaces in machine learning.

01 What a joint distribution is

The joint distribution of two random variables X and Y, defined on the same sample space, says how probability is spread over the pairs of values (x, y) they can take together. For discrete variables it is a table of probabilities p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y); for continuous ones it is a surface f(x, y) whose volume over a region is the probability that the pair lands in that region. The single-variable distributions of X and of Y, called the marginals, are recovered from it by summing or integrating out the other variable. The reverse is impossible: the marginals do not determine the joint, and everything this page calls "dependence" lives in the difference.

Two variables on one sample space

  • Where pairs come from. A random variable is a function on the sample space Ω (Random Variables & Discrete Distributions). Two random variables are two functions on the same Ω, and each outcome ω produces a pair (X(ω), Y(ω)). Throw two dice: Ω is the 36-point product space of ordered pairs of faces, and from one outcome we can read off the sum S, the maximum M, the first face D₁, all at once. Because they are computed from the same outcome, learning one of them can change what we believe about another.
  • The pair is a random point. (X, Y) is a random point in the plane, and its distribution is a distribution on the plane: for every region A ⊂ ℝ², a probability P((X, Y) ∈ A). The joint pmf, joint density and joint cdf below are three ways of writing that one object down.
  • More than two. For n variables the pair becomes a random vector X = (X₁, …, Xₙ), a random point in ℝⁿ, and everything on this page generalises with sums and integrals over n coordinates. Most statements are made for pairs because the pictures are planar; the vector versions are stated where they matter (§03, §09, §10).

The discrete case: a two-way table

  • Definition. The joint probability mass function of discrete X and Y is p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y), the probability that both happen. It satisfies p(x, y) ≥ 0 and Σₓ Σᵧ p(x, y) = 1, and for any set A of pairs, P((X, Y) ∈ A) = Σ_((x, y)∈A) p(x, y), by additivity over the disjoint events {X = x, Y = y}.
  • Worked: the sum and the maximum of two dice. Each of the 36 ordered pairs of faces has probability 1/36. Let S be the sum and M the maximum. The event {S = 7, M = 4} contains the outcomes (3, 4) and (4, 3), so p(7, 4) = 2/36. In general a pair with two different faces is counted twice and a double once, so every non-zero entry is 1/36 or 2/36. The table below lists all of them.
  • Where "marginal" comes from. The event {S = 6} is the disjoint union of {S = 6, M = 1}, …, {S = 6, M = 6}, so P(S = 6) = Σₘ p(6, m) = 0 + 0 + 1/36 + 2/36 + 2/36 + 0 = 5/36: a row sum. Writing the row sums in the right-hand margin of the table gives the distribution of S, and the column sums written in the bottom margin give the distribution of M. These are the marginal distributions, named after the margins of the table they are written in.

The marginals do not determine the joint

  • Take two 0/1 variables with the table p(0, 0) = ¼ − ε, p(0, 1) = ¼ + ε, p(1, 0) = ¼ + ε, p(1, 1) = ¼ − ε, for any ε between −¼ and ¼. Every row and every column sums to ½, so every member of this family has the same two marginals: two fair coins. At ε = 0 the coins are independent; at ε = ¼ they always disagree; at ε = −¼ they always agree. Looking at each coin separately cannot tell these situations apart.
  • What this means. Two marginal distributions are two lists of numbers; the joint is a table, with one more degree of freedom per extra cell. The dependence between the variables is exactly the information in the table that is not in its margins. Covariance (§03) summarises that information in one number; the copula (§11) captures it in full when the marginals are continuous (for discrete variables like these coins the copula is not unique, and the table itself is the honest description).
  • A practical consequence. Two separately collected datasets, one recording X for a population and another recording Y, can never reveal how X and Y are related, however large they are. To learn a joint distribution you must observe the pairs together. That is why a model of P(label | features) needs labelled examples, not a pile of features and a separate pile of labels.

The joint cdf

  • Definition. The joint distribution function is F(x, y) = P(X ≤ x, Y ≤ y), the probability that the random point falls in the quadrant to the south-west of (x, y). Unlike the pmf it is defined in the same way for discrete, continuous and mixed pairs, and it determines the joint distribution completely.
  • Rectangles by inclusion–exclusion. P(a < X ≤ b, c < Y ≤ d) = F(b, d) − F(a, d) − F(b, c) + F(a, c). The quadrant at (b, d) contains the rectangle plus two strips; subtracting the quadrants at (a, d) and (b, c) removes the strips but removes the quadrant at (a, c) twice, so it is added back. That this combination is never negative is the two-dimensional version of "a cdf is non-decreasing", and it is a genuine extra condition: a function can increase in each coordinate separately and still fail it.
  • Marginal cdfs are limits. F_X(x) = P(X ≤ x) = lim_(y→∞) F(x, y), because the events {X ≤ x, Y ≤ y} increase to {X ≤ x} as y grows. Likewise F_Y(y) = F(∞, y). For the dice, F(5, 3) = P(S ≤ 5, M ≤ 3) = 8/36, while F(5, 6) = P(S ≤ 5) = 10/36.

The continuous case: a density surface

  • Definition. X and Y have a joint density f(x, y) ≥ 0 if, for every region A of the plane, P((X, Y) ∈ A) = ∬_A f(x, y) dx dy. In particular ∬ f = 1 over the whole plane. The probability of a region is the volume under the surface above it, exactly as a one-dimensional probability is an area under a curve (densities).
  • Reading a density. For a small rectangle of sides dx and dy at (x, y), P(X ∈ [x, x + dx], Y ∈ [y, y + dy]) ≈ f(x, y) dx dy. So f is probability per unit area; it is not a probability, can exceed 1, and every single point and every curve in the plane has probability zero. Where the cdf is smooth, f(x, y) = ∂²F/∂x∂y.
  • Marginal densities by integrating out. f_X(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy and f_Y(y) = ∫ f(x, y) dx: the continuous version of row and column sums. Proof: P(X ≤ a) = ∫_(−∞)^a [∫ f(x, y) dy] dx, so the bracket is the density of X.
  • Worked: a density on a rectangle. Let f(x, y) = (2/75)(2x²y + xy²) for 0 ≤ x ≤ 3, 1 ≤ y ≤ 2, and 0 elsewhere. It integrates to 1. Then P(1 ≤ X ≤ 2, 4/3 ≤ Y ≤ 5/3) = 187/2025 = 0.0923; the marginals are f_X(x) = (2/225)(9x² + 7x) on [0, 3] and f_Y(y) = (3y² + 12y)/25 on [1, 2]. Section §03 computes its covariance.
  • Worked: a density on a curved region. Let f(x, y) = c x²y on the region x² ≤ y ≤ 1 (so −1 ≤ x ≤ 1). The constant: ∫₋₁¹ ∫_(x²)^1 x²y dy dx = ∫₋₁¹ x²(1 − x⁴)/2 dx = 4/21, so c = 21/4. The region does the work: for each x, y runs from the parabola to 1. Then P(X ≥ Y) = (21/4) ∫₀¹ ∫_(x²)^x x²y dy dx = 3/20, and the marginal of X is f_X(x) = (21/8) x²(1 − x⁴). On a non-rectangular region, the limits of integration of the inner integral depend on the outer variable, and getting them right is most of the work.

Random vectors, and a marginal that surprises

  • n variables. The joint pmf p(x₁, …, xₙ) = P(X₁ = x₁, …, Xₙ = xₙ) or joint density f(x₁, …, xₙ) describes the whole vector; the marginal of any sub-collection is obtained by summing or integrating out all the others. The joint distribution of a vector carries every marginal of every subset, and all the dependence between them.
  • Worked: drawing without replacement. Draw n balls one by one, without replacement, from N balls numbered 1 to N, and let Xᵢ be the number on the i-th ball. Every ordered sequence of distinct numbers has probability 1/(N(N − 1)⋯(N − n + 1)), so the draws are clearly dependent (no number can repeat). Yet summing out all coordinates but the i-th leaves (N − 1)(N − 2)⋯(N − n + 1) equal terms, and P(Xᵢ = k) = 1/N: the third ball, or the last, is exactly as likely to be any given number as the first. The draws are exchangeable, each one marginally uniform, which is the fact behind the hypergeometric mean nK/N (linearity) and the dependence that §03 measures.
  • Mixed pairs. One coordinate can be discrete and the other continuous: a class label C and a measured feature X, a diagnosis and a blood test, a count and an exposure time. The joint is then described by P(C = c, X ∈ B) = ∫_B p(c) f(x | c) dx, a pmf in one coordinate times a density in the other. This is the joint distribution behind every classifier, and §05 conditions it in both directions.
The joint is the whole story; the marginals are its shadows. Every probability statement about X alone, or Y alone, can be read off the marginals. Every statement about how they move together (whether a high X comes with a high Y, what Y is likely to be once X is seen, how variable X + Y is) needs the joint. The rest of this page is a set of tools for extracting those statements.

11 Where this feeds machine learning

A supervised learning problem is a joint distribution of features and a label; an unsupervised one is a joint distribution of features alone. The tools of this page are therefore the tools of the field: covariance matrices summarise feature dependence and drive PCA, correlated features destabilise linear models, conditional independence is the bet naive Bayes makes, the multivariate normal is conditioned to make Gaussian-process predictions, and mixtures and Dirichlets describe clusters and topics.

Covariance matrices and PCA

  • The variance in a direction. For a unit vector u, the projection uᵀX has variance uᵀΣu (§03). The direction of greatest variance maximises this quadratic form, and the maximiser is the top eigenvector of Σ, with the variance equal to its eigenvalue. Principal component analysis is exactly this: eigendecompose the (sample) covariance matrix Σ = VΛVᵀ and keep the leading directions (PCA from the quadratic form, PCA in practice).
  • Decorrelation and whitening. In the eigenbasis the coordinates Vᵀ(X − μ) are uncorrelated with variances λᵢ; dividing by √λᵢ whitens them to identity covariance. For a multivariate normal, uncorrelated means independent (§09), so PCA of Gaussian data yields independent components; for other data it only removes linear dependence.

Correlated features and multicollinearity

  • Unstable coefficients. In least squares, Var β̂ = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹. When features are correlated, XᵀX is close to singular and the coefficient variances inflate. For feature j, the inflation factor is VIF = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ), with R²ⱼ from regressing feature j on the others; for two features with correlation ρ it is 1/(1 − ρ²): 1.33 at ρ = 0.5, 5.26 at 0.9, 50.3 at 0.99. The data cannot tell how to split credit between nearly identical inputs (multicollinearity).
  • Predictions are fine, interpretations are not. The sum of the two coefficients is well determined; their difference is not. Ridge regularisation stabilises the split by adding a multiple of the identity to XᵀX (weight decay), and PCA removes the collinearity outright.

Naive Bayes: a bet on conditional independence

  • The assumption. Naive Bayes models P(x₁, …, x_d | c) = Π P(xⱼ | c): features conditionally independent given the class (§05), which cuts the parameters from exponential in d to linear (§02). Features are typically dependent overall, because they share the class as a common cause; the model only assumes the dependence vanishes once the class is known (the naive assumption).
  • What breaks when the bet fails. Correlated evidence is double-counted. Duplicate a feature whose likelihood ratio is 3: with prior odds 1, the posterior probability goes from 3/4 = 0.75 to 9/10 = 0.9 although no new information arrived. Naive Bayes rankings are often still good while its probabilities are overconfident, which is why its scores are usually recalibrated before being read as probabilities.
  • The Gaussian versions. With multivariate normal class-conditionals sharing one covariance matrix, the posterior log-odds are linear in x (linear discriminant analysis); with separate covariances they are quadratic. The one-dimensional case is the logistic responsibility of §10. See linear discriminant analysis.

Gaussian processes, in one paragraph

  • A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions f such that for any finite set of inputs x₁, …, xₙ, the vector (f(x₁), …, f(xₙ)) is multivariate normal, with covariances Cov(f(xᵢ), f(xⱼ)) = k(xᵢ, xⱼ) given by a kernel that makes nearby inputs strongly correlated. Prediction at a new input is the conditional distribution of §09, whose exactly linear conditional mean was first met in Expectation, Variance & Inequalities: with a zero-mean prior and K the kernel matrix of the observed inputs plus the noise term σ²I (drop it for noise-free observations), f(x*) | data is normal with mean k*ᵀK⁻¹y and variance k(x*, x*) − k*ᵀK⁻¹k*, a linear function of the observations and a Schur-complement variance that shrinks near observed points. That calibrated uncertainty is why Gaussian processes are the classic surrogate model in Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters.

More joint distributions in models

  • The reparametrisation trick. A variational autoencoder samples its latent code as z = μ + σ ⊙ ε (or μ + Lε with a Cholesky factor for full covariance): the construction X = μ + LZ of §09, used so that gradients can flow through μ and L (reparametrisation).
  • Mixtures. Gaussian mixture models, mixture density networks and mixture-of-experts layers all compute Σ wⱼ(x) fⱼ with input-dependent weights; a gating network is a learned responsibility (routing in mixtures of experts).
  • Co-occurrence and PMI. Word embeddings are learned from the joint distribution of words and their contexts. The pointwise mutual information ln[p(w, c)/(p(w)p(c))] measures how far a pair's joint probability is from the independence product, and count-based methods fit vectors whose dot products match log co-occurrences (global co-occurrence embeddings).
  • Correlated examples. The effective sample size of §04 applies to data: n examples with within-group correlation ρ are worth about n/(1 + (m − 1)ρ) for groups of size m, and splits must keep groups together, or validation scores will be optimistic.

Copulas: marginals plus dependence, in one honest paragraph

  • Sklar's theorem says every joint cdf can be written F(x, y) = C(F_X(x), F_Y(y)), where C, the copula, is a joint cdf on the unit square with uniform marginals (unique when the marginals are continuous). For continuous marginals it splits a joint distribution into its marginals and a pure dependence structure, which can be modelled separately: fit each marginal however suits it, then choose a copula. Rank correlations (Spearman's, Kendall's) then depend only on the copula. The honest caveat: the convenient Gaussian copula has no tail dependence, so joint extremes become asymptotically independent however high ρ is; models that used it for pooled credit risk badly underestimated how often many loans would fail together. Copulas make dependence modelling flexible, not automatic: the choice of C decides how extremes cluster, and that is exactly the part data are thinnest on.
