02 Independence of random variables

Two random variables are independent when every event about one is independent of every event about the other: P(X ∈ A, Y ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A) P(Y ∈ B) for all sets A and B. In terms of distributions, the joint is the product of the marginals: the table is an outer product of its margins, the density surface factorises. Independence is the assumption that turns joint distributions back into single-variable ones, it is inherited by functions of the variables, and the "independent and identically distributed" sample built from it is the starting point of almost all of statistics and machine learning.

Three equivalent definitions

  • Events. X and Y are independent (X ⊥ Y) if P(X ∈ A, Y ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A) · P(Y ∈ B) for all (measurable) sets A, B. That is the independence of events from Probability Foundations, required for every event determined by X and every event determined by Y at once.
  • The cdf factorises. It is enough to check the quadrants: F(x, y) = F_X(x) F_Y(y) for all x, y. The rectangle formula of §01 then factorises too, and every set can be built from rectangles. This version covers discrete, continuous and mixed variables at once.
  • The pmf or density factorises. For discrete variables, p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y) for every pair. For continuous ones, f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y) (up to sets of zero area): differentiate F(x, y) = F_X(x)F_Y(y) in x and y, or integrate the product to get the cdf back. A joint table is independent exactly when it is the outer product of its margins: every row is proportional to every other row.
  • n variables. X₁, …, Xₙ are (mutually) independent if F(x₁, …, xₙ) = Π F_(Xᵢ)(xᵢ), equivalently the joint pmf or density is Π fᵢ(xᵢ). As with events, pairwise independence is weaker: let X and Y be independent fair bits and Z = X ⊕ Y (1 when exactly one of them is 1). Each pair is independent, but Z is a function of the other two, so the three are not mutually independent (pairwise is not enough).
independence of random variablesP(X ∈ A, Y ∈ B) = P(X ∈ A)·P(Y ∈ B) for all A, B ⟺ F(x, y) = F_X(x)·F_Y(y) ⟺ p(x, y) = p_X(x)·p_Y(y) or f(x, y) = f_X(x)·f_Y(y) n variables: f(x₁, …, xₙ) = f₁(x₁)·f₂(x₂)⋯fₙ(xₙ) pairwise independence ⇏ mutual independence

Checking a table, and the factorisation shortcut

  • A table that factorises. The two-bit table p(0, 0) = 1/9, p(0, 1) = 2/9, p(1, 0) = 2/9, p(1, 1) = 4/9 has margins (⅓, ⅔) for both variables, and every cell equals the product of its margins (⅓ · ⅓ = 1/9, ⅓ · ⅔ = 2/9, ⅔ · ⅔ = 4/9): independent. The second row is twice the first, which is the "rows proportional" test.
  • One zero is enough to break it. If some cell is 0 while both its margins are positive, the product p_X(x)p_Y(y) is positive and the table cannot factorise. The dice table of §01 has many such zeros (P(S = 12, M = 1) = 0 while P(S = 12) P(M = 1) > 0), so S and M are dependent at a glance. More generally, if the set of possible pairs is not a rectangle (a product of the possible x values and the possible y values), the variables are dependent.
  • The factorisation shortcut. If f(x, y) = g(x) h(y) for some non-negative functions g and h, and the region where f > 0 is a rectangle (possibly infinite), then X ⊥ Y; g and h need not be densities, since the normalising constants can be shuffled between them. Example: f(x, y) = 2e^(−(x + 2y)) on x, y > 0 is e^(−x) · 2e^(−2y), so X ~ Exp(1) and Y ~ Exp(2) are independent.
  • The support trap. The density (21/4)x²y of §01 is a product of a function of x and a function of y, but its support x² ≤ y ≤ 1 is not a rectangle: learning X = 0.9 forces Y ≥ 0.81. So X and Y are dependent. Likewise f(x, y) = 8xy on 0 < x < y < 1 looks like a product and is not one. The shape of the region where the density lives is part of the formula.

Computing with independence

  • Worked: a sum below a threshold. X and Y are independent with the same density 2x on [0, 1]. The joint density is 4xy on the unit square, and P(X + Y ≤ 1) = ∫₀¹ ∫₀^(1−x) 4xy dy dx = ∫₀¹ 2x(1 − x)² dx = 1/6.
  • Worked: a race of exponentials. X ~ Exp(λ) and Y ~ Exp(μ) are independent (two components failing, two customers arriving). Then P(X < Y) = ∫₀^∞ λe^(−λx) e^(−μx) dx = λ/(λ + μ): the chance that X wins is its share of the total rate. And P(min(X, Y) > t) = P(X > t) P(Y > t) = e^(−(λ + μ)t), so the first failure is Exp(λ + μ). A unit with rate 1 racing one with rate 2 wins with probability ⅓, and the first of them fails after a mean time of ⅓.
  • Expectations of products. If X ⊥ Y then E[g(X) h(Y)] = E g(X) · E h(Y) for all functions with finite means, because the joint pmf or density factorises inside the double sum or integral; this is the product rule of Expectation, Variance & Inequalities. The converse also holds if it is required for all bounded g and h (take indicators of sets), but not if it is checked only for g(x) = x, h(y) = y: that weaker condition is "uncorrelated" (§03).
workedf = 4xy on [0, 1]²: P(X + Y ≤ 1) = ∫₀¹ 2x(1 − x)² dx = 1/6 f = 2e^(−(x + 2y)) on x, y > 0 = e^(−x)·2e^(−2y): independent X ~ Exp(λ) ⊥ Y ~ Exp(μ): P(X < Y) = λ/(λ + μ), min(X, Y) ~ Exp(λ + μ) λ = 1, μ = 2: P(X < Y) = ⅓, E min = ⅓

Functions of independent variables are independent

  • Statement. If X₁, …, Xₙ are independent and Yᵢ = hᵢ(Xᵢ), then Y₁, …, Yₙ are independent. Proof for two: P(h₁(X₁) ∈ A, h₂(X₂) ∈ B) = P(X₁ ∈ h₁⁻¹(A), X₂ ∈ h₂⁻¹(B)), which factorises because {X₁ ∈ h₁⁻¹(A)} is an event about X₁ alone.
  • Blocks. More generally, functions of disjoint groups of mutually independent variables are independent: X₁ + X₂ is independent of X₃ X₄, and the first half of an iid sample is independent of any summary of the second half. That is what licenses "split the data, compute a statistic on each half, treat them as independent".
  • Why it matters in simulation. To generate independent waiting times T and service times S from different distributions, feed separate uniform draws into separate inverse cdfs: T = F⁻¹(U₁), S = G⁻¹(U₂). Independence of the uniforms is inherited by the outputs. Sharing a uniform between them, by contrast, makes them perfectly dependent, the trick behind common random numbers.

iid: the modelling atom

  • Definition. X₁, …, Xₙ are independent and identically distributed (iid) with distribution F when they are mutually independent and each has marginal F. Their joint density is then Π f(xᵢ), one factor per observation. A "random sample of size n from F" means exactly this.
  • The likelihood is this product. If f depends on a parameter θ, the joint density of the observed data, read as a function of θ, is the likelihood L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ; θ), and its logarithm is a sum, Σ ln f(xᵢ; θ). Every average training loss of the form (1/n) Σ ℓ(xᵢ) is a negative log-likelihood of this shape, and the sum is only justified by the product, which is only justified by independence.
  • Identically distributed but dependent. The draws without replacement of §01 all have the same marginal but are not independent: they are exchangeable (the joint distribution is unchanged by permuting the draws) rather than iid. Exchangeability is weaker than iid and is often all that a method actually needs: the rank-based guarantees of §08 and the de Finetti view of §10 both run on it.

Checking independence versus assuming it

  • It is almost always an assumption. Independence is a property of the joint distribution, which is not observed; what is observed is a finite sample of pairs. With categorical data, independence can be tested by comparing observed cell counts with the products of the margins (the chi-square test of independence in a two-way table), but a test can only fail to find dependence, never establish independence.
  • Zero correlation is not a check. A sample correlation near zero rules out only linear association. The variables X and X² with X symmetric about 0 are uncorrelated and completely dependent (§03).
  • Where it fails in practice. Repeated measurements on the same patient, pixels of the same image, sentences from the same document, consecutive days of a time series, users who influence each other: all violate independence, and a method that assumes it will treat n correlated observations as if they were n independent pieces of evidence, overstating its certainty. §04 quantifies the damage: n observations with pairwise correlation ρ are worth only n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) independent ones. Random train/test splits of grouped or time-ordered data, which leak information between the halves, are the machine-learning form of the same mistake (leakage).
  • Independence given something. Often variables are dependent overall but independent once a common cause is known: two symptoms of the same disease, two features of the same class. That is conditional independence, defined in §05, and it is the assumption behind naive Bayes (§11).
Independence is the cheapest joint distribution. Specifying a general joint pmf on k values for each of n variables takes kⁿ − 1 numbers; under independence it takes n(k − 1). Ten binary features need 1,023 numbers in general and 10 under independence. That economy is why independence is assumed so often, and why it is the first assumption to question when a model's probabilities turn out to be badly calibrated.

03 Covariance: the gap in the product rule

For independent variables E XY = E X · E Y; for dependent ones the two sides differ, and the difference is the covariance, Cov(X, Y) = E XY − E X · E Y = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)]. Its sign says whether large values of one tend to come with large or small values of the other; its size enters every variance of a sum. This section develops it in full: the definition and its two forms, the rules that make it behave like a product, the general variance formula for sums of dependent variables, the counterexamples that separate "uncorrelated" from "independent", and the covariances that produce the finite-population correction of the hypergeometric and the negative dependence of multinomial counts.

Definition, and what the sign means

  • Definition. For variables with finite variances, Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)], the average product of the deviations from the means. It is computed from the joint distribution by LOTUS for pairs: Σₓ Σᵧ (x − μ_X)(y − μ_Y) p(x, y) or the corresponding double integral.
  • The shortcut. Expanding the product and using linearity, E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)] = E XY − μ_X E Y − μ_Y E X + μ_X μ_Y = E XY − E X · E Y. This is the "gap in the product rule" that Expectation, Variance & Inequalities left open: the product rule E XY = E X E Y holds exactly when the covariance is zero.
  • The four quadrants. Draw axes through the point of means (μ_X, μ_Y). A pair (x, y) in the north-east or south-west quadrant has deviations of the same sign and contributes a positive product; a pair in the north-west or south-east contributes a negative one. Each product is the signed area of the rectangle between the point and the centre. The covariance is the probability-weighted average of those areas: positive when the mass sits mostly in the agreeing quadrants (large with large, small with small), negative when it sits in the disagreeing ones, near zero when the two kinds balance.
  • Units. Cov(X, Y) is in units of X times units of Y: heights in centimetres and weights in kilograms give a covariance in cm·kg, and changing to inches divides it by 2.54. Its sign is meaningful, its size on its own is not; correlation (§04) is the scale-free version.
covarianceCov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)] = E XY − E X·E Y discrete: Σₓ Σᵧ (x − μ_X)(y − μ_Y)·p(x, y) continuous: ∬ (x − μ_X)(y − μ_Y)·f(x, y) dx dy Cov > 0: mass mostly in the agreeing quadrants Cov < 0: mostly in the disagreeing quadrants X ⊥ Y ⟹ Cov(X, Y) = 0

Worked covariances

  • The sum and the maximum of two dice. E S = 7 and E M = 161/36. Rather than summing 36 products, use symmetry. Let N be the minimum, so S = M + N. Replacing each face d by 7 − d leaves the distribution of the two dice unchanged but turns M into 7 − N and S into 14 − S, so Cov(S, M) = Cov(14 − S, 7 − N) = Cov(S, N). The two covariances add to Cov(S, M + N) = Var S = 35/6, so each is 35/12 = 2.917: a large maximum comes with a large sum, as it must.
  • A density on a rectangle. For f(x, y) = (2/75)(2x²y + xy²) on [0, 3] × [1, 2] of §01, the double integrals give E X = 109/50, E Y = 157/100 and E XY = 171/50, so Cov(X, Y) = 171/50 − (109/50)(157/100) = −13/5000 = −0.0026. The variables are very slightly negatively related, which is not visible in the formula for f; it takes the computation to see it.
  • Indicators. For events A and B, Cov(1_A, 1_B) = E[1_A 1_B] − P(A)P(B) = P(A ∩ B) − P(A)P(B). It is positive exactly when P(B | A) > P(B), when A makes B more likely. So the covariance of indicators is the "how far from independent" gap of event independence, and the two hat-matching events of the hat problem have covariance 1/(n(n − 1)) − 1/n² = 1/(n²(n − 1)) > 0: one person's match makes another's slightly more likely.

The rules: covariance behaves like a product

  • Symmetry and the diagonal. Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X), and Cov(X, X) = E(X − μ_X)² = Var X: variance is the covariance of a variable with itself.
  • Constants drop out. Cov(X, c) = 0 for a constant c, and Cov(X + a, Y + b) = Cov(X, Y): shifting either variable changes nothing, since covariance only sees deviations from the mean.
  • Bilinearity. Cov(aX + bY, Z) = a Cov(X, Z) + b Cov(Y, Z), and the same in the second argument. Proof: the deviation of aX + bY from its mean is a(X − μ_X) + b(Y − μ_Y); multiply by (Z − μ_Z) and use linearity of expectation. Iterating, Cov(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ, Σⱼ bⱼYⱼ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢbⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Yⱼ): expand like a product of sums, and every pair of terms contributes. In particular Cov(rX + s, tY + u) = rt Cov(X, Y).
  • Worked. With Cov(X, Y) = −13/5000 above, Cov(−2X + 7, 5Y − 3) = (−2)(5)(−13/5000) = 13/500: the sign flips once for the −2, and the scale grows by 10.
rulesCov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X) Cov(X, X) = Var X Cov(X, c) = 0 Cov(rX + s, tY + u) = r·t·Cov(X, Y) bilinearity: Cov(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ, Σⱼ bⱼYⱼ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢbⱼ·Cov(Xᵢ, Yⱼ) workeddice: Cov(S, M) = Var S/2 = 35/12 = 2.917 f = (2/75)(2x²y + xy²): Cov = 171/50 − (109/50)(157/100) = −13/5000 indicators: Cov(1_A, 1_B) = P(A ∩ B) − P(A)·P(B) Cov(−2X + 7, 5Y − 3) = −10·Cov(X, Y) = 13/500

Delivered: the variance of a sum, with every covariance term

  • Two variables. Bilinearity with X + Y in both slots: Var(X + Y) = Cov(X + Y, X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y). This is the correction term that Random Variables & Discrete Distributions promised: variances add exactly when the covariance is zero, and in general the extra term is twice the covariance. For a difference, Var(X − Y) = Var X + Var Y − 2 Cov(X, Y).
  • n variables. Var(Σᵢ Xᵢ) = Cov(Σᵢ Xᵢ, Σⱼ Xⱼ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) = Σᵢ Var Xᵢ + 2 Σ_(i<j) Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). There are n variance terms on the diagonal and n(n − 1) covariance terms off it, so with many variables the covariances can easily dominate: a small positive correlation between every pair adds up. This is the general formula that Expectation, Variance & Inequalities stated for the uncorrelated case.
  • With weights. Var(Σᵢ aᵢXᵢ) = Σᵢ Σⱼ aᵢaⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). Collect the covariances in the covariance matrix Σ with entries Σᵢⱼ = Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ); then the formula is the quadratic form Var(aᵀX) = aᵀΣa.
  • Worked: the rectangle density. Var X = 989/2500 = 0.3956 and Var Y = 791/10000 = 0.0791 add to 0.4747, while Var(X + Y) = 0.4747 + 2(−0.0026) = 0.4695 = 939/2000. The covariance term is small here, but it is the only thing standing between the naive sum and the truth.
  • Worked: a paired design. Two measurements of the same quantity on the same subject, before and after a treatment, each with standard deviation 10 and correlation 0.8 between them, have Var(after − before) = 100 + 100 − 2(0.8)(10)(10) = 40, a standard deviation of 6.3. On two independent groups the difference would have variance 200, standard deviation 14.1. The positive covariance is subtracted, and that is the whole advantage of pairing.

The covariance matrix

  • Definition. For a random vector X = (X₁, …, Xₙ) with mean vector μ, the covariance matrix is Σ = E[(X − μ)(X − μ)ᵀ], the n × n matrix of all pairwise covariances, with the variances on the diagonal. It is symmetric.
  • It is positive semi-definite. For every vector a, aᵀΣa = Var(aᵀX) ≥ 0, because a variance cannot be negative. So Σ has non-negative eigenvalues, and it is singular exactly when some linear combination aᵀX is constant, a linear dependence among the coordinates. Not every symmetric table of numbers with a positive diagonal is a covariance matrix; it must pass this test. The covariance matrix as a Gram matrix, and its eigenvectors as principal directions, are developed in the covariance matrix of the linear-algebra notes.
  • Linear maps. For a fixed matrix A and vector b, Y = AX + b has mean Aμ + b and covariance AΣAᵀ: bilinearity in matrix form. With A a single row aᵀ this is aᵀΣa again.
covariance matrixΣ = E[(X − μ)(X − μ)ᵀ], Σᵢⱼ = Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ), Σᵢᵢ = Var Xᵢ aᵀΣa = Var(aᵀX) ≥ 0 (positive semi-definite) Cov(AX + b) = AΣAᵀ

Uncorrelated is not independent

  • Independent ⟹ uncorrelated. If X ⊥ Y, the product rule gives E XY = E X E Y, so Cov(X, Y) = 0. Variables with zero covariance are called uncorrelated.
  • The converse fails: the classic counterexample. Let X be symmetric about 0 with E X³ finite, and Y = X². Then Cov(X, X²) = E X³ − E X · E X² = 0 − 0 = 0, because the odd moments of a symmetric variable vanish. Yet Y is a function of X: knowing X tells you Y exactly. With X uniform on {−1, 0, 1}: P(X = 0, Y = 0) = ⅓ while P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = ⅓ · ⅓ = 1/9. With X ~ N(0, 1) or X ~ U(−1, 1) the scatter of (X, X²) is a perfect parabola with correlation exactly zero. The positive products on the right arm cancel the negative products on the left arm.
  • The diamond. X and Y take values 0, 1, 2, with probability ¼ on each of the four points (1, 0), (0, 1), (2, 1), (1, 2). Both have mean 1, and E XY = (0 + 0 + 2 + 2)/4 = 1 = E X E Y: uncorrelated. But P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0 ≠ P(X = 0)P(Y = 0) = 1/16: dependent. Given X = 1, Y is 0 or 2; given X = 0, Y must be 1. The dependence is real but has no linear direction.
  • The circle. A point uniform on the unit circle, (cos Θ, sin Θ) with Θ ~ U(0, 2π): E cos Θ sin Θ = ½ E sin 2Θ = 0, so the coordinates are uncorrelated, and yet X² + Y² = 1 exactly. Likewise the sum and difference of two iid uniforms: Cov(U + V, U − V) = Var U − Var V = 0, but the pair lives on a tilted square, so learning U + V = 1.9 forces |U − V| ≤ 0.1.
  • When the converse does hold. For two indicators, uncorrelated does mean independent (P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) gives all four cells). And for a pair with a joint normal distribution, zero covariance implies independence (§09). Outside those cases, "uncorrelated" says only that there is no linear trend.

Delivered: the hypergeometric variance, through indicator covariances

  • The setting. A lot of N items contains K marked ones; draw n without replacement, and let X be the number of marked items drawn. Write X = I₁ + ⋯ + Iₙ, with Iᵢ the indicator that draw i is marked, and p = K/N. The hypergeometric section stated the variance with its finite-population correction and deferred the covariance that produces it.
  • Each indicator. By exchangeability (§01), every draw is marked with probability p, so E Iᵢ = p and Var Iᵢ = p(1 − p).
  • Each pair. Draws i ≠ j are both marked with probability (K/N)·((K − 1)/(N − 1)), the first-two-draws calculation, valid for any pair by exchangeability. So
    Cov(Iᵢ, Iⱼ) = K(K − 1)/(N(N − 1)) − K²/N² = K[N(K − 1) − K(N − 1)]/(N²(N − 1)) = K(K − N)/(N²(N − 1)) = −p(1 − p)/(N − 1)
    Negative, as it should be: a marked first draw leaves fewer marked items for the second.
  • The variance. n variances and n(n − 1) equal covariances: Var X = np(1 − p) − n(n − 1) p(1 − p)/(N − 1) = np(1 − p)[1 − (n − 1)/(N − 1)] = np(1 − p)·(N − n)/(N − 1). The bracket is the finite-population correction, and it comes entirely from the covariance term.
  • Worked: acceptance sampling. N = 100 parts with K = 5 defective, n = 10 inspected: p = 0.05, each pair of draws has covariance −0.05 × 0.95/99 = −0.00048 (correlation −1/99 = −0.0101), and Var X = 10 × 0.0475 × 90/99 = 0.432, against the binomial 0.475. A tiny correlation, multiplied by the 90 ordered pairs of draws, removes 9% of the variance. At n = N the correction is 0: drawing the whole lot always finds exactly K.

Delivered: why multinomial counts have covariance −n·pᵢ·pⱼ

  • The setting. n independent trials, each landing in category j with probability pⱼ; Nⱼ counts the trials in category j. The multinomial section stated that two different counts are negatively related with covariance −n pᵢ pⱼ.
  • Indicators per trial. Write Nᵢ = Σₜ Aₜ and Nⱼ = Σₜ Bₜ, where Aₜ indicates that trial t lands in i and Bₜ that it lands in j. By bilinearity, Cov(Nᵢ, Nⱼ) = Σₜ Σₛ Cov(Aₜ, Bₛ).
  • Different trials contribute nothing. For t ≠ s the trials are independent, so Cov(Aₜ, Bₛ) = 0.
  • The same trial contributes −pᵢpⱼ. One trial cannot land in two categories, so AₜBₜ = 0 and Cov(Aₜ, Bₜ) = 0 − pᵢpⱼ. Summing the n diagonal terms: Cov(Nᵢ, Nⱼ) = −n pᵢ pⱼ. The correlation is −√(pᵢpⱼ/((1 − pᵢ)(1 − pⱼ))), independent of n.
  • A cross-check by merging. Merge categories i and j: Nᵢ + Nⱼ ~ Bin(n, pᵢ + pⱼ), with variance n(pᵢ + pⱼ)(1 − pᵢ − pⱼ). Setting that equal to npᵢ(1 − pᵢ) + npⱼ(1 − pⱼ) + 2 Cov(Nᵢ, Nⱼ) and solving gives −npᵢpⱼ again.
  • Worked: 60 rolls of a fair die. Each count has variance 60 × (1/6)(5/6) = 8.33, any two counts have covariance −60/36 = −1.67, and correlation −(1/6)/(5/6) = −0.2. In matrix form the covariance matrix is n(diag(p) − ppᵀ); its rows sum to zero because Σ Nⱼ = n is constant, so it is singular, exactly the case "aᵀX constant" above with a = (1, …, 1).

04 Correlation: covariance without units

The correlation coefficient ρ(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) is the covariance of the standardised variables: it does not change when either variable is shifted or rescaled, it always lies between −1 and 1, and it reaches ±1 exactly when one variable is a linear function of the other. Those properties make it the standard summary of how strongly two quantities move together, and they also mark its limits: it measures linear association and nothing else. Its most consequential use is in averages: the variance of a mean of correlated quantities has a floor set by their correlation, which is why diversification works only up to a point and why an ensemble of similar models is worth fewer models than it contains.

Definition and invariance

  • Definition. For variables with positive finite variances, ρ(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y) = E[Z_X Z_Y], where Z_X = (X − μ_X)/σ_X and Z_Y are the z-scores. It is the average product of standardised deviations, a pure number.
  • Affine maps. ρ(aX + b, cY + d) = sign(ac) · ρ(X, Y) for a, c ≠ 0: the covariance picks up ac and the standard deviations pick up |a||c|. Converting temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit or heights from inches to centimetres leaves every correlation unchanged; reversing the direction of one scale flips its sign. For the rectangle density of §01 and §03, ρ(X, Y) = −0.0026/√(0.3956 × 0.0791) = −0.0147, and ρ(−2X + 7, 5Y − 3) = +0.0147.
  • Worked: two dice. ρ(S, M) = (35/12)/√((35/6)(2555/1296)) = 0.860: knowing the maximum says a lot about the sum, but not everything.

Delivered: |ρ| ≤ 1, with equality only for a straight line

  • From Cauchy–Schwarz. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the centred variables gives Cov(X, Y)² ≤ Var X · Var Y, hence |ρ| ≤ 1. Here is a second proof, using only the variance of a sum, that also identifies the equality case.
  • The variance proof. For the z-scores, 0 ≤ Var(Z_X + Z_Y) = 1 + 1 + 2ρ = 2(1 + ρ), so ρ ≥ −1; and 0 ≤ Var(Z_X − Z_Y) = 2(1 − ρ), so ρ ≤ 1.
  • The equality case. ρ = 1 means Var(Z_X − Z_Y) = 0, so Z_Y = Z_X with probability 1 (a zero-variance variable is constant, and this one has mean 0). Unwinding the standardisation: Y = μ_Y + (σ_Y/σ_X)(X − μ_X), a straight line with positive slope. Likewise ρ = −1 forces a line with negative slope. Conversely, if Y = a + bX with b ≠ 0, then ρ = sign(b). So |ρ| = 1 exactly when all the probability lies on a line.
  • The geometry. For centred variables, E XY is an inner product and σ is a length, so ρ is the cosine of the angle between X − μ_X and Y − μ_Y. Uncorrelated means orthogonal; ρ = ±1 means parallel. The same picture, with data vectors in place of random variables, gives the sample correlation as the cosine between centred columns.

What ρ² measures: the best line

  • The best linear predictor. Among all lines a + bX, the one minimising E(Y − a − bX)² has b = Cov(X, Y)/Var X = ρσ_Y/σ_X and a = μ_Y − bμ_X (set the two partial derivatives to zero; this is the population version of least squares, and it appeared in the best-predictor discussion).
  • Its error. Substituting, E(Y − a − bX)² = Var(Y − bX) = σ_Y² − 2bCov(X, Y) + b²σ_X² = σ_Y²(1 − ρ²). So ρ² is the fraction of the variance of Y that the best straight line removes, and 1 − ρ² the fraction left. A correlation of 0.5 removes only 25% of the variance; 0.9 removes 81%; even 0.99 leaves 2%. A correlation that "looks high" can leave most of the variance unexplained.
  • Correlation is symmetric, prediction is not. The best line for predicting Y from X has slope ρσ_Y/σ_X; the best line for predicting X from Y, drawn in the same plane, has slope σ_Y/(ρσ_X). They coincide only if |ρ| = 1. The two regression lines and the regression to the mean between them are drawn in §09.

Correlation measures linear association, and only that

  • Zero correlation, perfect dependence. Y = X² with X symmetric has ρ = 0 (§03). The same function on X ~ U(0, 1) gives Cov(X, X²) = E X³ − E X E X² = 1/4 − 1/6 = 1/12, Var X² = 1/5 − 1/9 = 4/45 and ρ = (1/12)/√((1/12)(4/45)) = 0.968. The dependence is identical in kind; the correlation changes from 0 to 0.97 because on (0, 1) the parabola happens to be nearly straight.
  • The same ρ, different pictures. Four small datasets can share their means, variances and a correlation of 0.82 while one is a clean linear cloud, one a smooth curve, one a perfect line with one outlier, and one a vertical stack with a single far point that creates the whole correlation. A correlation is a summary of a scatterplot; it is never a substitute for looking at one. The data-side treatment, with the plots, is in the correlation coefficient, and describing two variables covers the scatterplot itself.
  • One point can make or break it. Because correlation averages products of deviations, a single observation far from the centre in both coordinates contributes a huge product. Add one point at (6, 6) to 99 independent standard normal pairs: it contributes 36 to a sum of products that otherwise averages zero, against total sums of squares of about 99 + 36 in each coordinate, so the correlation jumps from about 0 to about 36/135 = 0.27. In the figure above, the "outlier" shape with n = 100 shows 0.24 at the default seed; with n = 300 the same single point is diluted to about 0.05 at the default seed (the heuristic 36/335 says about 0.1). The influence of one point shrinks only as the sample grows. Rank correlations (Spearman's, which correlates the ranks instead of the values) are the robust alternative; they measure monotone rather than linear association.
  • Correlations constrain each other. The correlation matrix of three variables must be positive semi-definite, which bounds ρ_XZ given ρ_XY and ρ_YZ: ρ_XZ ≥ ρ_XY ρ_YZ − √((1 − ρ_XY²)(1 − ρ_YZ²)). With ρ_XY = ρ_YZ = 0.6 the bound is 0.36 − 0.64 = −0.28: X can be negatively correlated with Z although both are positively correlated with Y. Correlation is not transitive, unless the two links are strong enough (ρ_XY² + ρ_YZ² > 1).
  • Correlation is not causation. A correlation between X and Y can arise because X affects Y, because Y affects X, or because a third variable drives both (shoe size and reading ability in schoolchildren, both driven by age). Nothing in the joint distribution alone distinguishes these; see association versus causation.

Averages of correlated quantities: why diversification has a floor

  • The formula. Let X₁, …, Xₙ each have variance σ² and every pair have correlation ρ. By §03, Var(Σ Xᵢ) = nσ² + n(n − 1)ρσ², so the average has
    Var X̄ = σ²/n + ((n − 1)/n)·ρσ² = σ²·(ρ + (1 − ρ)/n) → ρσ² as n → ∞
    The independent part (1 − ρ)σ²/n averages away; the shared part ρσ² does not. However many correlated quantities are averaged, the variance never falls below ρσ².
  • Effective sample size. Setting σ²(ρ + (1 − ρ)/n) = σ²/n_eff: n_eff = n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ), the number of independent quantities the average is worth. With ρ = 0.2, ten quantities are worth 3.6 independent ones, and infinitely many are worth at most 1/ρ = 5. With ρ = 0.05, a hundred are worth 16.8. This is the same "design effect" that makes clustered survey samples less informative than their size suggests.
  • A portfolio. Two assets with return standard deviations 0.20 and 0.30. A 50/50 mix has standard deviation √(0.25 × 0.04 + 0.25 × 0.09 + 2 × 0.25 × ρ × 0.06): 0.250 if ρ = 1 (no diversification, just the average risk), 0.218 at ρ = 0.5, 0.180 at ρ = 0, 0.132 at ρ = −0.5. The minimum-variance weight on the first asset is w* = (σ₂² − ρσ₁σ₂)/(σ₁² + σ₂² − 2ρσ₁σ₂); at ρ = 0.2 it is 0.736, with standard deviation 0.181, below that of either asset alone. Combining risky things reduces risk only to the extent that they are not perfectly correlated.
  • Why correlated errors ruin ensembles. An ensemble that averages B models' predictions has error variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B, where σ² is one model's variance and ρ the correlation between models' errors: the formula above, unchanged. Training the same architecture on the same data with different seeds gives highly correlated errors, and the ensemble gains little; bagging, random feature subsets and different model families exist to lower ρ. A random forest's per-split feature sampling is a decorrelation device (ensembles of trees, bagging). The same arithmetic explains why a "wisdom of crowds" average fails when the crowd shares its information.
  • Negative correlation helps. With ρ < 0 the floor disappears, and for a given n the variance is below σ²/n (a common negative ρ is only possible for n ≤ 1 + 1/|ρ|); the smallest possible common correlation among n variables is −1/(n − 1), at which the average is constant. Deliberately pairing each simulated draw with a negatively correlated twin (antithetic variates) is a standard variance-reduction trick, developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

05 Conditional distributions: slicing and renormalising

Once X = x is observed, the only pairs still possible are those on the line {X = x}, and the right distribution for Y is the joint distribution restricted to that line and rescaled to total 1. That is the conditional distribution of Y given X = x: a row or column of the joint table divided by its margin, or a slice of the joint density divided by the marginal density. Read the other way, it says that every joint distribution factors as a marginal times a conditional, which is how hierarchical models and two-stage experiments are built, and it gives Bayes' rule for densities, the engine of every posterior.

Discrete: a row of the table, renormalised

  • Definition. For x with p_X(x) > 0, the conditional pmf of Y given X = x is p_(Y|X)(y | x) = P(Y = y | X = x) = p(x, y)/p_X(x). It is conditional probability from Probability Foundations, applied to the events {Y = y} and {X = x}. For each fixed x it is a pmf in y: non-negative, and summing to p_X(x)/p_X(x) = 1.
  • Worked: the sum given the maximum. In the dice table of §01, the column M = 4 holds 2/36, 2/36, 2/36, 1/36 at S = 5, 6, 7, 8, with total P(M = 4) = 7/36. Dividing, S | M = 4 takes the values 5, 6, 7, 8 with probabilities 2/7, 2/7, 2/7, 1/7, and E[S | M = 4] = (10 + 12 + 14 + 8)/7 = 44/7 = 6.29. Seeing a maximum of 4 lowers the expected sum from 7 and confines it to four values out of eleven.
  • The shape is the slice, the height is irrelevant. Dividing by p_X(x) changes the scale of the slice, never its shape. So p_(Y|X)(y | x) ∝ p(x, y) as a function of y: to find a conditional distribution, write down the joint, treat x as a constant, and normalise. This "proportional to" reasoning is the working form of Bayes' rule below.
  • Conditional expectation is the mean of the slice. E[Y | X = x] = Σᵧ y p_(Y|X)(y | x), and Var(Y | X = x) its variance. The tower rule, the best-predictor property and the law of total variance of conditional expectation are all statements about these slice means and slice variances.

Continuous: a slice of the density, renormalised

  • Definition. For x with f_X(x) > 0, the conditional density of Y given X = x is f_(Y|X)(y | x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x), and P(Y ∈ B | X = x) = ∫_B f_(Y|X)(y | x) dy. Geometrically: cut the density surface with the vertical plane X = x, take the curve that the cut traces, and rescale it to have area 1. The area of the raw slice is exactly ∫ f(x, y) dy = f_X(x), which is why dividing by the marginal is the right rescaling.
  • Why conditioning on a probability-zero event is allowed. P(X = x) = 0 for a continuous X, so the elementary definition fails. The density version is justified as a limit: P(Y ≤ y | x ≤ X ≤ x + h) = ∫∫ f / ∫∫ f_X → ∫_(−∞)^y f(x, t)/f_X(x) dt as h → 0, when f is continuous. One honest caveat: the limit depends on how the zero-probability event is approached, and conditioning on the same line through two different families of shrinking regions can give different answers (the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox). In practice, conditioning on X = x for a named variable X, as here, is unambiguous.
  • Worked: x + y on the unit square. f(x, y) = x + y on [0, 1]² has f_Y(y) = y + ½, so f_(X|Y)(x | y) = (x + y)/(y + ½). Then P(X < ¼ | Y = ⅓) = ∫₀^¼ (x + ⅓)/(⅚) dx = (1/32 + 1/12)·(6/5) = 11/80 = 0.1375, below the unconditional P(X < ¼) = ∫₀^¼ (x + ½) dx = 5/32 = 0.156. Seeing a small Y makes a small X less likely: at y = 0 the slice is 2x, tilted hard toward large x, while at y = 1 it is (x + 1)/1.5, nearly flat. The pair is negatively related, with Cov(X, Y) = E XY − (E X)² = 1/3 − (7/12)² = −1/144, which the formula x + y does not advertise. Conditional distributions often move in directions that are hard to guess; compute them.
  • Worked: the curved region. For f(x, y) = (21/4)x²y on x² ≤ y ≤ 1, f_X(x) = (21/8)x²(1 − x⁴), so f_(Y|X)(y | x) = 2y/(1 − x⁴) on x² ≤ y ≤ 1. Given X = ½ this is 32y/15 on [¼, 1], with P(Y ≥ ¾ | X = ½) = ∫_¾¹ 32y/15 dy = 7/15 = 0.467 and E[Y | X = ½] = 2(1 − x⁶)/(3(1 − x⁴)) = 0.7. The x² factor, which made the joint look like a product, cancels completely: all the dependence on x sits in the support [x², 1].

Joint = marginal × conditional: the two-stage view

  • The multiplication rule for distributions. Rearranging the definition, f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_(Y|X)(y | x) = f_Y(y) f_(X|Y)(x | y), and the same with pmfs. A joint distribution can therefore be built in two stages: draw X from its marginal, then draw Y from the conditional given the value drawn. This is the form in which most models are written: a cause, then an effect given the cause.
  • Worked: a two-stage draw. X ~ U(0, 1); given X = x, Y ~ U(x, 1). Then f(x, y) = 1 · 1/(1 − x) on 0 < x < y < 1, and integrating out x, f_Y(y) = ∫₀^y dx/(1 − x) = −ln(1 − y) on (0, 1). Its mean is ¾, as the tower rule found in one line in conditional expectation; the density is what the two-stage view adds.
  • The law of total probability for densities. f_Y(y) = ∫ f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x) dx: the marginal of Y is an average of the conditional densities, weighted by the distribution of X. It is the continuous form of total probability, and when X is discrete it is a mixture (§10).
  • Independence in this language. X ⊥ Y exactly when the conditional distribution of Y given X = x is the same for every x (and then equals the marginal of Y). In the figure, independence would mean every slice has the same normalised shape.
  • Conditional independence. X and Y are conditionally independent given Z if f(x, y | z) = f(x | z) f(y | z) for every z. Two noisy thermometers in the same room are dependent (both track the room temperature) but independent given that temperature; this is the structure that naive Bayes assumes for features given the class, and that graphical models encode in their missing edges. Conditional independence neither implies nor is implied by independence: two independent coins become dependent once you are told whether they agreed.

Bayes' rule for densities

  • The rule. Equating the two factorisations, f_(X|Y)(x | y) = f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x)/f_Y(y), with f_Y(y) = ∫ f_(Y|X)(y | x′) f_X(x′) dx′. As a function of x, f_(X|Y)(x | y) ∝ f_(Y|X)(y | x) f_X(x): posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. It is Bayes' rule with densities in place of probabilities, and it holds just as well when one variable is discrete and the other continuous.
  • A discrete cause, a continuous observation. A class C with prior P(C = c) produces a feature with density f(x | c). Then P(C = c | X = x) = P(C = c) f(x | c)/Σ_c′ P(C = c′) f(x | c′): a probability computed from densities. Every generative classifier, from naive Bayes to a Gaussian mixture's "responsibilities" (§10), evaluates this ratio.
  • A continuous parameter, discrete data: the beta–binomial model. Let the unknown success probability be P ~ Beta(a, b), and given P = p, let K | P = p ~ Bin(n, p). The joint "density" (a density in p, a pmf in k) is
    f(p, k) = [p^(a−1)(1 − p)^(b−1)/B(a, b)] · C(n, k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ 0 < p < 1, k = 0, 1, …, n
    Treating k as fixed, the posterior is proportional to p^(a+k−1)(1 − p)^(b+n−k−1), the shape of Beta(a + k, b + n − k), the conjugate update of the beta distribution. What the joint adds is the other factorisation: integrating out p with the beta integral gives the marginal (prior predictive) distribution of the count, P(K = k) = C(n, k) B(a + k, b + n − k)/B(a, b), the beta-binomial distribution.
  • Worked: prior Beta(2, 2), 7 successes in 10. The posterior is Beta(9, 5): mean 9/14 = 0.643, central 95% interval 0.386 to 0.861, and P(p > ½ | data) = 0.867, up from ½ under the symmetric prior. Before the data, the beta-binomial gave P(K = 7) = 120 · B(9, 5)/B(2, 2) = 0.1119; its mean is 5 and its variance n·ab(a + b + n)/((a + b)²(a + b + 1)) = 7, far above the binomial 10 × ¼ = 2.5, because uncertainty about p adds the between-p variance of the law of total variance.
  • A flat prior gives a flat count. With a = b = 1, P(K = k) = C(n, k)·k!(n − k)!/(n + 1)! = 1/(n + 1) for every k: if the success probability is uniform, the number of successes is uniform on {0, …, n}. That is the uniform count found for the hierarchical binomial in the law of total variance, now derived from the joint distribution.

Two readings of one table. The joint f(p, k) can be sliced two ways. Slicing at a fixed p gives the sampling model, Bin(n, p): what data to expect if the parameter were known. Slicing at the observed k gives the posterior: what to believe about the parameter once the data are known. Frequentist and Bayesian calculations are the two directions of conditioning on the same joint distribution.
