06 Sums of random variables: convolution

The mean and variance of a sum need only the means, variances and covariances of the terms. The distribution of a sum needs more: Z = X + Y equals z along the whole line x + y = z, so its pmf or density is the total joint probability along that line. For independent terms that total is the convolution of the two distributions: flip one, slide it past the other, and integrate the product. This section computes it, proves with it that Poisson counts and gamma times are closed under addition, shows why two uniforms add to a triangle, and cross-checks every result against the moment generating function.

Discrete convolution

  • The line. {X + Y = z} is the disjoint union of {X = z − y, Y = y} over the possible y, so p_Z(z) = Σᵧ p(z − y, y) for any joint pmf. If X ⊥ Y the joint factorises and p_Z(z) = Σᵧ p_X(z − y) p_Y(y), the convolution p_X * p_Y.
  • Worked: two dice. P(S = s) = Σ_(j=1)^6 P(D₁ = s − j)·(1/6), and P(D₁ = s − j) = 1/6 exactly when 1 ≤ s − j ≤ 6. The number of such j is 6 − |s − 7|, so P(S = s) = (6 − |s − 7|)/36: the right margin of the table in §01, now as a convolution. The triangle is the overlap length of two shifted boxes.
  • Worked: a spare radio. A solo sailor has two radios, each failing independently with probability p = 1/75 each day it is switched on. The days until each fails are geometric, P(X = k) = (1 − p)^(k−1)p. Their sum has P(X + Y = k) = Σ_(j=1)^(k−1) (1 − p)^(k−j−1)p (1 − p)^(j−1)p = (k − 1)p²(1 − p)^(k−2) for k ≥ 2, the negative binomial with r = 2, not a geometric. The expected number of days of radio contact is E(X − 1) + E(Y − 1) = 148, comfortably over a 100-day crossing, yet P(X + Y − 2 ≤ 99) = Σ_(k=2)^(101) (k − 1)p²(1 − p)^(k−2) = 0.390. A mean that looks safe hides a 39% chance of losing contact.

Delivered: Poisson counts add, by convolution and the binomial theorem

  • The Poisson section stated that independent X ~ Poisson(μ₁) and Y ~ Poisson(μ₂) add to Poisson(μ₁ + μ₂), with a promise of a direct proof. Convolve, for n ≥ 0:
    P(X + Y = n) = Σ_(k=0)^n e^(−μ₁) μ₁ᵏ/k! · e^(−μ₂) μ₂ⁿ⁻ᵏ/(n − k)! = (e^(−(μ₁+μ₂))/n!) · Σ_(k=0)^n C(n, k) μ₁ᵏ μ₂ⁿ⁻ᵏ = e^(−(μ₁+μ₂)) (μ₁ + μ₂)ⁿ / n!
    The middle step multiplies and divides by n! to turn 1/(k!(n − k)!) into C(n, k)/n!; the last step is the binomial theorem. The result is the Poisson(μ₁ + μ₂) pmf. ∎
  • A bonus from the same algebra. Given the total, P(X = k | X + Y = n) = [e^(−μ₁)μ₁ᵏ/k! · e^(−μ₂)μ₂ⁿ⁻ᵏ/(n − k)!]/[e^(−(μ₁+μ₂))(μ₁ + μ₂)ⁿ/n!] = C(n, k)(μ₁/(μ₁ + μ₂))ᵏ(μ₂/(μ₁ + μ₂))ⁿ⁻ᵏ: X | X + Y = n ~ Bin(n, μ₁/(μ₁ + μ₂)). If 10 accidents occurred on two roads with rates 2 and 3 per month, the number on the first is Bin(10, 0.4). Each event independently "belongs" to a source in proportion to its rate, which is the race of exponentials of §02 seen through counts.
  • Binomials too. For independent Bin(n, p) and Bin(m, p), the convolution Σₖ C(n, k)C(m, j − k)pʲ(1 − p)^(n+m−j) collapses by Vandermonde's identity Σₖ C(n, k)C(m, j − k) = C(n + m, j) to Bin(n + m, p). The success probability must be shared; with different p the sum is not binomial.

Continuous convolution

  • Derivation. For independent X, Y with densities, F_Z(z) = P(X + Y ≤ z) = ∫ [∫_(−∞)^(z−y) f_X(x) dx] f_Y(y) dy = ∫ F_X(z − y) f_Y(y) dy. Differentiating under the integral, f_Z(z) = ∫ f_X(z − y) f_Y(y) dy. Without independence, f_Z(z) = ∫ f(z − y, y) dy, the joint density integrated along the line x + y = z (with a factor that §07 explains is 1).
  • Flip and slide. As a function of y, f_X(z − y) is f_X reflected and shifted so that its origin sits at z. The convolution at z is the area under the product of this flipped copy and f_Y. Sliding z from left to right traces out f_Z. The figure below does exactly this.
  • Worked: two uniforms make a triangle. For X, Y ~ U(0, 1) independent, f_X(z − y) f_Y(y) = 1 when both 0 ≤ y ≤ 1 and z − 1 ≤ y ≤ z, so f_Z(z) is the length of the overlap of [0, 1] and [z − 1, z]: z on [0, 1] and 2 − z on [1, 2]. The sum is triangular, not uniform: values near 1 can be made in many ways, values near 0 or 2 only by both terms being extreme. This is the distribution behind the "Not closed" remark in moment generating functions, whose mgf ((eᵗ − 1)/t)² is the square of the uniform's and not of the uniform form.
  • More uniforms. Convolving again gives the sum of three, piecewise quadratic: z²/2 on [0, 1], (−2z² + 6z − 3)/2 on [1, 2], (3 − z)²/2 on [2, 3], with peak ¾ at 1.5. Each convolution smooths one more derivative, and the shape rapidly approaches a normal curve: the sum of 12 uniforms minus 6 has mean 0 and variance 1 and was once a standard way to generate normal draws. Why sums become normal is the central limit theorem of Limit Theorems & Convergence.

Delivered: gamma times add, by convolution

  • Exponentials first. For independent T₁, T₂ ~ Exp(λ), f(z) = ∫₀^z λe^(−λ(z−y)) λe^(−λy) dy = λ²e^(−λz) ∫₀^z dy = λ²z e^(−λz): the exponents combine to e^(−λz) whatever y is, and the integral is just the length z. Convolving with a third gives λ³z²e^(−λz)/2, and by induction the sum of n is λⁿzⁿ⁻¹e^(−λz)/(n − 1)!, the Gamma(n, λ) (Erlang) density. A counter where customers arrive at rate ½ per minute receives its 30th customer within the first hour with probability P(Gamma(30, ½) ≤ 60) = 0.524.
  • General shapes. The gamma section stated that independent Gamma(α₁, β) and Gamma(α₂, β) (shape–rate, the same family as Gamma(α, λ) there) add to Gamma(α₁ + α₂, β). Convolve, for z > 0:
    f(z) = ∫₀^z [β^α₁ x^(α₁−1) e^(−βx)/Γ(α₁)] · [β^α₂ (z − x)^(α₂−1) e^(−β(z−x))/Γ(α₂)] dx = (β^(α₁+α₂) e^(−βz) / (Γ(α₁)Γ(α₂))) · ∫₀^z x^(α₁−1)(z − x)^(α₂−1) dx substitute x = z·t, dx = z dt: ∫₀^z x^(α₁−1)(z − x)^(α₂−1) dx = z^(α₁+α₂−1) ∫₀¹ t^(α₁−1)(1 − t)^(α₂−1) dt = z^(α₁+α₂−1) · B(α₁, α₂) so f(z) = [B(α₁, α₂)·Γ(α₁ + α₂)/(Γ(α₁)Γ(α₂))] · β^(α₁+α₂) z^(α₁+α₂−1) e^(−βz)/Γ(α₁ + α₂)
    The last factor is the Gamma(α₁ + α₂, β) density. The bracket is a constant, and since f and the gamma density both integrate to 1, the bracket must equal 1. So the sum is Gamma(α₁ + α₂, β). ∎
  • A free theorem. The bracket being 1 says B(α₁, α₂) = Γ(α₁)Γ(α₂)/Γ(α₁ + α₂): the beta–gamma identity drops out of the gamma convolution, because a probability density has no choice about its total. §07 proves the identity again directly by a change of variables, and extracts more from it.
  • Rates must match. With different rates the exponentials in the integrand no longer combine into a single e^(−βz), and the sum of Exp(1) and Exp(2) is 2(e^(−z) − e^(−2z)), not a gamma.
  • Normals. For independent N(0, 1) terms, f(z) = ∫ φ(z − y)φ(y) dy = (1/2π)∫ e^(−(2y² − 2yz + z²)/2) dy; completing the square, 2y² − 2yz + z² = 2(y − z/2)² + z²/2, so f(z) = e^(−z²/4)/√(4π), the N(0, 2) density. The general case is the same algebra with more symbols; the mgf route does it in one line.

Differences, and the mgf cross-check

  • Differences. Z = X − Y is X + (−Y), so f_Z(z) = ∫ f_X(z + y) f_Y(y) dy. For two iid Exp(λ) waits, the difference has the Laplace density (λ/2)e^(−λ|z|): symmetric, with exponential tails on both sides. That is the noise distribution whose maximum-likelihood fit is least absolute deviations.
  • Transforms turn convolution into multiplication. The moment generating function of a sum of independent terms is the product of their mgfs. In this language: the mgf (a Laplace transform, or the characteristic function, a Fourier transform) of f_X * f_Y is the product of the transforms. Every closure result above can be checked in one line: e^(μ₁(eᵗ−1))e^(μ₂(eᵗ−1)) = e^((μ₁+μ₂)(eᵗ−1)) for the Poisson, (β/(β − t))^α₁(β/(β − t))^α₂ for the gamma, ((eᵗ − 1)/t)² for the triangle. Convolution computes the density; the transform identifies it.
  • Which to use. When the answer is a named family, the mgf is faster. When it is not (the radio sum's tail probability, the triangle's density, a difference of exponentials with different rates), the convolution is the only direct route, and numerically it is just a sum, which is how the figure computes it.

07 Change of variables in two dimensions: the Jacobian

In one dimension, a smooth one-to-one transformation Y = g(X) changes a density by the factor |dx/dy|, the local stretching of the line (transformations). In two dimensions the line becomes the plane, lengths become areas, and the stretching factor becomes the absolute value of a Jacobian determinant. With that one formula, sums, differences, ratios, polar coordinates and the beta–gamma connection all become routine, and two classic results fall out: the normal constant √(2π), by passing to polar coordinates, and the Box–Muller method for turning uniform random numbers into normal ones.

The theorem

  • Statement. Let (X, Y) have joint density f_(X,Y), positive on an open set S. Let (U, V) = T(X, Y), where T is one-to-one from S onto a set T(S), and its inverse x = x(u, v), y = y(u, v) has continuous partial derivatives. Define the Jacobian
    J(u, v) = det [ ∂x/∂u ∂x/∂v ; ∂y/∂u ∂y/∂v ] = (∂x/∂u)(∂y/∂v) − (∂x/∂v)(∂y/∂u)
    and assume it is non-zero on T(S), and that f_(X,Y) vanishes outside S, so that P((X, Y) ∈ S) = 1. Then (U, V) has density f_(U,V)(u, v) = f_(X,Y)(x(u, v), y(u, v)) · |J(u, v)| on T(S), and 0 elsewhere.
  • Why the determinant. Near a point, a smooth map acts like its linear approximation, and a linear map multiplies every area by the absolute value of its determinant. A small rectangle of area du dv in the (u, v) plane comes from a small parallelogram of area |J| du dv in the (x, y) plane. Probability is conserved, f_(U,V) du dv = f_(X,Y) |J| du dv, and that is the formula. It is the same bookkeeping as the substitution rule for double integrals.
  • Forward or inverse. The Jacobian of the inverse map is the reciprocal of the Jacobian of the forward map: |J| = 1/|∂(u, v)/∂(x, y)|, evaluated at the matching point. Use whichever derivatives are easier.
  • Linear maps. For (U, V)ᵀ = A(X, Y)ᵀ + b with A invertible, f_(U,V)(w) = f_(X,Y)(A⁻¹(w − b))/|det A|. In n dimensions the statement is identical, with an n × n determinant.
  • When T is not one-to-one. If several points map to the same (u, v), split S into pieces on which T is one-to-one and add the contributions from each piece. Sorting a sample (§08) is the main example: n! pieces, one per ordering.
  • One output, not two. To find the density of a single function U = g(X, Y), invent a convenient second coordinate V (often V = Y or V = X), apply the theorem, and integrate V out. With U = X + Y and V = Y: x = u − v, y = v, J = 1, so f_U(u) = ∫ f(u − v, v) dv. The convolution formula of §06 is a corollary, and the "factor 1" it mentioned is this Jacobian.
change of variables in the planef_(U,V)(u, v) = f_(X,Y)(x(u, v), y(u, v)) · |J|, J = (∂x/∂u)(∂y/∂v) − (∂x/∂v)(∂y/∂u) = 1 / det ∂(u, v)/∂(x, y) linear: f_(AX+b)(w) = f_X(A⁻¹(w − b)) / |det A| one output: add V, transform, integrate V out not one-to-one: sum over the pieces

Worked: sums and differences

  • The map. U = X + Y, V = X − Y inverts to x = (u + v)/2, y = (u − v)/2, with J = det[½ ½; ½ −½] = −½ and |J| = ½: the map doubles areas (its matrix [1 1; 1 −1] has determinant −2: a reflection combined with a scaling by √2 in each direction).
  • Two iid normals. For X, Y ~ N(0, 1) independent, x² + y² = (u² + v²)/2, so f_(U,V)(u, v) = (1/2π) e^(−(u² + v²)/4) · ½ = [e^(−u²/4)/√(4π)] · [e^(−v²/4)/√(4π)]. The density factorises: U and V are independent, each N(0, 2). The sum and the difference of two iid normals carry separate information. (The spherical symmetry of the standard normal is all that is used, and remarkably the normal is the only distribution for which the sum and difference of iid copies are independent.)
  • Two iid uniforms. The same map sends the unit square to a diamond with corners (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 0), (1, −1) in the (u, v) plane, with density ½ inside. Cov(U, V) = Var X − Var Y = 0, so U and V are uncorrelated, but the diamond is not a rectangle, so they are dependent: at u = 1.9, v is confined to [−0.1, 0.1]. Integrating v out over the diamond's cross-section of length 2·min(u, 2 − u) gives f_U(u) = min(u, 2 − u), the triangle of §06 again.

Products and ratios, briefly

  • Ratio. For Z = X/Y, take W = Y: x = zw, y = w, J = det[w z; 0 1] = w, so f_Z(z) = ∫ f(zw, w) |w| dw. For independent standard normals, f_Z(z) = (1/2π)∫ |w| e^(−(1 + z²)w²/2) dw = (1/π) · 1/(1 + z²): the ratio of two independent standard normals is standard Cauchy, the distribution with no mean from Expectation, Variance & Inequalities. A ratio whose denominator can be near zero inherits heavy tails.
  • Product. For Z = XY, take W = X: y = z/w, |J| = 1/|w|, so f_Z(z) = ∫ f(w, z/w) dw/|w|. An architect's rectangular plots with independent sides uniform on [0, 10] metres have area density f(z) = (ln 100 − ln z)/100 on (0, 100), and cdf z(1 + 2 ln 10 − ln z)/100: P(area ≤ 25 m²) = 0.597, although the mean area is E X E Y = 25. The product of uniforms piles up near 0.
  • A ratio that is uniform. For independent X, Y ~ Exp(λ), X/(X + Y) ~ U(0, 1): given X + Y = s, the first arrival X is uniform on (0, s). It is the a = b = 1 case of the beta–gamma result below.

Delivered: the polar trick for the normal constant

  • The normal section evaluated I = ∫ e^(−x²/2) dx by squaring it and switching to polar coordinates, and deferred the justification of the area element r dr dθ. Here it is in full.
  • Square the integral. Since the integrand is positive and the integrals converge absolutely, I² = ∫ e^(−x²/2) dx · ∫ e^(−y²/2) dy = ∬ e^(−(x² + y²)/2) dx dy over the whole plane (Fubini's theorem). The one-dimensional integral has no elementary antiderivative; the two-dimensional one is about to have one.
  • The Jacobian. Polar coordinates x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, with r > 0 and 0 < θ < 2π, map the half-strip one-to-one onto the plane minus a ray (a set of zero area, which does not change the integral). Then
    J = det [ ∂x/∂r ∂x/∂θ ; ∂y/∂r ∂y/∂θ ] = det [ cos θ −r sin θ ; sin θ r cos θ ] = r cos²θ + r sin²θ = r
    The area element is dx dy = r dr dθ: a small polar box at radius r has sides dr and r dθ.
  • Integrate. x² + y² = r², so I² = ∫₀^(2π) ∫₀^∞ e^(−r²/2) r dr dθ = 2π · [−e^(−r²/2)]₀^∞ = 2π · 1 = 2π. The factor r supplied by the Jacobian is exactly the derivative of r²/2, which is what makes the inner integral elementary. Since I > 0, I = √(2π), and φ(z) = e^(−z²/2)/√(2π) integrates to 1. ∎
  • The same computation as a statement about random variables. Let Z₁, Z₂ be iid N(0, 1) and write the point (Z₁, Z₂) in polar form (R, Θ). The joint density of (R, Θ) is (1/2π)e^(−r²/2) · r = [1/(2π)] · [r e^(−r²/2)], which factorises: Θ ~ U(0, 2π), R has the Rayleigh density r e^(−r²/2), and they are independent. Moreover P(R² > s) = P(R > √s) = e^(−s/2), so R² = Z₁² + Z₂² ~ Exp(½), which is the chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom. The standard normal in the plane is a uniformly random direction times an independent radius.

Box–Muller: normals from uniforms

  • Run the polar fact backwards. Θ is uniform, so Θ = 2πU₂ for a uniform U₂. R² ~ Exp(½), and an exponential is the inverse cdf of a uniform: R² = −2 ln U₁ (inverse transform sampling). So, for independent U₁, U₂ ~ U(0, 1),
    Z₁ = √(−2 ln U₁) · cos(2πU₂), Z₂ = √(−2 ln U₁) · sin(2πU₂) are independent N(0, 1)
    This is the Box–Muller transform. The figures on this site generate their normal draws exactly this way.
  • A direct check by the Jacobian. The inverse map is u₁ = e^(−(z₁² + z₂²)/2), u₂ = (1/2π)·atan2(z₂, z₁) (taken mod 1, so that it lands in (0, 1)). Its Jacobian determinant works out to −(1/2π)e^(−(z₁² + z₂²)/2), so the density of (Z₁, Z₂) is 1 · (1/2π)e^(−(z₁² + z₂²)/2) = φ(z₁)φ(z₂): two independent standard normals. A small cell of the unit square maps to a sector of an annulus with exactly the same probability, which the figure below lets you check cell by cell.
  • In practice. Box–Muller costs a logarithm, a square root and two trigonometric functions per pair. The polar (Marsaglia) variant avoids the trigonometry by drawing a uniform point in the unit disc and rescaling, and the ziggurat method avoids most of the special functions altogether. These samplers, and the general recipes behind them, are developed in Simulation & Stochastic Processes.

Delivered: B(a, b) = Γ(a)Γ(b)/Γ(a + b), and breaking a gamma into fraction × total

  • The beta section stated B(a, b) = Γ(a)Γ(b)/Γ(a + b) and that G₁/(G₁ + G₂) is beta when G₁, G₂ are independent gammas, and deferred both to this page. Both are one change of variables.
  • The identity. Write Γ(a)Γ(b) = ∫₀^∞ ∫₀^∞ x^(a−1) y^(b−1) e^(−(x + y)) dx dy. Change to the total s = x + y ∈ (0, ∞) and the fraction t = x/(x + y) ∈ (0, 1), which inverts to x = st, y = s(1 − t). The Jacobian is
    J = det [ ∂x/∂s ∂x/∂t ; ∂y/∂s ∂y/∂t ] = det [ t s ; 1 − t −s ] = −ts − s(1 − t) = −s, |J| = s
    and the integrand becomes (st)^(a−1)(s(1 − t))^(b−1) e^(−s) · s = s^(a+b−1) e^(−s) · t^(a−1)(1 − t)^(b−1). It separates:
    Γ(a)Γ(b) = ∫₀^∞ s^(a+b−1) e^(−s) ds · ∫₀¹ t^(a−1)(1 − t)^(b−1) dt = Γ(a + b) · B(a, b) ∎
  • The same change of variables, read as random variables. Let G₁ ~ Gamma(a, β) and G₂ ~ Gamma(b, β) be independent, and set S = G₁ + G₂, T = G₁/S. With the rate included, the joint density of (S, T) is
    f(s, t) = [β^(a+b) s^(a+b−1) e^(−βs)/Γ(a + b)] · [t^(a−1)(1 − t)^(b−1)/B(a, b)] s > 0, 0 < t < 1
    using the identity just proved to split the constant. The density factorises on a rectangle, so three facts at once: S ~ Gamma(a + b, β) (the convolution result of §06 again), T ~ Beta(a, b), and S and T are independent.
  • Fraction × total. Reading it backwards: take a Gamma(a + b, β) total S and an independent Beta(a, b) fraction T; then ST ~ Gamma(a, β) and S(1 − T) ~ Gamma(b, β), and these two pieces are independent. A gamma can be broken into a fraction times a total, with the fraction carrying no information about the total. In a Poisson process with rate β, the time S of the (a + b)-th arrival and the fraction T of it taken up by the first a inter-arrival gaps are independent: how long the wait was says nothing about how it was split.
  • Why the beta draws work. PV.betaDraw, which generates every beta sample on these pages as G₁/(G₁ + G₂), is justified by this result. A Monte Carlo check with a = 2, b = 3 and 200,000 draws gives E T = 0.399 (exact 0.4), E S = 5.003 (exact 5), and sample correlations between S and T, S and T², S² and T all below 0.003 in absolute value, consistent with independence. (Remarkably, the gamma is the only family for which the total and the fraction are independent.)
  • More than two pieces. With K independent gammas sharing a rate, the vector of fractions (G₁/S, …, G_K/S) is Dirichlet and independent of the total: §10.

08 Order statistics

Sort a sample X₁, …, Xₙ and call the results X₍₁₎ ≤ X₍₂₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ X₍ₙ₎: the minimum, the second smallest, and so on up to the maximum. These order statistics are what a sample median, a percentile, a range, a record, a series system's first failure or a parallel system's last are made of. Even when the Xᵢ are iid, the sorted values are dependent (each is at least the one before), and their joint distribution is one of the cleanest applications of the change-of-variables theorem. For uniform samples every order statistic is a beta variable, and because every continuous distribution is a transformed uniform, that one fact explains why sample quantiles estimate population quantiles.

The extremes: minimum and maximum

  • Maximum. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be iid with cdf F and density f. The maximum is at most x exactly when every observation is, so by independence P(X₍ₙ₎ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ, and differentiating, f₍ₙ₎(x) = n F(x)ⁿ⁻¹ f(x).
  • Minimum. The minimum exceeds x exactly when every observation does: P(X₍₁₎ > x) = (1 − F(x))ⁿ and f₍₁₎(x) = n(1 − F(x))ⁿ⁻¹ f(x).
  • Worked: exponential lifetimes. Five components with independent Exp lifetimes of mean 10 years. In series (the system fails at the first failure) the lifetime is the minimum, P(min > t) = e^(−5t/10): exponential with mean 2 years. Five components make the system five times less durable. In parallel (the system works until the last fails) the lifetime is the maximum, with mean 10(1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + ⅕) = 22.83 years. The harmonic sum comes from the gaps: after the j-th failure, n − j components remain, and by memorylessness the wait for the next is Exp with rate (n − j)/10. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent exponentials.
  • Uniforms. For U(0, 1) samples, f₍ₙ₎(x) = nxⁿ⁻¹ (the Beta(n, 1) density) and f₍₁₎(x) = n(1 − x)ⁿ⁻¹ (Beta(1, n)): the extremes of a uniform sample sit about 1/(n + 1) from the ends.
extremes of an iid sampleP(X₍ₙ₎ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ, f₍ₙ₎ = n·F^(n−1)·f P(X₍₁₎ > x) = (1 − F(x))ⁿ, f₍₁₎ = n·(1 − F)^(n−1)·f Exp, mean 10, n = 5: series (min) ~ Exp, mean 2 parallel (max): mean 10·(1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + ⅕) = 22.83 U(0, 1): max ~ Beta(n, 1), min ~ Beta(1, n)

The joint density of all n order statistics

  • Statement. For an iid sample from a density f, the vector (X₍₁₎, …, X₍ₙ₎) has joint density n! · f(y₁) f(y₂) ⋯ f(yₙ) on the region y₁ < y₂ < ⋯ < yₙ, and 0 elsewhere.
  • Proof by the change-of-variables theorem. Ties have probability zero, so with probability 1 the sample is in one of n! regions, one for each ordering of the coordinates. On each region, sorting is a permutation of the coordinates: one-to-one, linear, with Jacobian determinant ±1. So each region contributes f(y₁)⋯f(yₙ) · 1 to the density at a sorted point (y₁, …, yₙ), and the n! regions contribute n! equal terms: the "not one-to-one, sum over the pieces" rule of §07.
  • A check. For n = 2 uniforms the joint density of (min, max) is 2 on the triangle 0 < y₁ < y₂ < 1, whose area is ½. Integrating out y₂ gives 2(1 − y₁), the Beta(1, 2) density of the minimum, as above.
  • Two order statistics. Integrating out the others gives, for j < k and x < y:
    f₍ⱼ,ₖ₎(x, y) = n!/((j − 1)!(k − j − 1)!(n − k)!) · F(x)^(j−1) · [F(y) − F(x)]^(k−j−1) · [1 − F(y)]^(n−k) · f(x) f(y)
    Read it as a multinomial: j − 1 observations below x, one at x, k − j − 1 between, one at y, n − k above.
joint density of the order statisticsf(y₁, …, yₙ) = n!·f(y₁)·f(y₂)⋯f(yₙ) on y₁ < y₂ < ⋯ < yₙ (n! orderings, each a permutation with |J| = 1)

The k-th order statistic

  • Its density. Integrating out everything except the k-th coordinate gives
    f₍ₖ₎(x) = n!/((k − 1)!(n − k)!) · F(x)^(k−1) · [1 − F(x)]^(n−k) · f(x)
    The multinomial reading makes it memorable: for the k-th smallest to be near x, one observation must be in [x, x + dx] (probability f(x)dx), k − 1 below (each with probability F(x)) and n − k above (each 1 − F(x)), and the coefficient counts the ways to assign the n labelled observations to those three roles. The beta section gave this as a heuristic; the joint density makes it a proof.
  • Its cdf, by counting. X₍ₖ₎ ≤ x exactly when at least k of the n observations are ≤ x, and the number that are is Bin(n, F(x)). So P(X₍ₖ₎ ≤ x) = Σ_(j=k)^n C(n, j) F(x)ʲ (1 − F(x))ⁿ⁻ʲ. Differentiating this sum, consecutive terms telescope and only the density above survives: a second proof.

Delivered: the k-th of n uniforms is Beta(k, n − k + 1)

  • The result. With F(x) = x and f(x) = 1 on (0, 1), the density of U₍ₖ₎ is n!/((k − 1)!(n − k)!) · x^(k−1)(1 − x)^(n−k). The constant is Γ(n + 1)/(Γ(k)Γ(n − k + 1)) = 1/B(k, n − k + 1) by §07, so U₍ₖ₎ ~ Beta(k, n − k + 1), with mean k/(n + 1) and variance k(n − k + 1)/((n + 1)²(n + 2)). The n sorted uniforms split (0, 1) into n + 1 gaps of equal expected length 1/(n + 1).
  • A numeric check. The median of 5 uniforms is U₍₃₎ ~ Beta(3, 3): mean ½, variance 9/(36 × 7) = 1/28 = 0.0357. The counting formula gives P(U₍₃₎ ≤ 0.3) = P(Bin(5, 0.3) ≥ 3) = 0.1323 + 0.0284 + 0.0024 = 0.1631, and the beta cdf I₀.₃(3, 3) gives the same 0.16308. A simulation of 200,000 sorted samples of 5 gives mean 0.5001 and variance 0.0357.
  • An identity for free. Equating the two cdfs: Σ_(j=k)^n C(n, j) xʲ(1 − x)ⁿ⁻ʲ = I_x(k, n − k + 1), a binomial tail is a regularised incomplete beta function. That is how binomial tail probabilities are computed in practice, including by the functions behind these figures.
  • Any continuous distribution. If F is continuous and increasing, Xᵢ = F⁻¹(Uᵢ) with Uᵢ uniform (the probability integral transform), and F⁻¹ preserves order, so X₍ₖ₎ = F⁻¹(U₍ₖ₎) and F(X₍ₖ₎) ~ Beta(k, n − k + 1) whatever F is. The fraction of the population below the k-th smallest observation has a known, distribution-free law.
  • Range and spacings. From the two-order-statistic density, the uniform sample range W = U₍ₙ₎ − U₍₁₎ has density n(n − 1)w^(n−2)(1 − w), the Beta(n − 1, 2) law, with mean (n − 1)/(n + 1): for n = 5, mean ⅔ and P(W > 0.9) = 0.081. Each of the n + 1 spacings (including the two end gaps) is Beta(1, n), and the spacings are exchangeable.

Why sample quantiles work

  • The sample quantile concentrates. Take k ≈ np. Then F(X₍ₖ₎) ~ Beta(k, n − k + 1) has mean about p and variance about p(1 − p)/n, which shrinks to 0. So the population fraction below the sample p-quantile converges to p, and when F is continuous and increasing at its p-quantile q_p, X₍ₖ₎ → q_p. That is the precise sense in which sample percentiles and quartiles estimate their population counterparts.
  • How precisely. A first-order expansion of F⁻¹ around p gives Var X₍ₖ₎ ≈ p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²). For the median of a normal sample (p = ½, f(q_p) = 1/(σ√(2π))) this is πσ²/(2n), against σ²/n for the mean: the median uses the data with efficiency 2/π = 0.637 under normality, and wins under heavy tails, as tabulated in efficiency of estimators. Where the density at the quantile is low, the quantile is hard to pin down: extreme percentiles need far more data than the median. The limit theorem behind the approximation is in Limit Theorems & Convergence.
  • Confidence intervals with no model. The population median m lies between X₍ⱼ₎ and X₍ₖ₎ exactly when between j and k − 1 observations fall below it, and the number below m is Bin(n, ½) for any continuous distribution. So P(X₍ⱼ₎ ≤ m < X₍ₖ₎) = Σ_(i=j)^(k−1) C(n, i)/2ⁿ. For n = 10, the interval from the 2nd to the 9th smallest observation covers the median with probability 1 − 2(1 + 10)/1024 = 0.979, whatever the distribution.
  • Conformal prediction runs on the same fact. If n calibration scores and a new score are exchangeable, the new score's rank among all n + 1 is uniform on {1, …, n + 1}. Taking the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest calibration score as a threshold therefore covers the new case with probability at least 1 − α (provided ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉ ≤ n and there are no ties, or ties are broken at random), for any model and any data distribution. Every distribution-free prediction interval is an order-statistic argument of this kind.
