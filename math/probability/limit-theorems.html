Limit Theorems & Convergence

Probability earns its keep in the limit. An average of many measurements settles on the true mean; a sum of many small disturbances looks normal whatever the disturbances are; a sample median, a log-odds, a correlation all come with a bell-shaped error bar once the sample is large. Each of these is a theorem about a sequence of random variables, and each is a statement in one particular sense of "converges". This page defines the four modes of convergence (in distribution, in probability, almost surely, in mean square), proves how they relate and builds the counterexamples that keep them apart. It proves the weak law of large numbers, states and partly proves the strong law with the Borel–Cantelli lemmas, and uses them to make the gambler's fallacy rigorous. It proves the central limit theorem by cumulants, measures its speed with the Berry–Esseen bound, and shows what replaces it when the variance is infinite. It then builds the toolkit that carries those two theorems to almost every statistic: the delta method, variance-stabilising transforms, Slutsky's theorem, the asymptotic normality of sample quantiles, and the very different limit of the maximum. It ends with the two theorems every simulation silently invokes and the places all of this does its work in machine learning.

01 What it means for random variables to converge

A sequence of numbers x₁, x₂, … converges to x when, for every tolerance ε > 0, the terms eventually stay within ε of x. A sequence of random variables X₁, X₂, … is a sequence of functions on a sample space, and "getting close to a limit" can mean several genuinely different things: the distributions get close; the variables are close with high probability; the realised sequence of numbers converges for almost every outcome; or the mean squared distance goes to zero. These are the four modes of convergence. They are not equivalent, every limit theorem in probability is a statement in one specific mode, and most confusions about the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem are confusions about which mode is being claimed.

Why "converges" needs a qualifier

  • A random variable is a function. Xₙ assigns a number Xₙ(ω) to each outcome ω of the sample space Ω (Random Variables & Discrete Distributions). A sequence of random variables is therefore a sequence of functions, and analysis already knows several ways for functions to converge: pointwise, pointwise except on a negligible set, in an average sense. Probability adds one more that has no analogue for functions: convergence of the distributions alone, with no reference to the functions at all.
  • Same distribution, no convergence. Let X₁, X₂, … be independent N(0, 1). Every term has exactly the same distribution, so in the weakest sense the sequence has "converged" from the start. But consecutive terms never get close to each other: Xₙ₊₁ − Xₙ ~ N(0, 2), so P(|Xₙ₊₁ − Xₙ| > 1) = 2(1 − Φ(1/√2)) = 0.4795 for every n. The realised sequence of numbers jumps about forever. Two continuous random variables are equal with probability 0, so no single random variable X can be "the limit" of these functions in any sense that compares values.
  • Concentrating, but never equal. Let Xₙ ~ N(0, 1/n). Intuitively Xₙ piles up at 0, yet P(Xₙ = 0) = 0 for every n, and the cdf at the limit point never moves: Fₙ(0) = ½ for every n, while the cdf of the constant 0 jumps from 0 to 1 at 0. Any sensible definition must call this convergence to 0, so it must tolerate failure at the jump.
  • The frequency of heads. The proportion p̂ₙ of heads in n tosses of a fair coin is a random variable for each n. "It tends to ½" might mean: its distribution piles up near ½; a miss by more than 0.01 becomes improbable; the particular infinite sequence of tosses you are living through has proportions that settle down; or its mean squared error 1/(4n) goes to zero. All four turn out to be true, but they are four different theorems with four different proofs, and the gap between the second and the third is exactly the gap between the weak and strong laws (§03, §04).

The four modes, defined

  • In distribution (also "weakly", "in law"): Xₙ ⇝ X if Fₙ(t) → F(t) at every t where the limit cdf F is continuous. Reading: histograms of Xₙ come to look like the histogram of X. Only the distributions are compared; Xₙ and X need not even be defined on the same sample space.
  • In probability: Xₙ →ᵖ X if, for every ε > 0, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. Reading: at a single large n, a miss bigger than any fixed tolerance is improbable.
  • Almost surely (with probability 1): Xₙ →ᵃˢ X if P({ω : Xₙ(ω) → X(ω)}) = 1. Reading: for almost every outcome, the realised sequence of numbers converges in the calculus sense.
  • In quadratic mean (in L²): Xₙ →ᴸ² X if E(Xₙ − X)² → 0; more generally in Lʳ if E|Xₙ − X|ʳ → 0. Reading: the average squared miss vanishes, which charges big misses heavily even when they are rare.
  • Constants. When the limit is a constant c (a random variable with P(X = c) = 1) the same definitions apply and we write Xₙ →ᵖ c, Xₙ ⇝ c, and so on. The laws of large numbers are statements of this kind.

Reading the definitions

  • Why only continuity points. Let Xₙ = 1/n, a constant. Surely Xₙ converges to 0. But Fₙ(0) = P(1/n ≤ 0) = 0 for every n, while the limit cdf has F(0) = 1. The cdfs converge everywhere except at the jump of F, and a definition that demanded convergence there would reject the most obvious example there is. The same thing happened with N(0, 1/n) above, where Fₙ(0) = ½ forever.
  • Three equivalent forms of convergence in distribution. Xₙ ⇝ X if and only if E g(Xₙ) → E g(X) for every bounded continuous function g; and (Lévy's continuity theorem) if and only if the characteristic functions φₙ(t) = E e^(itXₙ) converge to φ(t) for every t. When the moment generating functions exist on an interval around 0, their convergence there is also enough. That last form is the engine of the proof of the central limit theorem in §05, using the moment generating function.
  • What each mode looks at. Convergence in distribution looks only at the marginal distribution of each Xₙ. Convergence in probability and in L² look at the joint distribution of each pair (Xₙ, X), because |Xₙ − X| must be computed. Almost-sure convergence looks at the joint distribution of the whole sequence, because whether Xₙ(ω) converges depends on all the terms at once. The stronger the mode, the more of the joint structure it needs.
  • Same marginals, different verdicts. Let U ~ U(0, 1) and set Xₙ = 1 if U < 1/n, else 0: nested blips. Separately, let Y₁, Y₂, … be independent with P(Yₙ = 1) = 1/n. Each Xₙ has exactly the same distribution as Yₙ, Bernoulli(1/n). Yet Xₙ(ω) → 0 for every ω with U(ω) > 0 (once 1/n < U, every later term is 0), while Yₙ = 1 infinitely often with probability 1 (§04 proves it), so Yₙ does not converge almost surely. Both converge to 0 in probability, in L² and in distribution. Almost-sure convergence is a property of how the terms are coupled, not of their distributions.
  • The almost-sure tail form. The last equivalence in the box says: Xₙ →ᵃˢ X exactly when P(sup_(m≥n) |Xₘ − X| > ε) → 0. Compare it with convergence in probability, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. Both are probabilities that go to 0, but the almost-sure one asks about every later term at once, the in-probability one about a single term. That one word, "every", is the difference between the strong and the weak law of large numbers.

Worked: the shrinking normal in all four modes

  • In distribution. Xₙ ~ N(0, 1/n) has Fₙ(t) = Φ(t√n), which tends to 0 for t < 0 and to 1 for t > 0: the cdf of the constant 0 at every continuity point. So Xₙ ⇝ 0.
  • In probability. P(|Xₙ| > ε) = 2(1 − Φ(ε√n)). With ε = 0.1: 0.3173 at n = 100, 0.0455 at n = 400, tending to 0. So Xₙ →ᵖ 0.
  • In quadratic mean. E Xₙ² = Var Xₙ = 1/n → 0, so Xₙ →ᴸ² 0, and Chebyshev's bound P(|Xₙ| > ε) ≤ 1/(nε²) follows from it.
  • Almost surely. The question cannot be answered from the distributions. If Xₙ = Z/√n for a single Z ~ N(0, 1), every path is a deterministic sequence z/√n → 0. If the Xₙ are independent, it is still true, because Σ P(|Xₙ| > ε) is finite (normal tails are tiny), and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma of §04 turns a finite sum into "only finitely many misses".

A limit theorem is incomplete until its mode is named. "The sample mean converges to the population mean" is two theorems: the weak law (in probability) and the strong law (almost surely). "Sums are approximately normal" is a statement in distribution, and nothing more: it says how probabilities behave, not that any particular sum is close to any particular normal variable. Every statement on this page carries its mode, and §02 shows exactly what each one does and does not imply.

02 How the modes relate

The four modes form a hierarchy. Almost-sure convergence and convergence in quadratic mean each imply convergence in probability, which implies convergence in distribution; and convergence in distribution to a constant climbs back up to convergence in probability. Every other implication fails, and each failure has a concrete counterexample built on a single uniform random number. Knowing the counterexamples is the fastest way to know what a theorem in a given mode does not promise.

The four implications, proved

  • Quadratic mean ⟹ probability. Markov's inequality applied to (Xₙ − X)² (Markov, Chebyshev, Cantelli): P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) = P((Xₙ − X)² > ε²) ≤ E(Xₙ − X)²/ε² → 0. The same argument with |Xₙ − X|ʳ shows that Lʳ convergence for any r > 0 implies convergence in probability. And L² implies L¹, because (E|Y|)² ≤ E Y² (Jensen, or Cauchy–Schwarz).
  • Almost surely ⟹ probability. The event {|Xₙ − X| > ε} is contained in {|Xₘ − X| > ε for some m ≥ n}, whose probability tends to 0 when Xₙ →ᵃˢ X (§01's tail form). A single term is controlled by the whole tail.
  • Probability ⟹ distribution. Fix ε > 0 and a continuity point x of F. If Xₙ ≤ x then either X ≤ x + ε or |Xₙ − X| > ε, so Fₙ(x) ≤ F(x + ε) + P(|Xₙ − X| > ε). If X ≤ x − ε then either Xₙ ≤ x or |Xₙ − X| > ε, so F(x − ε) ≤ Fₙ(x) + P(|Xₙ − X| > ε). Let n → ∞: F(x − ε) ≤ liminf Fₙ(x) ≤ limsup Fₙ(x) ≤ F(x + ε). Now let ε → 0; continuity of F at x squeezes both sides to F(x). ∎
  • Distribution to a constant ⟹ probability. If Xₙ ⇝ c, then c − ε and c + ε are continuity points of the limit cdf, and P(|Xₙ − c| > ε) ≤ Fₙ(c − ε) + 1 − Fₙ(c + ε) → 0 + 1 − 1 = 0. ∎ This upgrade is used constantly: a statistic whose distribution collapses onto a point converges to that point in probability, which is the form Slutsky's theorem needs (§08).

The counterexamples, worked honestly

  • In probability but not almost surely: the typewriter. Let U ~ U(0, 1). Write each n ≥ 1 uniquely as n = 2ᵏ + j with 0 ≤ j < 2ᵏ, and let Xₙ = 1 if U lies in the interval [j/2ᵏ, (j + 1)/2ᵏ), else 0. In block k the interval of length 2⁻ᵏ slides across (0, 1) in 2ᵏ steps, like a typewriter carriage, then halves and starts again. So P(Xₙ ≠ 0) = 2⁻ᵏ → 0: Xₙ →ᵖ 0, and E Xₙ² = 2⁻ᵏ → 0: Xₙ →ᴸ² 0 too. But every point u is covered exactly once in every block, so for every outcome Xₙ(ω) = 1 infinitely often and Xₙ(ω) = 0 infinitely often. The sequence of numbers converges for no outcome at all. One example separates both "probability ⇏ almost surely" and "L² ⇏ almost surely".
  • Almost surely but not in mean: the growing spike. Let Xₙ = n if U < 1/n, else 0. For any outcome with U(ω) > 0, once n > 1/U(ω) every term is 0, so Xₙ →ᵃˢ 0, and hence Xₙ →ᵖ 0, with P(Xₙ ≠ 0) = 1/n. But E Xₙ = n · (1/n) = 1 for every n and E Xₙ² = n² · (1/n) = n → ∞. The spike gets rarer and taller at matching rates, so it never stops carrying a unit of mean. Convergence almost surely, and in probability, says nothing about moments: the limit is 0 but E Xₙ = 1 does not converge to E 0 = 0. With height √n instead (E Xₙ² = 1 for every n) it fails in L² while converging in L¹.
  • In distribution but not in probability: the flip. Let X ~ N(0, 1) and Xₙ = −X for every n. Since the normal is symmetric, −X has the same distribution as X, so Fₙ = F exactly and Xₙ ⇝ X trivially. But |Xₙ − X| = 2|X|, and P(2|X| > ε) is the same positive number for every n: with ε = ½ it is P(|X| > ¼) = 0.8026. Convergence in distribution makes no claim that Xₙ is near X; it only says the two have similar histograms.
  • Independent blips: probability without almost-sure. The independent Yₙ ~ Bernoulli(1/n) of §01 converge to 0 in probability and in L² (E Yₙ² = 1/n), but since Σ 1/n = ∞, the second Borel–Cantelli lemma (§04) says Yₙ = 1 infinitely often with probability 1: no almost-sure convergence. With P(Yₙ = 1) = 1/n² instead, Σ 1/n² = π²/6 < ∞ and the first lemma gives almost-sure convergence. The borderline between the two is whether the probabilities of the misses are summable.

Partial converses: what can be recovered

  • A subsequence converges almost surely. If Xₙ →ᵖ X, some subsequence Xₙ₍ₖ₎ converges almost surely: pick n(k) with P(|Xₙ₍ₖ₎ − X| > 1/k) < 2⁻ᵏ; the probabilities are summable, and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma does the rest. For the typewriter, the subsequence n = 2ᵏ (j = 0) is 1{U < 2⁻ᵏ}, which converges to 0 for every U > 0.
  • Moments, with domination. If Xₙ →ᵖ X and |Xₙ| ≤ Y for some Y with E Y < ∞ (dominated convergence), or more generally if the Xₙ are uniformly integrable (sup_n E[|Xₙ| 1{|Xₙ| > M}] → 0 as M → ∞), then E|Xₙ − X| → 0 and E Xₙ → E X. The growing spike is the standard example of what uniform integrability rules out: a vanishing probability carrying a non-vanishing amount of mean. Bounded variables are always uniformly integrable, which is why indicator averages never cause trouble.
  • Distribution plus a constant limit. The fourth implication above: in distribution and in probability coincide when the limit is a constant. All laws of large numbers have constant limits, so for them the weakest mode is already as strong as the second.
  • Continuous functions preserve every mode. If g is continuous and Xₙ → X almost surely, in probability, or in distribution, then g(Xₙ) → g(X) in the same mode: the continuous mapping theorem, used repeatedly from §07 on. (For L² it is false: g(x) = x² can destroy square-integrability.)

Which mode do the statistics need? Almost all of applied statistics runs on convergence in probability (an estimator is consistent if θ̂ₙ →ᵖ θ) and convergence in distribution (an estimator is asymptotically normal if √n(θ̂ₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, v)). Almost-sure convergence is what makes long-run frequencies meaningful along the single history we observe; L² convergence is what mean-squared-error calculations measure (bias, variance and MSE). The theorems below are stated in the strongest mode that is true and cheap to prove.

03 The weak law of large numbers

The weak law of large numbers says that the average of n independent draws from a distribution with a mean μ converges to μ in probability: for any tolerance you name, the chance that X̄ₙ misses μ by more than that tolerance goes to zero. With a finite variance the proof is two lines of Chebyshev and comes with an explicit error probability; with only a finite mean it needs a truncation argument and the explicit bound is lost. It is a statement about the distribution of X̄ₙ at each fixed n, and it says nothing at all about any single sequence of averages.

Statement and proof, finite variance

  • Setup. X₁, X₂, … are independent with common mean μ and common variance σ² < ∞, and X̄ₙ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ)/n. By linearity E X̄ₙ = μ, and by the variance of a sum of uncorrelated terms (variance, covariance), Var X̄ₙ = (1/n²)·nσ² = σ²/n.
  • Proof. Chebyshev's inequality (Markov, Chebyshev, Cantelli) applied to X̄ₙ:
    P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ Var X̄ₙ / ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0 as n → ∞, for every fixed ε > 0. ∎
    In fact the proof shows more: E(X̄ₙ − μ)² = σ²/n → 0, so X̄ₙ →ᴸ² μ, and the weak law in probability is the first implication of §02 applied to it.
  • What the proof actually used. Only that the terms are uncorrelated with a common mean and bounded variances: then Var X̄ₙ ≤ (max σᵢ²)/n. Independence and identical distribution are not needed. That is why the law covers sampling plans with unequal but bounded variances, and why it fails when the terms are correlated (below).
  • The quantifiers. ε is fixed first, then n → ∞. The statement is not that X̄ₙ is within σ/n of μ, or within any shrinking tolerance chosen in advance; it is one tolerance at a time. The rate at which a fixed tolerance becomes safe is what the bound σ²/(nε²) measures. The CLT (§05) suggests the approximation 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)), which is accurate while ε√n/σ is moderate; for a fixed ε and very large n the miss probability is a large deviation, whose true exponential decay rate is set by the whole distribution, not by the normal tail (§05).

Worked: how many tosses?

  • How large must n be so that a fair coin's proportion of heads lies in [0.4, 0.6] with probability at least 0.7? Here σ² = ¼ and ε = 0.1, so Chebyshev gives P(|p̂ₙ − ½| ≤ 0.1) ≥ 1 − 1/(4n · 0.01) = 1 − 25/n, which is at least 0.7 once n ≥ 84.
  • The truth is much cheaper. The exact binomial probability is already 0.737 at n = 20, and it is at least 0.7 for every n ≥ 29; in between it dips below 0.7 at n = 21, 23, 24, 26, 28, because whether the endpoints 0.4n and 0.6n are whole numbers changes how many lattice points the interval catches. The normal approximation, which needs the CLT, asks for 0.1√n/0.5 ≥ Φ⁻¹(0.85) = 1.036, that is n ≥ 27.
  • The moral. The weak law's bound is valid for every distribution with that variance, so it must cover the worst one, and it is loose by a factor of about three here. It is a theorem about the limit, with a free but crude rate attached; Hoeffding's inequality improves the rate to exponential for bounded variables, and the CLT gives the accurate approximation.

Only a finite mean: Khinchin's weak law

  • Delivered: the version that needs only a mean. Chebyshev's weak law promised "versions that need only E|X| < ∞". If X₁, X₂, … are iid with E|X| < ∞, then X̄ₙ →ᵖ μ = E X. No variance is needed. A Pareto variable with tail index α = 1.5 has a mean but infinite variance, and its averages still converge in probability.
  • What changes in the proof: truncation. Chebyshev needs a variance, so manufacture one. Fix δ > 0 and cut each term off at level δn: Yᵢ = Xᵢ · 1{|Xᵢ| ≤ δn}. Three facts finish the argument. (i) The truncation rarely bites: P(X̄ₙ ≠ Ȳₙ) ≤ n · P(|X| > δn) ≤ (1/δ)·E[|X| 1{|X| > δn}] → 0, because a finite mean forces its tail contributions to vanish. (ii) The truncated mean is nearly right: E Yᵢ = E[X 1{|X| ≤ δn}] → μ. (iii) The truncated variance is small: Var Ȳₙ ≤ E[X² 1{|X| ≤ δn}]/n ≤ δn·E|X|/n = δ E|X|. Chebyshev on Ȳₙ then gives limsup P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ δE|X|/ε², and δ was arbitrary.
  • What is lost. The explicit rate. With finite variance, σ²/(nε²) is a number you can compute; with only a finite mean, how fast P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) falls depends on how fast E[|X| 1{|X| > t}] falls, which depends on the tail. For a Pareto with 1 < α < 2, typical errors shrink only like n^(1/α − 1), slower than 1/√n (§05).
  • Even without a mean, sometimes. The weak law in its sharpest form (Feller's) says that X̄ₙ − E[X 1{|X| ≤ n}] →ᵖ 0 if and only if x · P(|X| > x) → 0. That can hold with E|X| = ∞, as long as the centring is allowed to drift. The St. Petersburg game, which pays 2ᴷ with probability 2⁻ᴷ and has infinite expectation, does not satisfy Feller's condition: x·P(X > x) oscillates between 1 and 2 instead of tending to 0. A modified weak law, with truncation at a level that grows with n and the normalisation n log₂ n, holds instead: the total prize after n games satisfies Sₙ/(n log₂ n) →ᵖ 1. A "fair" entry fee per game exists, but it grows like log₂ n with the number of games played.

What the weak law does not say

  • Nothing about any single path. The weak law is a sequence of statements, one for each n, each about the distribution of X̄ₙ at that n. It is consistent with a path that leaves the ε-band infinitely often, provided those excursions happen at n's where they are individually rare: the typewriter of §02 converges in probability with every path misbehaving forever. Ruling that out for averages is the strong law (§04), and it needs a different proof.
  • Nothing about sums. X̄ₙ − μ → 0 but Sₙ − nμ = n(X̄ₙ − μ) has standard deviation σ√n, which grows. The average is right because the denominator outgrows the error, not because the error shrinks (§04 makes this precise for the coin).
  • Nothing under strong dependence. If Xᵢ = W + eᵢ, with W one shared random effect and the eᵢ independent noise of variance σ², then X̄ₙ = W + ēₙ → W: the average converges, but to a random limit, not to μ = E W. The variance of the average is Var W + σ²/n, the floor ρ·Var X of averaging correlated variables. Data from one hospital, one user or one season converge to that hospital's, that user's or that season's mean.
  • Nothing when the mean does not exist. The Cauchy average never converges at all (§04), and the averages of a Pareto with α ≤ 1 drift upward forever (heavy tails).
The weak law, in one sentence. At any fixed large n, a big miss is unlikely. It is the mode that gives an error probability at a finite n, and it is what "consistent estimator" means. The applied reading, with the misreading that it compensates for runs of luck, is in the law of large numbers; the next section shows why runs of luck are diluted rather than compensated, and what "almost surely" adds.

04 The strong law of large numbers

The strong law says that, with probability 1, the realised sequence of averages X̄₁, X̄₂, X̄₃, … converges to μ: the particular infinite history you live through settles down and stays settled. It holds for iid variables exactly when E|X| < ∞. Its tools are the two Borel–Cantelli lemmas, which decide whether infinitely many of a sequence of events happen. With them this section proves the strong law under a fourth-moment assumption, proves that a finite mean is necessary, resolves the gambler's fallacy precisely, and shows why the Cauchy average never converges.

Statement, and what "almost surely" buys

  • The strong law (Kolmogorov). If X₁, X₂, … are iid, then X̄ₙ →ᵃˢ μ for some finite μ if and only if E|X| < ∞, and then μ = E X. The hypotheses for iid data are the same as for Khinchin's weak law; the conclusion is stronger.
  • What it adds. By §01's tail form, almost-sure convergence means P(|X̄ₘ − μ| > ε for some m ≥ n) → 0: after some random time N(ω), the path stays inside the ε-band forever. The weak law only controls each n separately, and is consistent with infinitely many excursions. For iid averages both are true, but they are different statements, and in situations beyond iid there are sequences satisfying the weak law and not the strong one.
  • Frequencies become probabilities. Apply the strong law to the indicators 1{Xᵢ ∈ A}, which have mean P(A): the long-run relative frequency of any event converges to its probability along almost every infinite history. The frequency reading of probability that Probability Foundations starts from is therefore not a definition but a theorem. Applied to the events {Xᵢ ≤ x} simultaneously for all x, the same idea gives the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem: sup_x |F̂ₙ(x) − F(x)| →ᵃˢ 0, the empirical cdf converges uniformly (the empirical cdf, the plug-in principle).

The Borel–Cantelli lemmas

  • "Infinitely often". For events A₁, A₂, …, the event "Aₙ happens for infinitely many n" is {Aₙ i.o.} = ∩ₙ ∪_(m≥n) Aₘ: for every n, some later Aₘ happens. A sequence converges almost surely to X exactly when, for every ε, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε i.o.) = 0.
  • First lemma. If Σ P(Aₙ) < ∞, then P(Aₙ i.o.) = 0. Proof: for every n, P(Aₙ i.o.) ≤ P(∪_(m≥n) Aₘ) ≤ Σ_(m≥n) P(Aₘ), the tail of a convergent series, which tends to 0. ∎ No independence is needed.
  • Second lemma. If the Aₙ are independent and Σ P(Aₙ) = ∞, then P(Aₙ i.o.) = 1. Proof: the probability that none of Aₙ, Aₙ₊₁, … happens is Π_(m≥n) (1 − P(Aₘ)) ≤ exp(−Σ_(m≥n) P(Aₘ)) = 0, using 1 − x ≤ e^(−x); so from every n on, some Aₘ happens with probability 1. ∎
  • The blips of §02. Independent Yₙ ~ Bernoulli(1/n): Σ 1/n = ∞, so Yₙ = 1 infinitely often, almost surely; the expected number of 1s among the first million terms is H₁₀₀₀₀₀₀ ≈ ln 10⁶ + 0.577 = 14.4, growing without bound. With P(Yₙ = 1) = 1/n², Σ = π²/6 = 1.645, and with probability 1 only finitely many terms are 1.

A proof of the strong law, with four moments

  • The honest scope. The full strong law needs only E|X| < ∞, and its proof (by truncation plus a careful maximal argument, or Etemadi's argument along geometric subsequences) is longer than this page should carry. With the extra assumption E X⁴ < ∞ there is a complete proof in four lines, and it shows exactly where the "almost surely" comes from: a summable bound on the miss probabilities.
  • The fourth moment of a sum. Let Yᵢ = Xᵢ − μ, so the Yᵢ are iid with mean 0, variance σ² and m₄ = E Y⁴ < ∞. Expand (Y₁ + ⋯ + Yₙ)⁴ and take expectations. Every term in which some index appears exactly once has expectation 0 (independence and E Y = 0). What survives: the n terms Yᵢ⁴, each with expectation m₄, and the terms Yᵢ²Yⱼ² with i ≠ j, each with expectation σ⁴. There are n(n − 1)/2 unordered pairs {i, j} and C(4, 2) = 6 ways to choose which two of the four factors carry the index i, so 3n(n − 1) such terms. So E(Σ Yᵢ)⁴ = n m₄ + 3n(n − 1)σ⁴ ≤ K n² for a constant K.
  • A summable bound. Markov's inequality on the fourth power: P(|Ȳₙ| > ε) = P((ΣYᵢ)⁴ > n⁴ε⁴) ≤ Kn²/(n⁴ε⁴) = K/(n²ε⁴). Compare Chebyshev's σ²/(nε²), which is not summable: the extra moments buy a faster tail, and a faster tail is what almost-sure convergence needs.
  • Borel–Cantelli finishes it. Σₙ K/(n²ε⁴) < ∞, so by the first lemma |Ȳₙ| > ε happens only finitely often, with probability 1. Take ε = 1, ½, ⅓, …; a countable intersection of probability-1 events has probability 1, so Ȳₙ → 0 almost surely, that is X̄ₙ →ᵃˢ μ. ∎

Why a finite mean is necessary

  • Suppose E|X| = ∞. Since E|X| ≤ Σ_(n≥0) P(|X| > n), the sum Σ P(|Xₙ| > n) diverges, and the events {|Xₙ| > n} are independent, so by the second lemma |Xₙ| > n infinitely often, almost surely. But if X̄ₙ converged to a finite c, then Xₙ/n = X̄ₙ − ((n − 1)/n)X̄ₙ₋₁ → c − c = 0, which would force |Xₙ| < n eventually. Contradiction: without a finite mean, no finite almost-sure limit exists. ∎ Single terms as large as the running total keep arriving, and each one moves the average by a fixed amount.

Delivered: the gambler's fallacy, made rigorous

  • The gambler's fallacy in Probability Foundations promised the precise statement of the law of large numbers and the difference between its weak and strong versions. Toss a fair coin; let Hₙ be the number of heads in n tosses, p̂ₙ = Hₙ/n the proportion, and Dₙ = Hₙ − Tₙ = 2Hₙ − n the gap between heads and tails. Then Dₙ = ξ₁ + ⋯ + ξₙ with independent ξᵢ = ±1, each with mean 0 and variance 1, and p̂ₙ − ½ = Dₙ/(2n).
  • The proportion converges, almost surely. The strong law applied to the indicators of heads: p̂ₙ →ᵃˢ ½. Along almost every infinite sequence of tosses, the proportion of heads tends to ½ and, after some point, stays within any tolerance you choose.
  • The gap wanders, on the √n scale. Var Dₙ = n · Var ξ = n, so SD(Dₙ) = √n, which grows. The typical size of the gap is E|Dₙ| ≈ √(2n/π): exactly 7.96 at n = 100 and 79.79 at n = 10,000. By the CLT, Dₙ/√n ⇝ N(0, 1), so P(|Dₙ| > √n) → 2(1 − Φ(1)) = 0.317: a gap of at least one standard deviation is not becoming rarer. The proportion's error Dₙ/(2n) has standard deviation 1/(2√n), which shrinks: the same fluctuation, divided by a denominator that grows faster.
  • Nothing pays the surplus back. Given the first n tosses, the future gap has conditional expectation E[Dₙ₊ₘ | D₁, …, Dₙ] = Dₙ + E[ξₙ₊₁ + ⋯ + ξₙ₊ₘ] = Dₙ for every m: a surplus of 10 heads is expected to remain a surplus of 10 heads, forever. (A sequence with this property is a martingale.) The proportion still converges, because (n + Dₙ + future)/(2(n + m)) dilutes a fixed surplus of 10 by an ever larger n + m. Dilution, not compensation.
  • Where the gap goes. The gap of a fair coin returns to 0 infinitely often, almost surely (the simple symmetric random walk is recurrent), yet it also exceeds every level infinitely often. Its precise envelope is the law of the iterated logarithm: limsup Dₙ/√(2n ln ln n) = 1 almost surely, and the same for liminf with −1. The envelope √(2n ln ln n) is 17.5 at n = 100, 211 at n = 10,000 and 2,292 at n = 10⁶, against √n = 10, 100 and 1,000. Dividing by 2n gives the exact almost-sure speed of the strong law for the coin: |p̂ₙ − ½| is eventually below (1 + δ)√(ln ln n/(2n)) and exceeds (1 − δ)√(ln ln n/(2n)) infinitely often.
  • Weak versus strong, for the gambler. The weak law says: at toss 10,000, the proportion is very probably within 0.02 of ½. The strong law says: your one infinite sequence of tosses will eventually stay within 0.02 of ½, and within 0.001, and within any tolerance. Neither says that the count of heads catches up with the count of tails, and the √n result says it typically drifts further apart.

When there is no mean: the average that never settles

  • The Cauchy average is Cauchy, forever. The standard Cauchy density 1/(π(1 + x²)) has no mean (the Cauchy distribution has no mean), so neither law applies, and the failure is total. Its moment generating function does not exist, but its characteristic function φ(t) = E e^(itX), which exists for every distribution, is e^(−|t|). For iid Cauchy terms the characteristic function of the average is φ(t/n)ⁿ = (e^(−|t|/n))ⁿ = e^(−|t|): X̄ₙ is exactly standard Cauchy for every n. Averaging a million Cauchy draws gives a number distributed exactly like one draw, so P(|X̄ₙ| > 3) = 1 − (2/π)·arctan 3 = 0.2048 at every n. The applied page shows this with a simulation (where the CLT fails outright).
  • Almost surely, it does not converge. E|X| = ∞, so the necessity argument above applies: terms with |Xₙ| > n keep arriving, and each moves the running average by about 1 or more. The paths in the figure's Cauchy mode jump at those moments and never settle.
  • Infinite mean. For non-negative variables with E X = +∞ the strong law still holds with limit +∞: X̄ₙ →ᵃˢ ∞. That is what the St. Petersburg averages and the averages of a Pareto with α ≤ 1 do (heavy tails): long stretches of drift down, broken by ever larger upward jumps.
What the strong law licenses. It is the theorem behind "the long-run frequency is the probability", behind the consistency of every Monte Carlo estimate along the one stream of random numbers a computer produces (§11), and behind the empirical cdf converging uniformly to the true one. It needs exactly one thing, a finite mean, and when that fails, it fails along almost every path, not just on average.

05 The central limit theorem

The laws of large numbers say that X̄ₙ − μ goes to zero. The central limit theorem says how: magnified by √n, the error has a limiting normal distribution, whatever the distribution of the terms, provided their variance is finite. This section states it, proves it by cumulants (every cumulant beyond the second dies at a known rate, which is the promise made in the expectation chapter), reads it as repeated convolution, measures its speed with the Berry–Esseen bound, and shows what happens instead when the variance is infinite (stable laws) or the terms are not identically distributed (Lindeberg–Feller).

The statement

  • Theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy). Let X₁, X₂, … be iid with mean μ and variance 0 < σ² < ∞, and Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ. Then
    Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) = √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ ⇝ N(0, 1), that is, P(Zₙ ≤ z) → Φ(z) for every real z.
  • Why √n. Var(Sₙ − nμ) = nσ², so dividing by σ√n is the unique scaling that keeps the variance at 1 for every n. Divide by less and the spread blows up; divide by more (say by n, giving X̄ₙ − μ) and the distribution collapses to the point 0, which is the law of large numbers. The CLT describes the fluctuations that the law of large numbers averages away, at the one magnification where they neither vanish nor explode.
  • The mode. Convergence in distribution: probabilities of events about Zₙ converge to normal probabilities. Since Φ is continuous, the convergence is automatically uniform in z (Pólya's theorem): sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| → 0. That uniform distance is the quantity the Berry–Esseen bound below controls.
  • The applied view. The five equivalent ways of writing the result, the hypotheses one at a time, and the things it is often misquoted as saying, are in the central limit theorem of the statistics series. This section is about why it is true and how fast.

Delivered: the proof by cumulants

  • Cumulants were introduced with a promise: the skewness of a sum of n iid terms falls like 1/√n and its excess kurtosis like 1/n, which is "the quantitative heart of the central limit theorem". The closing list of Expectation, Variance & Inequalities asked for that cumulant argument to be made rigorous, and the normal distribution asked for the theorem "with a proof sketch and its rate". Here is that heart, made into a proof; the rate is the Berry–Esseen bound below. Assume first that X has a moment generating function on an interval around 0 (the general case follows).
  • Standardise the terms. Let Yᵢ = (Xᵢ − μ)/σ, iid with mean 0 and variance 1, and cumulant generating function K(s) = ln E e^(sY) = s²/2 + κ₃s³/3! + κ₄s⁴/4! + ⋯, where κ₃ = γ₁ is the skewness of X and κ₄ = γ₂ its excess kurtosis (κ₁ = 0, κ₂ = 1 by the standardisation).
  • Two rules for cumulants. Cumulants of independent terms add (mgfs multiply, so their logarithms add), and scaling by c multiplies the j-th cumulant by cʲ. Since Zₙ = (Y₁ + ⋯ + Yₙ)/√n,
    κⱼ(Zₙ) = n · κⱼ / (√n)ʲ = κⱼ / n^((j−2)/2): κ₁ = 0, κ₂ = 1, κ₃(Zₙ) = γ₁/√n, κ₄(Zₙ) = γ₂/n, κ₅(Zₙ) = κ₅/n^(3/2), …
    Every cumulant of order 3 and higher vanishes, the j-th like n^(−(j−2)/2). The normal is the distribution whose cumulants beyond the second are all zero, so this is already the theorem, read cumulant by cumulant.
  • Making it a proof. The cumulant generating function of Zₙ is Kₙ(t) = n K(t/√n). Near 0, K(s) = s²/2 + R(s) with |R(s)| ≤ C|s|³ (Taylor's theorem; K is smooth where the mgf exists). So for fixed t and large n,
    Kₙ(t) = n[t²/(2n) + R(t/√n)] = t²/2 + n·R(t/√n), |n·R(t/√n)| ≤ C|t|³/√n → 0
    and E e^(tZₙ) → e^(t²/2), the mgf of N(0, 1), for every t near 0. Convergence of mgfs on an interval around 0 implies convergence in distribution (§01), so Zₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). ∎
  • Only a finite variance. Many distributions with a finite variance have no mgf (a t distribution with 3 degrees of freedom, a Pareto with α = 3). The repair is to use the characteristic function φ(t) = E e^(itY), which always exists. Two moments are exactly enough for φ(t) = 1 − t²/2 + o(t²) as t → 0, and then E e^(itZₙ) = φ(t/√n)ⁿ = (1 − t²/(2n) + o(1/n))ⁿ → e^(−t²/2), the characteristic function of N(0, 1). Lévy's continuity theorem converts this into Zₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). That is the complete proof, modulo Lévy's theorem; the finite variance is used precisely once, to get the o(t²).
  • Worked. For Exp(1) terms, γ₁ = 2 and γ₂ = 6, so Z₂₅ has skewness 0.4 and excess kurtosis 0.24, and Z₁₀₀ has 0.2 and 0.06. These are exact: Sₙ ~ Gamma(n, 1), whose skewness is 2/√n and excess kurtosis 6/n (the gamma distribution). For U(0, 1) terms, γ₁ = 0 and γ₂ = −1.2, so the sum of 12 uniforms minus 6 has mean 0, variance 1, skewness 0 and excess kurtosis −0.1: close enough to normal that it was once a standard way to generate normal draws.

Delivered: the convolution view, and why the normal

  • Sums are repeated convolutions. The density of Sₙ is the n-fold convolution f * f * ⋯ * f (sums of random variables: convolution, which asked why repeated convolutions become normal). Each convolution averages the density against a shifted copy of itself, which smooths it: two uniforms make a triangle, three make a piecewise quadratic, and the corners vanish one derivative at a time. The CLT says that, after recentring and rescaling, the shape this smoothing converges to does not depend on the shape it started from.
  • Why the normal in particular: a fixed point. Suppose some distribution with variance 1 were a limit of standardised sums. Then the standardised sum of two independent copies, (Y + Y′)/√2, must have the same distribution as Y (a limit of standardised sums of 2n terms is also the limit of standardised sums of n terms, taken in pairs). By the two cumulant rules, κⱼ((Y + Y′)/√2) = 2κⱼ/2^(j/2), so κⱼ(1 − 2^(1−j/2)) = 0, and for j ≥ 3 the bracket is not zero: κⱼ = 0. The only distribution with every cumulant beyond the second equal to zero is the normal. The normal is the unique finite-variance fixed point of "add two independent copies and rescale".
  • Information is lost, not created. Each convolution keeps only the mean and variance exactly (they add) and shrinks every standardised higher cumulant by a factor that tends to zero. The normal is what is left when everything except the mean and the variance has been averaged away. That is also why the normal has the largest entropy for a given variance (the normal distribution): it carries no other information.

What convergence in distribution buys, and what it costs

  • It buys interval probabilities, uniformly. P(a < Zₙ ≤ b) → Φ(b) − Φ(a) for every interval at once, which is all that a confidence interval or a test at a fixed level needs.
  • It does not buy relative accuracy in the tails. An absolute error of 10⁻³ in the cdf is negligible at z = 1 and catastrophic at z = 5, where 1 − Φ(5) = 2.9 × 10⁻⁷. Probabilities of large deviations, where X̄ₙ − μ is of fixed size rather than of size 1/√n, decay exponentially at a rate set by the whole mgf, not just the variance: that is the territory of the Chernoff bounds (Chernoff & Hoeffding), not of the CLT.
  • It does not buy densities. A binomial count has no density at all; it converges in distribution to a normal, but its pmf is a comb. A separate local limit theorem says P(Sₙ = k) ≈ φ((k − nμ)/(σ√n))/(σ√n) for integer-valued terms whose values are not confined to a sub-lattice; it is a different theorem with extra hypotheses, and it is what justifies the continuity correction (§06).
  • It does not say Zₙ is near any particular normal variable. As the flip of §02 showed, convergence in distribution is about histograms. Two independent replications of an experiment each have an approximately normal Zₙ; they are not approximately equal.
  • The vector version. For iid random vectors with mean μ and covariance matrix Σ, √n(X̄ₙ − μ) ⇝ N(0, Σ) (the multivariate normal). The proof reduces to the scalar case: a random vector converges in distribution exactly when every linear combination aᵀX̄ₙ does (the Cramér–Wold device), and each aᵀX̄ₙ is a scalar average with variance aᵀΣa.

How fast: the Berry–Esseen bound

  • Statement. If, in addition, ρ = E|X − μ|³ < ∞, then for every n
    sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C · ρ / (σ³ √n)
    with an absolute constant C that works for every distribution and every n. The rate is 1/√n, and the distribution enters only through the standardised third absolute moment ρ/σ³, a measure of lopsidedness close to the skewness.
  • The constant, honestly. The best constant proved for iid terms is below 0.47 (C ≤ 0.4690). It cannot be pushed below (√10 + 3)/(6√(2π)) = 0.4097, Esseen's lower bound, which a particular two-point distribution attains in the limit. Recent work indicates that 0.4097 itself suffices once n exceeds a universal but astronomically large threshold; for every practical n, the usable constant remains 0.4690.
  • Worked: exponential terms. For Exp(1), σ = 1 and ρ = E|X − 1|³ = ∫₀¹(1 − x)³e^(−x) dx + ∫₁^∞(x − 1)³e^(−x) dx = (6/e − 2) + 6/e = 12/e − 2 = 2.4146. At n = 100 the bound is 0.4690 × 2.4146/10 = 0.1132. The true distance, computed exactly from the Gamma(100, 1) cdf, is 0.0133: the bound is about 8.5 times too pessimistic, because it must also cover distributions far worse than the exponential.
  • Worked: a fair coin, where the bound is nearly tight. For Bernoulli(½), |X − ½| = ½ always, so ρ = σ³ = 1/8 and the bound is 0.4690/√n: 0.0469 at n = 100. The true distance is 0.0398. A lattice distribution cannot be approximated by a smooth curve to better than half its largest jump, and P(S₁₀₀ = 50) = 0.0796: discreteness, not skewness, is the binding constraint here, and it is why the worst cases for the constant are two-point distributions.
  • The bound versus the truth. Berry–Esseen is a guarantee: convergence is never slower than 1/√n when ρ is finite. It is a poor estimate of the actual error for continuous distributions. The leading Edgeworth term (§06) estimates it accurately, and the sample-size rule built on it, with a catalogue of real skewnesses, is in how fast? the rate of convergence.

When the variance is infinite: stable laws

  • The generalised central limit theorem. If the tails of X decay like a power, P(|X| > x) ≈ c·x^(−α) with 0 < α < 2, the variance is infinite and the CLT fails, but sums still have a limit shape: (Sₙ − bₙ)/n^(1/α) converges in distribution to an α-stable law. Stable laws are exactly the fixed points of "add independent copies and rescale" once the finite-variance requirement is dropped: for a symmetric α-stable X, X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ has the same distribution as n^(1/α)X. The normal is the case α = 2.
  • The Cauchy is the α = 1 stable law. Then n^(1/α) = n and X̄ₙ = Sₙ/n has the distribution of a single term, the result §04 proved with characteristic functions. More generally the average of symmetric α-stable terms is distributed as n^(1/α − 1) times one term: it shrinks when α > 1, stays put at α = 1, and grows when α < 1. See heavy tails for the Pareto and the Cauchy as distributions.
  • Between 1 and 2: a mean, but a slow one. For a Pareto with 1 < α < 2, the strong law holds (E X < ∞), but the typical error of X̄ₙ shrinks like n^(1/α − 1), not n^(−1/2): at α = 1.5, like n^(−1/3). Quadrupling the sample cuts the error by 4^(1/3) = 1.59, not 2. The limiting shape is a stable law skewed toward the heavy tail, so intervals of the form x̄ ± 2s/√n are both too narrow and centred in the wrong place.
  • Exactly at α = 2. A Pareto with α = 2 has an infinite variance by the narrowest of margins: E[X² 1{X ≤ x}] grows like 2 ln x. The limit is still normal, but the scaling picks up a logarithm: (Sₙ − nμ)/√(n ln n) ⇝ N(0, 1). The boundary between the normal and the stable world is the boundary between a truncated variance that converges or grows slowly enough, and one that grows like a power.

Independent but not identical: Lindeberg–Feller

  • Triangular arrays. In many applications the terms of the sum change with n: the n-th sum is Xₙ,₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ,ₖₙ, a row of a "triangular array" of independent terms with means 0 and variances summing to 1. A binomial with p depending on n, the count of successes among trials with different probabilities, and the proof sketch for sample quantiles in §09 are all of this form.
  • The Lindeberg condition. For every ε > 0, Σᵢ E[Xₙ,ᵢ² · 1{|Xₙ,ᵢ| > ε}] → 0: the share of the total variance carried by terms that are individually large must vanish. Under it, Σᵢ Xₙ,ᵢ ⇝ N(0, 1) (Lindeberg); and if no single term's variance is a non-vanishing share of the total, the condition is also necessary (Feller). No proof here; the content is "a democracy of many small contributions", and the applied page catalogues what happens when one term dominates (where the CLT fails outright).
  • A checkable version (Lyapunov). If Σᵢ E|Xₙ,ᵢ|³ → 0 (after the same standardisation), Lindeberg's condition holds. Worked: let Bᵢ ~ Bernoulli(pᵢ) be independent, with total variance sₙ² = Σ pᵢ(1 − pᵢ). Since |Bᵢ − pᵢ| ≤ 1, Σ E|Bᵢ − pᵢ|³ ≤ Σ pᵢ(1 − pᵢ) = sₙ², and after dividing by sₙ³ Lyapunov's ratio is at most 1/sₙ. So the number of successes is asymptotically normal whenever sₙ → ∞, whatever the pᵢ are. With pᵢ = 1/i (the indicators that the i-th of a sequence of iid continuous draws is a new record, §10), sₙ² ≈ ln n → ∞: the number of records in n draws is asymptotically normal with mean and variance about ln n, though the convergence is glacial. If instead the pᵢ in each row are all small and their total stays near a fixed λ, the limit is Poisson(λ), not normal (the Poisson approximation).

06 Normal approximations, revisited as theory

The binomial, Poisson and gamma distributions all become approximately normal for the same reason: each is a sum of many independent pieces. The CLT says the approximation becomes exact in the limit; this section says what the error is at a finite n and where it comes from. For a continuous distribution the leading error is the skewness term of the Edgeworth expansion, of size 1/√n; for a count there is a second error of the same size from the jumps, and the continuity correction is the device that removes it.

Delivered: why the binomial, Poisson and gamma go normal

  • The binomial and both roads lead to the normal curve promised the theorem behind the bell shape, with a proof sketch and its rate. The theorem is §05's. Bin(n, p) is a sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) terms, so (X − np)/√(np(1 − p)) ⇝ N(0, 1): the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, the first CLT ever proved. Poisson(μ) with integer μ is a sum of μ independent Poisson(1) counts (Poisson counts add), so (X − μ)/√μ ⇝ N(0, 1) as μ → ∞, and monotonicity in μ covers non-integer means. Gamma(α, λ) with integer α is a sum of α exponentials, and (X − α/λ)/(√α/λ) ⇝ N(0, 1) as α → ∞.
  • The rate. By Berry–Esseen, the cdf error is at most 0.469·ρ/(σ³√n), and by the cumulant computation the skewness of the standardised variable is γ₁/√n: (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)) for the binomial, 1/√μ for the Poisson, 2/√α for the gamma. The normal curve takes over at the rate the skewness dies, which is why np(1 − p) (not n) is the quantity in every rule of thumb, and why p near ½ helps so much.

Where the error comes from: the Edgeworth expansion

  • Keep one more cumulant. In §05's proof, Kₙ(t) = t²/2 + γ₁t³/(6√n) + O(1/n). Exponentiate and keep the first-order term: E e^(tZₙ) ≈ e^(t²/2)(1 + γ₁t³/(6√n)).
  • Which density has that mgf? Integration by parts three times gives ∫ e^(tx)·(−φ‴(x)) dx = t³e^(t²/2), and −φ‴(x) = (x³ − 3x)φ(x). So the density of Zₙ is approximately φ(x)[1 + γ₁(x³ − 3x)/(6√n)]: the normal, tilted by a cubic that pushes mass to the side of the long tail. Integrating, since the derivative of −(x² − 1)φ(x) is (x³ − 3x)φ(x):
    P(Zₙ ≤ x) = Φ(x) − φ(x) · γ₁ (x² − 1) / (6√n) + O(1/n)
  • Reading it. The leading error is proportional to the skewness and to 1/√n, and its largest size, at x = 0, is |γ₁|φ(0)/(6√n) = 0.0665·|γ₁|/√n. It vanishes at x = ±1: the tilt moves mass around, but the net amount it moves below those two points is zero. For a symmetric distribution γ₁ = 0, the term vanishes, and the error is O(1/n): a whole order faster.
  • Delivered: the 0.524 of the gamma convolution. Gamma times add found P(Gamma(30, ½) ≤ 60) = 0.524, the probability that the 30th arrival of a rate-½ process comes by its mean time. The normal approximation says 0.5 exactly. The Edgeworth correction at x = 0, with γ₁ = 2/√30 = 0.3651, is +φ(0)·γ₁/6 = 0.0243, giving 0.5243; the exact value is 0.52428. The whole discrepancy is the skewness term.
  • Two-sided intervals are better than one-sided ones. The correction φ(x)(x² − 1) is an even function of x, so it cancels in P(−z < Zₙ ≤ z) = Fₙ(z) − Fₙ(−z). A symmetric interval's coverage error is O(1/n), while each one-sided tail is off by O(1/√n), in opposite directions. Symmetric confidence intervals look accurate partly because their two tail errors offset.

The continuity correction, as a theorem

  • The second error. For an integer-valued Sₙ, the cdf is a staircase with jumps of size about φ(x)/(σ√n), and no smooth curve can be closer to a staircase than half a step. So a lattice distribution has a second 1/√n error term, a sawtooth of amplitude φ(x)/(2σ√n), on top of the skewness term.
  • Where the half comes from. By the local limit theorem (§05), P(Sₙ = j) ≈ φ((j − nμ)/(σ√n))/(σ√n), which is the midpoint-rule approximation to the normal area over [j − ½, j + ½]. Summing over j ≤ k gives the area up to k + ½: P(Sₙ ≤ k) ≈ Φ((k + ½ − nμ)/(σ√n)). Since {Sₙ ≤ k} = {Sₙ < k + 1}, any point in [k, k + 1) is "correct" for the staircase; the midpoint is the one where the sawtooth error crosses zero, and evaluating there removes the lattice term to leading order, leaving the skewness term.
  • Worked: a symmetric case. Bin(100, ½): P(S ≤ 55) = 0.86437 exactly. Without the correction, Φ(1) = 0.84134, off by 0.0230; the sawtooth predicts φ(1)·½/5 = 0.0242. With it, Φ(1.1) = 0.86433, off by 4 × 10⁻⁵: symmetry removed the skewness term, the correction removed the lattice term, and only O(1/n) is left.
  • Worked: a skewed case. Bin(100, 0.1), mean 10, σ = 3, γ₁ = 0.8/3 = 0.267: P(S ≤ 5) = 0.0576 exactly. The corrected normal gives Φ(−1.5) = 0.0668, still 16% too large. Adding the Edgeworth term at x = −1.5, −φ(1.5)·0.267·1.25/6 = −0.0072, gives 0.0596. The correction handles the jumps; only the skewness term handles the skew.
  • Worked: the Poisson. Poisson(100): P(X ≤ 110) = 0.85286. Without the correction Φ(1.0) = 0.84134; with it Φ(1.05) = 0.85314 (the values quoted in the Poisson section); adding the Edgeworth term with γ₁ = 0.1 gives 0.85275, cutting the remaining error from 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ to 1.1 × 10⁻⁴.

07 The delta method

The CLT is about averages, but the quantities people report are usually functions of averages: a log of a mean, an odds ratio, a ratio of two means, a correlation, a rate 1/x̄. The delta method says that a smooth function of an asymptotically normal estimate is itself asymptotically normal, with its standard deviation multiplied by the slope |g′| at the true value. It is a first-order Taylor expansion with a convergence theorem attached, and it is also the tool for designing variance-stabilising transforms, functions chosen so that the standard error stops depending on the unknown parameter.

Statement and proof

  • Theorem. Suppose √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ²), and g is differentiable at θ with g′(θ) ≠ 0. Then
    √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) ⇝ N(0, g′(θ)² σ²), in working form g(Yₙ) ≈ N(g(θ), g′(θ)²σ²/n).
    The conditions are mild: differentiability is needed only at the single point θ, and Yₙ need not be an average, only asymptotically normal at rate √n. (With g′(θ) = 0 the statement remains true but says only that the limit is the point 0; the second-order version below takes over.)
  • Why it is true: zoom in until g is a line. Yₙ concentrates in a window of width about σ/√n around θ. Over a window that narrow, a differentiable g is indistinguishable from its tangent line g(θ) + g′(θ)(y − θ), and a linear function of an approximately normal variable is approximately normal, with its standard deviation multiplied by the slope.
  • Proof. Differentiability at θ means g(y) − g(θ) = [g′(θ) + r(y)](y − θ) with r(y) → 0 as y → θ (set r(θ) = 0). Then
    √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) = g′(θ)·√n(Yₙ − θ) + r(Yₙ)·√n(Yₙ − θ).
    The first term converges in distribution to g′(θ)·N(0, σ²). For the second: Yₙ − θ = n^(−1/2)·√n(Yₙ − θ), a vanishing constant times a variable with a limit distribution, so Yₙ →ᵖ θ (Slutsky, §08); r is continuous at θ, so r(Yₙ) →ᵖ 0 (continuous mapping, §02); and a variable converging in probability to 0, times one converging in distribution, converges to 0 (Slutsky again). Adding a term that tends to 0 in probability does not change the limit (Slutsky a third time). ∎
  • The multivariate version. If √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, Σ) for a vector Yₙ and g is differentiable at θ with gradient ∇g ≠ 0, then √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) ⇝ N(0, ∇gᵀΣ∇g). The proof is the same with a gradient in place of the derivative, and the multivariate CLT of §05 supplies the hypothesis whenever Yₙ is a vector of averages.

What the delta method does not promise

  • It is the variance of the limit, not the limit of the variances. If X̄ is exactly normal, 1/X̄ has no finite mean at any n, because the normal density is positive at 0 and ∫ |1/y| dy diverges there. Yet √n(1/X̄ − 1/μ) ⇝ N(0, σ²/μ⁴) for any μ ≠ 0. Both statements are true: the delta method describes where almost all the probability is, and says nothing about the vanishing sliver near 0 that ruins the moments (the growing spike of §02 in another form). In practice: a delta-method standard error is meaningful as the scale of the typical error, not as the square root of a mean squared error.
  • It needs g to be nearly linear over the spread of Yₙ. The approximation is first-order in σ/√n. When the window of width σ/√n is wide relative to the scale on which g bends (a log near 0, a reciprocal near 0, odds near p = 1), the exact distribution of g(Yₙ) is skewed and its centre is biased by about ½g″(θ)σ²/n. Variance-stabilising transforms

  • The problem. For many estimators the standard error depends on the very parameter being estimated: √(p(1 − p)/n) for a proportion, √(μ/n) for a Poisson mean, (1 − ρ²)/√n for a correlation. Intervals then need the unknown to compute their width, plug-in widths are worst where the estimate is least reliable, and comparisons across groups mix differences in level with differences in noise.
  • The recipe: run the delta method backwards. If Var Yₙ ≈ v(θ)/n, find g with g′(θ)²·v(θ) = constant, that is g′(θ) = 1/√v(θ) and
    g(θ) = ∫ dθ / √v(θ) ⟹ Var g(Yₙ) ≈ 1/n, whatever θ is.
  • Derived in full: the arcsine transform for proportions. Here v(p) = p(1 − p), so g(p) = ∫ dp/√(p(1 − p)). Substitute p = sin²u, dp = 2 sin u cos u du, and √(p(1 − p)) = sin u cos u: the integrand becomes 2 du, so g = 2u = 2 arcsin √p. Check by differentiating: d/dp [arcsin √p] = (1/√(1 − p))·(1/(2√p)), so g′(p) = 1/√(p(1 − p)), and g′(p)²·p(1 − p) = 1. Hence 2 arcsin √p̂ ≈ N(2 arcsin √p, 1/n). A simulation at n = 100 gives n·Var(2 arcsin √p̂) = 1.14, 1.02, 1.01 at p = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, against n·Var p̂ = 0.047, 0.160, 0.250: the raw variance changes fivefold, the transformed one by 13%, and that residue comes from the region near p = 0 where the transform is most curved.
  • The square root for Poisson counts. v(μ) = μ, so g(μ) = ∫ dμ/√μ = 2√μ, and 2√X̄ ≈ N(2√μ, 1/n); for a single count X ~ Poisson(μ), Var √X → ¼. Exactly: 0.306, 0.261, 0.252 at μ = 4, 10, 50. The refinement √(X + 3/8) (Anscombe) brings these to 0.2497, 0.2502, 0.2500, by cancelling the next term of the expansion. This is why count data are square-rooted before methods that assume constant variance.
  • Fisher's z for a correlation. For bivariate normal data, the sample correlation satisfies √n(r − ρ) ⇝ N(0, (1 − ρ²)²) (itself a multivariate delta method applied to five sample moments). So v(ρ) = (1 − ρ²)² and g(ρ) = ∫ dρ/(1 − ρ²) = ½ ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) = artanh ρ, with Var(artanh r) ≈ 1/n, refined to 1/(n − 3). 08 Slutsky's theorem and continuous mapping

The CLT standardises by the true σ, which nobody knows. Every practical interval and test replaces it with an estimate, and every delta-method argument needs to combine a variable that converges in distribution with one that converges in probability. Two short theorems license all of this. The continuous mapping theorem says continuous functions preserve convergence; Slutsky's theorem says that a variable converging in probability to a constant can be added to, multiplied by, or divided into a variable converging in distribution, as if it were the constant. Together with the LLN and the CLT they assemble the t statistic, the bridge from probability to inference.

The two theorems

  • Continuous mapping. If g is continuous (it is enough that the set where it is discontinuous has probability 0 under the limit) and Xₙ → X almost surely, in probability, or in distribution, then g(Xₙ) → g(X) in the same mode. For convergence in distribution the proof is one line with §01's equivalent form: for bounded continuous h, h ∘ g is bounded and continuous, so E h(g(Xₙ)) → E h(g(X)). For almost-sure convergence it is the calculus fact that continuous functions preserve limits, applied outcome by outcome.
  • Slutsky's theorem. If Xₙ ⇝ X and Yₙ →ᵖ c for a constant c, then
    Xₙ + Yₙ ⇝ X + c, Yₙ·Xₙ ⇝ c·X, Xₙ/Yₙ ⇝ X/c (if c ≠ 0).
  • Why the constant matters. The proof's key step is that (Xₙ, Yₙ) ⇝ (X, c) jointly. That is automatic when the second limit is a constant: whatever dependence there is between Xₙ and Yₙ cannot survive in the limit, because a constant is independent of everything. Then the continuous mapping theorem applied to (x, y) ↦ x + y, xy or x/y finishes it. For the sum there is also a direct sandwich, as in §02: P(Xₙ + Yₙ ≤ t) ≤ P(Xₙ ≤ t − c + ε) + P(|Yₙ − c| > ε) and the matching lower bound, then let n → ∞ and ε → 0.
  • Two corollaries used constantly. If Xₙ ⇝ X and aₙ → 0 (numbers), then aₙXₙ →ᵖ 0: a vanishing constant times something with a limit distribution vanishes, which is how §07 got Yₙ →ᵖ θ from √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ²). And if Xₙ − Yₙ →ᵖ 0 and Yₙ ⇝ Y, then Xₙ ⇝ Y: asymptotically negligible differences can be ignored, which is how every "remainder term" in an asymptotic argument is disposed of.

The wrong generalisation

  • Marginal limits do not add. It is tempting to conclude from Xₙ ⇝ X and Yₙ ⇝ Y that Xₙ + Yₙ ⇝ X + Y. But the right-hand side is not even defined until the joint distribution of (X, Y) is specified, and marginal convergence does not determine it. Let Z ~ N(0, 1), Xₙ = Z and Yₙ = −Z: both converge in distribution to N(0, 1), and Xₙ + Yₙ = 0. With Yₙ = Z′, an independent copy, the same marginals give Xₙ + Yₙ ~ N(0, 2).
  • When sums are safe. Sums and products of limits are legal when the convergence is joint: (Xₙ, Yₙ) ⇝ (X, Y). That holds if one of the limits is a constant (Slutsky), if Xₙ and Yₙ are independent for each n (then the limit is the product distribution), or if the pair is a vector of averages covered by the multivariate CLT. In probability the problem does not arise: Xₙ →ᵖ X and Yₙ →ᵖ Y on the same space do imply Xₙ + Yₙ →ᵖ X + Y, because that mode already compares values, not just distributions.

The t statistic, assembled

  • The object. For iid data with mean μ and finite variance σ² > 0, with sample standard deviation sₙ,
    Tₙ = √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/sₙ = [√n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ] · [σ/sₙ].
  • Step 1, the CLT. The first factor converges in distribution to N(0, 1).
  • Step 2, the LLN and continuous mapping. sₙ² = (n/(n − 1))·[(1/n)ΣXᵢ² − X̄ₙ²]. The weak law applied to the Xᵢ² (which have mean σ² + μ²; only E X² < ∞ is needed, Khinchin's version of §03) and to the Xᵢ gives (1/n)ΣXᵢ² →ᵖ σ² + μ² and X̄ₙ →ᵖ μ; the continuous map (a, b) ↦ a − b² and n/(n − 1) → 1 give sₙ² →ᵖ σ²; the continuous map x ↦ σ/√x (continuous at σ² > 0) gives σ/sₙ →ᵖ 1.
  • Step 3, Slutsky. A product of a factor converging in distribution to N(0, 1) and a factor converging in probability to 1: Tₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). ∎ Every "±1.96 × estimated SE" interval in statistics is justified by exactly this three-step argument, with the CLT and the estimate of the SE replaced by whatever the estimator calls for.
  • Plug-in standard errors are legal. The same argument shows that any consistent estimate of a standard error may replace the true one without changing the limit: (θ̂ₙ − θ)/ŜEₙ ⇝ N(0, 1) whenever (θ̂ₙ − θ)/SEₙ ⇝ N(0, 1) and ŜEₙ/SEₙ →ᵖ 1. That covers the Wald interval for a proportion (SE computed at p̂), delta-method standard errors evaluated at the estimate, and the standard errors of a maximum likelihood estimate computed from the observed information. It is a limit statement: the finite-n coverage of the Wald interval for a proportion is notoriously poor near p = 0 or 1 (intervals for a proportion).
  • Where t, rather than z, comes from. For normal data Tₙ has exactly the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom (the t distribution), and t_(n−1) ⇝ N(0, 1), consistent with Slutsky. For non-normal data neither is exact; the t quantiles are a correction for the randomness of sₙ that is exact only under normality, and the leading error is the skewness. To first order the skewness of Tₙ is −2γ₁/√n: twice that of the standardised mean, and of the opposite sign. For right-skewed data, a sample with a large mean usually also has a large sₙ (the same big observations inflate both), which pulls Tₙ back toward 0; a sample with a small mean has a small sₙ, which pushes Tₙ further out. Simulated with Exp(1) data: skewness of Tₙ −1.09 at n = 20 (first-order −0.89) and −0.29 at n = 200 (−0.28).
  • What it costs in coverage. At n = 20 with Exp(1) data, the two-sided t interval at nominal 95% covers 91.9% of the time; the true mean lies above the interval 7.6% of the time (Tₙ < −t*) and below it 0.6% of the time, against 2.5% each (400,000 simulated samples). The two one-sided errors are of order 1/√n and of opposite sign, so they partly cancel in the two-sided coverage (§06). One-sided tests on skewed data are where this matters most (choosing a test).

09 Sample quantiles are asymptotically normal

A sample median or percentile is not an average, so the CLT does not apply to it directly. It is nevertheless asymptotically normal, with a variance that has a strikingly simple form: p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²), the variance of a sample proportion divided by the squared height of the density at the quantile. The proof turns a question about an order statistic into a question about a binomial count. The formula explains why the median is robust but less efficient than the mean for normal data, why it still works perfectly for the Cauchy, and why extreme percentiles and quantiles in flat regions need far more data.

Delivered: the theorem and a proof sketch

  • Order statistics derived the exact distribution of the k-th smallest observation and an approximation Var X₍ₖ₎ ≈ p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²), deferring "the limit theorem behind the approximation" to this page, and the closing list of Joint Distributions & Dependence named "the asymptotic normality of sample quantiles" among the results to come, beside the central limit theorem (§05) and the strong law (§04). Here it is.
  • Theorem. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be iid with cdf F, let q_p satisfy F(q_p) = p, and suppose F is differentiable at q_p with density f(q_p) > 0. Let q̂_p = X₍ₖ₎ with k = ⌈np⌉. Then
    √n(q̂_p − q_p) ⇝ N(0, p(1 − p) / f(q_p)²).
  • Proof sketch: count instead of sort. Fix t and put xₙ = q_p + t/√n. The k-th smallest observation is at most xₙ exactly when at least k observations are, and the number that are is Nₙ ~ Bin(n, pₙ) with pₙ = F(xₙ) = p + f(q_p)t/√n + o(1/√n). So
    P(√n(q̂_p − q_p) ≤ t) = P(Nₙ ≥ k) = P( (Nₙ − npₙ)/√(npₙ(1 − pₙ)) ≥ (k − npₙ)/√(npₙ(1 − pₙ)) ).
    On the right, k − npₙ = (np + O(1)) − np − √n f(q_p)t + o(√n), so the threshold tends to −f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p)). The standardised binomial on the left is a sum of bounded independent terms whose success probability changes with n, a triangular array, and Lindeberg's theorem (§05) applies: it converges to N(0, 1). Hence the probability tends to P(Z ≥ −f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p))) = Φ(f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p))), the cdf of N(0, p(1 − p)/f(q_p)²) at t. ∎
  • The intuition in one line. If the sample quantile is off by δ, the fraction of the population below it is off by about f(q_p)·δ. The fraction of the sample below the true quantile is a proportion with standard error √(p(1 − p)/n). Matching the two: δ ≈ √(p(1 − p)/n)/f(q_p). A quantile's standard error is a proportion's standard error divided by the density height. (This is the delta method applied to the inverse of the empirical cdf; the precise version, q̂_p ≈ q_p + (p − F̂ₙ(q_p))/f(q_p) with an error smaller than 1/√n, is the Bahadur representation.)

Worked: the median of an exponential

  • The asymptotic answer. For Exp(1), the median is q = ln 2 = 0.6931 and the density there is f(ln 2) = e^(−ln 2) = ½. With p = ½: Var ≈ (¼)/(n · ¼) = 1/n. The sample median has standard error 1/√n: 0.2 at n = 25. For Exp(λ), everything scales by 1/λ.
  • The exact answer. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent exponentials (order statistics): X₍ₖ₎ = E₁/n + E₂/(n − 1) + ⋯ + Eₖ/(n − k + 1) with iid Eⱼ ~ Exp(1). So the median of n = 2m + 1 draws has mean Σ_(j=0)^m 1/(n − j) and variance Σ_(j=0)^m 1/(n − j)². At n = 25: mean 0.7127 (against ln 2 = 0.6931) and standard deviation 0.2019 (against 0.2). At n = 101: 0.0997 against 0.0995; at n = 401: 0.04997 against 0.04994. The asymptotic formula is already accurate to 1% at n = 25.
  • Efficiency. To estimate the mean θ of an exponential from its median, use θ̂ = q̂/ln 2, with standard error θ/(ln 2·√n) = 1.443θ/√n; the sample mean has θ/√n. The median-based estimator has asymptotic relative efficiency (ln 2)² = 0.480: it needs about 2.1 times as much data for the same precision. That is the price of robustness for this distribution; for the normal it is 2/π = 0.637 (f(μ) = 1/(σ√(2π)) gives Var = πσ²/(2n)), as tabulated in efficiency of estimators.
  • The Cauchy, rescued. The Cauchy average never converges (§04), but the Cauchy median has f(0) = 1/π and so SE = π/(2√n) = 1.571/√n: a perfectly ordinary 1/√n estimator of the centre. The quantile theorem needs only a positive density at the quantile, not a single moment. This is the constructive half of where the CLT fails outright: change the statistic, and the machinery returns.

Where f is flat, the quantile is hard to pin down

  • Extreme percentiles. For a normal population, the 99th percentile has f(q) = φ(2.326) = 0.0267/σ and SE ≈ √(0.99 × 0.01)/0.0267 · σ/√n = 3.73σ/√n, against 1.25σ/√n for the median: three times the standard error, nine times the data. For an exponential, the 90th percentile ln 10 = 2.303 has f = 0.1 and SE = 0.3/(0.1√n) = 3/√n, against 1/√n for the median. Tail quantiles sit where the density is low, and they are correspondingly uncertain; latency percentiles like p99 and p99.9 need large samples for exactly this reason.
  • A trough at the median. Mix N(−2, 1) and N(2, 1) equally. The median is 0, where the density is φ(2) = 0.054, so the sample median has SE ≈ 0.5/(0.054√n) = 9.3/√n, while the population standard deviation is √5 = 2.24 and the mean has SE = 2.24/√n. Here the "robust" estimator is four times as noisy as the mean, in the limit. The limit is also approached slowly: the exact standard deviation of the sample median (from its order-statistic density) is 0.65 at n = 101, where the formula says 0.92, then 0.39 against 0.46 at n = 401 and 0.218 against 0.231 at n = 1,601. The density climbs steeply on either side of the trough, so at moderate n the median's spread reaches places where f is larger than f(q_p); the formula is a large-n statement, accurate once that spread is small compared with the width of the trough. Even at n = 101 the median is three times as noisy as the mean (0.65 against 0.22). Robustness is a statement about outliers, not about efficiency in every shape.
  • A gap in the support. If f(q_p) = 0, the theorem's hypothesis fails and so can the conclusion: for a population uniform on [−2, −1] ∪ [1, 2], any point of [−1, 1] is a median, and the sample median keeps jumping between values near −1 and near 1, converging in distribution (to a fair choice between −1 and 1) but in no stronger mode. A quantile is well defined and estimable only where the cdf is strictly increasing.

10 What the maximum does instead

The mean of n draws concentrates at a fixed value with normal fluctuations of size 1/√n. The maximum of n draws does something completely different: it keeps growing, its fluctuations do not shrink, and its limiting shape, after the right recentring and rescaling, is never normal. Extreme-value theory says that only three shapes are possible (Gumbel, Fréchet and Weibull) and that the tail of the population decides which one appears. This is the mathematics of worst cases: the slowest request, the highest flood, the largest loss, the luckiest of many random configurations.

The three limits

  • The exact law is easy. Mₙ = max(X₁, …, Xₙ) has P(Mₙ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ (the extremes of an iid sample). The question is what this looks like for large n. Since F(x)ⁿ → 0 for every x below the top of the support, Mₙ runs off to the upper end, and a limit shape exists only after centring by some bₙ and scaling by some aₙ.
  • The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem. If (Mₙ − bₙ)/aₙ ⇝ G for some non-degenerate G, then G is, up to location and scale, one of three types: Gumbel Λ(x) = exp(−e^(−x)) on the whole line; Fréchet exp(−x^(−α)) for x > 0; or (reversed) Weibull exp(−(−x)^α) for x ≤ 0. They are the fixed points of "take the maximum of independent copies and rescale", exactly as the normal and the stable laws are fixed points of "add and rescale". The three combine into one family, the generalised extreme-value distribution exp(−(1 + ξx)^(−1/ξ)), with ξ = 0 (read as a limit) for Gumbel, ξ > 0 for Fréchet and ξ < 0 for Weibull.
  • Which one: the tail decides. For continuous distributions with smooth tails, tails that decay exponentially or faster (exponential, gamma, normal, lognormal) give the Gumbel; some distributions, including many discrete ones such as the Poisson and the geometric, have no extreme-value limit at all. Power-law tails P(X > x) ≈ x^(−α) (Pareto, t, Cauchy) give the Fréchet with the same α. A bounded support, approached polynomially at the top endpoint (uniform, beta), gives the Weibull. The rest of the distribution is irrelevant: the maximum only ever sees the tail.

Worked: the exponential maximum is Gumbel

  • The computation. For Exp(1), F(x) = 1 − e^(−x). Centre at bₙ = ln n and do not rescale (aₙ = 1):
    P(Mₙ − ln n ≤ x) = F(x + ln n)ⁿ = (1 − e^(−x)/n)ⁿ → exp(−e^(−x)), using (1 − c/n)ⁿ → e^(−c).
    The maximum of n exponential draws is ln n plus a Gumbel variable.
  • Its moments, exactly. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent, the j-th from the top being Exp with rate j, so Mₙ = E₁/1 + E₂/2 + ⋯ + Eₙ/n. Hence E Mₙ = Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n and Var Mₙ = Σ 1/j² → π²/6. So E(Mₙ − ln n) = Hₙ − ln n: 0.6264 at n = 10, 0.5822 at 100, 0.5777 at 1,000, tending to Euler's constant γ = 0.5772, the Gumbel mean; and the standard deviation tends to π/√6 = 1.2825, the Gumbel's.
  • Three contrasts with the mean. (i) The location of Mₙ grows, like ln n, while the mean's is fixed. (ii) The spread of Mₙ does not shrink: about 1.28 at every large n, while the mean's falls like 1/√n. (iii) The shape is skewed (Gumbel skewness 1.14) at every n, while the mean's skewness dies. And the convergence is fast: the largest cdf distance to the Gumbel is 0.0280 at n = 10, 0.0027 at 100, 0.00027 at 1,000, falling like 1/n.

The other two, stated

  • Pareto → Fréchet. For a Pareto with minimum 1 and tail index α, P(Mₙ ≤ n^(1/α)x) = (1 − x^(−α)/n)ⁿ → exp(−x^(−α)): the maximum grows like a power of n, n^(1/α), not a logarithm. With α = 3, the maximum scales by n^(1/3): 10 for 1,000 draws and 100 for a million, which makes the typical maximum about 9 and 90 times the typical single draw (the median maximum of 1,000 draws is about 10 × (ln 2)^(−1/3) = 11.3, against a single-draw median of 2^(1/3) = 1.26). Heavy-tailed maxima are dominated by single, enormous observations (heavy tails).
  • Uniform → Weibull. For U(0, 1), P(n(1 − Mₙ) > x) = (1 − x/n)ⁿ → e^(−x): the gap between the maximum and the endpoint is about Exp(1)/n. Here the maximum converges to a finite endpoint at rate 1/n, faster than any average. Estimating the endpoint θ of U(0, θ) by the sample maximum is therefore unusually precise, and also the classic case where the bootstrap fails (where the bootstrap fails).
  • The normal is Gumbel, slowly. For N(0, 1), (Mₙ − bₙ)/aₙ ⇝ Λ with aₙ = 1/√(2 ln n) and bₙ = √(2 ln n) − (ln ln n + ln 4π)/(2√(2 ln n)), but the error falls only like 1/ln n. At practical sizes the first-order growth √(2 ln n) is badly off: the expected maximum of 100 standard normals is 2.51 (against √(2 ln 100) = 3.03), and of 1,000 it is 3.24 (against 3.72).

Why this matters

  • Worst cases do not average out. A service that handles n requests per second with exponential-tailed latencies sees a slowest request that grows like ln n; with power-law latencies, like n^(1/α). Scaling traffic up does not make the worst case typical; it makes it worse. Capacity planning for maxima (timeouts, buffer sizes, flood defences, insurance reserves) uses the extreme-value family fitted to block maxima or to exceedances over a high threshold, not a normal fitted to everything.
  • The luckiest of many is lucky. If K configurations, models or features are compared on noisy scores, the best observed score is a maximum, and even when all are equally good it exceeds their common mean by about √(2 ln K) noise standard deviations to first order. For K = 1,000 independent normal scores that is 3.72, and the exact expected maximum is 3.24 standard deviations. That is the winner's curse behind optimistic leaderboards and hyperparameter searches, and the reason a held-out test set must be touched once (p-hacking and multiple comparisons).
  • Records. The i-th of a sequence of iid continuous draws is a new record with probability 1/i, independently of the others, so the expected number of records in n draws is Hₙ ≈ ln n + 0.577: 5.19 in 100 draws, 7.49 in 1,000. Records keep coming, but ever more rarely, and by §05's Lindeberg example their count is asymptotically normal.
The CLT is about sums; extreme-value theory is about maxima. Both are limit theorems in distribution, both have a small family of possible limits characterised as fixed points, and both are decided by the tail: the CLT by whether the variance is finite, extreme-value theory by how fast the tail decays. Applying the normal to a maximum, or to a quantity driven by one, is the most common way to underestimate a risk.

11 Why simulation works

Every Monte Carlo computation, from a single simulated probability to a bootstrap to the figures on this page, estimates a number θ = E h(X) by an average θ̂_N = (1/N) Σ h(Xᵢ) of simulated draws. Two theorems are silently invoked each time. The strong law says that the estimate converges to the right answer along the one stream of random numbers the computer produces. The central limit theorem, with Slutsky, says how far off it is: about s_h/√N, whatever the dimension of the problem. When h(X) has infinite variance, the first still holds and the second fails, and the reported error bar becomes fiction.

Consistency from the LLN, error bars from the CLT

  • Consistency. If E|h(X)| < ∞, the strong law (§04) gives θ̂_N →ᵃˢ θ. A computer runs one simulation, producing one infinite stream; "almost surely" is exactly the guarantee that this stream, not just a typical one, leads to the answer.
  • Error bars. If also σ_h² = Var h(X) < ∞, the CLT gives √N(θ̂_N − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ_h²), and replacing σ_h by the sample standard deviation s_h of the simulated values is legal by Slutsky (§08). So θ̂_N ± 1.96·s_h/√N is an approximate 95% interval for θ, computed from the simulation itself.
  • The 1/√N law and its two faces. The error does not depend on the dimension of X: integrating over 1,000 variables costs no more draws than integrating over one, for the same σ_h. That is why simulation beats grids in high dimensions. But each extra decimal digit costs 100 times the draws, which is why reducing σ_h (variance reduction) matters more than raw speed.
  • Worked: π by darts. Throw N uniform points into the unit square and let h = 4·1{U₁² + U₂² ≤ 1}, so θ = 4·(π/4) = π. Here σ_h = 4√(p(1 − p)) with p = π/4, which is 1.642. For a 95% error of ±0.005 (two correct decimals, reliably), N = (1.96 × 1.642/0.005)² ≈ 414,000 darts. For three decimals, 41 million.

When the variance is infinite

  • An innocent-looking integral. θ = ∫₀¹ x^(−0.7) dx = 1/0.3 = 3.333. The obvious estimator averages h(U) = U^(−0.7) over uniform draws. Its mean is finite, so the strong law holds and the estimate converges. But E h² = ∫₀¹ x^(−1.4) dx = ∞.
  • What goes wrong. P(h(U) > t) = P(U < t^(−1/0.7)) = t^(−1.43): h(U) is a Pareto variable with tail index α = 1/0.7 = 1.43. By §05 the error shrinks like N^(1/α − 1) = N^(−0.3), not N^(−1/2): a hundred times more draws cut the error by a factor of 4, not 10. Worse, s_h does not settle down: it grows with N in jumps, and the interval θ̂ ± 1.96 s_h/√N has no guaranteed coverage. Typically the estimate sits below 3.333 with an error bar too small to reach it, until a rare tiny U produces a huge h and the estimate and its error bar both jump.
  • The fix is mathematical, not computational. Substituting x = u^(1/0.3) turns the integral into ∫₀¹ (1/0.3) du, an estimator with zero variance. In general, choosing where to sample (importance sampling) so that h·f/g has a finite variance restores the CLT. Checking that the variance of what you average is finite is the first diagnostic of any simulation.

Beyond independent draws

  • Dependent draws. Markov chain Monte Carlo produces a dependent sequence. The law of large numbers is replaced by the ergodic theorem (time averages converge to the stationary expectation), and the CLT by a Markov-chain CLT whose variance is σ_h²(1 + 2Σₖ ρₖ), inflated by the chain's autocorrelations ρₖ. Using the iid error bar s_h/√N for correlated draws understates the error, by the same mechanism as averaging correlated variables (correlation).
  • Resampling is simulation too. The bootstrap is a Monte Carlo approximation to an integral over the empirical distribution, and the number of resamples B needed for a given precision follows the same 1/√B law (the Monte Carlo method).
  • Where this continues. Variance reduction (antithetic and control variates, importance sampling, stratification), the samplers that produce the draws, and the ergodic theorem and Markov-chain CLT that replace the iid laws when the draws come from a Markov chain: Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
Every simulated number is a sample mean. Report it with its error bar s_h/√N, check that the error bar shrinks like 1/√N when N is quadrupled (if it does not, suspect an infinite variance or dependent draws), and remember that the error bar is itself an estimate justified only by Slutsky's theorem.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Machine learning estimates and minimises expectations from averages. A test score is a sample mean, a training loss is a sample mean, a minibatch gradient is a sample mean, and a fitted model's parameters are asymptotically normal for the same reasons a sample mean is. The limit theorems decide when these averages can be trusted, how wide their error bars are, and where finite-sample guarantees must replace asymptotic ones.

Test-set scores: asymptotic error bars versus finite-sample bounds

  • The score is an average. For a fixed model and n independent test cases, the accuracy is the mean of n indicators with mean a, the true accuracy. The CLT gives SE = √(a(1 − a)/n): at a = 0.85 and n = 1,000, SE = 0.0113 and a 95% interval of ±0.022. The applied translation, including paired comparisons of two models on the same test set, is in a validation score is a statistic.
  • Hoeffding instead. Hoeffding's inequality guarantees P(|â − a| ≥ ε) ≤ 2e^(−2nε²) for every n and every distribution of a loss in [0, 1]. Setting the right side to 0.05 gives ε = √(ln(2/0.05)/(2n)) = 0.043 at n = 1,000: about twice the CLT half-width.
  • Which to use. The CLT interval is an approximation whose error is O(1/√n) per tail (§06); Hoeffding's is a guarantee that pays for its validity with width. For a single pre-registered model, the CLT is standard practice. For guarantees that must hold uniformly over many models, a union bound over Hoeffding is the route (concentration in action: the generalisation guarantee), because the maximum over models is an extreme-value problem (§10), not a CLT one.
  • Correlated test cases. Examples from the same user, document or patient are not independent, and the effective number of test cases is smaller than n: the weak law's variance σ²/n acquires covariance terms (§03).
test accuracy 0.85 on n = 1,000 cases, 95%CLT: ±1.96·√(0.85 × 0.15/1000) = ±0.022 Hoeffding: ±√(ln 40/2000) = ±0.043 both shrink like 1/√n

Training: empirical risk and stochastic gradients

  • Empirical risk. For a fixed model, the average training loss converges to the expected loss by the strong law. For the model chosen to minimise the training loss, that is not enough: the minimiser depends on the same data, and convergence must hold uniformly over the model class (a uniform law of large numbers, the content of generalisation bounds). The gap between the two is the reason training loss is an optimistic estimate of test loss.
  • Minibatch gradients. A minibatch gradient ĝ = (1/B) Σ ∇ℓ(θ; xᵢ) is an unbiased average of B per-example gradients, with covariance Σ_g/B: the LLN says larger batches give less noisy directions, at the 1/√B rate. That is intuition, not a convergence proof for training. The parameter θ moves as it is updated, so the iterates form a dependent sequence; their convergence is the subject of stochastic approximation (with step sizes satisfying Σ ηₜ = ∞ and Σ ηₜ² < ∞, the Robbins–Monro conditions, plus convexity or other structure), and averaged iterates obey their own CLT. None of that is proved here.

Asymptotic normality of fitted parameters

  • The maximum likelihood estimator. Under regularity conditions (smooth log-likelihood, true parameter in the interior, identifiability), √n(θ̂ − θ) ⇝ N(0, I(θ)⁻¹), with I the Fisher information per observation (maximum likelihood, Fisher information). The proof is this page in miniature: expand the score equation 0 = Sₙ(θ̂) ≈ Sₙ(θ) + Hₙ(θ)(θ̂ − θ); the score Sₙ(θ)/√n is a normalised sum of iid mean-zero terms with variance I (CLT); the Hessian −Hₙ(θ)/n is an average converging to I (LLN); Slutsky combines them into √n(θ̂ − θ) ≈ I⁻¹·Sₙ(θ)/√n ⇝ N(0, I⁻¹). When the model is misspecified, the two averages converge to different matrices A and B, and the limit is the "sandwich" N(0, A⁻¹BA⁻¹).
  • Standard errors for any smooth summary. A prediction, an odds ratio, an elasticity: a differentiable function of θ̂, whose standard error is the multivariate delta method of §07 with Σ = I⁻¹/n.

The bootstrap, and calibrated intervals

  • Why the bootstrap works. It replaces the unknown F by the empirical F̂ₙ, which converges uniformly by Glivenko–Cantelli (§04), and recomputes the statistic on resamples. For averages and smooth functions of averages, the CLT and the delta method hold at F̂ₙ just as at F, so the bootstrap distribution of √n(θ̂* − θ̂) has the same normal limit as that of √n(θ̂ − θ). It fails exactly where those theorems fail: for the maximum (§10), for heavy tails (§05), and for statistics that are not smooth at F (where the bootstrap fails).
  • Calibration of confidence intervals. An interval with nominal level 95% is calibrated if it covers the truth 95% of the time. Large-sample intervals are calibrated only in the limit. By §06, a symmetric two-sided interval built on the normal approximation has coverage error O(1/n), but each one-sided bound is off by O(1/√n), driven by the skewness. Refined intervals (the bootstrap-t and BCa) remove the 1/√n term (two better intervals), and simulation can measure actual coverage directly (what the intervals actually cover).
  • Calibration of predicted probabilities. A reliability diagram groups predictions into bins and compares the mean predicted probability with the observed frequency in each bin: a law of large numbers per bin, with a binomial standard error √(p(1 − p)/n_b) that is large for sparsely populated bins. Apparent miscalibration in a bin with 20 cases is mostly noise.
