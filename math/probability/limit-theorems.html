Limit Theorems & Convergence

Probability earns its keep in the limit. An average of many measurements settles on the true mean; a sum of many small disturbances looks normal whatever the disturbances are; a sample median, a log-odds, a correlation all come with a bell-shaped error bar once the sample is large. Each of these is a theorem about a sequence of random variables, and each is a statement in one particular sense of "converges". This page defines the four modes of convergence (in distribution, in probability, almost surely, in mean square), proves how they relate and builds the counterexamples that keep them apart. It proves the weak law of large numbers, states and partly proves the strong law with the Borel–Cantelli lemmas, and uses them to make the gambler's fallacy rigorous. It proves the central limit theorem by cumulants, measures its speed with the Berry–Esseen bound, and shows what replaces it when the variance is infinite. It then builds the toolkit that carries those two theorems to almost every statistic: the delta method, variance-stabilising transforms, Slutsky's theorem, the asymptotic normality of sample quantiles, and the very different limit of the maximum. It ends with the two theorems every simulation silently invokes and the places all of this does its work in machine learning.

01 What it means for random variables to converge

A sequence of numbers x₁, x₂, … converges to x when, for every tolerance ε > 0, the terms eventually stay within ε of x. A sequence of random variables X₁, X₂, … is a sequence of functions on a sample space, and "getting close to a limit" can mean several genuinely different things: the distributions get close; the variables are close with high probability; the realised sequence of numbers converges for almost every outcome; or the mean squared distance goes to zero. These are the four modes of convergence. They are not equivalent, every limit theorem in probability is a statement in one specific mode, and most confusions about the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem are confusions about which mode is being claimed.

Why "converges" needs a qualifier

  • A random variable is a function. Xₙ assigns a number Xₙ(ω) to each outcome ω of the sample space Ω (Random Variables & Discrete Distributions). A sequence of random variables is therefore a sequence of functions, and analysis already knows several ways for functions to converge: pointwise, pointwise except on a negligible set, in an average sense. Probability adds one more that has no analogue for functions: convergence of the distributions alone, with no reference to the functions at all.
  • Same distribution, no convergence. Let X₁, X₂, … be independent N(0, 1). Every term has exactly the same distribution, so in the weakest sense the sequence has "converged" from the start. But consecutive terms never get close to each other: Xₙ₊₁ − Xₙ ~ N(0, 2), so P(|Xₙ₊₁ − Xₙ| > 1) = 2(1 − Φ(1/√2)) = 0.4795 for every n. The realised sequence of numbers jumps about forever. Two continuous random variables are equal with probability 0, so no single random variable X can be "the limit" of these functions in any sense that compares values.
  • Concentrating, but never equal. Let Xₙ ~ N(0, 1/n). Intuitively Xₙ piles up at 0, yet P(Xₙ = 0) = 0 for every n, and the cdf at the limit point never moves: Fₙ(0) = ½ for every n, while the cdf of the constant 0 jumps from 0 to 1 at 0. Any sensible definition must call this convergence to 0, so it must tolerate failure at the jump.
  • The frequency of heads. The proportion p̂ₙ of heads in n tosses of a fair coin is a random variable for each n. "It tends to ½" might mean: its distribution piles up near ½; a miss by more than 0.01 becomes improbable; the particular infinite sequence of tosses you are living through has proportions that settle down; or its mean squared error 1/(4n) goes to zero. All four turn out to be true, but they are four different theorems with four different proofs, and the gap between the second and the third is exactly the gap between the weak and strong laws (§03, §04).

The four modes, defined

  • In distribution (also "weakly", "in law"): Xₙ ⇝ X if Fₙ(t) → F(t) at every t where the limit cdf F is continuous. Reading: histograms of Xₙ come to look like the histogram of X. Only the distributions are compared; Xₙ and X need not even be defined on the same sample space.
  • In probability: Xₙ →ᵖ X if, for every ε > 0, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. Reading: at a single large n, a miss bigger than any fixed tolerance is improbable.
  • Almost surely (with probability 1): Xₙ →ᵃˢ X if P({ω : Xₙ(ω) → X(ω)}) = 1. Reading: for almost every outcome, the realised sequence of numbers converges in the calculus sense.
  • In quadratic mean (in L²): Xₙ →ᴸ² X if E(Xₙ − X)² → 0; more generally in Lʳ if E|Xₙ − X|ʳ → 0. Reading: the average squared miss vanishes, which charges big misses heavily even when they are rare.
  • Constants. When the limit is a constant c (a random variable with P(X = c) = 1) the same definitions apply and we write Xₙ →ᵖ c, Xₙ ⇝ c, and so on. The laws of large numbers are statements of this kind.
the four modes of convergencein distribution: Xₙ ⇝ X ⟺ Fₙ(t) → F(t) at every continuity point t of F in probability: Xₙ →ᵖ X ⟺ P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0 almost surely: Xₙ →ᵃˢ X ⟺ P(Xₙ → X) = 1 ⟺ P(|Xₘ − X| > ε for some m ≥ n) → 0 for every ε > 0 in quadratic mean: Xₙ →ᴸ² X ⟺ E(Xₙ − X)² → 0 (in Lʳ: E|Xₙ − X|ʳ → 0)

Reading the definitions

  • Why only continuity points. Let Xₙ = 1/n, a constant. Surely Xₙ converges to 0. But Fₙ(0) = P(1/n ≤ 0) = 0 for every n, while the limit cdf has F(0) = 1. The cdfs converge everywhere except at the jump of F, and a definition that demanded convergence there would reject the most obvious example there is. The same thing happened with N(0, 1/n) above, where Fₙ(0) = ½ forever.
  • Three equivalent forms of convergence in distribution. Xₙ ⇝ X if and only if E g(Xₙ) → E g(X) for every bounded continuous function g; and (Lévy's continuity theorem) if and only if the characteristic functions φₙ(t) = E e^(itXₙ) converge to φ(t) for every t. When the moment generating functions exist on an interval around 0, their convergence there is also enough. That last form is the engine of the proof of the central limit theorem in §05, using the moment generating function.
  • What each mode looks at. Convergence in distribution looks only at the marginal distribution of each Xₙ. Convergence in probability and in L² look at the joint distribution of each pair (Xₙ, X), because |Xₙ − X| must be computed. Almost-sure convergence looks at the joint distribution of the whole sequence, because whether Xₙ(ω) converges depends on all the terms at once. The stronger the mode, the more of the joint structure it needs.
  • Same marginals, different verdicts. Let U ~ U(0, 1) and set Xₙ = 1 if U < 1/n, else 0: nested blips. Separately, let Y₁, Y₂, … be independent with P(Yₙ = 1) = 1/n. Each Xₙ has exactly the same distribution as Yₙ, Bernoulli(1/n). Yet Xₙ(ω) → 0 for every ω with U(ω) > 0 (once 1/n < U, every later term is 0), while Yₙ = 1 infinitely often with probability 1 (§04 proves it), so Yₙ does not converge almost surely. Both converge to 0 in probability, in L² and in distribution. Almost-sure convergence is a property of how the terms are coupled, not of their distributions.
  • The almost-sure tail form. The last equivalence in the box says: Xₙ →ᵃˢ X exactly when P(sup_(m≥n) |Xₘ − X| > ε) → 0. Compare it with convergence in probability, P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0. Both are probabilities that go to 0, but the almost-sure one asks about every later term at once, the in-probability one about a single term. That one word, "every", is the difference between the strong and the weak law of large numbers.

Worked: the shrinking normal in all four modes

  • In distribution. Xₙ ~ N(0, 1/n) has Fₙ(t) = Φ(t√n), which tends to 0 for t < 0 and to 1 for t > 0: the cdf of the constant 0 at every continuity point. So Xₙ ⇝ 0.
  • In probability. P(|Xₙ| > ε) = 2(1 − Φ(ε√n)). With ε = 0.1: 0.3173 at n = 100, 0.0455 at n = 400, tending to 0. So Xₙ →ᵖ 0.
  • In quadratic mean. E Xₙ² = Var Xₙ = 1/n → 0, so Xₙ →ᴸ² 0, and Chebyshev's bound P(|Xₙ| > ε) ≤ 1/(nε²) follows from it.
  • Almost surely. The question cannot be answered from the distributions. If Xₙ = Z/√n for a single Z ~ N(0, 1), every path is a deterministic sequence z/√n → 0. If the Xₙ are independent, it is still true, because Σ P(|Xₙ| > ε) is finite (normal tails are tiny), and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma of §04 turns a finite sum into "only finitely many misses".

A limit theorem is incomplete until its mode is named. "The sample mean converges to the population mean" is two theorems: the weak law (in probability) and the strong law (almost surely). "Sums are approximately normal" is a statement in distribution, and nothing more: it says how probabilities behave, not that any particular sum is close to any particular normal variable. Every statement on this page carries its mode, and §02 shows exactly what each one does and does not imply.

11 Why simulation works

Every Monte Carlo computation, from a single simulated probability to a bootstrap to the figures on this page, estimates a number θ = E h(X) by an average θ̂_N = (1/N) Σ h(Xᵢ) of simulated draws. Two theorems are silently invoked each time. The strong law says that the estimate converges to the right answer along the one stream of random numbers the computer produces. The central limit theorem, with Slutsky, says how far off it is: about s_h/√N, whatever the dimension of the problem. When h(X) has infinite variance, the first still holds and the second fails, and the reported error bar becomes fiction.

Consistency from the LLN, error bars from the CLT

  • Consistency. If E|h(X)| < ∞, the strong law (§04) gives θ̂_N →ᵃˢ θ. A computer runs one simulation, producing one infinite stream; "almost surely" is exactly the guarantee that this stream, not just a typical one, leads to the answer.
  • Error bars. If also σ_h² = Var h(X) < ∞, the CLT gives √N(θ̂_N − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ_h²), and replacing σ_h by the sample standard deviation s_h of the simulated values is legal by Slutsky (§08). So θ̂_N ± 1.96·s_h/√N is an approximate 95% interval for θ, computed from the simulation itself.
  • The 1/√N law and its two faces. The error does not depend on the dimension of X: integrating over 1,000 variables costs no more draws than integrating over one, for the same σ_h. That is why simulation beats grids in high dimensions. But each extra decimal digit costs 100 times the draws, which is why reducing σ_h (variance reduction) matters more than raw speed.
  • Worked: π by darts. Throw N uniform points into the unit square and let h = 4·1{U₁² + U₂² ≤ 1}, so θ = 4·(π/4) = π. Here σ_h = 4√(p(1 − p)) with p = π/4, which is 1.642. For a 95% error of ±0.005 (two correct decimals, reliably), N = (1.96 × 1.642/0.005)² ≈ 414,000 darts. For three decimals, 41 million.

When the variance is infinite

  • An innocent-looking integral. θ = ∫₀¹ x^(−0.7) dx = 1/0.3 = 3.333. The obvious estimator averages h(U) = U^(−0.7) over uniform draws. Its mean is finite, so the strong law holds and the estimate converges. But E h² = ∫₀¹ x^(−1.4) dx = ∞.
  • What goes wrong. P(h(U) > t) = P(U < t^(−1/0.7)) = t^(−1.43): h(U) is a Pareto variable with tail index α = 1/0.7 = 1.43. By §05 the error shrinks like N^(1/α − 1) = N^(−0.3), not N^(−1/2): a hundred times more draws cut the error by a factor of 4, not 10. Worse, s_h does not settle down: it grows with N in jumps, and the interval θ̂ ± 1.96 s_h/√N has no guaranteed coverage. Typically the estimate sits below 3.333 with an error bar too small to reach it, until a rare tiny U produces a huge h and the estimate and its error bar both jump.
  • The fix is mathematical, not computational. Substituting x = u^(1/0.3) turns the integral into ∫₀¹ (1/0.3) du, an estimator with zero variance. In general, choosing where to sample (importance sampling) so that h·f/g has a finite variance restores the CLT. Checking that the variance of what you average is finite is the first diagnostic of any simulation.

Beyond independent draws

  • Dependent draws. Markov chain Monte Carlo produces a dependent sequence. The law of large numbers is replaced by the ergodic theorem (time averages converge to the stationary expectation), and the CLT by a Markov-chain CLT whose variance is σ_h²(1 + 2Σₖ ρₖ), inflated by the chain's autocorrelations ρₖ. Using the iid error bar s_h/√N for correlated draws understates the error, by the same mechanism as averaging correlated variables (correlation).
  • Resampling is simulation too. The bootstrap is a Monte Carlo approximation to an integral over the empirical distribution, and the number of resamples B needed for a given precision follows the same 1/√B law (the Monte Carlo method).
  • Where this continues. Variance reduction (antithetic and control variates, importance sampling, stratification), the samplers that produce the draws, and the ergodic theorem and Markov-chain CLT that replace the iid laws when the draws come from a Markov chain: Simulation & Stochastic Processes.
Every simulated number is a sample mean. Report it with its error bar s_h/√N, check that the error bar shrinks like 1/√N when N is quadrupled (if it does not, suspect an infinite variance or dependent draws), and remember that the error bar is itself an estimate justified only by Slutsky's theorem.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Machine learning estimates and minimises expectations from averages. A test score is a sample mean, a training loss is a sample mean, a minibatch gradient is a sample mean, and a fitted model's parameters are asymptotically normal for the same reasons a sample mean is. The limit theorems decide when these averages can be trusted, how wide their error bars are, and where finite-sample guarantees must replace asymptotic ones.

Test-set scores: asymptotic error bars versus finite-sample bounds

  • The score is an average. For a fixed model and n independent test cases, the accuracy is the mean of n indicators with mean a, the true accuracy. The CLT gives SE = √(a(1 − a)/n): at a = 0.85 and n = 1,000, SE = 0.0113 and a 95% interval of ±0.022. The applied translation, including paired comparisons of two models on the same test set, is in a validation score is a statistic.
  • Hoeffding instead. Hoeffding's inequality guarantees P(|â − a| ≥ ε) ≤ 2e^(−2nε²) for every n and every distribution of a loss in [0, 1]. Setting the right side to 0.05 gives ε = √(ln(2/0.05)/(2n)) = 0.043 at n = 1,000: about twice the CLT half-width.
  • Which to use. The CLT interval is an approximation whose error is O(1/√n) per tail (§06); Hoeffding's is a guarantee that pays for its validity with width. For a single pre-registered model, the CLT is standard practice. For guarantees that must hold uniformly over many models, a union bound over Hoeffding is the route (concentration in action: the generalisation guarantee), because the maximum over models is an extreme-value problem (§10), not a CLT one.
  • Correlated test cases. Examples from the same user, document or patient are not independent, and the effective number of test cases is smaller than n: the weak law's variance σ²/n acquires covariance terms (§03).
test accuracy 0.85 on n = 1,000 cases, 95%CLT: ±1.96·√(0.85 × 0.15/1000) = ±0.022 Hoeffding: ±√(ln 40/2000) = ±0.043 both shrink like 1/√n

Training: empirical risk and stochastic gradients

  • Empirical risk. For a fixed model, the average training loss converges to the expected loss by the strong law. For the model chosen to minimise the training loss, that is not enough: the minimiser depends on the same data, and convergence must hold uniformly over the model class (a uniform law of large numbers, the content of generalisation bounds). The gap between the two is the reason training loss is an optimistic estimate of test loss.
  • Minibatch gradients. A minibatch gradient ĝ = (1/B) Σ ∇ℓ(θ; xᵢ) is an unbiased average of B per-example gradients, with covariance Σ_g/B: the LLN says larger batches give less noisy directions, at the 1/√B rate. That is intuition, not a convergence proof for training. The parameter θ moves as it is updated, so the iterates form a dependent sequence; their convergence is the subject of stochastic approximation (with step sizes satisfying Σ ηₜ = ∞ and Σ ηₜ² < ∞, the Robbins–Monro conditions, plus convexity or other structure), and averaged iterates obey their own CLT. None of that is proved here.

Asymptotic normality of fitted parameters

  • The maximum likelihood estimator. Under regularity conditions (smooth log-likelihood, true parameter in the interior, identifiability), √n(θ̂ − θ) ⇝ N(0, I(θ)⁻¹), with I the Fisher information per observation (maximum likelihood, Fisher information). The proof is this page in miniature: expand the score equation 0 = Sₙ(θ̂) ≈ Sₙ(θ) + Hₙ(θ)(θ̂ − θ); the score Sₙ(θ)/√n is a normalised sum of iid mean-zero terms with variance I (CLT); the Hessian −Hₙ(θ)/n is an average converging to I (LLN); Slutsky combines them into √n(θ̂ − θ) ≈ I⁻¹·Sₙ(θ)/√n ⇝ N(0, I⁻¹). When the model is misspecified, the two averages converge to different matrices A and B, and the limit is the "sandwich" N(0, A⁻¹BA⁻¹).
  • Standard errors for any smooth summary. A prediction, an odds ratio, an elasticity: a differentiable function of θ̂, whose standard error is the multivariate delta method of §07 with Σ = I⁻¹/n.

The bootstrap, and calibrated intervals

  • Why the bootstrap works. It replaces the unknown F by the empirical F̂ₙ, which converges uniformly by Glivenko–Cantelli (§04), and recomputes the statistic on resamples. For averages and smooth functions of averages, the CLT and the delta method hold at F̂ₙ just as at F, so the bootstrap distribution of √n(θ̂* − θ̂) has the same normal limit as that of √n(θ̂ − θ). It fails exactly where those theorems fail: for the maximum (§10), for heavy tails (§05), and for statistics that are not smooth at F (where the bootstrap fails).
  • Calibration of confidence intervals. An interval with nominal level 95% is calibrated if it covers the truth 95% of the time. Large-sample intervals are calibrated only in the limit. By §06, a symmetric two-sided interval built on the normal approximation has coverage error O(1/n), but each one-sided bound is off by O(1/√n), driven by the skewness. Refined intervals (the bootstrap-t and BCa) remove the 1/√n term (two better intervals), and simulation can measure actual coverage directly (what the intervals actually cover).
  • Calibration of predicted probabilities. A reliability diagram groups predictions into bins and compares the mean predicted probability with the observed frequency in each bin: a law of large numbers per bin, with a binomial standard error √(p(1 − p)/n_b) that is large for sparsely populated bins. Apparent miscalibration in a bin with 20 cases is mostly noise.
