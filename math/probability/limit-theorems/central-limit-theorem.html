05 The central limit theorem

The laws of large numbers say that X̄ₙ − μ goes to zero. The central limit theorem says how: magnified by √n, the error has a limiting normal distribution, whatever the distribution of the terms, provided their variance is finite. This section states it, proves it by cumulants (every cumulant beyond the second dies at a known rate, which is the promise made in the expectation chapter), reads it as repeated convolution, measures its speed with the Berry–Esseen bound, and shows what happens instead when the variance is infinite (stable laws) or the terms are not identically distributed (Lindeberg–Feller).

The statement

  • Theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy). Let X₁, X₂, … be iid with mean μ and variance 0 < σ² < ∞, and Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ. Then
    Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) = √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ ⇝ N(0, 1), that is, P(Zₙ ≤ z) → Φ(z) for every real z.
  • Why √n. Var(Sₙ − nμ) = nσ², so dividing by σ√n is the unique scaling that keeps the variance at 1 for every n. Divide by less and the spread blows up; divide by more (say by n, giving X̄ₙ − μ) and the distribution collapses to the point 0, which is the law of large numbers. The CLT describes the fluctuations that the law of large numbers averages away, at the one magnification where they neither vanish nor explode.
  • The mode. Convergence in distribution: probabilities of events about Zₙ converge to normal probabilities. Since Φ is continuous, the convergence is automatically uniform in z (Pólya's theorem): sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| → 0. That uniform distance is the quantity the Berry–Esseen bound below controls.
  • The applied view. The five equivalent ways of writing the result, the hypotheses one at a time, and the things it is often misquoted as saying, are in the central limit theorem of the statistics series. This section is about why it is true and how fast.

Delivered: the proof by cumulants

  • Cumulants were introduced with a promise: the skewness of a sum of n iid terms falls like 1/√n and its excess kurtosis like 1/n, which is "the quantitative heart of the central limit theorem". The closing list of Expectation, Variance & Inequalities asked for that cumulant argument to be made rigorous, and the normal distribution asked for the theorem "with a proof sketch and its rate". Here is that heart, made into a proof; the rate is the Berry–Esseen bound below. Assume first that X has a moment generating function on an interval around 0 (the general case follows).
  • Standardise the terms. Let Yᵢ = (Xᵢ − μ)/σ, iid with mean 0 and variance 1, and cumulant generating function K(s) = ln E e^(sY) = s²/2 + κ₃s³/3! + κ₄s⁴/4! + ⋯, where κ₃ = γ₁ is the skewness of X and κ₄ = γ₂ its excess kurtosis (κ₁ = 0, κ₂ = 1 by the standardisation).
  • Two rules for cumulants. Cumulants of independent terms add (mgfs multiply, so their logarithms add), and scaling by c multiplies the j-th cumulant by cʲ. Since Zₙ = (Y₁ + ⋯ + Yₙ)/√n,
    κⱼ(Zₙ) = n · κⱼ / (√n)ʲ = κⱼ / n^((j−2)/2): κ₁ = 0, κ₂ = 1, κ₃(Zₙ) = γ₁/√n, κ₄(Zₙ) = γ₂/n, κ₅(Zₙ) = κ₅/n^(3/2), …
    Every cumulant of order 3 and higher vanishes, the j-th like n^(−(j−2)/2). The normal is the distribution whose cumulants beyond the second are all zero, so this is already the theorem, read cumulant by cumulant.
  • Making it a proof. The cumulant generating function of Zₙ is Kₙ(t) = n K(t/√n). Near 0, K(s) = s²/2 + R(s) with |R(s)| ≤ C|s|³ (Taylor's theorem; K is smooth where the mgf exists). So for fixed t and large n,
    Kₙ(t) = n[t²/(2n) + R(t/√n)] = t²/2 + n·R(t/√n), |n·R(t/√n)| ≤ C|t|³/√n → 0
    and E e^(tZₙ) → e^(t²/2), the mgf of N(0, 1), for every t near 0. Convergence of mgfs on an interval around 0 implies convergence in distribution (§01), so Zₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). ∎
  • Only a finite variance. Many distributions with a finite variance have no mgf (a t distribution with 3 degrees of freedom, a Pareto with α = 3). The repair is to use the characteristic function φ(t) = E e^(itY), which always exists. Two moments are exactly enough for φ(t) = 1 − t²/2 + o(t²) as t → 0, and then E e^(itZₙ) = φ(t/√n)ⁿ = (1 − t²/(2n) + o(1/n))ⁿ → e^(−t²/2), the characteristic function of N(0, 1). Lévy's continuity theorem converts this into Zₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). That is the complete proof, modulo Lévy's theorem; the finite variance is used precisely once, to get the o(t²).
  • Worked. For Exp(1) terms, γ₁ = 2 and γ₂ = 6, so Z₂₅ has skewness 0.4 and excess kurtosis 0.24, and Z₁₀₀ has 0.2 and 0.06. These are exact: Sₙ ~ Gamma(n, 1), whose skewness is 2/√n and excess kurtosis 6/n (the gamma distribution). For U(0, 1) terms, γ₁ = 0 and γ₂ = −1.2, so the sum of 12 uniforms minus 6 has mean 0, variance 1, skewness 0 and excess kurtosis −0.1: close enough to normal that it was once a standard way to generate normal draws.

Delivered: the convolution view, and why the normal

  • Sums are repeated convolutions. The density of Sₙ is the n-fold convolution f * f * ⋯ * f (sums of random variables: convolution, which asked why repeated convolutions become normal). Each convolution averages the density against a shifted copy of itself, which smooths it: two uniforms make a triangle, three make a piecewise quadratic, and the corners vanish one derivative at a time. The CLT says that, after recentring and rescaling, the shape this smoothing converges to does not depend on the shape it started from.
  • Why the normal in particular: a fixed point. Suppose some distribution with variance 1 were a limit of standardised sums. Then the standardised sum of two independent copies, (Y + Y′)/√2, must have the same distribution as Y (a limit of standardised sums of 2n terms is also the limit of standardised sums of n terms, taken in pairs). By the two cumulant rules, κⱼ((Y + Y′)/√2) = 2κⱼ/2^(j/2), so κⱼ(1 − 2^(1−j/2)) = 0, and for j ≥ 3 the bracket is not zero: κⱼ = 0. The only distribution with every cumulant beyond the second equal to zero is the normal. The normal is the unique finite-variance fixed point of "add two independent copies and rescale".
  • Information is lost, not created. Each convolution keeps only the mean and variance exactly (they add) and shrinks every standardised higher cumulant by a factor that tends to zero. The normal is what is left when everything except the mean and the variance has been averaged away. That is also why the normal has the largest entropy for a given variance (the normal distribution): it carries no other information.

What convergence in distribution buys, and what it costs

  • It buys interval probabilities, uniformly. P(a < Zₙ ≤ b) → Φ(b) − Φ(a) for every interval at once, which is all that a confidence interval or a test at a fixed level needs.
  • It does not buy relative accuracy in the tails. An absolute error of 10⁻³ in the cdf is negligible at z = 1 and catastrophic at z = 5, where 1 − Φ(5) = 2.9 × 10⁻⁷. Probabilities of large deviations, where X̄ₙ − μ is of fixed size rather than of size 1/√n, decay exponentially at a rate set by the whole mgf, not just the variance: that is the territory of the Chernoff bounds (Chernoff & Hoeffding), not of the CLT.
  • It does not buy densities. A binomial count has no density at all; it converges in distribution to a normal, but its pmf is a comb. A separate local limit theorem says P(Sₙ = k) ≈ φ((k − nμ)/(σ√n))/(σ√n) for integer-valued terms whose values are not confined to a sub-lattice; it is a different theorem with extra hypotheses, and it is what justifies the continuity correction (§06).
  • It does not say Zₙ is near any particular normal variable. As the flip of §02 showed, convergence in distribution is about histograms. Two independent replications of an experiment each have an approximately normal Zₙ; they are not approximately equal.
  • The vector version. For iid random vectors with mean μ and covariance matrix Σ, √n(X̄ₙ − μ) ⇝ N(0, Σ) (the multivariate normal). The proof reduces to the scalar case: a random vector converges in distribution exactly when every linear combination aᵀX̄ₙ does (the Cramér–Wold device), and each aᵀX̄ₙ is a scalar average with variance aᵀΣa.

How fast: the Berry–Esseen bound

  • Statement. If, in addition, ρ = E|X − μ|³ < ∞, then for every n
    sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C · ρ / (σ³ √n)
    with an absolute constant C that works for every distribution and every n. The rate is 1/√n, and the distribution enters only through the standardised third absolute moment ρ/σ³, a measure of lopsidedness close to the skewness.
  • The constant, honestly. The best constant proved for iid terms is below 0.47 (C ≤ 0.4690). It cannot be pushed below (√10 + 3)/(6√(2π)) = 0.4097, Esseen's lower bound, which a particular two-point distribution attains in the limit. Recent work indicates that 0.4097 itself suffices once n exceeds a universal but astronomically large threshold; for every practical n, the usable constant remains 0.4690.
  • Worked: exponential terms. For Exp(1), σ = 1 and ρ = E|X − 1|³ = ∫₀¹(1 − x)³e^(−x) dx + ∫₁^∞(x − 1)³e^(−x) dx = (6/e − 2) + 6/e = 12/e − 2 = 2.4146. At n = 100 the bound is 0.4690 × 2.4146/10 = 0.1132. The true distance, computed exactly from the Gamma(100, 1) cdf, is 0.0133: the bound is about 8.5 times too pessimistic, because it must also cover distributions far worse than the exponential.
  • Worked: a fair coin, where the bound is nearly tight. For Bernoulli(½), |X − ½| = ½ always, so ρ = σ³ = 1/8 and the bound is 0.4690/√n: 0.0469 at n = 100. The true distance is 0.0398. A lattice distribution cannot be approximated by a smooth curve to better than half its largest jump, and P(S₁₀₀ = 50) = 0.0796: discreteness, not skewness, is the binding constraint here, and it is why the worst cases for the constant are two-point distributions.
  • The bound versus the truth. Berry–Esseen is a guarantee: convergence is never slower than 1/√n when ρ is finite. It is a poor estimate of the actual error for continuous distributions. The leading Edgeworth term (§06) estimates it accurately, and the sample-size rule built on it, with a catalogue of real skewnesses, is in how fast? the rate of convergence.

When the variance is infinite: stable laws

  • The generalised central limit theorem. If the tails of X decay like a power, P(|X| > x) ≈ c·x^(−α) with 0 < α < 2, the variance is infinite and the CLT fails, but sums still have a limit shape: (Sₙ − bₙ)/n^(1/α) converges in distribution to an α-stable law. Stable laws are exactly the fixed points of "add independent copies and rescale" once the finite-variance requirement is dropped: for a symmetric α-stable X, X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ has the same distribution as n^(1/α)X. The normal is the case α = 2.
  • The Cauchy is the α = 1 stable law. Then n^(1/α) = n and X̄ₙ = Sₙ/n has the distribution of a single term, the result §04 proved with characteristic functions. More generally the average of symmetric α-stable terms is distributed as n^(1/α − 1) times one term: it shrinks when α > 1, stays put at α = 1, and grows when α < 1. See heavy tails for the Pareto and the Cauchy as distributions.
  • Between 1 and 2: a mean, but a slow one. For a Pareto with 1 < α < 2, the strong law holds (E X < ∞), but the typical error of X̄ₙ shrinks like n^(1/α − 1), not n^(−1/2): at α = 1.5, like n^(−1/3). Quadrupling the sample cuts the error by 4^(1/3) = 1.59, not 2. The limiting shape is a stable law skewed toward the heavy tail, so intervals of the form x̄ ± 2s/√n are both too narrow and centred in the wrong place.
  • Exactly at α = 2. A Pareto with α = 2 has an infinite variance by the narrowest of margins: E[X² 1{X ≤ x}] grows like 2 ln x. The limit is still normal, but the scaling picks up a logarithm: (Sₙ − nμ)/√(n ln n) ⇝ N(0, 1). The boundary between the normal and the stable world is the boundary between a truncated variance that converges or grows slowly enough, and one that grows like a power.

Independent but not identical: Lindeberg–Feller

  • Triangular arrays. In many applications the terms of the sum change with n: the n-th sum is Xₙ,₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ,ₖₙ, a row of a "triangular array" of independent terms with means 0 and variances summing to 1. A binomial with p depending on n, the count of successes among trials with different probabilities, and the proof sketch for sample quantiles in §09 are all of this form.
  • The Lindeberg condition. For every ε > 0, Σᵢ E[Xₙ,ᵢ² · 1{|Xₙ,ᵢ| > ε}] → 0: the share of the total variance carried by terms that are individually large must vanish. Under it, Σᵢ Xₙ,ᵢ ⇝ N(0, 1) (Lindeberg); and if no single term's variance is a non-vanishing share of the total, the condition is also necessary (Feller). No proof here; the content is "a democracy of many small contributions", and the applied page catalogues what happens when one term dominates (where the CLT fails outright).
  • A checkable version (Lyapunov). If Σᵢ E|Xₙ,ᵢ|³ → 0 (after the same standardisation), Lindeberg's condition holds. Worked: let Bᵢ ~ Bernoulli(pᵢ) be independent, with total variance sₙ² = Σ pᵢ(1 − pᵢ). Since |Bᵢ − pᵢ| ≤ 1, Σ E|Bᵢ − pᵢ|³ ≤ Σ pᵢ(1 − pᵢ) = sₙ², and after dividing by sₙ³ Lyapunov's ratio is at most 1/sₙ. So the number of successes is asymptotically normal whenever sₙ → ∞, whatever the pᵢ are. With pᵢ = 1/i (the indicators that the i-th of a sequence of iid continuous draws is a new record, §10), sₙ² ≈ ln n → ∞: the number of records in n draws is asymptotically normal with mean and variance about ln n, though the convergence is glacial. If instead the pᵢ in each row are all small and their total stays near a fixed λ, the limit is Poisson(λ), not normal (the Poisson approximation).

The hypotheses, each doing a job. Independence kills the covariance terms, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances. A finite variance gives the o(t²) in the characteristic function. Identical distribution is a convenience, replaceable by Lindeberg's "no term dominates". Remove the finite variance and the limit becomes a stable law with a different scaling; remove independence and the variance of the sum acquires covariance terms that can stop it from growing at all (averaging correlated variables).

06 Normal approximations, revisited as theory

The binomial, Poisson and gamma distributions all become approximately normal for the same reason: each is a sum of many independent pieces. The CLT says the approximation becomes exact in the limit; this section says what the error is at a finite n and where it comes from. For a continuous distribution the leading error is the skewness term of the Edgeworth expansion, of size 1/√n; for a count there is a second error of the same size from the jumps, and the continuity correction is the device that removes it.

Delivered: why the binomial, Poisson and gamma go normal

  • The binomial and both roads lead to the normal curve promised the theorem behind the bell shape, with a proof sketch and its rate. The theorem is §05's. Bin(n, p) is a sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) terms, so (X − np)/√(np(1 − p)) ⇝ N(0, 1): the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, the first CLT ever proved. Poisson(μ) with integer μ is a sum of μ independent Poisson(1) counts (Poisson counts add), so (X − μ)/√μ ⇝ N(0, 1) as μ → ∞, and monotonicity in μ covers non-integer means. Gamma(α, λ) with integer α is a sum of α exponentials, and (X − α/λ)/(√α/λ) ⇝ N(0, 1) as α → ∞.
  • The rate. By Berry–Esseen, the cdf error is at most 0.469·ρ/(σ³√n), and by the cumulant computation the skewness of the standardised variable is γ₁/√n: (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)) for the binomial, 1/√μ for the Poisson, 2/√α for the gamma. The normal curve takes over at the rate the skewness dies, which is why np(1 − p) (not n) is the quantity in every rule of thumb, and why p near ½ helps so much.

Where the error comes from: the Edgeworth expansion

  • Keep one more cumulant. In §05's proof, Kₙ(t) = t²/2 + γ₁t³/(6√n) + O(1/n). Exponentiate and keep the first-order term: E e^(tZₙ) ≈ e^(t²/2)(1 + γ₁t³/(6√n)).
  • Which density has that mgf? Integration by parts three times gives ∫ e^(tx)·(−φ‴(x)) dx = t³e^(t²/2), and −φ‴(x) = (x³ − 3x)φ(x). So the density of Zₙ is approximately φ(x)[1 + γ₁(x³ − 3x)/(6√n)]: the normal, tilted by a cubic that pushes mass to the side of the long tail. Integrating, since the derivative of −(x² − 1)φ(x) is (x³ − 3x)φ(x):
    P(Zₙ ≤ x) = Φ(x) − φ(x) · γ₁ (x² − 1) / (6√n) + O(1/n)
  • Reading it. The leading error is proportional to the skewness and to 1/√n, and its largest size, at x = 0, is |γ₁|φ(0)/(6√n) = 0.0665·|γ₁|/√n. It vanishes at x = ±1: the tilt moves mass around, but the net amount it moves below those two points is zero. For a symmetric distribution γ₁ = 0, the term vanishes, and the error is O(1/n): a whole order faster.
  • Delivered: the 0.524 of the gamma convolution. Gamma times add found P(Gamma(30, ½) ≤ 60) = 0.524, the probability that the 30th arrival of a rate-½ process comes by its mean time. The normal approximation says 0.5 exactly. The Edgeworth correction at x = 0, with γ₁ = 2/√30 = 0.3651, is +φ(0)·γ₁/6 = 0.0243, giving 0.5243; the exact value is 0.52428. The whole discrepancy is the skewness term.
  • Two-sided intervals are better than one-sided ones. The correction φ(x)(x² − 1) is an even function of x, so it cancels in P(−z < Zₙ ≤ z) = Fₙ(z) − Fₙ(−z). A symmetric interval's coverage error is O(1/n), while each one-sided tail is off by O(1/√n), in opposite directions. Symmetric confidence intervals look accurate partly because their two tail errors offset.

The continuity correction, as a theorem

  • The second error. For an integer-valued Sₙ, the cdf is a staircase with jumps of size about φ(x)/(σ√n), and no smooth curve can be closer to a staircase than half a step. So a lattice distribution has a second 1/√n error term, a sawtooth of amplitude φ(x)/(2σ√n), on top of the skewness term.
  • Where the half comes from. By the local limit theorem (§05), P(Sₙ = j) ≈ φ((j − nμ)/(σ√n))/(σ√n), which is the midpoint-rule approximation to the normal area over [j − ½, j + ½]. Summing over j ≤ k gives the area up to k + ½: P(Sₙ ≤ k) ≈ Φ((k + ½ − nμ)/(σ√n)). Since {Sₙ ≤ k} = {Sₙ < k + 1}, any point in [k, k + 1) is "correct" for the staircase; the midpoint is the one where the sawtooth error crosses zero, and evaluating there removes the lattice term to leading order, leaving the skewness term.
  • Worked: a symmetric case. Bin(100, ½): P(S ≤ 55) = 0.86437 exactly. Without the correction, Φ(1) = 0.84134, off by 0.0230; the sawtooth predicts φ(1)·½/5 = 0.0242. With it, Φ(1.1) = 0.86433, off by 4 × 10⁻⁵: symmetry removed the skewness term, the correction removed the lattice term, and only O(1/n) is left.
  • Worked: a skewed case. Bin(100, 0.1), mean 10, σ = 3, γ₁ = 0.8/3 = 0.267: P(S ≤ 5) = 0.0576 exactly. The corrected normal gives Φ(−1.5) = 0.0668, still 16% too large. Adding the Edgeworth term at x = −1.5, −φ(1.5)·0.267·1.25/6 = −0.0072, gives 0.0596. The correction handles the jumps; only the skewness term handles the skew.
  • Worked: the Poisson. Poisson(100): P(X ≤ 110) = 0.85286. Without the correction Φ(1.0) = 0.84134; with it Φ(1.05) = 0.85314 (the values quoted in the Poisson section); adding the Edgeworth term with γ₁ = 0.1 gives 0.85275, cutting the remaining error from 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ to 1.1 × 10⁻⁴.
