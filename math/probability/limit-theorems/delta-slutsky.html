07 The delta method

The CLT is about averages, but the quantities people report are usually functions of averages: a log of a mean, an odds ratio, a ratio of two means, a correlation, a rate 1/x̄. The delta method says that a smooth function of an asymptotically normal estimate is itself asymptotically normal, with its standard deviation multiplied by the slope |g′| at the true value. It is a first-order Taylor expansion with a convergence theorem attached, and it is also the tool for designing variance-stabilising transforms, functions chosen so that the standard error stops depending on the unknown parameter.

Statement and proof

  • Theorem. Suppose √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ²), and g is differentiable at θ with g′(θ) ≠ 0. Then
    √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) ⇝ N(0, g′(θ)² σ²), in working form g(Yₙ) ≈ N(g(θ), g′(θ)²σ²/n).
    The conditions are mild: differentiability is needed only at the single point θ, and Yₙ need not be an average, only asymptotically normal at rate √n. (With g′(θ) = 0 the statement remains true but says only that the limit is the point 0; the second-order version below takes over.)
  • Why it is true: zoom in until g is a line. Yₙ concentrates in a window of width about σ/√n around θ. Over a window that narrow, a differentiable g is indistinguishable from its tangent line g(θ) + g′(θ)(y − θ), and a linear function of an approximately normal variable is approximately normal, with its standard deviation multiplied by the slope.
  • Proof. Differentiability at θ means g(y) − g(θ) = [g′(θ) + r(y)](y − θ) with r(y) → 0 as y → θ (set r(θ) = 0). Then
    √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) = g′(θ)·√n(Yₙ − θ) + r(Yₙ)·√n(Yₙ − θ).
    The first term converges in distribution to g′(θ)·N(0, σ²). For the second: Yₙ − θ = n^(−1/2)·√n(Yₙ − θ), a vanishing constant times a variable with a limit distribution, so Yₙ →ᵖ θ (Slutsky, §08); r is continuous at θ, so r(Yₙ) →ᵖ 0 (continuous mapping, §02); and a variable converging in probability to 0, times one converging in distribution, converges to 0 (Slutsky again). Adding a term that tends to 0 in probability does not change the limit (Slutsky a third time). ∎
  • The multivariate version. If √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, Σ) for a vector Yₙ and g is differentiable at θ with gradient ∇g ≠ 0, then √n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) ⇝ N(0, ∇gᵀΣ∇g). The proof is the same with a gradient in place of the derivative, and the multivariate CLT of §05 supplies the hypothesis whenever Yₙ is a vector of averages.

Worked: three functions of means

  • The log of a mean. Waiting times are exponential with unknown mean θ, so Var X = θ² and X̄ ≈ N(θ, θ²/n). With g = ln, g′(θ) = 1/θ, and ln X̄ ≈ N(ln θ, 1/n): the standard error of ln X̄ is 1/√n, whatever θ is. At n = 50 that is 0.1414. Here the exact answer is available, because nX̄/θ ~ Gamma(n, 1) and the variance of the log of a gamma is the trigamma function: the exact standard deviation is √ψ′(50) = 0.1421. The log has turned a standard error proportional to the unknown mean into a constant, which is the idea of variance stabilisation below.
  • Odds and log-odds. A proportion p̂ from n trials has SE = √(p(1 − p)/n). The odds g(p) = p/(1 − p) have g′(p) = 1/(1 − p)², so SE(odds) ≈ √(p(1 − p)/n)/(1 − p)²; the log-odds g(p) = ln(p/(1 − p)) have g′(p) = 1/(p(1 − p)), so SE(log-odds) ≈ 1/√(np(1 − p)). At p = 0.2, n = 400: SE(p̂) = 0.02, SE(odds) ≈ 0.0313, SE(log-odds) ≈ 0.125. A simulation of 400,000 replications gives 0.0314 and 0.126. Logistic-regression coefficients are on the log-odds scale, and their standard errors are this calculation in many dimensions.
  • A ratio of means. Clicks per session, cost per unit, revenue per user: R̂ = X̄/Ȳ estimates R = μ_X/μ_Y from n paired observations. With g(x, y) = x/y, ∇g = (1/μ_Y, −μ_X/μ_Y²), and the multivariate delta method gives
    Var R̂ ≈ (R²/n)·(CV_X² − 2ρ·CV_X·CV_Y + CV_Y²), CV = σ/μ
    With μ_X = 2, μ_Y = 10, σ_X = 1, σ_Y = 4, ρ = 0.6 and n = 100: R = 0.2, CV_X = 0.5, CV_Y = 0.4, and SE(R̂) ≈ 0.2·√((0.25 − 0.24 + 0.16)/100) = 0.0082; a simulation gives 0.0083. The positive correlation between numerator and denominator reduces the variance (the −2ρ term): when both move together, their ratio moves less. Treating the denominator as fixed, as naive per-unit metrics do, would give 0.2 × 0.5/10 = 0.010, and treating them as independent would give 0.0128.

What the delta method does not promise

  • It is the variance of the limit, not the limit of the variances. If X̄ is exactly normal, 1/X̄ has no finite mean at any n, because the normal density is positive at 0 and ∫ |1/y| dy diverges there. Yet √n(1/X̄ − 1/μ) ⇝ N(0, σ²/μ⁴) for any μ ≠ 0. Both statements are true: the delta method describes where almost all the probability is, and says nothing about the vanishing sliver near 0 that ruins the moments (the growing spike of §02 in another form). In practice: a delta-method standard error is meaningful as the scale of the typical error, not as the square root of a mean squared error.
  • It needs g to be nearly linear over the spread of Yₙ. The approximation is first-order in σ/√n. When the window of width σ/√n is wide relative to the scale on which g bends (a log near 0, a reciprocal near 0, odds near p = 1), the exact distribution of g(Yₙ) is skewed and its centre is biased by about ½g″(θ)σ²/n.   • The second-order case. If g′(θ) = 0 but g″(θ) ≠ 0, the tangent is flat and the curvature takes over: g(Yₙ) − g(θ) ≈ ½g″(θ)(Yₙ − θ)², so
    n(g(Yₙ) − g(θ)) ⇝ ½ g″(θ) σ² · χ²₁
    with a faster scaling (n instead of √n) and a skewed, one-sided limit. Worked: the estimated variance p̂(1 − p̂) of a fair coin. Here g(p) = p(1 − p), g′(½) = 0, g″ = −2, σ² = ¼, so n(p̂(1 − p̂) − ¼) ⇝ −¼χ²₁: the estimate is always at most ¼, and its error has mean −1/(4n). A simulation at n = 400 gives a mean of −0.249 for n(p̂(1 − p̂) − ¼), and the limit's 5% point, −¼ × 3.841 = −0.960, is passed with frequency 0.051. A standard error computed by the first-order formula would be exactly 0, and wrong.

Variance-stabilising transforms

  • The problem. For many estimators the standard error depends on the very parameter being estimated: √(p(1 − p)/n) for a proportion, √(μ/n) for a Poisson mean, (1 − ρ²)/√n for a correlation. Intervals then need the unknown to compute their width, plug-in widths are worst where the estimate is least reliable, and comparisons across groups mix differences in level with differences in noise.
  • The recipe: run the delta method backwards. If Var Yₙ ≈ v(θ)/n, find g with g′(θ)²·v(θ) = constant, that is g′(θ) = 1/√v(θ) and
    g(θ) = ∫ dθ / √v(θ) ⟹ Var g(Yₙ) ≈ 1/n, whatever θ is.
  • Derived in full: the arcsine transform for proportions. Here v(p) = p(1 − p), so g(p) = ∫ dp/√(p(1 − p)). Substitute p = sin²u, dp = 2 sin u cos u du, and √(p(1 − p)) = sin u cos u: the integrand becomes 2 du, so g = 2u = 2 arcsin √p. Check by differentiating: d/dp [arcsin √p] = (1/√(1 − p))·(1/(2√p)), so g′(p) = 1/√(p(1 − p)), and g′(p)²·p(1 − p) = 1. Hence 2 arcsin √p̂ ≈ N(2 arcsin √p, 1/n). A simulation at n = 100 gives n·Var(2 arcsin √p̂) = 1.14, 1.02, 1.01 at p = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, against n·Var p̂ = 0.047, 0.160, 0.250: the raw variance changes fivefold, the transformed one by 13%, and that residue comes from the region near p = 0 where the transform is most curved.
  • The square root for Poisson counts. v(μ) = μ, so g(μ) = ∫ dμ/√μ = 2√μ, and 2√X̄ ≈ N(2√μ, 1/n); for a single count X ~ Poisson(μ), Var √X → ¼. Exactly: 0.306, 0.261, 0.252 at μ = 4, 10, 50. The refinement √(X + 3/8) (Anscombe) brings these to 0.2497, 0.2502, 0.2500, by cancelling the next term of the expansion. This is why count data are square-rooted before methods that assume constant variance.
  • Fisher's z for a correlation. For bivariate normal data, the sample correlation satisfies √n(r − ρ) ⇝ N(0, (1 − ρ²)²) (itself a multivariate delta method applied to five sample moments). So v(ρ) = (1 − ρ²)² and g(ρ) = ∫ dρ/(1 − ρ²) = ½ ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) = artanh ρ, with Var(artanh r) ≈ 1/n, refined to 1/(n − 3). At n = 30, simulated: the standard deviation of r is 0.186, 0.142, 0.038 at ρ = 0, 0.5, 0.9, while that of artanh r is 0.192, 0.192, 0.191, against 1/√27 = 0.192. The interval tanh(artanh r ± z*/√(n − 3)) in the correlation coefficient is built this way, and the transform is one of the standard ways to get intervals on a bounded parameter (building an estimator).

The delta-method lens: a normal through a curve, against its tangent g · μ · spread s = σ/√n
One derivative buys a standard error. The delta method is how a standard error reaches almost every reported statistic: log-transformed means, odds ratios, relative risks, ratios of averages, elasticities, any smooth function of a fitted model's parameters. Its fine print is that it is a first-order statement, valid when the estimate is precise relative to the curvature of g, and silent about moments. When the estimate is not that precise, transform first (to a scale where g is nearly linear and the variance is stable), or bootstrap (Resampling).

08 Slutsky's theorem and continuous mapping

The CLT standardises by the true σ, which nobody knows. Every practical interval and test replaces it with an estimate, and every delta-method argument needs to combine a variable that converges in distribution with one that converges in probability. Two short theorems license all of this. The continuous mapping theorem says continuous functions preserve convergence; Slutsky's theorem says that a variable converging in probability to a constant can be added to, multiplied by, or divided into a variable converging in distribution, as if it were the constant. Together with the LLN and the CLT they assemble the t statistic, the bridge from probability to inference.

The two theorems

  • Continuous mapping. If g is continuous (it is enough that the set where it is discontinuous has probability 0 under the limit) and Xₙ → X almost surely, in probability, or in distribution, then g(Xₙ) → g(X) in the same mode. For convergence in distribution the proof is one line with §01's equivalent form: for bounded continuous h, h ∘ g is bounded and continuous, so E h(g(Xₙ)) → E h(g(X)). For almost-sure convergence it is the calculus fact that continuous functions preserve limits, applied outcome by outcome.
  • Slutsky's theorem. If Xₙ ⇝ X and Yₙ →ᵖ c for a constant c, then
    Xₙ + Yₙ ⇝ X + c, Yₙ·Xₙ ⇝ c·X, Xₙ/Yₙ ⇝ X/c (if c ≠ 0).
  • Why the constant matters. The proof's key step is that (Xₙ, Yₙ) ⇝ (X, c) jointly. That is automatic when the second limit is a constant: whatever dependence there is between Xₙ and Yₙ cannot survive in the limit, because a constant is independent of everything. Then the continuous mapping theorem applied to (x, y) ↦ x + y, xy or x/y finishes it. For the sum there is also a direct sandwich, as in §02: P(Xₙ + Yₙ ≤ t) ≤ P(Xₙ ≤ t − c + ε) + P(|Yₙ − c| > ε) and the matching lower bound, then let n → ∞ and ε → 0.
  • Two corollaries used constantly. If Xₙ ⇝ X and aₙ → 0 (numbers), then aₙXₙ →ᵖ 0: a vanishing constant times something with a limit distribution vanishes, which is how §07 got Yₙ →ᵖ θ from √n(Yₙ − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ²). And if Xₙ − Yₙ →ᵖ 0 and Yₙ ⇝ Y, then Xₙ ⇝ Y: asymptotically negligible differences can be ignored, which is how every "remainder term" in an asymptotic argument is disposed of.

The wrong generalisation

  • Marginal limits do not add. It is tempting to conclude from Xₙ ⇝ X and Yₙ ⇝ Y that Xₙ + Yₙ ⇝ X + Y. But the right-hand side is not even defined until the joint distribution of (X, Y) is specified, and marginal convergence does not determine it. Let Z ~ N(0, 1), Xₙ = Z and Yₙ = −Z: both converge in distribution to N(0, 1), and Xₙ + Yₙ = 0. With Yₙ = Z′, an independent copy, the same marginals give Xₙ + Yₙ ~ N(0, 2).
  • When sums are safe. Sums and products of limits are legal when the convergence is joint: (Xₙ, Yₙ) ⇝ (X, Y). That holds if one of the limits is a constant (Slutsky), if Xₙ and Yₙ are independent for each n (then the limit is the product distribution), or if the pair is a vector of averages covered by the multivariate CLT. In probability the problem does not arise: Xₙ →ᵖ X and Yₙ →ᵖ Y on the same space do imply Xₙ + Yₙ →ᵖ X + Y, because that mode already compares values, not just distributions.

The t statistic, assembled

  • The object. For iid data with mean μ and finite variance σ² > 0, with sample standard deviation sₙ,
    Tₙ = √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/sₙ = [√n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ] · [σ/sₙ].
  • Step 1, the CLT. The first factor converges in distribution to N(0, 1).
  • Step 2, the LLN and continuous mapping. sₙ² = (n/(n − 1))·[(1/n)ΣXᵢ² − X̄ₙ²]. The weak law applied to the Xᵢ² (which have mean σ² + μ²; only E X² < ∞ is needed, Khinchin's version of §03) and to the Xᵢ gives (1/n)ΣXᵢ² →ᵖ σ² + μ² and X̄ₙ →ᵖ μ; the continuous map (a, b) ↦ a − b² and n/(n − 1) → 1 give sₙ² →ᵖ σ²; the continuous map x ↦ σ/√x (continuous at σ² > 0) gives σ/sₙ →ᵖ 1.
  • Step 3, Slutsky. A product of a factor converging in distribution to N(0, 1) and a factor converging in probability to 1: Tₙ ⇝ N(0, 1). ∎ Every "±1.96 × estimated SE" interval in statistics is justified by exactly this three-step argument, with the CLT and the estimate of the SE replaced by whatever the estimator calls for.
  • Plug-in standard errors are legal. The same argument shows that any consistent estimate of a standard error may replace the true one without changing the limit: (θ̂ₙ − θ)/ŜEₙ ⇝ N(0, 1) whenever (θ̂ₙ − θ)/SEₙ ⇝ N(0, 1) and ŜEₙ/SEₙ →ᵖ 1. That covers the Wald interval for a proportion (SE computed at p̂), delta-method standard errors evaluated at the estimate, and the standard errors of a maximum likelihood estimate computed from the observed information. It is a limit statement: the finite-n coverage of the Wald interval for a proportion is notoriously poor near p = 0 or 1 (intervals for a proportion).
  • Where t, rather than z, comes from. For normal data Tₙ has exactly the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom (the t distribution), and t_(n−1) ⇝ N(0, 1), consistent with Slutsky. For non-normal data neither is exact; the t quantiles are a correction for the randomness of sₙ that is exact only under normality, and the leading error is the skewness. To first order the skewness of Tₙ is −2γ₁/√n: twice that of the standardised mean, and of the opposite sign. For right-skewed data, a sample with a large mean usually also has a large sₙ (the same big observations inflate both), which pulls Tₙ back toward 0; a sample with a small mean has a small sₙ, which pushes Tₙ further out. Simulated with Exp(1) data: skewness of Tₙ −1.09 at n = 20 (first-order −0.89) and −0.29 at n = 200 (−0.28).
  • What it costs in coverage. At n = 20 with Exp(1) data, the two-sided t interval at nominal 95% covers 91.9% of the time; the true mean lies above the interval 7.6% of the time (Tₙ < −t*) and below it 0.6% of the time, against 2.5% each (400,000 simulated samples). The two one-sided errors are of order 1/√n and of opposite sign, so they partly cancel in the two-sided coverage (§06). One-sided tests on skewed data are where this matters most (choosing a test).

Assembling the t statistic: s → σ, times the CLT data · n · new seed
The bridge to inference. Every large-sample interval and test in the statistics series has the same skeleton: an estimator that is asymptotically normal by a CLT (possibly through the delta method), a standard error that is consistent by an LLN and continuous mapping, and Slutsky to glue them. The interval is Estimation & Confidence Intervals; the test is Hypothesis Testing; and the guarantee both offer is asymptotic, with finite-n errors governed by the skewness terms of §06.
