Sample Quantiles & Extreme Values

Limits for order statistics: sample quantiles are asymptotically normal with variance set by the density at the quantile, worked for the exponential median and hard where the density is flat, while the maximum follows one of three extreme-value limits, with the exponential maximum shown to be Gumbel.

09 Sample quantiles are asymptotically normal

A sample median or percentile is not an average, so the CLT does not apply to it directly. It is nevertheless asymptotically normal, with a variance that has a strikingly simple form: p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²), the variance of a sample proportion divided by the squared height of the density at the quantile. The proof turns a question about an order statistic into a question about a binomial count. The formula explains why the median is robust but less efficient than the mean for normal data, why it still works perfectly for the Cauchy, and why extreme percentiles and quantiles in flat regions need far more data.

Delivered: the theorem and a proof sketch

  • Order statistics derived the exact distribution of the k-th smallest observation and an approximation Var X₍ₖ₎ ≈ p(1 − p)/(n f(q_p)²), deferring "the limit theorem behind the approximation" to this page, and the closing list of Joint Distributions & Dependence named "the asymptotic normality of sample quantiles" among the results to come, beside the central limit theorem (§05) and the strong law (§04). Here it is.
  • Theorem. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be iid with cdf F, let q_p satisfy F(q_p) = p, and suppose F is differentiable at q_p with density f(q_p) > 0. Let q̂_p = X₍ₖ₎ with k = ⌈np⌉. Then
    √n(q̂_p − q_p) ⇝ N(0, p(1 − p) / f(q_p)²).
  • Proof sketch: count instead of sort. Fix t and put xₙ = q_p + t/√n. The k-th smallest observation is at most xₙ exactly when at least k observations are, and the number that are is Nₙ ~ Bin(n, pₙ) with pₙ = F(xₙ) = p + f(q_p)t/√n + o(1/√n). So
    P(√n(q̂_p − q_p) ≤ t) = P(Nₙ ≥ k) = P( (Nₙ − npₙ)/√(npₙ(1 − pₙ)) ≥ (k − npₙ)/√(npₙ(1 − pₙ)) ).
    On the right, k − npₙ = (np + O(1)) − np − √n f(q_p)t + o(√n), so the threshold tends to −f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p)). The standardised binomial on the left is a sum of bounded independent terms whose success probability changes with n, a triangular array, and Lindeberg's theorem (§05) applies: it converges to N(0, 1). Hence the probability tends to P(Z ≥ −f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p))) = Φ(f(q_p)t/√(p(1 − p))), the cdf of N(0, p(1 − p)/f(q_p)²) at t. ∎
  • The intuition in one line. If the sample quantile is off by δ, the fraction of the population below it is off by about f(q_p)·δ. The fraction of the sample below the true quantile is a proportion with standard error √(p(1 − p)/n). Matching the two: δ ≈ √(p(1 − p)/n)/f(q_p). A quantile's standard error is a proportion's standard error divided by the density height. (This is the delta method applied to the inverse of the empirical cdf; the precise version, q̂_p ≈ q_p + (p − F̂ₙ(q_p))/f(q_p) with an error smaller than 1/√n, is the Bahadur representation.)

Worked: the median of an exponential

  • The asymptotic answer. For Exp(1), the median is q = ln 2 = 0.6931 and the density there is f(ln 2) = e^(−ln 2) = ½. With p = ½: Var ≈ (¼)/(n · ¼) = 1/n. The sample median has standard error 1/√n: 0.2 at n = 25. For Exp(λ), everything scales by 1/λ.
  • The exact answer. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent exponentials (order statistics): X₍ₖ₎ = E₁/n + E₂/(n − 1) + ⋯ + Eₖ/(n − k + 1) with iid Eⱼ ~ Exp(1). So the median of n = 2m + 1 draws has mean Σ_(j=0)^m 1/(n − j) and variance Σ_(j=0)^m 1/(n − j)². At n = 25: mean 0.7127 (against ln 2 = 0.6931) and standard deviation 0.2019 (against 0.2). At n = 101: 0.0997 against 0.0995; at n = 401: 0.04997 against 0.04994. The asymptotic formula is already accurate to 1% at n = 25.
  • Efficiency. To estimate the mean θ of an exponential from its median, use θ̂ = q̂/ln 2, with standard error θ/(ln 2·√n) = 1.443θ/√n; the sample mean has θ/√n. The median-based estimator has asymptotic relative efficiency (ln 2)² = 0.480: it needs about 2.1 times as much data for the same precision. That is the price of robustness for this distribution; for the normal it is 2/π = 0.637 (f(μ) = 1/(σ√(2π)) gives Var = πσ²/(2n)), as tabulated in efficiency of estimators.
  • The Cauchy, rescued. The Cauchy average never converges (§04), but the Cauchy median has f(0) = 1/π and so SE = π/(2√n) = 1.571/√n: a perfectly ordinary 1/√n estimator of the centre. The quantile theorem needs only a positive density at the quantile, not a single moment. This is the constructive half of where the CLT fails outright: change the statistic, and the machinery returns.

Where f is flat, the quantile is hard to pin down

  • Extreme percentiles. For a normal population, the 99th percentile has f(q) = φ(2.326) = 0.0267/σ and SE ≈ √(0.99 × 0.01)/0.0267 · σ/√n = 3.73σ/√n, against 1.25σ/√n for the median: three times the standard error, nine times the data. For an exponential, the 90th percentile ln 10 = 2.303 has f = 0.1 and SE = 0.3/(0.1√n) = 3/√n, against 1/√n for the median. Tail quantiles sit where the density is low, and they are correspondingly uncertain; latency percentiles like p99 and p99.9 need large samples for exactly this reason.
  • A trough at the median. Mix N(−2, 1) and N(2, 1) equally. The median is 0, where the density is φ(2) = 0.054, so the sample median has SE ≈ 0.5/(0.054√n) = 9.3/√n, while the population standard deviation is √5 = 2.24 and the mean has SE = 2.24/√n. Here the "robust" estimator is four times as noisy as the mean, in the limit. The limit is also approached slowly: the exact standard deviation of the sample median (from its order-statistic density) is 0.65 at n = 101, where the formula says 0.92, then 0.39 against 0.46 at n = 401 and 0.218 against 0.231 at n = 1,601. The density climbs steeply on either side of the trough, so at moderate n the median's spread reaches places where f is larger than f(q_p); the formula is a large-n statement, accurate once that spread is small compared with the width of the trough. Even at n = 101 the median is three times as noisy as the mean (0.65 against 0.22). Robustness is a statement about outliers, not about efficiency in every shape.
  • A gap in the support. If f(q_p) = 0, the theorem's hypothesis fails and so can the conclusion: for a population uniform on [−2, −1] ∪ [1, 2], any point of [−1, 1] is a median, and the sample median keeps jumping between values near −1 and near 1, converging in distribution (to a fair choice between −1 and 1) but in no stronger mode. A quantile is well defined and estimable only where the cdf is strictly increasing.

Quantile standard errors in practice. The formula needs f(q_p), which is unknown and awkward to estimate (a density, at a point). That is why quantile intervals are usually built without it: the distribution-free interval between two order statistics, from the binomial count identity above (order statistics), or the bootstrap, which works for quantiles of continuous distributions (Resampling). The formula's job is to tell you, in advance, where those intervals will be wide. Sample quantiles as descriptive statistics are in position: quantiles and the five-number summary.

10 What the maximum does instead

The mean of n draws concentrates at a fixed value with normal fluctuations of size 1/√n. The maximum of n draws does something completely different: it keeps growing, its fluctuations do not shrink, and its limiting shape, after the right recentring and rescaling, is never normal. Extreme-value theory says that only three shapes are possible (Gumbel, Fréchet and Weibull) and that the tail of the population decides which one appears. This is the mathematics of worst cases: the slowest request, the highest flood, the largest loss, the luckiest of many random configurations.

The three limits

  • The exact law is easy. Mₙ = max(X₁, …, Xₙ) has P(Mₙ ≤ x) = F(x)ⁿ (the extremes of an iid sample). The question is what this looks like for large n. Since F(x)ⁿ → 0 for every x below the top of the support, Mₙ runs off to the upper end, and a limit shape exists only after centring by some bₙ and scaling by some aₙ.
  • The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem. If (Mₙ − bₙ)/aₙ ⇝ G for some non-degenerate G, then G is, up to location and scale, one of three types: Gumbel Λ(x) = exp(−e^(−x)) on the whole line; Fréchet exp(−x^(−α)) for x > 0; or (reversed) Weibull exp(−(−x)^α) for x ≤ 0. They are the fixed points of "take the maximum of independent copies and rescale", exactly as the normal and the stable laws are fixed points of "add and rescale". The three combine into one family, the generalised extreme-value distribution exp(−(1 + ξx)^(−1/ξ)), with ξ = 0 (read as a limit) for Gumbel, ξ > 0 for Fréchet and ξ < 0 for Weibull.
  • Which one: the tail decides. For continuous distributions with smooth tails, tails that decay exponentially or faster (exponential, gamma, normal, lognormal) give the Gumbel; some distributions, including many discrete ones such as the Poisson and the geometric, have no extreme-value limit at all. Power-law tails P(X > x) ≈ x^(−α) (Pareto, t, Cauchy) give the Fréchet with the same α. A bounded support, approached polynomially at the top endpoint (uniform, beta), gives the Weibull. The rest of the distribution is irrelevant: the maximum only ever sees the tail.

Worked: the exponential maximum is Gumbel

  • The computation. For Exp(1), F(x) = 1 − e^(−x). Centre at bₙ = ln n and do not rescale (aₙ = 1):
    P(Mₙ − ln n ≤ x) = F(x + ln n)ⁿ = (1 − e^(−x)/n)ⁿ → exp(−e^(−x)), using (1 − c/n)ⁿ → e^(−c).
    The maximum of n exponential draws is ln n plus a Gumbel variable.
  • Its moments, exactly. The gaps between exponential order statistics are independent, the j-th from the top being Exp with rate j, so Mₙ = E₁/1 + E₂/2 + ⋯ + Eₙ/n. Hence E Mₙ = Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n and Var Mₙ = Σ 1/j² → π²/6. So E(Mₙ − ln n) = Hₙ − ln n: 0.6264 at n = 10, 0.5822 at 100, 0.5777 at 1,000, tending to Euler's constant γ = 0.5772, the Gumbel mean; and the standard deviation tends to π/√6 = 1.2825, the Gumbel's.
  • Three contrasts with the mean. (i) The location of Mₙ grows, like ln n, while the mean's is fixed. (ii) The spread of Mₙ does not shrink: about 1.28 at every large n, while the mean's falls like 1/√n. (iii) The shape is skewed (Gumbel skewness 1.14) at every n, while the mean's skewness dies. And the convergence is fast: the largest cdf distance to the Gumbel is 0.0280 at n = 10, 0.0027 at 100, 0.00027 at 1,000, falling like 1/n.

The other two, stated

  • Pareto → Fréchet. For a Pareto with minimum 1 and tail index α, P(Mₙ ≤ n^(1/α)x) = (1 − x^(−α)/n)ⁿ → exp(−x^(−α)): the maximum grows like a power of n, n^(1/α), not a logarithm. With α = 3, the maximum scales by n^(1/3): 10 for 1,000 draws and 100 for a million, which makes the typical maximum about 9 and 90 times the typical single draw (the median maximum of 1,000 draws is about 10 × (ln 2)^(−1/3) = 11.3, against a single-draw median of 2^(1/3) = 1.26). Heavy-tailed maxima are dominated by single, enormous observations (heavy tails).
  • Uniform → Weibull. For U(0, 1), P(n(1 − Mₙ) > x) = (1 − x/n)ⁿ → e^(−x): the gap between the maximum and the endpoint is about Exp(1)/n. Here the maximum converges to a finite endpoint at rate 1/n, faster than any average. Estimating the endpoint θ of U(0, θ) by the sample maximum is therefore unusually precise, and also the classic case where the bootstrap fails (where the bootstrap fails).
  • The normal is Gumbel, slowly. For N(0, 1), (Mₙ − bₙ)/aₙ ⇝ Λ with aₙ = 1/√(2 ln n) and bₙ = √(2 ln n) − (ln ln n + ln 4π)/(2√(2 ln n)), but the error falls only like 1/ln n. At practical sizes the first-order growth √(2 ln n) is badly off: the expected maximum of 100 standard normals is 2.51 (against √(2 ln 100) = 3.03), and of 1,000 it is 3.24 (against 3.72).

Why this matters

  • Worst cases do not average out. A service that handles n requests per second with exponential-tailed latencies sees a slowest request that grows like ln n; with power-law latencies, like n^(1/α). Scaling traffic up does not make the worst case typical; it makes it worse. Capacity planning for maxima (timeouts, buffer sizes, flood defences, insurance reserves) uses the extreme-value family fitted to block maxima or to exceedances over a high threshold, not a normal fitted to everything.
  • The luckiest of many is lucky. If K configurations, models or features are compared on noisy scores, the best observed score is a maximum, and even when all are equally good it exceeds their common mean by about √(2 ln K) noise standard deviations to first order. For K = 1,000 independent normal scores that is 3.72, and the exact expected maximum is 3.24 standard deviations. That is the winner's curse behind optimistic leaderboards and hyperparameter searches, and the reason a held-out test set must be touched once (p-hacking and multiple comparisons).
  • Records. The i-th of a sequence of iid continuous draws is a new record with probability 1/i, independently of the others, so the expected number of records in n draws is Hₙ ≈ ln n + 0.577: 5.19 in 100 draws, 7.49 in 1,000. Records keep coming, but ever more rarely, and by §05's Lindeberg example their count is asymptotically normal.
The CLT is about sums; extreme-value theory is about maxima. Both are limit theorems in distribution, both have a small family of possible limits characterised as fixed points, and both are decided by the tail: the CLT by whether the variance is finite, extreme-value theory by how fast the tail decays. Applying the normal to a maximum, or to a quantity driven by one, is the most common way to underestimate a risk.
