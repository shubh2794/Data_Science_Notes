Simulation & Stochastic Processes

When a probability or an expectation cannot be computed, it can be estimated: build the random experiment in code, run it many times, and average. This page makes that idea exact. It starts with where the randomness comes from (a deterministic generator whose output passes for independent uniform numbers), turns uniforms into draws from any distribution by the inverse transform and by rejection, and collects the samplers for every family in the series. It then treats the average of simulated values as an estimator with a Monte Carlo error bar, explains why that error bar ignores the dimension of the problem, and shows the five standard ways to shrink it: antithetic variates, control variates, stratification, importance sampling and common random numbers. The second half lets randomness evolve in time. The Bernoulli and Poisson processes count events; Markov chains forget everything but the present and settle into a stationary distribution; the ergodic theorem and the Markov-chain central limit theorem replace the iid laws when draws are dependent, with an error bar inflated by the chain's autocorrelation; Markov chain Monte Carlo builds a chain whose stationary distribution is any target you can write down up to a constant; and the M/M/1 queue shows two Poisson processes in tension. It ends with the places all of this runs inside machine learning.

01 What a simulation is, and what a stochastic process is

A simulation replaces a calculation with an experiment. If a quantity can be written as a probability P(A) or an expectation θ = E h(X), and if the random experiment that produces X can be carried out in code, then running it N times and averaging h over the runs estimates θ, with an error that shrinks like 1/√N and can itself be estimated from the runs. A stochastic process is a random experiment that unfolds in time: a collection of random variables X_t, one for each time t, whose joint behaviour is the object of study. The two ideas meet constantly, because most processes worth studying are too complicated to analyse exactly and are understood by simulating them, and because the most powerful simulation method of all, Markov chain Monte Carlo, works by building a stochastic process on purpose.

Replacing analysis with sampling

  • Three ingredients. A simulation needs (i) a model that can be run forward, step by step, from random inputs; (ii) a supply of random inputs, in practice uniform numbers on (0, 1); (iii) a theorem saying that averages of the outputs converge to the quantity wanted and how fast. The third ingredient is the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, applied to the simulated values (why simulation works). This page supplies the second ingredient and shows how to make the third one cheaper.
  • A problem with no closed form. A shared computer has 250 independent subscribers; each is active on a given day with probability 0.3; an active subscriber submits a geometric number of jobs (success probability 0.15, so 1/0.15 = 6.67 jobs on average); each job needs a Gamma(10, 3) time in minutes (shape 10, rate 3, mean 3.33). What is the probability that the day's total demand fits in 24 hours = 1,440 minutes? The total is a random sum of random sums. Its mean is easy by Wald's identity, 250 × 0.3 × 6.67 × 3.33 = 1,666.7 minutes, and its variance follows from the law of total variance (law of total variance) applied twice: SD = 240.8 minutes. But its distribution has no usable formula.
  • The simulation. Draw the number of active users (a Bin(250, 0.3) count), a geometric number of jobs for each, and the total time (a sum of 10k exponentials for k jobs, that is Gamma(10k, 3)); record whether it is below 1,440; repeat. With 100,000 simulated days the estimate is P̂ = 0.1734 with standard error √(P̂(1 − P̂)/N) = 0.0012. The normal approximation with the exact mean and SD gives 0.1732, which the simulation confirms rather than assumes. To guarantee an error below ±0.01 with probability 0.99 whatever P is, the bound P(1 − P) ≤ ¼ gives N ≥ ¼ × (2.576/0.01)² = 16,589 simulated days.
  • What simulation cannot do. It cannot fix a wrong model, and it cannot see events rarer than about 1/N unless it is told where to look (importance sampling, §06). A simulated probability of 0 from 10,000 runs says only that the true value is probably below 3/10,000 (the "rule of three": (1 − p)ᴺ = 0.05 at p ≈ 3/N).

Pseudo-random numbers, honestly

  • They are not random. A computer's random numbers come from a deterministic recurrence: a state sₙ is updated by a fixed rule sₙ₊₁ = f(sₙ), and an output uₙ = g(sₙ) ∈ [0, 1) is read off each state. The first state is the seed. Run twice from the same seed, the generator produces the same stream, bit for bit. The numbers are pseudo-random: a fixed sequence designed so that no statistical test you are likely to apply can tell it from independent uniform draws.
  • Worked: the 32-bit mixer used throughout these pages. Every figure in this series draws from one small generator. Its state is a single 32-bit integer a. Each call adds a fixed odd constant, a ← a + 1,831,565,813 (mod 2³²) (the constant is 6D2B79F5 in hexadecimal), and then scrambles a copy of a with three rounds of shifts, exclusive-ors and integer multiplications, dividing the 32-bit result by 2³². The state sequence alone is a trivial arithmetic progression; all the apparent randomness comes from the scramble. From seed 1 the first state is 1 + 1,831,565,813 = 1,831,565,814, which scrambles to the integer 2,693,262,067, so u₁ = 2,693,262,067/2³² = 0.627074; the next two outputs are 0.002736 and 0.527447. From seed 42 the stream starts 0.601104, 0.448291, 0.852466.
  • Period. Because the increment is odd, the state visits all 2³² = 4,294,967,296 values before repeating: the period is exactly 2³². That is ample for figures that use a few million draws, and too short for a large study: a run that consumes a sizable fraction of the period starts to see its own pattern (for instance, a 32-bit state cannot produce more than 2³² distinct values, so repeats among draws appear far sooner than they would for truly continuous uniforms). Generators for serious work carry 128 to about 20,000 bits of state and periods of 2¹²⁸ or more.
  • Reproducibility is a feature. Recording the seed makes a simulation repeatable exactly, which is why every figure on this page states its seed and every number in a caption can be regenerated. It also makes debugging possible: a run that crashes can be replayed. The flip side is that results computed from one seed are one draw from the simulation's own sampling distribution, so the honest report is an estimate with its Monte Carlo error bar, not "the answer".
  • What "random enough" means. A good generator's outputs are (i) uniform: in a long run, every interval of [0, 1) receives its share; (ii) equidistributed in several dimensions: consecutive pairs, triples, …, k-tuples fill the unit cube evenly, up to a dimension much larger than any simulation uses; (iii) apparently independent: no test based on a feasible amount of output detects a pattern (batteries of such tests exist and good generators pass them); (iv) fast and with a long period. Statistical randomness is not unpredictability: knowing a few outputs of a simulation generator usually lets an adversary reconstruct its state, which is why cryptography uses a different class of generators.
  • How it goes wrong. A multiplicative generator widely used in the 1960s and 1970s computed xₙ₊₁ = 65,539·xₙ mod 2³¹. Since 65,539 = 2¹⁶ + 3, squaring gives xₙ₊₂ = 6xₙ₊₁ − 9xₙ (mod 2³¹): every consecutive triple satisfies a fixed linear relation, so the points (uₙ, uₙ₊₁, uₙ₊₂) fall on just 15 parallel planes in the unit cube. Any three-dimensional simulation run on it was quietly sampling a set of planes instead of a cube. Every linear congruential generator has a lattice structure of this kind; good ones push it into dimensions and scales where it does not matter.
  • Zero and one. The mixer's outputs are multiples of 2⁻³² in [0, 1), so U = 0 has probability 2⁻³². Any recipe that takes ln U or divides by U must reject a zero; the shared helpers in these pages redraw when U = 0.

Delivered: U(0, 1) is the atom every simulation is built from

  • The uniform distribution was introduced as "the raw material of all simulation", with the promise that this page would explain how generators produce such numbers and how uniform draws become Monte Carlo estimates. Everything below is a transformation of independent U(0, 1) draws U₁, U₂, …:
  • The simplest transformations. A Bernoulli(p) trial is 1{U < p}, because P(U < p) = p. A uniform on (a, b) is a + (b − a)U. A fair die is ⌈6U⌉. A uniform point in a rectangle is a pair of scaled uniforms. A Bin(n, p) count is the number of Uᵢ < p among n uniforms.
  • The general recipes. Any distribution with a computable cdf: the inverse transform (§02). Any density bounded by a multiple of one you can sample: rejection (§03). Special structure: sums, ratios and polar coordinates of simpler draws (§04). Distributions known only up to a constant, in high dimension: Markov chain Monte Carlo (§10).
  • Why uniform and not something else. A uniform on (0, 1) is the distribution of an infinite sequence of fair coin flips read as a binary fraction, so it carries exactly "independent fair bits", the simplest randomness there is, and the probability integral transform (§02) shows it can be turned into anything.

What a stochastic process is

  • Definition. A stochastic process is a family of random variables {X_t : t ∈ 𝒯} on one probability space, indexed by a set 𝒯 of times. For each outcome ω, the function t ↦ X_t(ω) is a sample path (a realisation): a whole random history, not a single number. The values X_t take lie in the state space.
  • Four kinds, by time and state. Time can be discrete (t = 0, 1, 2, …) or continuous (t ≥ 0); states can be discrete (a count, a category) or continuous (a price, a position). Discrete time and discrete states: a Markov chain (§08), the number of successes in a Bernoulli process (§07), a gambler's fortune. Continuous time, discrete states: the Poisson process (§07), the number of jobs in a queue (§11). Discrete time, continuous states: a random walk with normal steps, an autoregressive series, the sequence of parameters produced by stochastic gradient descent. Continuous time and continuous states: Brownian motion and the diffusions behind diffusion models (§12).
  • What specifies a process. Its finite-dimensional distributions: the joint distribution of (X_(t₁), …, X_(tₖ)) for every finite set of times. A process with independent increments has X_(t₂) − X_(t₁), X_(t₃) − X_(t₂), … independent for t₁ < t₂ < ⋯; one with stationary increments has the distribution of X_(t+s) − X_t depending on s only. A stationary process has finite-dimensional distributions unchanged by a shift of time. These three properties, together with the Markov property of §08, carry most of the theory.
  • Worked: two descriptions of one history. Toss a fair coin once a second and let Sₙ be heads minus tails after n tosses. Sₙ has independent and stationary increments (each toss adds ±1); it is not stationary (its variance n grows); it is Markov (the next value depends on the past only through Sₙ). The proportion of heads p̂ₙ = (n + Sₙ)/(2n) is a different process on the same outcomes: it carries the same information as Sₙ at each n, so it is also Markov (but time-inhomogeneous), its increments are neither independent nor stationary, and its paths converge almost surely to ½ (the strong law).

02 The inverse transform

To draw from any distribution with cdf F, draw a uniform U and return the smallest x with F(x) ≥ U. Geometrically: put U on the vertical axis of the graph of F, walk right until you hit the curve, and drop down to the horizontal axis. Steep parts of F (where the density is high) catch many values of U in a short stretch of x; flat parts catch few. The method works for every distribution, discrete, continuous or mixed, uses one uniform per draw, and preserves order, which is what makes it the backbone of the variance-reduction tricks of §06. Its only cost is computing the inverse.

The method, and why it works

  • The generalised inverse. For any cdf F define F⁻¹(u) = min{x : F(x) ≥ u} for 0 < u < 1. The minimum exists because F is non-decreasing and right-continuous, so the set {x : F(x) ≥ u} is a closed half-line [F⁻¹(u), ∞). Where F is continuous and strictly increasing this is the ordinary inverse; where F jumps (an atom), a whole interval of u maps to the same x; where F is flat (a gap in the support), the minimum picks the left end, so no value in the gap is ever returned.
  • The key equivalence. For every u ∈ (0, 1) and every x: F⁻¹(u) ≤ x ⟺ u ≤ F(x). Proof. If u ≤ F(x), then x belongs to the set {x′ : F(x′) ≥ u}, whose minimum is F⁻¹(u), so F⁻¹(u) ≤ x. Conversely, if F⁻¹(u) ≤ x, then F(x) ≥ F(F⁻¹(u)) ≥ u, using that F is non-decreasing and that the minimum itself belongs to the closed set. ∎
  • The theorem. If U ~ U(0, 1), then X = F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. Proof: P(X ≤ x) = P(F⁻¹(U) ≤ x) = P(U ≤ F(x)) = F(x), by the equivalence and the uniform cdf. ∎ No continuity, no density, no discreteness is assumed: this is the backward direction of the probability integral transform proved in transforming a random variable, whose forward direction says that F(X) ~ U(0, 1) when F is continuous.

Discrete distributions: a search in the cdf table

  • The recipe. For a discrete X with values x₀ < x₁ < ⋯ and cumulative probabilities F(x₀) < F(x₁) < ⋯, the inverse transform returns xₖ exactly when F(xₖ₋₁) < U ≤ F(xₖ). The unit interval is cut into consecutive pieces of lengths p₀, p₁, …, and X is the label of the piece that U lands in, so P(X = xₖ) = pₖ, the length of the piece.
  • Worked: Bin(10, ¼). The cdf table is F(0) = 0.0563, F(1) = 0.2440, F(2) = 0.5256, F(3) = 0.7759, F(4) = 0.9219, F(5) = 0.9803, … For U = 0.5, F(1) = 0.2440 < 0.5 ≤ F(2) = 0.5256, so X = 2, which is also the median found in the PMF & CDF section: the median is F⁻¹(½). For U = 0.2440 exactly, the rule returns 1 (the inequality U ≤ F(1) already holds), for U = 0.2441 it returns 2.
  • The cost is a search. A sequential scan from x₀ stops at the (k + 1)-th comparison when X = xₖ, so it costs 1 + E X comparisons on average: 3.5 for Bin(10, ¼), 1 + μ for a Poisson(μ), which is slow for large μ. Three fixes: scan the values in decreasing order of probability (cost Σ rank × probability); start at the mode and search outwards (cost of order √μ for the Poisson, because the draw is typically within a few standard deviations of the mode); or binary-search the table, ⌈log₂ K⌉ comparisons for K values. For many draws from one fixed distribution, the alias method of §04 does it in constant time.
  • Closed forms exist for some. The geometric (trials to the first success, success probability p) has F(k) = 1 − (1 − p)ᵏ, and the inverse transform gives X = ⌈ln U / ln(1 − p)⌉, using 1 − U ~ U(0, 1). That is the ceiling of an exponential variable with rate −ln(1 − p): the geometric is the discretised exponential, the relation behind the memoryless geometric.

Continuous distributions: solve F(x) = U

  • Exponential. With rate λ and scale θ = 1/λ, F(x) = 1 − e^(−x/θ), so F⁻¹(u) = −θ ln(1 − u). Since 1 − U is uniform too, X = −θ ln U works and saves a subtraction. Worked: arrivals at rate λ = 2 per hour (θ = 0.5 h) and U = 0.3 give a waiting time of −0.5 ln 0.3 = 0.602 h, about 36 minutes. With U = 0.7 and θ = 1 (the figure's default), −ln(1 − 0.7) = 1.204.
  • The other closed forms. Pareto xm·U^(−1/α), Weibull s(−ln U)^(1/k), Cauchy tan(π(U − ½)), logistic ln(U/(1 − U)), Gumbel −ln(−ln U), Laplace ±(−ln U) with a random sign, Rayleigh √(−2 ln U): the catalogue is in transforming a random variable.
  • Truncation for free. To draw X conditioned on a < X ≤ b, squeeze U into the matching slice of probability: X = F⁻¹(F(a) + U·(F(b) − F(a))). No draw is wasted, however small the slice. Worked: Exp(1) truncated to (1, 2] has F(1) = 0.6321, F(2) = 0.8647; U = 0.5 maps to 0.7484 and then to −ln(1 − 0.7484) = 1.380.
  • Order is preserved. F⁻¹ is non-decreasing, so larger uniforms give larger draws, and F⁻¹(U) and F⁻¹(1 − U) are the most negatively dependent pair with marginal F. The first fact makes common random numbers effective and the second is the antithetic method (§06). It also means the Kolmogorov distance between a sample and its target is unchanged by the map: the figure's three continuous targets, fed the same 200 uniforms, all show the same distance 0.0484, which is the distance of the uniforms themselves to U(0, 1).

When F⁻¹ is expensive

  • No closed form. The normal, gamma, beta, t and χ² cdfs have no elementary inverse. For the normal, a rational approximation of Φ⁻¹ accurate to about 9 digits costs a few dozen arithmetic operations (and a logarithm in the tails), and one Halley correction step, which needs one evaluation of Φ, brings it to about 15 digits; this is how the shared helper ST.normQuant in these pages works, and it is fast enough. For the gamma and beta families, F itself is an incomplete gamma or beta function computed by a series or continued fraction, and inverting it needs a root-finder (bisection or Newton) that evaluates F 10 to 50 times per draw. That is one to two orders of magnitude slower than the rejection methods of §03 and §04, which is why libraries use those instead.
  • Large discrete supports. A distribution over a vocabulary of 50,000 tokens needs a 50,000-entry table and a search per draw; the alias method (§04) or the Gumbel-max trick (§04) is used instead.
  • Several dimensions. A random vector has no single cdf to invert. The conditional inverse transform draws X₁ from its marginal, then X₂ from the conditional cdf F_(X₂|X₁)(· | x₁), and so on, which is exact but needs every conditional cdf in usable form (conditional distributions). When only the joint density is available, rejection (§03) or MCMC (§10) take over.

The inverse transform is the default whenever F⁻¹ is cheap. It is exact, uses one uniform per draw, never wastes a draw, handles truncation, and keeps the order of the uniforms, so that two simulations driven by the same stream respond smoothly to a change of parameter. 03 Rejection sampling

To sample from a density f that is hard to invert, find a density q that is easy to sample and a constant c with f(x) ≤ c·q(x) everywhere. Draw X from q, draw a height uniformly between 0 and c·q(X), and keep X if the height falls under f(X); otherwise try again. The accepted points are exact draws from f, a fraction 1/c of the proposals is accepted, and f need only be known up to a constant. The method is the workhorse for one-dimensional families, the engine of the fastest normal and gamma generators, and the clearest illustration of why naive sampling collapses in high dimension.

The theorem, with proof

  • The algorithm. Suppose f(x) ≤ c·q(x) for all x. Repeat: draw X ~ q and U ~ U(0, 1) independently; if U ≤ f(X)/(c·q(X)), return X. Necessarily c ≥ 1, since integrating f ≤ cq gives 1 ≤ c.
  • Acceptance probability. Conditioning on X = x, a proposal is accepted with probability f(x)/(c·q(x)), so P(accept) = ∫ q(x)·f(x)/(c·q(x)) dx = (1/c)∫ f = 1/c.
  • Accepted draws have density f. For any set A, P(X ∈ A, accept) = ∫_A q(x)·f(x)/(c·q(x)) dx = (1/c)∫_A f. Dividing by P(accept) = 1/c: P(X ∈ A | accept) = ∫_A f. ∎ Each round is independent of the last, so the returned value is the first accepted proposal and has exactly this conditional distribution.
  • The cost. The number of proposals per accepted draw is geometric with success probability 1/c: mean c, variance c(c − 1). Every unit of slack in c is paid for in proposals, so the best constant is c* = sup_x f(x)/q(x).
  • Unnormalised targets. If only f̃ = Z·f is known (a posterior known up to its normalising constant Z), use any M with f̃ ≤ M·q and accept when U ≤ f̃(X)/(M·q(X)). The proof is unchanged with c = M/Z; the acceptance rate Z/M is then unknown in advance but is estimated by the observed rate, which also estimates Z.

The geometric view: points under a curve

  • A density is a region. If (X, Y) is uniform on the region {(x, y) : 0 ≤ y ≤ f(x)} under the graph of f, the region has area 1, so the joint density is 1 there, and the marginal density of X is ∫₀^f(x) 1 dy = f(x). Sampling from f is the same as throwing a uniform point under its graph and keeping the horizontal coordinate.
  • Rejection throws points under a bigger curve. The pair (X, U·c·q(X)) with X ~ q is uniform under the graph of c·q (by the same calculation in reverse). Keeping the points that fall under f leaves a uniform point under f, by the restriction property of uniform distributions. The acceptance rate is the ratio of the two areas: 1/c.
  • The box. The simplest envelope for a bounded density on [a, b] is a rectangle of height max f: q uniform, c = (b − a)·max f. Worked: Beta(5.5, 3.1) has its mode at 4.5/6.6 = 0.682 with height 2.4217, so the tightest box accepts 1/2.4217 = 41.3% of the points; a box of height 2.5 would accept 40%.

Worked: the normal from a Laplace or a Cauchy envelope

  • Laplace. Target φ(x) = e^(−x²/2)/√(2π), proposal q(x) = ½e^(−|x|) (drawn by inverse transform as an exponential with a random sign). The ratio φ/q = √(2/π)·e^(|x| − x²/2) is largest where |x| − x²/2 is, at |x| = 1, so c* = √(2/π)·e^(½) = √(2e/π) = 1.3155 and the acceptance rate is 1/c* = 0.7602. The test simplifies to U ≤ e^(−(|X| − 1)²/2).
  • Cauchy. With q(x) = 1/(π(1 + x²)), φ/q = √(π/2)·(1 + x²)e^(−x²/2) is largest at x² = 1, so c* = √(π/2)·2e^(−½) = √(2π/e) = 1.5203, acceptance 0.6577. The Cauchy has heavier tails than needed, so more of its proposals land where the normal has nothing and are wasted.
  • The rule the other way round. A light-tailed proposal cannot cover a heavy-tailed target: for a Cauchy target and a normal proposal, f/q → ∞ in the tails, no finite c exists, and the method does not apply. The envelope's tails must be at least as heavy as the target's, the same rule that importance sampling will need (§06).

The squeeze

  • Evaluating f can be the expensive step (a logarithm, a power, a special function). A squeeze is a cheap function s(x) ≤ f(x): accept at once if U·c·q(X) ≤ s(X), and only evaluate f in the thin band between s and f. The accepted draws are unchanged, so the method stays exact; only the cost changes. The gamma generator in the shared helpers uses one: its first test, U < 1 − 0.0331·Z⁴, is a polynomial lower bound on the true acceptance probability, and for shape 2 it decides about 92% of all proposals without computing a single logarithm.

Delivered: the polar method, and the ziggurat

  • The polar (Marsaglia) method. Change of variables: Jacobians derived Box–Muller, Z₁ = √(−2 ln U₁)·cos(2πU₂), Z₂ = √(−2 ln U₁)·sin(2πU₂), and promised its faster relatives. The polar method removes the trigonometry by rejection. Draw V₁, V₂ uniform on (−1, 1) and let S = V₁² + V₂²; reject unless 0 < S < 1 (the point must land in the unit disc, probability π/4 = 0.7854). Then return Z₁ = V₁·√(−2 ln S / S) and Z₂ = V₂·√(−2 ln S / S).
  • Why it works. An accepted point is uniform on the disc. In polar coordinates (R, Θ) its angle is uniform on [0, 2π), and P(R² ≤ s) = (area of radius √s)/(area of radius 1) = s, so S = R² ~ U(0, 1), independent of the angle. Thus (V₁, V₂)/√S = (cos Θ, sin Θ) supplies Box–Muller's direction and S supplies its uniform U₁: the output is exactly Box–Muller with U₁ = S and 2πU₂ = Θ. It costs one logarithm, one square root and one division per pair, and uses 4/π = 1.27 pairs of uniforms per accepted pair.
  • The ziggurat. Cover the (half) normal density with a stack of horizontal rectangles of equal area, typically 128 or 256 of them, plus a tail piece. Pick a layer uniformly, pick a horizontal position uniformly inside it; if the position is inside the part of the rectangle that lies wholly under the curve (most of it), return it: one table lookup, one multiplication and one comparison. Only in the thin ragged edge does it evaluate the density, and only in the tail does it call a special tail algorithm. With 128 or more layers roughly 98 to 99% of draws take the fast path, which makes it the fastest normal (and exponential) generator in common use. It is rejection sampling with a staircase envelope and a built-in squeeze.

Why rejection dies in high dimension

  • Volumes collapse. The obvious way to sample uniformly from the unit ball in d dimensions is to sample the cube [−1, 1]ᵈ and reject points outside the ball. The acceptance rate is the volume ratio π^(d/2)/(Γ(d/2 + 1)·2ᵈ): 0.785 in 2 dimensions, 0.524 in 3, 0.164 in 5, 0.0025 in 10, 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ in 20, 1.5 × 10⁻²⁸ in 50. Almost all of a high-dimensional cube is in its corners.
  • Envelopes compound. If the target and proposal factorise over coordinates, c multiplies: a d-dimensional normal N(0, I) proposed from N(0, σ²I) needs c = σᵈ. With a proposal only 10% too wide (σ = 1.1), the acceptance rate is 0.91 in one dimension, 0.39 in 10, 7.3 × 10⁻⁵ in 100 and 4 × 10⁻⁴² in 1,000. Any fixed mismatch between proposal and target is paid exponentially in the dimension.
  • What replaces it. In high dimension one either exploits structure (draw coordinate by coordinate from conditionals, use a linear transformation such as the Cholesky factor of §04) or gives up independence and builds a Markov chain that moves locally through the region of high probability (§10). The same collapse, in the form of weights that are almost all zero, afflicts importance sampling (§06).

Rejection is exact and cheap in low dimension, and hopeless in high dimension. Its cost is the constant c, the ratio of the area under the envelope to the area under the target. In one dimension a good envelope keeps c near 1; in d dimensions every per-coordinate mismatch multiplies, and c grows exponentially. That one fact explains why high-dimensional sampling is done with Markov chains.

04 Sampling the rest of the zoo

Most families in this series are built from simpler ones: a gamma is a sum of exponentials, a beta is a ratio of gammas, a t is a normal divided by a chi, a multivariate normal is a linear map of independent normals, a Dirichlet is a vector of gammas divided by their total. Each of those constructions, derived earlier as a fact about distributions, is also a sampler. This section collects them, adds the two methods for categorical draws that matter at scale (the alias table and the Gumbel-max trick), and states which method libraries actually use for each family.

Gamma, chi-square, beta, t and F

  • Gamma with integer shape. A Gamma(k, λ) variable (shape k, rate λ, scale θ = 1/λ) is the waiting time to the k-th event of a Poisson process, a sum of k independent exponentials (gamma & chi-square). So X = −θ·(ln U₁ + ⋯ + ln Uₖ). Summing logarithms rather than taking the log of the product avoids underflow when k is large; the cost is k logarithms per draw, which is why this is used only for small k.
  • Gamma with any shape: a named rejection method. For shape α ≥ 1, the Marsaglia–Tsang method sets d = α − ⅓, c = 1/√(9d), and repeats: draw Z ~ N(0, 1) and U ~ U(0, 1), let V = (1 + cZ)³; if V > 0 and ln U < Z²/2 + d − dV + d·ln V, return d·V/λ. It is rejection sampling on a scale where the gamma looks nearly normal (the cube-root transformation), so its acceptance rate is high: 0.958 at α = 1 (counting only proposals with V > 0; per normal draw it is 0.952), 0.981 at α = 2, 0.994 at α = 5, from 100,000 accepted draws each. For α < 1, draw G ~ Gamma(α + 1, 1) and return G·U^(1/α)/λ, a boosting identity that follows from the beta–gamma algebra. This is the generator behind every gamma, beta and Dirichlet figure in the series.
  • Chi-square. χ²ₖ is a sum of k squared standard normals and also Gamma(k/2, ½) (rate ½), so either construction works; for large k the gamma generator is cheaper.
  • Beta from two gammas. If G₁ ~ Gamma(a, 1) and G₂ ~ Gamma(b, 1) are independent, G₁/(G₁ + G₂) ~ Beta(a, b), independent of G₁ + G₂: the beta–gamma connection proved with a Jacobian in change of variables. For integer parameters there is a second route: the k-th smallest of n uniforms is Beta(k, n − k + 1) (order statistics).
  • Student t, F and Cauchy. t_ν = Z/√(V/ν) with V ~ χ²_ν independent of Z; F = (V₁/d₁)/(V₂/d₂); the Cauchy is t₁ = Z₁/Z₂, or tan(π(U − ½)) by the inverse transform.
  • Lognormal, and anything that is a function of a normal. exp(μ + σZ); more generally, if Y = g(X) and X is easy, Y is easy (transforming a random variable).

Delivered: multivariate normals by Cholesky factors

  • The recipe. To draw X ~ N(μ, Σ) in k dimensions, factor the covariance matrix as Σ = LLᵀ with L lower triangular (the Cholesky factor, which exists and is unique with positive diagonal whenever Σ is symmetric positive definite: factorizations), draw Z = (Z₁, …, Zₖ) independent standard normals, and return X = μ + LZ. The multivariate normal promised this construction.
  • Why it works. X is a linear function of a normal vector, so it is normal; its mean is μ; its covariance is Cov(LZ) = L·Cov(Z)·Lᵀ = L·I·Lᵀ = Σ. Any L with LLᵀ = Σ works, including the symmetric square root VΛ^(1/2)Vᵀ from the eigendecomposition Σ = VΛVᵀ (eigendecomposition and SVD); Cholesky is preferred because it is an order of magnitude or more faster than an eigendecomposition (about k³/3 operations against roughly 9k³) and triangular, so LZ costs k(k + 1)/2 multiplications.
  • Worked, two dimensions. Σ = [[4, 1.2], [1.2, 1]] (standard deviations 2 and 1, correlation 1.2/(2 × 1) = 0.6). Cholesky: L₁₁ = √4 = 2, L₂₁ = 1.2/2 = 0.6, L₂₂ = √(1 − 0.6²) = 0.8. So X₁ = μ₁ + 2Z₁, X₂ = μ₂ + 0.6Z₁ + 0.8Z₂; check: Var X₂ = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1, Cov(X₁, X₂) = 2 × 0.6 = 1.2. With unit variances this is exactly the bivariate construction Y = ρZ₁ + √(1 − ρ²)Z₂ used throughout the bivariate normal: that construction is the 2 × 2 Cholesky factor.
  • The costs and the traps. Factorising costs about k³/3 operations once; each draw then costs O(k²). A covariance matrix that is only positive semi-definite (a perfectly correlated pair, more variables than observations behind an estimated Σ) has no Cholesky factor with positive diagonal and the factorisation fails at a zero pivot; the eigendecomposition with negative rounding errors set to zero still works. Running the recipe backwards, Z = L⁻¹(X − μ) by a triangular solve, whitens data and evaluates the density's quadratic form cheaply.

Delivered: Dirichlets by normalised gammas

  • The recipe. Draw independent Gᵢ ~ Gamma(αᵢ, 1) for i = 1, …, K and return pᵢ = Gᵢ/(G₁ + ⋯ + G_K). The vector p is Dirichlet(α₁, …, α_K) and is independent of the total G₁ + ⋯ + G_K ~ Gamma(Σαᵢ, 1): the K-dimensional version of the beta–gamma connection, as promised in mixtures, hierarchies and the Dirichlet. With K = 2 it is the beta recipe above. The shared helper PV.dirichletDraw does exactly this; for Dir(2, 3, 5) its draws have means 0.1999, 0.2998, 0.5003 against 0.2, 0.3, 0.5.
  • Stick-breaking, the other route. Break off p₁ ~ Beta(α₁, α₂ + ⋯ + α_K), then a Beta(α₂, α₃ + ⋯ + α_K) fraction of what remains, and so on. It gives the same distribution and is the construction that extends to infinitely many components (the Dirichlet process of nonparametric Bayes).

Poisson, binomial and other counts

  • Poisson by counting exponential gaps. Events of a rate-μ Poisson process in one unit of time number Poisson(μ) (§07), and the gaps are exponential, so: add −ln(Uᵢ)/μ until the total exceeds 1; the number of complete gaps is the draw. Multiplying through by −μ and exponentiating: N = max{k : U₁U₂⋯Uₖ ≥ e^(−μ)}, which needs no logarithms at all. It uses μ + 1 uniforms on average, fine for small μ; for large μ, libraries switch to an inverse transform started at the mode or to a rejection method with a bounded cost per draw.
  • Binomial. A sum of n Bernoulli indicators (cost n), an inverse transform (cost about np), or for large n a rejection method; the shared helper ST.binom counts indicators.
  • Negative binomial, two ways. A sum of r geometric draws, or the gamma–Poisson mixture: draw Λ ~ Gamma(r, rate p/(1 − p)) and then Y ~ Poisson(Λ); the result counts failures before the r-th success and works for non-integer r, which is how overdispersed counts are simulated (the negative binomial).
  • Mixtures. To sample Σⱼ wⱼ fⱼ, pick a component j with probability wⱼ, then draw from fⱼ: the hierarchy of mixtures run forwards.

Categorical draws at scale: the alias method and the Gumbel-max trick

  • The alias method. For K categories with probabilities p₁, …, p_K, build a table of K columns, each of height 1/K, each holding at most two categories: its own, with probability prob[i] within the column, and one alias alias[i] filling the rest. A draw picks a column uniformly and then flips a biased coin: O(1) work, independent of K, after an O(K) set-up. One uniform suffices: v = KU, column i = ⌊v⌋, and the fractional part v − i serves as the coin; with 32-bit uniforms that leaves about 2³²/K distinct coin levels (about 86,000 at K = 50,000), fine in practice, and two independent uniforms give the exact version.
  • Why the table always exists. Scale the probabilities to Kpᵢ (average 1). As long as some column is underfull (Kpₛ < 1), some other is overfull (Kpₗ > 1); fill the underfull column with mass from the overfull one, which loses 1 − Kpₛ and is then underfull, exactly full or still overfull. Each step finishes one column, so after at most K − 1 steps every column is full.
  • Worked: p = (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.3). Scaled: (0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 1.2). The construction gives prob = (0.4, 0.8, 1, 0.4) and alias = (3, 3, 2, 2): column 0 is category 0 with probability 0.4, else 3; column 1 is 1 with probability 0.8, else 3; column 2 is always 2; column 3 is 3 with probability 0.4, else 2. Check: P(3) = ¼(0.6 + 0.2 + 0.4) = 0.3 and P(2) = ¼(1 + 0.6) = 0.4. The shared helper PV.aliasTable builds this table, and 400,000 draws from it give 0.1001, 0.2008, 0.3995, 0.2997.
  • The Gumbel-max trick. If G₁, …, G_K are independent standard Gumbel variables (Gᵢ = −ln(−ln Uᵢ)), then argmaxᵢ (ln pᵢ + Gᵢ) is a draw from the categorical distribution, and the pᵢ need not be normalised: unnormalised log-probabilities (logits) work directly. Proof. Write Eᵢ = −ln Uᵢ ~ Exp(1), so ln pᵢ + Gᵢ = ln pᵢ − ln Eᵢ = −ln(Eᵢ/pᵢ). Maximising it means minimising Eᵢ/pᵢ ~ Exp(rate pᵢ): a race of independent exponential clocks, which clock i wins with probability pᵢ/Σⱼpⱼ (competing exponentials). ∎ With 200,000 draws for p = (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.3) the frequencies are 0.0984, 0.2002, 0.3992, 0.3021. Its relevance is §12: sampling the next token from a language model is a categorical draw from logits.

05 Monte Carlo estimation and its error bar

Every Monte Carlo computation estimates an expectation θ = E h(X) by the average θ̂_N = (1/N)Σ h(Xᵢ) of N simulated draws. The estimator is unbiased, the strong law makes it consistent, and the central limit theorem gives it an error bar, ±1.96·s/√N, estimated from the same draws. The 1/√N rate is slow (each extra decimal digit costs a hundred times the work) but it does not depend on the dimension of X, which is why Monte Carlo is the method of choice for integrals in more than a handful of dimensions. This section states the recipe every reader should be able to apply without thinking, works it on an integral and on the dartboard, and compares it honestly with grid-based quadrature.

Delivered: the estimator, its consistency and its error bar

  • The promise. The closing list of Expectation, Variance & Inequalities sent "Monte Carlo estimation, its error bars and its variance-reduction tricks" here, and why simulation works proved the two theorems this section turns into a recipe.
  • Unbiased. By linearity, E θ̂_N = (1/N)·N·θ = θ for every N (linearity, proved).
  • Variance. The draws are independent, so Var θ̂_N = σ_h²/N with σ_h² = Var h(X): the standard deviation of the estimate is σ_h/√N.
  • Consistent. If E|h(X)| < ∞, the strong law gives θ̂_N → θ almost surely: the one stream of random numbers a computer produces leads to the right answer (the strong law).
  • Error bar. If σ_h² < ∞, the CLT gives √N(θ̂_N − θ) ⇝ N(0, σ_h²), and replacing σ_h by the sample standard deviation s_h of the simulated values is legal by Slutsky's theorem (Slutsky). So θ̂_N ± 1.96·s_h/√N is an approximate 95% confidence interval, built from the simulation's own output.
  • Probabilities are expectations. With h = 1_A, θ = P(A), σ_h² = P(A)(1 − P(A)), and the error bar is 1.96·√(P̂(1 − P̂)/N). The relative error is √((1 − P)/(P·N)), which explodes for rare events: estimating P = 10⁻⁴ to within 10% at 95% needs N = 1.96² × (1 − P)/(P × 0.01) ≈ 3.8 million draws.

1/√N against quadrature, and where Monte Carlo wins

  • Grids in d dimensions. A product rule with m points per axis uses n = mᵈ points. If the one-dimensional rule has error O(m^(−r)) (r = 2 for the midpoint and trapezoid rules, r = 4 for Simpson's, for smooth integrands), the product rule's error is O(m^(−r)) = O(n^(−r/d)). Monte Carlo's is O(n^(−1/2)) in every dimension. The rates cross at r/d = ½: Monte Carlo has the better rate beyond 4 dimensions against the midpoint rule and beyond 8 against Simpson's.
  • A test integrand with an exact answer. Let g(x) = Πⱼ e^(xⱼ)/(e − 1) on [0, 1]ᵈ, so ∫g = 1 in every dimension. Because g factorises, the product midpoint rule is exactly Qₘᵈ with Qₘ = s/sinh(s), s = 1/(2m), so its error is |Qₘᵈ − 1| ≈ d/(24m²), and the Monte Carlo variance is σ² = (1 + 0.08198)ᵈ − 1, which grows with d (0.082 at d = 1, 0.604 at 6, 1.199 at 10). Monte Carlo's constant gets worse with dimension even though its rate does not.
  • The verdict, with 10⁵ evaluations. At d = 1, the grid's error is 4 × 10⁻¹² against Monte Carlo's 9 × 10⁻⁴: no contest. At d = 4 the rates are equal, and the grid (m = 17, n = 83,521, error 5.8 × 10⁻⁴) keeps its smaller constant ahead of Monte Carlo's 2.1 × 10⁻³. At d = 6 the grid (m = 6, n = 46,656) has error 6.9 × 10⁻³ against Monte Carlo's 3.6 × 10⁻³ at the same n. At d = 10 the largest affordable grid has 3 points per axis and error 0.045, ten times Monte Carlo's. At d = 20, two points per axis already cost a million evaluations.
  • Honest caveats. Monte Carlo's error is random and its bar is only approximate; the grid's error is deterministic. Methods that exploit smoothness in moderate dimension beat both: sparse grids, and quasi-Monte Carlo, which replaces random points by low-discrepancy sequences and achieves errors close to O((ln n)ᵈ/n) for smooth integrands. Monte Carlo's unbeatable virtues are generality (any integrand you can evaluate, any dimension, discontinuities included) and a built-in error estimate.

06 Variance reduction

Since the error of a Monte Carlo estimate is σ/√N, halving it costs four times the draws, while halving σ costs nothing but thought. Variance reduction rewrites the same expectation as the mean of a different random quantity with a smaller variance, keeping the estimator unbiased (or nearly so). Five ideas cover almost all practice: pair each draw with a negatively correlated twin (antithetic variates); subtract a correlated quantity with a known mean (control variates); force the draws to spread evenly over the input space (stratification); sample more where the integrand matters and reweight (importance sampling); and, when comparing two systems, drive both with the same random numbers (common random numbers). All five are compared on one integral in the figure.

Delivered: antithetic variates

  • The idea. Averaging correlated variables showed that the mean of two variables with correlation ρ has variance σ²(1 + ρ)/2, below the iid σ²/2 when ρ < 0, and promised that deliberately pairing each draw with a negatively correlated twin is a standard trick. For θ = E g(U), the twin of U is 1 − U: same distribution, so (g(U) + g(1 − U))/2 is unbiased, and its variance is (σ² + Cov(g(U), g(1 − U)))/2 per pair, that is per two evaluations.
  • Why it helps for monotone g. If g is monotone, g(U) and g(1 − U) are negatively correlated. Proof. Let h(u) = g(1 − u), which moves opposite to g, and let U, V be iid uniforms. For every pair of values, (g(U) − g(V)) and (h(U) − h(V)) have opposite signs (or one is zero), so E[(g(U) − g(V))(h(U) − h(V))] ≤ 0. Expanding the product, this expectation equals 2Cov(g(U), h(U)). ∎ The same argument works for any monotone function of a vector of uniforms when every coordinate is flipped.
  • Worked. For g = eˣ: Cov(e^U, e^(1−U)) = E e^1 − (e − 1)² = e − (e − 1)² = −0.23421, a correlation of −0.9677. Per evaluation, the variance falls from 0.24204 to 0.24204 − 0.23421 = 0.00783: the same accuracy from 31 times fewer evaluations.
  • When it backfires. For a function symmetric about ½, g(1 − U) = g(U): the twin is the same number, the correlation is +1, and each pair is one evaluation paid for twice, doubling the variance per evaluation. For g(x) = (π/2)·sin(πx) the antithetic estimator is exactly twice as bad as plain Monte Carlo at the same cost. For a non-monotone g the sign of the covariance must be checked, or estimated from a pilot run.

Control variates

  • The idea. Suppose that alongside each Y = h(X) you can compute a C whose mean E C = c₀ is known exactly and which is correlated with Y. Then Y − β(C − c₀) has the same mean as Y for every β, and its variance is Var Y − 2β·Cov(Y, C) + β²·Var C, a quadratic in β.
  • The optimal coefficient. Setting the derivative to zero: β* = Cov(Y, C)/Var C, the slope of the regression of Y on C. The minimum variance is Var Y·(1 − ρ²_YC): a control with correlation 0.9 removes 81% of the variance, one with 0.99 removes 98%. In practice β* is estimated by the least-squares slope from the same draws; that introduces a bias of order 1/N, negligible next to the 1/√N noise. Several controls at once is multiple regression.
  • Worked. For Y = e^U take C = U, with known mean ½ and variance 1/12. Cov(e^U, U) = E[U e^U] − ½(e − 1) = 1 − 0.85914 = 0.14086, so β* = 0.14086 × 12 = 1.6903 and ρ² = 0.14086²/(0.24204/12) = 0.98372. The variance per evaluation falls to 0.24204 × (1 − 0.98372) = 0.00394: a factor of 61.
  • Where good controls come from. A simpler version of the same model whose answer is known in closed form: the arithmetic-average option price as a control for the geometric one, a linearised model for a nonlinear one, the input itself as above. In machine learning the baseline subtracted from a policy-gradient estimate is a control variate (§12).

Stratification

  • The idea. Cut the input space into strata S₁, …, S_K with probabilities w₁, …, w_K, draw nₖ points inside each stratum, and combine the stratum means with the known weights: θ̂_S = Σ wₖ·Ȳₖ. It is unbiased, and its variance is Σ wₖ²σₖ²/nₖ, with σₖ² the variance of h within stratum k.
  • Never worse with proportional allocation. With nₖ = N·wₖ the variance becomes (1/N)·Σ wₖσₖ². By the law of total variance (law of total variance), plain Monte Carlo has Var θ̂ = (1/N)·[Σ wₖσₖ² + Σ wₖ(μₖ − θ)²]: within-stratum variance plus between-stratum variance. Proportional stratification deletes the second term, so it can only help, and it helps most when the stratum means μₖ differ a lot. ∎ The Neyman allocation nₖ ∝ wₖσₖ does better still, (1/N)(Σ wₖσₖ)², which is at most the proportional value by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; it needs the σₖ, usually from a pilot. The survey-sampling version of the same argument is in why stratification wins.
  • Worked. For e^U with K equal strata [(k − 1)/K, k/K) and proportional allocation, the variance per evaluation is (1/K)·Σσₖ²: 0.0649 for K = 2 (factor 3.7), 0.0165 for K = 4 (14.6), 0.00266 for K = 10 (91) and 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ for K = 100 (about 9,100). For a smooth integrand, σₖ shrinks like the stratum width, so the gain grows like K²; with one point per stratum the error falls like N^(−3/2) instead of N^(−1/2). In several dimensions the scalable version is Latin hypercube sampling, which stratifies every coordinate separately.

Importance sampling

  • The identity. For any density q that is positive wherever h·f is non-zero, θ = ∫ h f = ∫ h·(f/q)·q = E_q[h(X)·w(X)] with the importance weight w = f/q. So draw Xᵢ ~ q and average h(Xᵢ)w(Xᵢ): the estimator is unbiased for every such q.
  • The variance. Var_q(h·w) = ∫ h²f²/q − θ². It is small when q is roughly proportional to |h|·f, and the choice q* = |h|f/∫|h|f minimises it (for h ≥ 0 it gives variance 0: every draw returns exactly θ). Proof: by Jensen, E_q[(hw)²] ≥ (E_q|hw|)² = (∫|h|f)² for every q, and q* attains it. ∎ Using q* requires knowing ∫|h|f, which is essentially the answer, so the theorem is a guide, not a recipe: imitate the shape of |h|f.
  • Worked: a rare event. θ = P(Z > 3) = 0.0013499 for Z ~ N(0, 1). Plain Monte Carlo has standard deviation √(θ(1 − θ)) = 0.0367 per draw and needs about 284,000 draws for a 10% relative error at 95%. Sample instead from q = N(3, 1), centred on the event: the weight is φ(x)/φ(x − 3) = e^(4.5 − 3x), and completing the square gives E_q[w²·1{X > 3}] = e⁹·(1 − Φ(6)) = 7.994 × 10⁻⁶, so the variance per draw is 7.994 × 10⁻⁶ − θ² = 6.17 × 10⁻⁶ (SD 0.00248). That is 218 times smaller, and about 1,300 draws now suffice.
  • The danger: a light-tailed q. The weights explode wherever q is much smaller than f. For the target N(0, 1) and proposal N(0, σ²), E_q[w²] = σ²/√(2σ² − 1): 1 at σ = 1, 1.51 at σ = 2 (too wide costs a little), 5.6 at σ = 0.71, and infinite for σ² ≤ ½. Then the estimator is still unbiased and consistent, but its variance is infinite, the CLT fails, and the reported error bar is fiction: long stretches of small weights end in a single enormous one. This is the heavy-tail failure of when the variance is infinite, manufactured by a bad proposal; the rule is the one rejection sampling imposed in §03: the proposal's tails must be at least as heavy as the target's (heavy tails).
  • Self-normalised weights. When f is known only up to a constant (a posterior), use θ̂ = Σ wᵢh(Xᵢ)/Σ wᵢ with unnormalised weights. It is consistent with a bias of order 1/N. The effective sample size of the weights, (Σwᵢ)²/Σwᵢ², reports how many equally weighted draws the sample is worth; when it is a tiny fraction of N (as it becomes in high dimension, where weights concentrate on a single draw), the estimate cannot be trusted.

Delivered: common random numbers

  • The idea. To estimate a difference θ_A − θ_B between two systems, the variance of the estimated difference is (Var Y_A + Var Y_B − 2Cov(Y_A, Y_B))/N. Independent simulations make the covariance zero; driving both systems with the same random numbers makes it positive whenever the two outputs respond to the inputs in the same direction, and the difference becomes much more precise. It is the paired design of an experiment, applied to a simulation, and the covariance formula is the one in correlation.
  • Worked. Compare θ_A = E e^(1.1U) = 1.82197 with θ_B = E e^U = 1.71828; the true difference is 0.10369. With independent uniforms the variance of one simulated difference is 0.32816 + 0.24204 = 0.57020; with the same U for both it is 0.00659, 87 times smaller. With 1,000 pairs, the first gives 0.104 ± 0.047, an interval almost as wide as the effect itself; the second gives 0.104 ± 0.005.
  • In practice. Synchronise the randomness: give each source of randomness (arrivals, service times, failures) its own stream, so that a change in one part of the model does not shift which uniform is used where. The inverse transform helps because it maps each uniform monotonically to a draw. This is how simulation-based optimisation and A/B comparisons of policies are done; it does not change either estimate's own variance, only that of the difference.

07 The Bernoulli and Poisson processes

The simplest stochastic processes count events. The Bernoulli process runs a fair-or-biased coin once per time frame and counts successes; its counts are binomial and its gaps geometric. Shrink the frame while keeping the rate of events fixed and it becomes the Poisson process, the model of events that occur "completely at random" in continuous time: counts in disjoint windows are independent and Poisson, the gaps between events are independent exponentials, and the waiting time to the k-th event is gamma. The Poisson process is closed under merging and under random deletion, which makes it the arrival model of queues, networks and point patterns, and it produces one famous trap: a random moment falls, on average, in a gap twice as long as the typical one.

The Bernoulli process

  • Definition. Divide time into frames of length Δ; in each frame, independently, an event occurs with probability p (at most one per frame). X(n), the number of events in the first n frames, is Bin(n, p) (binomial). The number of frames until the next event is geometric with success probability p, and the number until the k-th is negative binomial. The process has independent and stationary increments and is a Markov chain that moves from i to i + 1 with probability p and stays put otherwise. Its rate is λ = p/Δ events per unit time.
  • Worked: jobs arriving at a server. Jobs arrive at 2 per minute. With frames of Δ = 3 seconds = 0.05 minutes, p = λΔ = 0.1. In one minute (20 frames) the count is Bin(20, 0.1), and P(more than 3 jobs) = 0.1330. The gap between jobs is T = YΔ with Y geometric: E T = Δ/p = 1/λ = 0.5 minutes and Var T = (1 − p)/λ² = 0.225. The chance of no job in the next 30 seconds (10 frames) is (1 − p)¹⁰ = 0.9¹⁰ = 0.3487.

Delivered: the Poisson process as the continuous-time limit

  • The limit. Keep λ fixed and let Δ → 0, so p = λΔ → 0 and the number of frames in [0, t], n = t/Δ, grows. The count is Bin(t/Δ, λΔ) with mean λt, which converges to Poisson(λt) (the Poisson approximation); the gap satisfies P(T > t) = (1 − λΔ)^(t/Δ) → e^(−λt), an exponential (the geometric limit). The Poisson distribution, the exponential and the family tree all promised that the process itself would be developed here.
  • Three equivalent definitions. A counting process N(t) with N(0) = 0 is a Poisson process of rate λ if any one of these holds: (a) increments over disjoint intervals are independent, and N(s + t) − N(s) ~ Poisson(λt); (b) the gaps T₁, T₂, … between successive events are iid Exp(λ); (c) increments are independent and, in a short interval of length h, P(one event) = λh + o(h) and P(two or more) = o(h). Definition (c) is the "rare events" description, (b) is the simulation recipe, and (a) is what one computes with.
  • Worked: the same server, in continuous time. With λ = 2 per minute and no frames, P(more than 3 jobs in a minute) = 1 − P(Poisson(2) ≤ 3) = 0.1429, P(no job in 30 seconds) = e^(−1) = 0.3679 and Var T = 1/λ² = 0.25. Forbidding two jobs per frame made the Bernoulli version slightly less variable; refining the frame closes the gap: (1 − 2Δ)^(0.5/Δ) = 0.3487, 0.3642, 0.3675 for Δ = 0.05, 0.01, 0.001 minutes, tending to 0.3679. For 10 minutes, P(more than 30 jobs) is 0.0095 in the Bernoulli version (exact Bin(200, 0.1)) and 0.0135 in the Poisson version; the normal approximation with continuity correction, 0.0067, understates the binomial tail by about 30%, a skewness effect.

Gaps, waiting times, and the count–time duality

  • Gaps are exponential. P(T₁ > t) = P(N(t) = 0) = e^(−λt), so the first gap is Exp(λ). Given that the first event happened at time s, the next gap exceeds t exactly when (s, s + t] contains no event, which by independent increments has probability e^(−λt) whatever s is: the second gap is Exp(λ) and independent of the first, and so on. (Conditioning on T₁ = s, an event of probability zero, is made rigorous by conditioning on T₁ lying in a small interval.)
  • Waiting times are gamma. The time of the k-th event is Sₖ = T₁ + ⋯ + Tₖ ~ Gamma(k, λ), with mean k/λ and variance k/λ² (the gamma distribution).
  • The duality. The k-th event has happened by time t exactly when at least k events have occurred in [0, t]: {Sₖ ≤ t} = {N(t) ≥ k}. So a gamma cdf is a Poisson tail, P(Sₖ ≤ t) = 1 − Σ_(j<k) e^(−λt)(λt)ʲ/j!, and the identity can be used in whichever direction is easier.
  • Worked. Calls arrive at 2 per hour. The probability that the third call arrives within the first hour is P(S₃ ≤ 1) = P(N(1) ≥ 3) = 1 − e^(−2)(1 + 2 + 2) = 1 − 5e^(−2) = 0.3233, computed without touching a gamma density. A web site gets 7 hits per minute; the 10,000-th hit arrives at S ~ Gamma(10,000, 7), with mean 10,000/7 = 1,428.6 minutes (23.8 hours) and standard deviation 100/7 = 14.3 minutes. The probability that it arrives within 24 hours = 1,440 minutes is exactly P(N(1,440) ≥ 10,000) with N(1,440) ~ Poisson(10,080), which is 0.7885; the normal approximation to the gamma gives 0.7881.
  • Where the points fall, given how many. Given N(t) = n, the n event times are distributed like n independent U(0, t) points, sorted (order statistics). For one point: P(S₁ ≤ s | N(t) = 1) = P(N(s) = 1, N(t) − N(s) = 0)/P(N(t) = 1) = λs e^(−λs)·e^(−λ(t−s))/(λt e^(−λt)) = s/t. This gives a second simulation recipe (draw the count, then scatter uniform points), the one that generalises to Poisson processes in the plane or in space, where "gaps" have no meaning.

Superposition and thinning, proved by construction

  • Superposition. If N₁ and N₂ are independent Poisson processes with rates λ₁ and λ₂, the merged process N = N₁ + N₂ is a Poisson process of rate λ₁ + λ₂. Proof. Increments of N over disjoint intervals are sums of independent increments of N₁ and N₂, so they are independent; over an interval of length t the increment is a sum of independent Poisson(λ₁t) and Poisson(λ₂t) counts, which is Poisson((λ₁ + λ₂)t) (sums: convolution). That is definition (a). ∎ Moreover each merged event comes from the first stream with probability λ₁/(λ₁ + λ₂), independently of everything else: the next event is the winner of a race between two exponential clocks, and memorylessness restarts the race after every event (competing exponentials).
  • Worked. Two servers complete jobs at rates 2 and 3 per hour. Completions form a Poisson process of rate 5: the mean gap is 12 minutes, and each completion is server 1's with probability 0.4, independently of which server finished the previous job.
  • Thinning. Keep each event of a rate-λ Poisson process independently with probability p. The kept events form a Poisson process of rate pλ, the discarded ones a Poisson process of rate (1 − p)λ, and the two are independent. Proof. Over an interval of length t, with K kept and D discarded, P(K = k, D = d) = P(N = k + d)·C(k + d, k)pᵏ(1 − p)ᵈ = e^(−λt)(λt)^(k+d)/(k + d)! · (k + d)!/(k!d!) · pᵏ(1 − p)ᵈ, which factorises as [e^(−pλt)(pλt)ᵏ/k!]·[e^(−(1−p)λt)((1−p)λt)ᵈ/d!]: independent Poisson counts. Independence across disjoint intervals is inherited from N and the independent coin flips. ∎
  • Worked, and a surprise. Calls arrive at 10 per hour and 30% are sales: sales calls form a Poisson process of rate 3, so the chance of an hour with no sales call is e^(−3) = 0.0498. And the number of non-sales calls in an hour says nothing about the number of sales calls. That is special to the Poisson: for a fixed total count, the kept and discarded counts would be negatively dependent (binomial split of a fixed n).

The inspection paradox, honestly

  • The puzzle. Buses arrive as a Poisson process with a mean gap of 10 minutes. You arrive at a random time. How long do you wait on average? Not 5 minutes, half a gap: 10 minutes. And the gap you land in, from the previous bus to the next, averages 20 minutes in the long run, twice the typical gap (at a fixed time t it is 2/λ − e^(−λt)/λ, because the first gap is cut off at 0); the 10-minute wait is exact at every time.
  • Why: length-biased sampling. A random moment is more likely to fall in a long gap than in a short one, in proportion to its length. If the gaps have density f(x) and mean m, the gap covering a random time has density x·f(x)/m. For exponential gaps that is λ²x e^(−λx), a Gamma(2, λ) with mean 2/λ. Your wait is the remaining part of that gap; by memorylessness it is Exp(λ), mean 1/λ, and so is the time back to the previous bus, in the long run. Nothing about the buses is paradoxical; the sampling is.
  • For any gap distribution. The covering gap has mean E[G²]/E[G] = m + σ²/m and the expected wait is E[G²]/(2E[G]). Perfectly regular buses every 10 minutes give a wait of 5; exponential gaps (σ = m) give 10; bunched buses with σ > m give more. Reducing the variability of the gaps, not just their mean, is what shortens waits.
  • The same bias elsewhere. The average class size a student experiences exceeds the average class size a school reports; your friends have more friends than you do on average; a length-of-stay survey of patients currently in hospital overrepresents long stays. Each is a length-biased sample of the units, weighted by the very quantity being averaged.

08 Markov chains

A Markov chain is a random sequence that remembers nothing but its present state: given where it is now, where it goes next does not depend on how it got there. That single assumption turns the whole future into powers of one matrix of transition probabilities, and under mild conditions (the chain is finite, or more generally positive recurrent; every state can reach every other; and the chain is not trapped in a cycle) the distribution of the state converges, from any start, to a unique stationary distribution π solving πP = π. This section sets up the matrix algebra, classifies states, proves that detailed balance implies stationarity, solves a three-state chain exactly, and computes ruin probabilities for a chain that is absorbed instead of settling down.

The Markov property

  • Definition. A sequence X₀, X₁, X₂, … on a countable state space is a Markov chain if, for every n and all states, P(Xₙ₊₁ = j | Xₙ = i, Xₙ₋₁ = iₙ₋₁, …, X₀ = i₀) = P(Xₙ₊₁ = j | Xₙ = i). It is homogeneous when this probability pᵢⱼ does not depend on n; all chains here are. "The future is conditionally independent of the past given the present" (conditional probability).
  • The present means the exact state. The property conditions on Xₙ = i. Knowing only that the present state lies in a set (Xₙ ∈ A) does not in general make the past irrelevant: if the states in A have different futures, the past tells you which one you are probably in. A definition of the Markov property written with sets in place of states is wrong for that reason, and lumping states of a Markov chain together usually destroys the property.
  • Path probabilities. By the chain rule and the Markov property, P(X₀ = i₀, X₁ = i₁, …, Xₙ = iₙ) = μ₀(i₀)·p_(i₀i₁)·p_(i₁i₂)⋯p_(iₙ₋₁iₙ), where μ₀ is the initial distribution. The initial distribution and the transition matrix determine everything.
  • What is and is not Markov. The count of a Bernoulli or Poisson process, a gambler's fortune, a queue length with exponential times, a random walk on a graph: Markov. Tomorrow's weather given only today's is a modelling assumption (it often fails, and the fix is to enlarge the state to "today and yesterday", which restores the property). A stock price's level given its trend is not Markov in the price alone. The sequence of tokens produced by a language model is not Markov in the last token, but it is Markov in the whole prefix (§12).

  • The matrix. P = (pᵢⱼ), row i = the distribution of the next state from i. Every entry is non-negative and every row sums to 1: a stochastic matrix.
  • n steps. The n-step probabilities pᵢⱼ⁽ⁿ⁾ = P(Xₘ₊ₙ = j | Xₘ = i) satisfy the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations pᵢⱼ⁽ᵐ⁺ⁿ⁾ = Σₖ pᵢₖ⁽ᵐ⁾pₖⱼ⁽ⁿ⁾. Classifying states: working definitions

  • Communication. State j is accessible from i if pᵢⱼ⁽ⁿ⁾ > 0 for some n; i and j communicate if each is accessible from the other. Communication splits the states into classes. The chain is irreducible if there is one class: every state can reach every other. A set of states the chain can never leave is closed; a closed single state (pᵢᵢ = 1) is absorbing.
  • Recurrence. State i is recurrent if, starting from i, the chain returns to i with probability 1, and transient otherwise. Equivalently, i is recurrent if and only if Σₙ pᵢᵢ⁽ⁿ⁾ = ∞, the expected number of returns (if each return has probability r < 1 of being followed by another, the number of returns is geometric with finite mean). A recurrent state is positive recurrent if its mean return time mᵢ is finite and null recurrent otherwise. Recurrence, positivity and the period below are class properties.
  • Facts for finite chains. A finite chain has at least one recurrent class, and every recurrent state of a finite chain is positive recurrent. An irreducible finite chain is positive recurrent.
  • An infinite example. The ±1 random walk on the integers with up-probability p has p₀₀⁽²ⁿ⁾ = C(2n, n)(pq)ⁿ ≈ (4pq)ⁿ/√(πn) by Stirling. The sum diverges only when 4pq = 1, that is p = ½: the fair walk is recurrent (null recurrent: it returns surely, but the mean return time is infinite), and any biased walk is transient. This is the return of the fair coin's heads-minus-tails gap in the strong law.
  • Period. The period of i is d(i) = gcd{n ≥ 1 : pᵢᵢ⁽ⁿ⁾ > 0}; the state is aperiodic if d(i) = 1. A deterministic 3-cycle has period 3. A single self-loop (pᵢᵢ > 0) makes an irreducible chain aperiodic, which is why "lazy" versions of chains (stay put with probability ½) are used to kill periodicity.

The stationary distribution

  • Definition. A probability vector π is stationary (invariant) if πP = π, that is πⱼ = Σᵢ πᵢpᵢⱼ for every j: if X₀ ~ π then X₁ ~ πP = π, and by induction Xₙ ~ π for every n. The chain still moves; only the distribution of its position stays put. Equivalently, π is a left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1, normalised to sum to 1.
  • Existence and uniqueness. An irreducible chain has a stationary distribution if and only if it is positive recurrent, and then it is unique and πᵢ = 1/mᵢ, the reciprocal of the mean return time (Kac's formula): a state the chain returns to every 4 steps on average holds a quarter of the stationary mass. In particular every irreducible finite chain has a unique π, with all entries positive. A chain with two closed classes has a whole segment of stationary distributions (any mixture of the two).
  • Computing it. The equations πP = π are linearly dependent (they always have the solution 0), so replace one of them by Σπᵢ = 1 and solve; the shared helper PV.stationary does exactly this. Balance across a cut is often quicker: in stationarity, the probability flow from a set A to its complement equals the flow back.
  • Worked: the shared device, two ways. Directly, the balance equations give π₁ = 0.8π₀ and π₂ = 0.16π₀, and normalising, π = (1, 0.8, 0.16)/1.96 = (25/49, 20/49, 4/49) = (0.5102, 0.4082, 0.0816). Structurally, each user is an independent two-state chain that connects at rate 0.2 and disconnects at 0.5, so it is connected a fraction 0.2/(0.2 + 0.5) = 2/7 of the time, and the number connected is Bin(2, 2/7): ((5/7)², 2(2/7)(5/7), (2/7)²) = (25, 20, 4)/49. Both users are connected about 8% of the minutes, and the mean time between such minutes is 1/π₂ = 49/4 = 12.25 minutes.

The convergence theorem

  • Statement. If the chain is irreducible, aperiodic and positive recurrent (for a finite chain: irreducible and aperiodic), with stationary distribution π, then for every initial state i and every j, pᵢⱼ⁽ⁿ⁾ → πⱼ as n → ∞; equivalently the total-variation distance ‖μₙ − π‖ = ½Σⱼ|μₙ(j) − πⱼ| → 0 from any start. The rows of Pⁿ all converge to π: the chain forgets where it started.
  • Why, in one idea (coupling). Run one copy from i and an independent copy from π. Irreducibility and aperiodicity guarantee that the pair, itself a finite irreducible chain, eventually visits a diagonal state (k, k); from then on let the two copies move together. After the meeting time τ, the first copy has the distribution of the second, which is π, so ‖μₙ − π‖ ≤ P(τ > n) → 0. ∎ (sketch)
  • Both conditions are needed. Periodic: the cycle 0 → 1 → 2 → 0 has π = (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) but Pⁿ rotates forever. Reducible: with two absorbing states, the limit depends on which one the chain falls into, so on the start.
  • How fast. For a finite chain the distance decays geometrically, at a rate set by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus |λ₂| of P (all eigenvalues satisfy |λ| ≤ 1, and 1 is the eigenvalue of π): roughly ‖μₙ − π‖ ≈ C·|λ₂|ⁿ. The spectral gap 1 − |λ₂| measures mixing speed. For the shared device the eigenvalues are 1, 0.3 and 0.09 (each user's two-state chain has eigenvalue 1 − 0.2 − 0.5 = 0.3), and from two connected users the distances after 1 to 5 steps are 0.2602, 0.0877, 0.0272, 0.0082 and 0.0025, each about 0.3 times the last.

Reversibility and detailed balance

  • Detailed balance. A distribution π satisfies detailed balance with P if πᵢpᵢⱼ = πⱼpⱼᵢ for all i, j: in stationarity, the flow of probability from i to j equals the flow from j to i, pair by pair.
  • Theorem: detailed balance implies stationarity. Proof. Sum detailed balance over i: Σᵢ πᵢpᵢⱼ = Σᵢ πⱼpⱼᵢ = πⱼ Σᵢ pⱼᵢ = πⱼ, because row j of P sums to 1. That is πP = π. ∎ The converse fails: stationarity balances the total flow into each state, detailed balance balances every pair.
  • Reversibility. A stationary chain satisfying detailed balance is reversible: P(X₀ = i, X₁ = j) = πᵢpᵢⱼ = πⱼpⱼᵢ = P(X₀ = j, X₁ = i), and the same for whole paths, so a film of the chain looks statistically the same played backwards. Birth–death chains (moves only to neighbouring states) are always reversible, because balance across each cut between k and k + 1 is detailed balance for that pair. A random walk on an undirected graph, moving to a uniformly chosen neighbour, is reversible with πᵢ ∝ deg(i), since πᵢ·(1/deg(i)) is the same constant for every edge.
  • Worked. The shared device is reversible: π₀p₀₁ = (25/49)(0.32) = 8/49 = (20/49)(0.40) = π₁p₁₀; π₀p₀₂ = 1/49 = π₂p₂₀; π₁p₁₂ = 2/49 = π₂p₂₁. A lazy cycle, P = [[½, ½, 0], [0, ½, ½], [½, 0, ½]], has the uniform stationary distribution (every column also sums to 1) but violates detailed balance, since π₀p₀₁ = 1/6 while π₁p₁₀ = 0: it circulates. Its other eigenvalues are complex, ¼ ± 0.433i, of modulus ½.
  • Why it matters. Detailed balance is a local equation, checkable one pair at a time, and it is how Markov chain Monte Carlo is designed: choose transition probabilities that satisfy detailed balance with a target π, and π is automatically stationary (§10).

Absorbing chains and the gambler's ruin

  • Absorption. When some states are absorbing, the questions change from "where does the chain settle" to "where is it absorbed, and when". Order the states as transient then absorbing, so that P = [[Q, R], [0, I]]. The fundamental matrix N = (I − Q)⁻¹ = I + Q + Q² + ⋯ counts expected visits to each transient state; absorption probabilities are B = NR, and expected times to absorption are the row sums of N.
  • The gambler's ruin. A gambler with fortune i bets 1 unit at a time, winning with probability p and losing with q = 1 − p, and stops at 0 (ruin) or at a target N. Let hᵢ be the probability of reaching N first. Conditioning on the first bet, hᵢ = p·hᵢ₊₁ + q·hᵢ₋₁, with h₀ = 0, h_N = 1. The solution is hᵢ = (1 − (q/p)ⁱ)/(1 − (q/p)ᴺ) for p ≠ ½ and hᵢ = i/N for a fair game. The expected number of bets is i(N − i) for a fair game.
  • Worked. Betting on red at roulette (p = 18/38), starting with 10 and aiming for 20, succeeds with probability 0.2585, not 0.5, and lasts 91.8 bets on average; a fair coin game succeeds with probability 0.5 and lasts 100 bets. The fair game is a martingale (the expected fortune never changes), yet against an opponent with unlimited funds (N → ∞) i/N → 0: ruin is certain, though it takes a long time. That is the precise counterpart of the gambler's fallacy: nothing pulls the fortune back toward where it started.

09 The ergodic theorem and the Markov-chain CLT

Simulating a Markov chain produces one long dependent sequence, not independent draws. Two theorems replace the iid laws. The ergodic theorem says that time averages along a single path converge to averages under the stationary distribution, for any start, as long as the chain is irreducible and positive recurrent; aperiodicity is not needed. The Markov-chain central limit theorem says that the error of a time average is approximately normal with variance σ_h²(1 + 2Σₖρₖ)/N, inflated by the chain's autocorrelations. The inflation factor τ = 1 + 2Σρₖ is the integrated autocorrelation time, and N/τ is the effective sample size, the number every MCMC diagnostic reports.

Delivered: the ergodic theorem

  • Statement. Let X₀, X₁, … be an irreducible, positive recurrent Markov chain with stationary distribution π, started anywhere, and let h satisfy E_π|h| = Σⱼ πⱼ|h(j)| < ∞. Then, with probability 1, (1/N)·Σₙ₌₀^(N−1) h(Xₙ) → E_π h = Σⱼ πⱼh(j). In particular, taking h = 1_{j}, the long-run fraction of time spent in j is πⱼ. Why simulation works promised exactly this replacement of the law of large numbers for dependent draws.
  • Why (regeneration). Fix a state i. Each time the chain returns to i it starts afresh (the strong Markov property), so the excursions between successive visits are iid. The sum of h over an excursion has a finite mean, and so does the excursion's length mᵢ. Applying the strong law to the excursion sums and to the excursion lengths separately, the time average converges to E[sum over an excursion]/mᵢ, and that ratio equals E_π h (it is how π can be constructed in the first place). ∎ (sketch)
  • The conditions, honestly. Irreducible: otherwise the limit depends on which closed class the path ends up in. Positive recurrent: for the null-recurrent fair random walk on the integers, the fraction of time spent in every state tends to 0, and there is no stationary distribution to converge to. Not needed: aperiodicity. The deterministic 3-cycle has Pⁿ rotating forever, yet every path spends exactly a third of its time in each state; the time average converges even though the distribution at a fixed time does not. Aperiodicity is required for μₙ → π, not for averages. For chains on continuous state spaces the corresponding condition is Harris recurrence, which holds for all the standard MCMC samplers under mild conditions on the target and proposal.

Estimating τ from the output

  • Batch means. Cut the run into b batches of length L, much longer than τ. The batch means are then nearly independent, each with variance about σ²_as/L, so L × (sample variance of the batch means) estimates σ²_as. Too-short batches underestimate it, too few batches make the estimate noisy.
  • Truncated autocorrelation sums. Summing all empirical autocorrelations is useless (the far lags are pure noise, and their sum does not converge). The initial positive sequence rule sums adjacent pairs ρ̂₂ₘ + ρ̂₂ₘ₊₁, which are positive for a reversible chain, and stops at the first negative pair. The shared helper PV.ess implements it; on an AR(1) series with r = 0.9 and 200,000 draws it returns τ̂ between 18.2 and 19.3 across four seeds, against the exact 19.

10 Markov chain Monte Carlo

Rejection and importance sampling need a proposal that covers the whole target, and in high dimension no such proposal exists. Markov chain Monte Carlo turns the problem around: instead of independent draws from the target π, it builds a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is π, runs it, and averages along the path, relying on the ergodic theorem for consistency and on the Markov-chain CLT for the error bar. The Metropolis–Hastings rule makes any proposal mechanism satisfy detailed balance with any target known up to a constant; Gibbs sampling updates one coordinate at a time from its full conditional distribution. The price is dependence: the draws are correlated, the chain must first forget its start, and no diagnostic can prove that it has explored everything.

The idea

  • The setting. The target is a density or pmf π(x) = π̃(x)/Z where π̃ can be evaluated and the normalising constant Z = ∫ π̃ cannot. The central example is a Bayesian posterior, π(θ | data) ∝ L(θ)·p(θ), whose constant is the intractable evidence ∫ L(θ)p(θ) dθ. Conjugate pairs such as the beta prior with binomial data (the beta and conjugacy) are the rare cases where the posterior is a known family; hierarchical models (mixtures and hierarchies), logistic regression and almost everything else are not.
  • The plan. (i) Design a transition rule p(x, y) that satisfies detailed balance with π, using only ratios π̃(y)/π̃(x), in which Z cancels; (ii) make sure the chain is irreducible and aperiodic; (iii) run it from some start, discard an initial stretch, and estimate E_π h by the time average, with the error bar σ̂_h√(τ̂/N) of §09.

Metropolis–Hastings

  • The algorithm. From the current state x, propose y ~ q(· | x) from any convenient proposal distribution. Accept the move with probability α(x, y) = min{1, π̃(y)q(x | y) / (π̃(x)q(y | x))}; otherwise stay at x (the current state is repeated in the output; dropping repeats would bias the averages).
  • Theorem: π satisfies detailed balance. For y ≠ x the transition density is p(x, y) = q(y | x)·α(x, y). Then π(x)p(x, y) = π(x)q(y | x)·min{1, π(y)q(x | y)/(π(x)q(y | x))} = min{π(x)q(y | x), π(y)q(x | y)}, an expression symmetric in x and y, so it also equals π(y)p(y, x). The rejection mass that keeps the chain at x satisfies detailed balance trivially (it moves nothing from x to y ≠ x). By §08, π is stationary. ∎ The constant Z appears in both π(y) and π(x) and cancels.
  • The extra conditions. Stationarity is not convergence. A proposal that can reach every region of positive π (a normal random-walk step, for instance) makes the chain irreducible, and a positive probability of rejection makes it aperiodic; then the ergodic theorem applies. The Markov-chain CLT additionally needs geometric ergodicity, which random-walk Metropolis has for targets with light (exponential or lighter) tails and can lack for heavy-tailed targets.
  • Random-walk Metropolis. With a symmetric proposal y = x + s·Z, q(y | x) = q(x | y) and α = min{1, π̃(y)/π̃(x)}: always accept an uphill move, accept a downhill move with probability equal to the density ratio. Worked, on the two-mode target π̃(x) = ½φ(x + 3) + ½φ(x − 3): from x = 3 (the top of the right mode), a proposal of 1.5 is accepted with probability π̃(1.5)/π̃(3) = 0.325, a proposal of 0 (the valley) with probability 0.022, and a proposal of −3 (the other peak) always.
  • Tuning the step. Too small a step is almost always accepted but crawls (a random walk with tiny steps); too large a step is almost always rejected and the chain sits still; both give large autocorrelation. For roughly normal targets the efficient acceptance rate is about 0.44 in one dimension, falling to about 0.23 in high dimension, where the optimal step shrinks like 2.38/√d standard deviations. On N(0, 1) with 5,000 iterations: step 2.4 accepts 45% and gives τ = 3.6; step 0.1 accepts 97% and gives τ = 313; step 40 accepts 3% and gives τ = 34.
  • The independence sampler. With a fixed proposal q(y), α = min{1, w(y)/w(x)} with the importance weight w = π̃/q of §06. It works well when q resembles π with heavier tails, and gets stuck for long stretches at any point with a huge weight when q is too light-tailed: the same failure as importance sampling, now visible as a flat trace.
  • Gradient-based proposals. Langevin proposals y = x + (s²/2)∇ln π̃(x) + s·Z drift uphill before diffusing; Hamiltonian Monte Carlo simulates a particle moving on the surface −ln π̃ for many steps and proposes the far endpoint. Both are Metropolis–Hastings with clever proposals, and both still accept or reject to keep π exactly stationary. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, with an automatic choice of path length, is the default in modern probabilistic programming.

Delivered: Gibbs sampling, from full conditionals only

  • The algorithm. For x = (x₁, …, x_d), the full conditional of coordinate i is π(xᵢ | x₋ᵢ), its distribution given all the others (conditional distributions). A Gibbs sweep replaces x₁ by a draw from π(x₁ | x₂, …, x_d), then x₂ by a draw from π(x₂ | x₁, x₃, …) using the new x₁, and so on. The closing list of Joint Distributions & Dependence promised "Markov chains that sample from joint distributions using only their full conditionals": this is that chain.
  • Why it is exact: Metropolis–Hastings that always accepts. Updating coordinate i is Metropolis–Hastings with the proposal q(y | x) = π(yᵢ | x₋ᵢ), where y differs from x only in coordinate i. The ratio is π(y)π(xᵢ | x₋ᵢ) / (π(x)π(yᵢ | x₋ᵢ)); writing π(y) = π(yᵢ | x₋ᵢ)π(x₋ᵢ) and π(x) = π(xᵢ | x₋ᵢ)π(x₋ᵢ), everything cancels and the ratio is 1. ∎ Each coordinate update leaves π stationary, so the sweep does too. No tuning, no rejections; the price is that full conditionals must be samplable (when one is not, a Metropolis step for that coordinate is substituted, "Metropolis within Gibbs").
  • Worked: conjugacy makes Gibbs easy. In the hierarchy p ~ Beta(a, b), X | p ~ Bin(n, p), the full conditionals are X | p ~ Bin(n, p) and, by beta–binomial conjugacy, p | X ~ Beta(a + X, b + n − X). Alternating the two draws samples the joint distribution, and the X-coordinate alone samples the beta-binomial marginal. Larger models built from conjugate pieces (hierarchical normal models, mixtures with latent labels, topic models) are sampled the same way, block by block.
  • Worked: where Gibbs is slow. For a standard bivariate normal with correlation ρ, the full conditionals are X | Y = y ~ N(ρy, 1 − ρ²) and the same with the roles swapped (the bivariate normal). One sweep maps X to ρ²X + noise, an AR(1) with coefficient ρ², so by §09 τ = (1 + ρ²)/(1 − ρ²): 1.67 at ρ = 0.5, 9.5 at ρ = 0.9, 99.5 at ρ = 0.99. Coordinate-wise moves along a narrow diagonal ridge are tiny. The cures are to update correlated coordinates together (block Gibbs) or to reparameterise so they are less correlated. Restricted Boltzmann machines are trained with exactly this kind of block Gibbs sampling (block Gibbs sampling).

Burn-in, mixing, and what diagnostics can and cannot certify

  • Burn-in. A chain started far from where π lives spends its first iterations travelling there; averages that include them are biased by the start. Discarding an initial stretch (burn-in or warm-up) removes most of that bias. The ergodic theorem guarantees the bias dies out anyway; for a geometrically ergodic chain it does so at rate 1/N; burn-in matters for runs that are not much longer than the travel time.
  • Mixing. How fast the chain forgets its start and moves across the target: the spectral gap of §08 for finite chains, the autocorrelation time τ of §09 in practice. A chain "mixes well" if its trace plot looks like stationary noise across the whole range of the target (a "fuzzy caterpillar"), and badly if it shows trends, long flat stretches (rejections) or long stays in one region.
  • Thinning. Keeping every k-th draw saves storage and reduces the autocorrelation of what is kept, but never increases the effective sample size of the full run; it discards information. Thin for memory, not for accuracy.
  • R̂ (potential scale reduction). Run m ≥ 4 chains from deliberately dispersed starting points. With W the average within-chain variance and B/n the variance between the chain means, R̂ = √(((n − 1)/n·W + B/n)/W). If the chains have converged to the same distribution, between-chain and within-chain variability agree and R̂ ≈ 1; chains that disagree push it above 1. Current practice computes it on split, rank-normalised chains and asks for R̂ < 1.01 together with an effective sample size of at least a few hundred for every quantity reported.
  • What they cannot certify. R̂, trace plots and effective sample sizes are all computed from the draws, so they can only detect problems visible in the draws: chains that disagree, trends, strong autocorrelation. They cannot detect a region of the target that no chain has visited. In the figure, with modes at ±5 and all four chains started in the right-hand mode, every chain stays there: R̂ = 1.001, the effective sample size is a healthy 550, and the pooled mean is 4.97 against the true 0. Diagnostics are necessary checks, not proofs of convergence; dispersed starts, tempering between modes, and knowledge of the target's structure are the only protection against what was never seen.

11 Queues: two Poisson processes in tension

A single-server queue is a contest between two processes: customers arrive as a Poisson process of rate λ, and a busy server completes them as a Poisson process of rate μₛ. The number in the system is a birth–death Markov chain in continuous time, its stationary distribution is geometric with ratio ρ = λ/μₛ, the utilisation, and the mean number in the system is ρ/(1 − ρ). That formula is gentle at ρ = ½ and explosive near ρ = 1: a busy server is a slow server. Little's law, L = λW, then converts between the number in the system and the time spent in it, for any queue whatsoever.

The M/M/1 queue

  • The model. In the notation A/S/c (arrival process / service distribution / number of servers), M/M/1 has Poisson ("Markov") arrivals at rate λ, exponential service times with rate μₛ (mean 1/μₛ), one server, first-come first-served, unlimited waiting room. (Here ρ denotes utilisation, not an autocorrelation.)
  • Why it is Markov. Because inter-arrival and service times are exponential, both clocks are memoryless: at any moment, the time to the next arrival is Exp(λ) and, if the server is busy, the remaining service is Exp(μₛ), whatever has happened before. So the number in the system N(t) is a continuous-time Markov chain that steps up at rate λ and down at rate μₛ when N > 0. Which happens first is a race of exponential clocks (competing exponentials): from a busy state, up with probability λ/(λ + μₛ).

The stationary distribution is geometric

  • Balance across a cut. In stationarity, the rate of transitions from n to n + 1 equals the rate from n + 1 to n: λπₙ = μₛπₙ₊₁. This is detailed balance for a birth–death chain (§08), and the same equations come out of the discrete-frame version of the queue in the limit of short frames. So πₙ₊₁ = ρπₙ and πₙ = ρⁿπ₀.
  • Normalisation needs ρ < 1. Σ ρⁿπ₀ = π₀/(1 − ρ) = 1 gives π₀ = 1 − ρ, so πₙ = (1 − ρ)ρⁿ: a geometric distribution counting failures before a success of probability 1 − ρ (the geometric). The server is idle a fraction π₀ = 1 − ρ of the time and busy a fraction ρ. For ρ ≥ 1 the series diverges: no stationary distribution exists, the chain is null recurrent (ρ = 1) or transient (ρ > 1), and the queue grows without bound.
  • Moments. L = E N = ρ/(1 − ρ), Var N = ρ/(1 − ρ)², and the mean number waiting (not in service) is L_q = L − ρ = ρ²/(1 − ρ).

Little's law, and the time in the system

  • Statement. For any queueing system in a stable long-run regime, L = λW: the mean number in the system equals the arrival rate times the mean time each customer spends in it. No assumption on the arrival process, the service distribution, the number of servers or the order of service is needed.
  • Why (the area argument). Draw each customer as a horizontal bar from arrival to departure. The total area of the bars up to time T can be computed two ways: integrating the number of bars crossing each instant gives ∫₀ᵀ N(t) dt ≈ T·L̄; adding bar lengths gives Σ Wᵢ ≈ A(T)·W̄, with A(T) arrivals, up to the bars still open at T. Divide by T: L̄ ≈ (A(T)/T)·W̄ = λ̂·W̄, and the edge effect vanishes as T → ∞ for a stable system. ∎ (sketch) Applied to the waiting line alone, L_q = λW_q; applied to the server alone, ρ = λ·E S.
  • The M/M/1 sojourn time. By Little's law, W = L/λ = 1/(μₛ − λ) and W_q = ρ/(μₛ − λ). The same answer comes directly: an arriving customer sees the stationary distribution (Poisson arrivals see time averages), finds N customers, and must wait for N exponential services (the one in progress included, by memorylessness) plus its own, so W = (L + 1)/μₛ = 1/(μₛ − λ). In fact the time in the system is exactly Exp(μₛ − λ) for first-come first-served.
  • Worked: a message channel. Messages arrive at 5 per minute and take 10 seconds each on average (μₛ = 6 per minute). Then ρ = 5/6: the channel is busy 83% of the time, L = 5 messages are in the system on average, L_q = 4.17 are waiting, W = 1/(6 − 5) = 1 minute and W_q = 50 seconds; 16% of the time there are 10 or more (ρ¹⁰ = 0.1615). With 10% more traffic (λ = 5.5, ρ = 11/12): L = 11, L_q = 10.08, W = 2 minutes, W_q = 110 seconds, and 10 or more messages 42% of the time. A 10% increase in load doubles the response time.

Why everything explodes near ρ = 1

  • The mean. L = ρ/(1 − ρ) is 1 at ρ = 0.5, 4 at 0.8, 9 at 0.9, 19 at 0.95 and 99 at 0.99. Each halving of the idle fraction 1 − ρ roughly doubles the queue.
  • The fluctuations. SD(N) = √ρ/(1 − ρ) is about as large as the mean itself (4.5 at ρ = 0.8, 9.5 at 0.9): a heavily loaded queue is not reliably long, it is wildly variable.
  • The memory. The queue's relaxation time, the time scale on which it forgets its current state, is 1/(μₛ(1 − √ρ)²): about 12 mean service times at ρ = 0.5, 90 at 0.8, 380 at 0.9 and 40,000 at 0.99. That is the mixing time of §08 in a queueing costume, and it decides how long a simulation must run: the time-average queue length over a run of length T has standard error about √(2ρ(1 + ρ)/((1 − ρ)⁴μₛT)), which grows like (1 − ρ)⁻². At ρ = 0.8 and T = 20,000 service times it is 0.30 (150 replicate runs give 0.28); at ρ = 0.95 it is 5.4.
  • Variability, not only load. For a single server with Poisson arrivals and any service distribution (M/G/1), the mean wait in line is W_q = λE[S²]/(2(1 − ρ)) = ρ(1 + c_s²)/(2(1 − ρ))·E S, with c_s the coefficient of variation of the service time. Exponential service has c_s = 1; perfectly regular service (M/D/1, c_s = 0) halves the wait in line: at ρ = 0.8, W = 3 service times instead of 5 and L = 2.4 instead of 4. Reducing variability buys what extra capacity buys, the queueing face of the inspection paradox of §07.

12 Where this feeds machine learning

Machine learning samples constantly: to generate text and images, to estimate gradients of expectations, to explore in reinforcement learning, to average over uncertainty, to rank pages. Most of these are Monte Carlo estimates with variance-reduction tricks under other names, or Markov chains whose stationary distribution is the object of interest.

Reinforcement learning: Markov chains with actions and rewards

  • An MDP is a controlled Markov chain. A Markov decision process has states, actions, transition probabilities P(s′ | s, a) and rewards (the MDP formalism). Fixing a policy π(a | s) (in this subsection only, π is the policy, as is standard in reinforcement learning, not a stationary distribution) collapses it to an ordinary Markov chain with P_π(s, s′) = Σ_a π(a | s)P(s′ | s, a). The value of the policy then solves a linear system, V = r_π + γP_πV, so V = (I − γP_π)⁻¹r_π: the fundamental matrix of an absorbing chain (§08) in which each step "survives" with probability γ, so that the expected horizon is 1/(1 − γ) steps. On-policy objectives weight states by the chain's stationary distribution under π.
  • Monte Carlo returns and their variance. Monte Carlo policy evaluation averages sampled returns, with the 1/√N error of §05. The REINFORCE gradient E[∇ ln π(a | s)·G] is an expectation estimated from sampled trajectories, and subtracting a baseline b(s) from the return leaves it unbiased (because E[∇ ln π(a | s)] = 0) while reducing its variance: the baseline is a control variate (§06), and the advantage function of actor-critic methods is the near-optimal one (policy gradient & actor-critic). Off-policy evaluation reweights returns by products of ratios π(a | s)/μ(a | s) for a behaviour policy μ: importance sampling whose weights multiply along the trajectory, so their variance can grow exponentially with the horizon; clipping and per-decision weights are the standard compromises.

PageRank is a stationary distribution

  • A "random surfer" follows a uniformly chosen out-link with probability d (typically 0.85) and jumps to a uniformly random page otherwise; a page without out-links jumps uniformly. The transition matrix G = d·P + (1 − d)·(1/n)·11ᵀ is irreducible and aperiodic because of the random jump, so it has a unique stationary distribution, and that distribution is the PageRank vector. Power iteration μ ← μG converges geometrically, with |λ₂| ≤ d, so about 50 iterations reach three-digit accuracy at d = 0.85 whatever the size of the graph (graphs in ML; the same computation on a sentence-similarity graph ranks sentences for extractive summarisation).
  • Worked. Four pages with links A→B, A→C, B→C, C→A, D→C and d = 0.85: PageRank is (0.3725, 0.1958, 0.3941, 0.0375) for A, B, C, D. C collects links from three pages and ranks first; A ranks above B because its single in-link comes from C, which passes on all its weight; D, with no in-links, keeps only the random-jump share (1 − d)/4 = 0.0375. Without the random jump (on an undirected graph) the stationary distribution would be proportional to the degrees, the reversible random walk of §08.

Hidden Markov models

  • A hidden Markov model is a Markov chain of unobserved states, each emitting an observation (hidden Markov models). Its likelihood is a sum over all state paths, computed by the forward recursion αₜ₊₁(j) = Σᵢ αₜ(i)·pᵢⱼ·bⱼ(oₜ₊₁): Chapman–Kolmogorov (§08) with each step reweighted by the evidence. The stationary distribution of the hidden chain is the natural initial distribution when the sequence starts mid-stream.

Diffusion models are learned Markov chains

  • The forward chain. A diffusion model corrupts data by a Gaussian Markov chain, xₜ = √(1 − βₜ)·xₜ₋₁ + √βₜ·εₜ (the forward process). Each step is an AR(1) map with coefficient √(1 − βₜ), and its stationary distribution is N(0, I): if Var xₜ₋₁ = 1, then Var xₜ = (1 − βₜ) + βₜ = 1. The chain forgets its start geometrically, xₜ | x₀ ~ N(√ᾱₜ·x₀, (1 − ᾱₜ)I) with ᾱₜ = Π(1 − βₛ); with βₜ = 0.01 for 1,000 steps, ᾱ = 0.99¹⁰⁰⁰ = 4.3 × 10⁻⁵, so the end of the chain is noise. Generation runs a learned reverse chain from noise back to data.
  • Langevin dynamics. The update x ← x + (η/2)·∇ln p(x) + √η·z is a Markov chain whose stationary distribution approaches p as η → 0; adding a Metropolis–Hastings accept step makes it exact for every η (§10). Score-based generative models run it with a learned score ∇ln p (score matching and SDEs, samplers).

Decoding a language model is sampling from a categorical

  • At each step a language model outputs logits z over the vocabulary, and the next token is drawn from softmax(z/T), a categorical distribution (categorical & multinomial), with temperature T; top-k keeps the k most probable tokens and renormalises, top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set with total probability at least p (decoding). Worked, logits (2, 1, 0.5, −1): at T = 1 the probabilities are (0.609, 0.224, 0.136, 0.030); at T = 0.5, (0.842, 0.114, 0.042, 0.002); at T = 2, (0.434, 0.263, 0.205, 0.097); top-2 at T = 1 renormalises to (0.731, 0.269). The draw itself is an inverse transform on the cumulative probabilities, an alias-table lookup, or the Gumbel-max trick of §04 applied directly to z/T.
  • Generation as a whole is a Markov chain on prefixes (the next token depends on everything so far, and the prefix is the state), absorbed at the end-of-sequence token. Estimating anything about the distribution of completions (the probability that an answer is correct, an expected reward) is Monte Carlo over sampled completions, with the error bars of §05.

Noise in training, and averaging at test time

  • SGD is a Markov chain. Each stochastic gradient step depends only on the current parameters and a fresh minibatch, so the iterates form a Markov chain on parameter space. With a constant step size they do not converge to a point but fluctuate in a stationary distribution around a minimum, whose spread grows with the step size and the gradient noise; with step sizes satisfying Σηₜ = ∞, Σηₜ² < ∞ they converge under convexity-type conditions. Descriptions of SGD as a Langevin diffusion sampling from a "posterior" are approximations that hold only under assumptions on the noise (isotropy, Gaussianity, small steps) that real minibatch noise usually violates; treat them as intuition.
  • Dropout and Monte Carlo averaging. Training with dropout optimises an average over randomly thinned sub-networks. Keeping dropout on at test time and averaging T stochastic forward passes ("MC dropout") is a Monte Carlo estimate of that average prediction, with standard error s/√T across passes: the spread is a measure of the network's sensitivity to dropout, and only under further assumptions an estimate of predictive uncertainty.
  • The reparameterisation trick. Gradients of E_(q_φ) f(Z) with respect to the parameters of q are estimated by writing Z = μ + σ·ε with ε ~ N(0, 1) (a location–scale transform, as in the inverse transform of §02) and differentiating inside a Monte Carlo average; it typically has far lower variance than the score-function estimator, which needs control variates to be usable.

The bootstrap is Monte Carlo on the empirical distribution

  • A bootstrap standard error or interval is an expectation under the empirical distribution F̂ₙ, approximated by B resamples: every rule of §05 applies, including the 1/√B error of the bootstrap's own Monte Carlo layer (the Monte Carlo method, choosing B). The two layers of error, the statistical one (finite n) and the simulation one (finite B), are separate, and only the second shrinks by computing more.
