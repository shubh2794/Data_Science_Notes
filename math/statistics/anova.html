ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons

Two groups were easy: subtract the means, divide by the standard error of the difference, look it up. Three groups are not three times as hard — they are a different problem, because the moment there is more than one comparison to make there is more than one chance to be wrong, and the arithmetic of that second fact turns out to be the harder half of the subject. This page is therefore two pages welded together. The first half generalises part 5's two-sample t into a single omnibus test of k group means, by splitting the total variation into a between-group piece and a within-group piece and taking their ratio — the analysis of variance. The second half confronts what happens next: the F says the means are not all equal but not which ones differ, and every attempt to find out multiplies the error rate. Part 6 already built the F ratio for a regression slope and proved t² = F; this page inherits that table wholesale and changes only what sits in the numerator. Part 7 owes this page a permutation F, and §13 delivers it. Part 8 promised that the additivity of chi-squares would let a total sum of squares be split into independent pieces; §05 is where that promise is cashed. And part 5, §13 named Bonferroni, Holm, Tukey and the false discovery rate and then deferred all four — §§17–20 are the deferred material, in that order.

01 One test for many groups, and the price of asking again

The question is old and concrete. Three teaching regimes, one exam at the end, forty-one students randomised between them: do the regimes differ? There is a two-sample test for two groups and no obvious extension to three, because "the difference in means" is not a single number any more. The analysis of variance supplies one — not by comparing means directly but by comparing two estimates of the same variance, one of which is inflated if and only if the means differ.

the data
k groups, nⱼ numbers each
A single numeric outcome measured on units that fall into k unordered classes. Part 8 asked the same "are these groups alike" question about counts; this is its measurement-valued twin.
the model
yᵢⱼ = μ + τⱼ + εᵢⱼ
One grand level, one offset per group, and independent errors with a common variance. The null is that every offset is zero. §08.
the statistic
F = MS_between / MS_within
Two independent estimates of σ², one of which is only unbiased under the null. Their ratio is about 1 when nothing is going on and large when something is. §04–05.
the aftermath
Which groups differ?
The F is an omnibus test and answers nothing specific. Every follow-up comparison is another test at another 5%, and §02 measures exactly how fast that compounds. §§16–20.

The two halves, and why they are one page

It would be tidier to separate them. They belong together because the second problem is created by the first. An omnibus test is the only way to ask a question about k groups with a single error rate; but an omnibus answer is almost never what anyone wants, so the omnibus test is always followed by the specific comparisons it was designed to avoid. Every honest treatment of the analysis of variance is therefore half a treatment of multiplicity, and the historical development ran the same way: Tukey's honest significant difference and Scheffé's method were invented for this aftermath, and Benjamini and Hochberg's false discovery rate is what happens when the aftermath has ten thousand comparisons in it instead of three.

02 The obvious thing to do, and exactly how badly it fails

The obvious plan is: run a two-sample t test on every pair of groups and see which come out significant. It is obvious, it is common, and it is wrong — not subtly wrong, but wrong by a factor that is easy to state and easy to measure. This section measures it, because the size of the failure is the entire motivation for everything that follows.

The arithmetic, first as an approximation

with k groups there are C(k, 2) pairwise comparisonsC(k, 2) = k(k − 1)/2 k = 3 → 3 k = 5 → 10 k = 10 → 45 if the comparisons were INDEPENDENT, each at level α, the chance of at least one error would beFWER = 1 − (1 − α)^C the family-wise error rate at α = 0.05 that givesk = 3 C = 3 1 − 0.95³ = 0.1426 k = 5 C = 10 1 − 0.95¹⁰ = 0.4013 k = 10 C = 45 1 − 0.95⁴⁵ = 0.9006
That formula is not the answer, and saying it is would be stating a non-theorem as a theorem. The C(k, 2) pairwise t tests are not independent. They share group means — the comparison 1 vs 2 and the comparison 1 vs 3 both contain ȳ₁ — and if a pooled variance is used they share a denominator as well. The dependence is positive, which makes the errors cluster: when one comparison goes wrong, its neighbours are more likely to go wrong too, so the chance that at least one goes wrong is lower than independence predicts. 1 − (1 − α)^C is an upper bound in practice, not the value, and the only way to know the value is to measure it.

The measured answer

Two hundred thousand simulated experiments per row. Every group is Normal(0, 1) with n = 10, so every null hypothesis is true by construction and any rejection is an error. The pooled standard deviation from all k groups is used, as it would be in practice.

Three responses, only one of which is the analysis of variance

response 1
Ask one question instead of many
Replace C comparisons by a single omnibus test of "all means equal". One test, one α, no multiplicity at all. That is this page's first half, and it is the response with the best statistical properties and the worst practical usefulness — because a rejection tells you nothing about which groups.
response 2
Keep the comparisons, raise the bar
Do all C comparisons but at a stricter threshold chosen so that the family error rate is 5%. Bonferroni (§17), Holm (§17), Tukey (§18) and Scheffé (§19) are four ways to choose that threshold, with increasing cleverness and decreasing generality.
response 3
Change what you are controlling
When there are ten thousand comparisons, guaranteeing zero false positives leaves no power for anything. Control the proportion of discoveries that are false instead of the probability of any — the false discovery rate, §20.

Those three responses are not competitors so much as answers to three different questions, and the rest of the page is organised around them. The one thing that is never acceptable is the fourth response — do all the comparisons at 5% each and report the ones that came out — which is the 61% row of the table above.

03 Splitting the variation: one observation, three pieces

Everything rests on a single algebraic identity, and the identity rests on a single cancellation. Write each observation as the grand mean plus the group's departure from it plus the observation's departure from its own group mean. Square and sum, and the cross terms vanish — exactly as they did for the regression line in part 6, §11, and for exactly the same reason.

A notation collision inside this series, resolved here explicitly. These three quantities have no agreed abbreviations, and two incompatible conventions are both standard.
  • Part 6, §11 wrote SST = SSR + SSE, with SST meaning the total sum of squares, SSR the regression (explained) piece and SSE the error piece. That is the regression convention and it is the more common one in regression texts.
  • Many experimental-design texts write TSS = SST + SSE, with SST meaning the treatment sum of squares and TSS the total. The same three letters, SST, denote the total in one convention and the between-group piece in the other — and the two appear in the same equation.
  • This page uses neither. It writes SS_total, SS_between and SS_within throughout, which cannot be misread. Where part 6's table is quoted, its SST/SSR/SSE are kept and the translation is given: SST → SS_total, SSR → SS_between, SSE → SS_within. §10 shows that this is not merely a translation of labels but a genuine identity — the two decompositions are the same decomposition.
  • When reading anything else, check which SST is meant before trusting a number. The reliable tell is the degrees of freedom: the total always has N − 1, the between-group piece always has k − 1.

Read as geometry: it is Pythagoras

In ℝᴺ, put the centred data y − ȳ̄·1 as one vector. It splits into a vector that is constant within each group — the group-effects vector — and a vector of within-group deviations. Those two are orthogonal: their inner product is exactly the cross term that vanished above. So the identity is the Pythagorean theorem on a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the centred data, and SS_between and SS_within are the squared lengths of the two legs. Compare projections and least squares: the group-effects vector is the orthogonal projection of the data onto the k-dimensional subspace of vectors that are constant within groups, and the residual vector is what is left in the orthogonal complement.

That leftover is precisely what the F test calibrates against, and it is the reason F is compared to 1 rather than to 0.
  • The squares view is the honest picture of what a p-value is measuring. Not "is the between-group square big" but "is it big relative to the within-group square, allowing for how many degrees of freedom each one had to spend" — which is the next section.

04 Degrees of freedom, mean squares, and why the ratio is a test

Two sums of squares cannot be compared directly: they are built from different numbers of free quantities, so the bigger one is bigger partly for a trivial reason. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom converts both into variance estimates on a common scale — and then the comparison means something, because under the null both estimate the same σ².

Counting the degrees of freedom

SS_between is built from k group means, tied by one constraintthe k deviations (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) satisfy Σⱼ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) = 0 so df = k − 1 SS_within is built from N residuals, tied by one constraint per groupwithin group j: Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) = 0 ⟹ nⱼ − 1 free residuals df = Σⱼ (nⱼ − 1) = N − k SS_total is built from N deviations tied by one constraintdf = N − 1 and (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1 ✓ the df add, like the SS the two mean squaresMS_between = SS_between/(k − 1) MS_within = SS_within/(N − k) the statisticF = MS_between / MS_within

MS_within is the pooled variance of the two-sample pooled t test extended to k groups: a weighted average of the k sample variances with weights nⱼ − 1.

What each mean square estimates — the reason the ratio is a test

the within-group mean square is unbiased for σ², null or no nullE[MS_within] = σ² ALWAYS the between-group mean square carries an extra, non-negative termE[MS_between] = σ² + ( Σⱼ nⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)² ) / (k − 1) where μ̄ = Σⱼ nⱼμⱼ/N so under H₀: μ₁ = … = μ_k the extra term is 0 and both estimate the same thingE[MS_between] = E[MS_within] = σ² ⟹ F should be near 1 under H₁ only the NUMERATOR inflates — which is why the test is ONE-SIDEDreject for LARGE F only. A small F means the group means are closer together than chance would predict, which is not evidence of a difference.
The one-sidedness is often misread. The F test is one-sided in the statistic and two-sided — many-sided, really — in the hypothesis. It rejects only for large F, but the alternative it is testing against is "the means are not all equal", which has no direction at all. A conspicuously small F is not evidence for the null; it is a signal that something is wrong with the design — data that were fabricated to look tidy, a blocking variable that was ignored, or observations that are not independent within groups. Fisher used exactly this reasoning to argue that a famous set of genetics data was too good to be true.

The expected-mean-square identity also says something practical: the non-null part of the numerator is Σ nⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)²/(k − 1), which grows with the group sizes. Doubling every nⱼ doubles the excess while leaving σ² alone, so F grows roughly linearly in n for a fixed pattern of means. That is the same "significance can be bought with sample size" fact that part 8, §18 made about χ² = nφ², and §11 turns it into an effect-size warning.

05 Why the ratio is an F: two independent chi-squares

"The ratio should be about 1" is not a test until there is a distribution for how far from 1 it wanders. That distribution is Fisher's F, and it appears for a reason that part 8, §04 set up and left as a promissory note: chi-squares add, and orthogonal pieces of a normal vector are independent. Both facts are needed, and both are doing real work.

The two ingredients

FACT 1 — additivity, from part 8. Sums of squares of independent normals are chi-squares, and independent chi-squares add, degrees of freedom and allX ~ χ²(a), Y ~ χ²(b), X ⫫ Y ⟹ X + Y ~ χ²(a + b) FACT 2 — the definition of the F distributionU ~ χ²(d₁), V ~ χ²(d₂), U ⫫ V ⟹ (U/d₁)/(V/d₂) ~ F(d₁, d₂) applied to the decomposition, under H₀ and the normal modelSS_between/σ² ~ χ²(k − 1) SS_within/σ² ~ χ²(N − k) and they are INDEPENDENT F = (SS_between/(k−1)) / (SS_within/(N−k)) ~ F(k − 1, N − k) note σ² cancels — which is why the test needs no knowledge of the error variance
Where part 8's promise is cashed. Part 8, §05 noted that "the additivity property … is also what will let part 9 decompose a total sum of squares into independent pieces." Here is the mechanism, and it is worth being precise, because additivity alone is not enough. Additivity says that if two chi-squares are independent their sum is a chi-square. What is needed here is the converse direction: SS_total/σ² ~ χ²(N − 1) is known, and it has been written as a sum of two pieces with (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1 degrees of freedom. Cochran's theorem says that when a chi-square is split into quadratic forms whose ranks add up to its degrees of freedom, the pieces are automatically independent chi-squares. The dimensions adding is not a bookkeeping coincidence — it is the hypothesis of the theorem, and it is what buys independence.

Why independence holds, geometrically

The reason is the orthogonality of §03, plus one special property of the normal distribution. If z is a standard normal vector in ℝᴺ and V₁ ⟂ V₂ are orthogonal subspaces, then the projections P₁z and P₂z are independent, not merely uncorrelated. That is a fact about the multivariate normal specifically: its density is spherically symmetric, so the coordinates in any orthonormal basis are independent, and a projection onto a subspace only involves the coordinates spanning it. Rotate the basis so that the first k − 1 axes span the group-effects space and the remaining N − k span the residual space, and the two sums of squares are sums over disjoint sets of independent squared normals.

the chain, with the load-bearing step markednormal errors ⟹ spherical symmetry ⟹ independent coordinates in ANY orthonormal basis ⟹ orthogonal projections are INDEPENDENT ⟹ the two sums of squares are independent ⟹ their scaled ratio is an F. NORMALITY is the assumption doing this work — not the CLT, not large Nfor non-normal errors, uncorrelated ≠ independent, and the F distribution is only an approximation. §09 measures how good an approximation, and it is surprisingly good.

Reading the distribution

  • The mean of F(d₁, d₂) is d₂/(d₂ − 2) for d₂ > 2 — just above 1, and tending to 1 as the error degrees of freedom grow. "Around 1 under the null" is exactly right and slightly conservative.
  • It is right-skewed and lives on (0, ∞), so the 5% critical value is well above 1: F(2, 38) has F* = 3.2448. Small numerator df give fat tails; the critical value falls as either df grows.
  • F(1, ν) = t(ν)² exactly — the identity part 6, §13 proved. §10 uses it for k = 2.
  • d₁·F(d₁, ∞) = χ²(d₁). When the error variance is effectively known (huge N) the denominator is 1 and the F test collapses into a chi-square test — the same limit that makes part 8's statistic a chi-square rather than an F.
  • 1/F(d₁, d₂) ~ F(d₂, d₁), which is how one-tailed F tables were used before software: the lower tail of one is the upper tail of the other.

06 The ANOVA table — and an audit of a published one

Everything above fits in five columns and three rows, and the layout has been stable for ninety years. It is worth knowing not because the arithmetic is hard but because the table is self-checking: the sums of squares must add, the degrees of freedom must add, each mean square must equal its sum of squares over its own degrees of freedom, and the F must be the ratio of the two mean squares. Four constraints on eleven numbers. This section works the table for the running data and then applies those four checks to a published table, which fails two of them.

07 Why you cannot see it in the boxplots: the square-root law, drawn

The natural first move with k groups is side-by-side boxplots, and the natural next move is to decide from them whether the groups differ. That decision cannot be made by eye, and the reason is not impatience or lack of skill — it is arithmetic. Boxplots show the spread of the data. The test compares the spread of the means. Those two spreads differ by a factor of √n, so a separation that is overwhelming to the test can be genuinely invisible in the picture.

The square-root law, applied to this problem

from part 3: the sampling distribution of a group meanSD(individual observations in group j) = σ SD(ȳⱼ) = σ/√nⱼ ← smaller by √nⱼ so a difference in means of δ is, in the two currencies:measured against the DATA's spread: δ/σ — what a boxplot shows measured against the MEANS' spread: δ√n/σ — what the test uses with n = 30, the test sees a difference √30 = 5.48 times larger than the eye doesδ = 3.5, σ = 10: δ/σ = 0.35 of a boxplot's width — invisible δ√n/σ = 1.92 standard errors — clearly detectable

This is why the analysis of variance exists as a computation rather than as a rule for reading pictures, and it is the one point about the method that everybody who uses it should internalise. It also runs the other way: with n = 4 per group, boxes that barely touch can be entirely consistent with chance. The picture and the test disagree in both directions, and the test is right.

Six experiments to run in it

  1. Start at the defaults and read the two panels against each other. Three groups of thirty, noise σ = 10, means 3.5 apart. The boxes on the left overlap so heavily that no honest reader would call them different; the means in the middle panel, plotted with their standard errors, are visibly separated. The readout reports a significant F. That contradiction is the point of the section, and it is not an illusion in either direction — both panels are drawing the same numbers on different scales.
  2. Now drag n down to 6 without touching anything else. The boxes look about the same — the data's spread has not changed — but the standard errors in the middle panel expand by √5 and the p-value collapses to nothing. Same effect, same noise, five times fewer subjects, and the evidence is gone.
  3. Set the separation to exactly zero. The three means are identical in the population, and the middle panel still shows them scattered. Press "draw a new sample" a dozen times and count how often p < 0.05: it should be about one time in twenty, which is the definition of the significance level and the cheapest demonstration of it available.
  4. Push k to 6 at zero separation. The F test's rejection rate stays at one in twenty even though there are now fifteen pairs of means to compare. This is §02's table, live.
  5. Take the separation up until the boxes visibly separate. On the default settings that needs δ around 12 — by which point p is smaller than 10⁻¹⁰. By the time an effect is visible in a boxplot it is many orders of magnitude past significance. The eye's threshold and the test's threshold are not close.
  6. Load the running data. Three real groups, p = 0.0888, and boxes that overlap almost completely — the honest case, where the picture and the test agree that there is not enough here.
The corollary nobody likes. If a boxplot cannot be trusted to reveal a significant difference, it also cannot be trusted to reveal an important one — and those are different questions. A separation of 0.35σ is invisible and highly significant at n = 30; whether it matters is a question about exam scores, not about statistics. That is §11's subject, and it is the reason a p-value should never be reported without an effect size.

What the translation buys, in both directions

  • Every regression diagnostic applies to an ANOVA. Residual plots, quantile plots, leverage, Cook's distance (part 6, §16) — all of them, unchanged. In a one-way layout the leverage of an observation is exactly 1/nⱼ, so small groups are automatically high-leverage, which is a precise version of the warning that unbalanced designs are fragile.
  • Every ANOVA fact applies to a regression. The overall F of a multiple regression is testing exactly what the one-way F tests: "does the model explain more than noise".
  • Continuous and categorical predictors mix freely. Add a numeric covariate to the indicator columns and you have analysis of covariance; the machinery does not notice the difference. That is the whole of the general linear model, and it is why part 2's block and factorial designs are all ordinary regressions.
  • The computation is one least-squares solve. See projections and least squares: the fitted values are P₁y for the projection onto the group-indicator column space, exactly as §03 said.

If the three regimes are entered as a single numeric column 1, 2, 3, the regression fits a straight line across the group index and spends one degree of freedom instead of two. It tests something much narrower — "do the means increase or decrease linearly across the groups in the order I happened to list them" — and it can be badly wrong when the ordering is arbitrary.

11 Effect size: what the F refuses to tell you

A significant F says the group means are further apart than chance explains. It says nothing whatever about how far apart they are, because the statistic is a ratio in which the sample size appears and the p-value is a monotone function of that ratio. §04 showed the mechanism: the non-null part of E[MS_between] is proportional to n. Report a p-value alone and you have reported something that can be made arbitrarily small by collecting more data from an unchanged world.

12 Power, and how many subjects the design needed

The non-significant F of §06 raises an obvious question that §11 half-answered: could this experiment have detected the effect it was looking for? The permutation F does not fix unequal variances, and this is the single most useful thing to know about it. The reason is exactly part 7, §12's: permutation tests the strong null — the groups have the same distribution — and when the variances differ that null is false even though every mean is equal. What the permutation F is actually for

  • Non-normal errors with equal variances — and here the gain is not small. Row 3 of the table above is the clean demonstration: with lognormal errors the tabulated F runs at 3.6% against a nominal 5%, and the permutation F at 5.2%. The permutation test is exact under exchangeability, so it recovers the whole of the lost 1.4 percentage points, which is lost power. Welch does not recover it (3.65%), because the problem is not unequal variances. Read rows 3 and 5 together: the permutation F repairs non-normality completely and repairs heteroscedasticity not at all, and the two failures are constantly confused.
  • Statistics with no distribution theory. The recipe never used the fact that the statistic was F. Permute a ratio of trimmed-mean spreads, a median-based statistic, or anything else you can compute, and the p-value is still exact under exchangeability. That freedom is the real reason to own the technique.
  • A diagnostic. When the permutation and tabulated p-values disagree materially, something specific is wrong — heavy tails, a wild point, or unequal spreads with unequal group sizes — and the disagreement tells you to look.
  • Not for unequal variances. Use Welch (§14). If you want a permutation test that survives them, permute a studentised statistic — Welch's F itself — which is part 7, §12's repair applied here unchanged.

15 Kruskal–Wallis: the F test run on ranks

The rank-based alternative to one-way ANOVA is usually presented as a separate method with its own table. It is not. Part 7, §13 showed that the classical rank tests are permutation tests with a statistic chosen so that its null distribution could be tabulated once; Kruskal–Wallis is the one-way ANOVA of that family, and the connection is an identity, not an analogy.

What it assumes, which is more than people think

  • Its null is "the k distributions are identical", not "the k medians are equal". That is the strong null of part 7, §12, and it means Kruskal–Wallis rejects for a difference in spread or shape just as readily as for a difference in location. §14's table shows the consequence: 11.3% and 13.3% type I error when only the variances differ.
  • Reading it as a test of medians requires an extra assumption — that the distributions differ only by a shift. Under that assumption it is a test of location; without it, a rejection means "these samples do not look like they came from one distribution", which is a weaker conclusion than most reports claim.
  • It is not distribution-free in the sense people want. It is distribution-free under the strong null, which is a real and useful guarantee, and it is not a repair for unequal variances.
  • The efficiency cost is small and the robustness gain is real. Against normal data, the asymptotic relative efficiency is 3/π = 0.955 — a 4.5% loss — and against heavy-tailed data it can be far more efficient than the F. Against a single wild point it is essentially immune, where §09 showed the F is not.

16 The multiple testing fallacy, and the look-elsewhere effect

The second half of the page starts here, and it starts with a real failure. A large study set out to test whether living near a power line was associated with adverse health outcomes. It reported a highly significant increase in one of them — childhood leukaemia — at better than the 1% level. A careful follow-up failed to confirm it. Nothing was fabricated and no arithmetic was wrong. The study examined eight hundred health outcomes.

The arithmetic that explains it

from part 5: under a true null the p-value is UNIFORM on (0, 1)P(p < 0.01) = 0.01 exactly, by construction, for every one of the tests so with m = 800 independent tests and NOT ONE REAL EFFECT anywhereE[# results with p < 0.01] = 800 × 0.01 = 8 P(at least one such result) = 1 − 0.99⁸⁰⁰ = 0.99968 P(at least 8) = 0.5477 P(at least 15) = 0.0168 so finding one "highly significant" outcome among 800 is not evidence of anything. Finding EIGHT is the expected yield of pure noise.
This is the look-elsewhere effect, and its formal name is worth having. The p-value answers "how surprising is this result, at this location". What a reader wants to know is "how surprising is the most extreme result anywhere I looked", and those are different questions with different answers whenever the search space is larger than one. The name comes from particle physics, where a bump in a spectrum has to be discounted by the width of the window it could have appeared in; the statistical content is identical. Whenever a result is reported as the most striking of many, the reported p-value is the answer to a question nobody asked.

Counting m is the easy case: the researcher knows the number and can correct for it. Data snooping — also called data dredging — is the case where m is unknown, often to the analyst as much as to the reader.

explicit multiplicity
m is known
Eight hundred outcomes, twenty subgroups, three pairwise comparisons. Correctable, by everything in §§17–20. The researcher can state m and the reader can check it.
implicit multiplicity
m is unknown
Every choice made after seeing the data — which outliers to drop, which transformation, which covariates, where to cut a continuous variable, when to stop collecting — is a fork, and the effective m is the number of paths through the forking tree. Nobody can count it, so nobody can correct it.

17 Controlling the family-wise error rate: Bonferroni, Šidák, Holm

The first thing to control is the probability of making any false claim in the whole family — the family-wise error rate. Three procedures do it with no assumptions worth speaking of, they are ordered by power, and the best of the three is not the famous one.

The target, stated precisely

a family of m hypotheses, of which an unknown subset are trueFWER = P( at least one TRUE null hypothesis is rejected ) two strengths of guarantee, and the difference mattersWEAK control : FWER ≤ α when ALL m nulls are true (the "global null") STRONG control: FWER ≤ α for EVERY configuration of true and false nulls strong control is what you want; weak control is what an omnibus F gives you. Bonferroni, Holm, Šidák, Tukey and Scheffé all control it STRONGLY.
Weak control is why the omnibus F is not enough. The F test guarantees 5% under the complete null and says nothing about the case where two of five groups genuinely differ and the other three do not — which is the realistic case. §18 measures what happens then: "run the F, and if it rejects, do unadjusted pairwise t tests" has a family-wise error rate of 12.0% at k = 4 when one group is genuinely different, against a nominal 5%.

Bonferroni

two equivalent statements of the same rulereject Hᵢ when pᵢ ≤ α/m — lower the threshold or report p̃ᵢ = min(1, m·pᵢ) — inflate the p-value, then read it at α the proof is one line, and it assumes NOTHING about dependenceP(∪ᵢ {pᵢ ≤ α/m}) ≤ Σᵢ P(pᵢ ≤ α/m) = m₀ · α/m ≤ α — Boole's inequality, over the m₀ true nulls. No independence, no normality, no model.
  • Its universality is its selling point. Boole's inequality holds for any dependence structure whatever, which is why Bonferroni is the correction you can always apply and never have to defend.
  • Its conservatism is real and measurable. §02's simulation: at k = 10 with 45 pairwise comparisons, Bonferroni's measured FWER is 0.0353 against a nominal 0.05. It is spending 30% of its error budget on nothing, and that budget is power.
  • The conservatism grows with m and with positive dependence, which is exactly the situation of pairwise comparisons — they overlap heavily, so the union bound is loose.
  • It "guards against having even one false positive", which is the right goal for a regulatory submission and the wrong goal for an exploratory scan of ten thousand genes, where it leaves no power at all. §20 is the alternative.

Šidák — the same idea, assuming independence

if the m tests really were independent, the exact threshold is availableFWER = 1 − (1 − α_each)^m = α ⟹ α_each = 1 − (1 − α)^(1/m) m = 3, α = 0.05Šidák 1 − 0.95^(1/3) = 0.016952 Bonferroni 0.05/3 = 0.016667 m = 45Šidák 1 − 0.95^(1/45) = 0.0011392 Bonferroni 0.05/45 = 0.0011111 so Šidák is very slightly less strict — and it BUYS ALMOST NOTHING

Šidák's threshold is uniformly a shade larger than Bonferroni's, and the gap is a fraction of a per cent even at m = 45. In exchange it requires independence, which pairwise comparisons never have. It is worth knowing and rarely worth using: the correction it improves on is the one that needs no assumptions, and it gives up that property for a rounding error.

Holm's step-down — strictly better than Bonferroni, for free

sort the p-values, then walk DOWN the list with a threshold that relaxes as you go1. order them: p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍ₘ₎ 2. compare p₍ᵢ₎ against α/(m − i + 1) 3. reject p₍₁₎, …, p₍ᵣ₎ where r is the LAST index before the first failure; once a comparison fails, STOP — everything from there on is retained. the sequence of thresholds for m = 5 at α = 0.05α/5 = 0.0100 α/4 = 0.0125 α/3 = 0.0167 α/2 = 0.0250 α/1 = 0.0500 the first step IS Bonferroni; every later step is strictly more generous as adjusted p-values, with the monotonicity enforcedp̃₍ᵢ₎ = max over j ≤ i of min(1, (m − j + 1)·p₍ⱼ₎)
Holm uniformly dominates Bonferroni, and the phrase means something exact. Every hypothesis Bonferroni rejects, Holm also rejects — because Holm's first threshold is α/m — and Holm sometimes rejects more. It controls the FWER strongly, under arbitrary dependence, exactly as Bonferroni does. There is therefore no situation in which Bonferroni is the right choice and Holm is not. The only reasons Bonferroni is more common are that it is one line of arithmetic and that it produces a single fixed threshold, which is convenient to quote. Neither is a statistical reason.
a worked comparison, m = 5, α = 0.05sorted p: 0.008 0.011 0.030 0.041 0.190 Bonferroni threshold 0.010 for all five → rejects ONE (0.008) Holm: 0.008 ≤ 0.0100 ✓ 0.011 ≤ 0.0125 ✓ 0.030 ≤ 0.0167 ✗ → STOP → rejects TWO and note the step-down STOP rule: 0.041 is not tested against α/2 = 0.025 — once the walk halts, everything after it is retained regardless of its value.

A caution about the measured FWER. §02's simulation gives Bonferroni and Holm the identical family-wise error rate at every k — 0.0422, 0.0393, 0.0377, 0.0353. That is not a bug in the simulation and not a contradiction of the dominance claim. Under the complete null, where every hypothesis is true, Holm rejects something if and only if its first step fires, and its first step is Bonferroni's rule. The two procedures can only diverge when some hypotheses are false — which is where Holm's extra rejections are true discoveries. Holm's advantage is in power, not in level, and a global-null simulation is structurally incapable of showing it.

Where Tukey does not apply

  • Only for all pairwise comparisons. The studentised range is the distribution of the largest of all C(k,2) differences. Do a subset and Tukey is conservative; do anything that is not a pairwise difference — "the average of groups 1 and 2 against group 3" — and it does not apply at all. That is §19's territory.
  • Only with a pooled variance. Unequal variances break it exactly as they break the F. The Games–Howell procedure is the Welch-flavoured version, using each pair's own standard error and Welch degrees of freedom with the same q critical value.
  • Dunnett's test is the specialist when there is a control. Comparing k − 1 treatments against one control is k − 1 comparisons, not C(k,2), and Dunnett's distribution exploits that to give shorter intervals than Tukey. If the design has a control group, use it.

19 Contrasts: the conceptual heart of the multiplicity problem

Everything in §§17–18 corrects for the number of comparisons. This section explains why the number matters at all, and the answer is not "because more tests means more chances". It is that the selection is part of the procedure. A comparison chosen before the data and the identical comparison chosen after seeing which one looks biggest are two different statistical acts with two different error rates, and no amount of arithmetic on the data can tell them apart. That is the whole problem, and contrasts are where it becomes visible.

Neither target is more rigorous than the other. They answer different questions, and the design decision is which question the study is asking.

Benjamini–Hochberg

the procedure, in three steps1. sort the p-values ascending: p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍ₘ₎ 2. find the LARGEST k such that p₍ₖ₎ ≤ (k/m)·α 3. reject H₍₁₎, …, H₍ₖ₎ — all of them, including any p above its own line it is a STEP-UP procedure: walk from the largest p-value DOWNWARD looking for the first crossing, then reject everything below it. Contrast Holm (§17), which walks from the smallest UPWARD and stops at the first failure. the theorem: for independent (or positively dependent, PRDS) test statisticsFDR ≤ (m₀/m)·α ≤ α where m₀ is the number of TRUE nulls as adjusted p-values, with monotonicity enforced from the top downp̃₍ᵢ₎ = min over j ≥ i of min(1, (m/j)·p₍ⱼ₎)
Two features of the rule that look like mistakes and are not. First, step 3 rejects hypotheses whose own p-value is above their own threshold, as long as some larger-ranked p-value cleared its line. That is deliberate: BH controls a property of the whole rejected set, not of each member, and the crossing point is what licenses everything below it. Second, the threshold at rank m is α itself — the largest p-value is compared against the uncorrected level. If every p-value is tiny, BH rejects everything, which is correct: when almost all the hypotheses are false, there is almost nothing to protect.

21 Two factors, interaction, and blocking

One grouping variable was §03's whole story. Two is not twice the work — it is a different structure, because two factors can interact, and when they do, the separate "effect of each factor" stops being a well-defined quantity. This section is conceptual rather than exhaustive; the arithmetic is §03's, applied twice and then once more to what is left over.

The decomposition, extended

  • Interaction is a property of the scale, not only of the world. Blocking: the same arithmetic used to remove nuisance variation

A block is a grouping the experimenter does not care about but that carries a lot of variation — subject, day, batch, machine, litter. If every treatment appears once in every block, the block-to-block variation can be pulled out of the error term instead of inflating it. The model is a two-way layout with no interaction.

The validation-set discipline

There is one procedure that solves implicit multiplicity outright, and it is not a correction — it is a change of protocol.

  1. Split the data before looking at any of it, into a model-building set and a validation set. The split must be made first, and it must be recorded.
  2. Snoop freely on the model-building set. Try everything. Look at every subgroup, every transformation, every interaction. There is no multiplicity penalty because nothing here is being reported as a finding — it is all hypothesis generation.
  3. Write down the hypothesis that came out of step 2, in advance and in full: the comparison, the statistic, the threshold.
  4. Test it once on the validation set. Once. The p-value is honest because the hypothesis was fixed before the data were seen.
  5. Do not look at the validation set during step 2. This is the entire discipline and it is where the procedure fails in practice, because a single glance — "let me just check the split is balanced" — reintroduces the dependence it exists to remove.
Why this works when a correction cannot. Bonferroni, Holm and BH all require you to know m. Implicit multiplicity (§16) is exactly the case where m is unknowable, because it is the number of analyses you would have run had the data come out differently. A held-out set sidesteps the count entirely: whatever happened during exploration, the final test involves data that took no part in it, so its error rate is the nominal one. This is also the exact justification for a test set in machine learning, and the exact reason that reusing one destroys the guarantee. Part 3, §11 made the same point about a test set being a sample; the two statements are one statement.

The related practice on the experimental side is pre-registration: the analysis plan is deposited before the data are collected, which makes m = 1 a fact rather than a claim. It is the same idea with the split moved earlier in time. Neither is a statistical technique; both are protocols that make a statistical technique honest, and no amount of subsequent arithmetic can substitute for either.

23 Where it goes wrong

Fifteen failures, in the order they occur in a real analysis. Most of them are stated somewhere above; collected here they make a checklist.

"The F was significant, so the groups are different." The F says not all equal. With five groups that is one of thirty possible patterns and the test does not choose between them. Which groups differ is §18's question and needs its own error rate.
"The F was not significant, so the groups are the same." Absence of evidence. The running data have a large effect size (Cohen f = 0.37) and p = 0.089, on a design with 53% power (§12). "We could not detect a difference" and "there is no difference" are different sentences.
"I ran all the pairwise t tests and two were significant." At k = 5 the family-wise error rate of that procedure is 27.8% (§02). Use Tukey, Holm or BH; which one depends on §20's question about what a false discovery costs.
"I ran the F first, so my follow-up t tests are protected." Only at k = 3. Measured at k = 4 with one genuinely different group, protected LSD makes a false claim 12.0% of the time (§19). The F's protection is weak control and it evaporates as soon as any null is false.
"The boxplots overlap, so there is nothing there." Boxplots show the data's spread; the test compares the means' spread. The two differ by √n (§07). A difference of 0.35σ is invisible in a boxplot and highly significant at n = 30.
"I checked the residuals for normality and they were fine, so the ANOVA is valid." Normality is the least important of the four assumptions (§09). With balanced groups the F survives lognormal errors; it does not survive unequal variances with unequal group sizes, and no quantile plot will show you that.
"Levene's test was not significant, so I used the ordinary F." A two-stage procedure whose combined error rate is not nominal (§14), and variance tests have least power exactly when unequal variances do most damage. Decide in advance; decide on Welch.
"The variances were unequal, so I used Kruskal–Wallis." Kruskal–Wallis does not fix unequal variances — measured type I error 11.3% and 13.3% in the two bad configurations of §14. It fixes non-normality and outliers. Welch fixes unequal variances.
"I used a permutation test, so I need no assumptions." You need exchangeability, which unequal variances violate (§13). The permutation F hits 27.6% type I error in exactly the configuration where the classical one hits 28.2%.
η² = 0.12, so 12% of the variation is explained. Of which 5 percentage points would appear under a true null (§11), since E[η²] = (k−1)/(N−1). Report ω² = 0.072 when the sample is small.
"The interaction was not significant, so I dropped it and interpreted the main effects." Interaction tests are badly underpowered (§21) — roughly four times the sample size for the same detectability. A non-significant interaction is weak evidence for additivity, not a licence.
"The main effect of the treatment was +3 points." If the interaction is real, that number describes nobody (§21). It was +6 in one band and 0 in the other. Report the simple effects.
"I coded the three groups as 1, 2, 3 and regressed on it." That is a one-degree-of-freedom linear trend, not an ANOVA (§10). On the running data it captures 22.6% of SS_between and returns p = 0.304 against the real p = 0.089. Legitimate for an ordered factor as a planned contrast; an error for an unordered one.
"I applied Bonferroni, so I was conservative and therefore safe." Conservative is not free. At k = 10 Bonferroni runs at 3.5% of its 5% budget (§18) and finds 15% of real effects where BH finds 50% (§20). Over-correcting produces false negatives, which are also errors, and they are the invisible kind.
"I removed the outlier and the result became significant." Report both. On the running data the single low score works the other way — deleting it takes F from 2.582 to 1.792 (§09) — and either direction, the decision to delete was made after seeing its effect, which is §16's implicit multiplicity in miniature.
"Students who chose the peer-assessment option scored higher, so peer assessment works." A significant F licenses a causal claim only under randomisation (part 2, §08). Self-selected groups differ in every way at once, and the test cannot tell which one produced the difference.

24 Practice

Work these against the figures; every number is recoverable from the page.

Read and decide

  1. An ANOVA table reports df = 3, 96, 99 and SS = 240, 1920, 2160. Fill in the mean squares, the F, and say how many groups and how many observations there were. Then compute η² and ω².
  2. A colleague reports F(4, 45) = 2.1, p = 0.096, and concludes the five treatments are equivalent. Write the two sentences you would replace that conclusion with, one about power and one about effect size.
  3. A table gives SS_between = 60 on 2 df, SS_within = 380 on 40 df, and F = 3.4. Apply the four self-checks of §06. Which one fails, and what is the correct F?
  4. With k = 4 groups of 12, the largest pairwise difference is 5.0 and the pooled SD is 6.0. Is it significant by unadjusted t? By Tukey? (q₀.₉₅(4, 44) = 3.7760, t₀.₉₇₅(44) = 2.0154.) Which answer should be reported, and why does it depend on whether the pair was chosen in advance?
  5. Twenty p-values are reported; the three smallest are 0.001, 0.006 and 0.007, and the rest are above 0.2. How many does Bonferroni reject at 5%? How many does Holm? How many does BH? The answers are 1, 1 and 3 — work out why BH rejects a hypothesis whose own p-value, 0.006, is above its own threshold of (2/20)×0.05 = 0.005.

Derive

  1. Show that the cross term in §03 vanishes, stating exactly which property of a mean you used and where the constancy of (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) within group j enters.
  2. Prove E[MS_within] = σ² whether or not the null holds, and derive E[MS_between] = σ² + Σ nⱼτⱼ²/(k − 1). Then explain why the test rejects only for large F.
  3. Show that SS_between = Σⱼ Sⱼ²/nⱼ − T²/N where Sⱼ is the jth group total and T the grand total, and verify it on the running data's totals 664, 691, 592.
  4. Show that for k = 2 the one-way F equals the square of the pooled two-sample t, by writing both in terms of ȳ₁ − ȳ₂.
  5. Show that Kruskal–Wallis H equals (N − 1)·SS_between/SS_total computed on the ranks, and use Σ i = N(N+1)/2 and Σ i² = N(N+1)(2N+1)/6 to recover the constants 12 and 3(N+1).
  6. Prove that Holm controls the FWER strongly. (Hint: consider the smallest p-value among the true nulls, and note that it is compared against a threshold at least as large as α divided by the number of true nulls.)
  7. Show that Holm rejects every hypothesis Bonferroni rejects, directly from the two rules.
  8. Show that two contrasts with Σⱼ c₁ⱼc₂ⱼ/nⱼ = 0 have uncorrelated estimates, and hence that their sums of squares add.
  9. Show that the contrast maximising SS(L) has weights proportional to nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄), and that its SS(L) equals SS_between. Explain why that makes Scheffé's threshold and the omnibus F equivalent.
  10. Show that under the complete null the FDR of any procedure equals its FWER.

Experiment in the figures

  1. In §07's laboratory, find the smallest separation at which you can see the difference in the boxplots, then read the p-value. How many orders of magnitude past 0.05 is it?
  2. In §07, set the separation to zero and press "draw a new sample" forty times, counting rejections. Compare with 2.
  3. In §09, choose lognormal errors and switch between the balanced and unbalanced designs. Which direction does the error rate move in each, and why does balance protect you?
  4. In §13, choose the heteroscedastic preset and confirm that the permutation and classical p-values agree with each other and that both are wrong.
  5. In §17, drag the third-smallest p-value below Holm's third threshold and watch how many extra rejections appear. Explain why moving a large p-value can change whether a small one is rejected under BH but not under Holm.
  6. In §20, set π₀ = 1 and confirm that BH's measured FDR is its FWER. Then set π₀ = 0.2 and confirm the measured FDR is near 0.2α.
  7. In §21, drag one cell mean until the lines cross, and watch both main-effect p-values while the interaction p-value falls. Find a configuration where both main effects are non-significant and the interaction is highly significant.
  8. In §18, raise k with the error df fixed and watch the four critical half-widths. At what k does Tukey overtake Bonferroni, and at what k does Bonferroni overtake Scheffé?

What you should be able to do now

  • Build a one-way ANOVA table from raw data and check it four ways.
  • State what a significant F does and does not license, and give an effect size with it.
  • Choose between the classical F, Welch, Kruskal–Wallis and a permutation test on the basis of which assumption is in doubt.
  • Design a set of planned contrasts, check their orthogonality with the right criterion, and know why they need no adjustment.
  • Pick between Bonferroni, Holm, Tukey, Scheffé and Benjamini–Hochberg from the shape of the family and the cost of a false discovery.
  • Recognise an interaction, refuse to report a main effect over the top of one, and know why blocking is the cheapest power available.
  • Recognise the multiplicity problem in its ML disguises, and know that a held-out set is the only defence against the version where m is unknown.

25 Closing the series: what nine parts add up to

This is the last part, so there is no "where this goes next" pointing at a part 10. What there is instead is a through-line, and it is worth stating, because the nine parts were not nine topics — they were one argument told in nine instalments, and the argument only becomes visible from the end of it.

The through-line

a batch of numbers→where the numbers came from→a statistic is random→an interval around it→a decision from it→two variables→computation instead of formulas→counts instead of measurements→many groups, and many questions at once
  1. Part 1 established that a summary hides more than it reveals, and that the first obligation is to look. Every later part inherited that: §07 of this page is the same lesson in reverse — a picture can hide what a computation reveals, and neither is a substitute for the other.
  2. Part 2 established that the analysis cannot repair the design. Randomisation is what makes a causal claim available, stratification is what makes an estimate precise, and no amount of arithmetic recovers either after the fact. This page's §08 assumption A2 and §16's data-snooping problem are both that principle again.
  3. Part 3 supplied the single idea everything else is built on: a statistic computed from a sample is itself random, it has a distribution, and that distribution is knowable. The √n law it established is what makes §07's boxplots misleading and what makes §12's sample-size arithmetic possible.
  4. Part 4 turned that distribution into an interval, and insisted that the interval, not the point, is the estimate. It also planted the multiplicity problem — twenty 95% intervals contain, on average, one miss — and deferred it to here.
  5. Part 5 turned it into a decision, and spent as much space on what a p-value is not as on what it is. It named Bonferroni, Holm, Tukey and the false discovery rate and handed all four to this page; §§17–20 are that debt paid.
  6. Part 6 did all of it again with two variables, and produced the sum-of-squares decomposition and the F ratio that this page inherited wholesale. §10 showed the two pages are computing the same thing.
  7. Part 7 removed the formulas, replacing distributional assumptions with computation, and established the pattern that recurred twice afterwards: the classical test is the large-sample approximation to an enumeration. §13 is the third instance.
  8. Part 8 replaced measurements with counts, and supplied the chi-square whose additivity §05 needed to explain why an F is an F at all.
  9. This part closed the loop. It generalised part 5's two-group test, reused part 6's table, cashed part 7's permutation promise and part 8's additivity promise, and then spent its second half on the problem that all eight earlier parts kept deferring: what happens when you ask more than one question of the same data.

Four things the nine parts agree on

one
Design outranks analysis
Part 2 said it, part 5's power section said it, §12 and §21 said it again. The largest gains available anywhere in this subject are in randomisation, balance, blocking and sample size — all decided before any data exist, and none recoverable afterwards.
two
A number without its uncertainty is not a result
Part 3 made a statistic random, part 4 made the interval the deliverable, part 6 put bands on a line, and §11 refused to let a p-value stand alone. The same sentence in four costumes.
three
Every assumption should be measured, not asserted
Part 7 measured bootstrap coverage, part 8 measured the expected-count rule, and §§02, 09, 14 and 18 measured everything they could. Three of the eight tables this series reproduced from published sources contained arithmetic errors, found by recomputation. Rules of thumb are hypotheses.
four
The analysis you did not report is part of the analysis you did
Part 5's forking paths, part 4's post-hoc level choice, part 8's residual scanning, and this page's entire second half. It is the one failure that no computation can detect from the data, and the only defences are protocol: pre-registration, held-out data, and honesty about what was tried.

What the series deliberately did not build

  • Probability itself. Nine parts of statistics were written without a probability page under them, and that was a choice, not an oversight — the series routed around it, defining what it needed where it needed it: the sampling distribution in part 3, the normal and t in parts 3 and 4, the binomial and chi-square in part 8, the F and the studentised range here. It is honest to name the gap. A Probability node — sample spaces, random variables, expectation and variance as operators, the standard families and their relationships, conditional probability and Bayes, the laws of large numbers and the central limit theorem proved rather than illustrated, and generating functions — would sit under all nine of these pages and would let several of them stop hand-waving. It does not exist yet.
  • Bayesian inference. Every interval in the series is a confidence interval and every test is frequentist. The Bayesian treatment of all nine topics is a parallel series, not a section.
  • Multivariate methods. MANOVA, discriminant analysis, and the multivariate generalisations of parts 6 and 9 — although PCA and the linear algebra pages cover much of the machinery.
  • Time series and dependent data. The independence assumption is load-bearing in all nine parts and is the one that fails most often in practice.
  • Mixed models, repeated measures, and the modelling generalisation of §21; and, on the categorical side, the log-linear models that part 8, §18 named and left uncovered. Both are the same step — from a decomposition to a model — taken in two different directions.
Where this goes sideways. Into model evaluation, where comparing m models on one test set is §18's problem with different nouns and §22's translation table is the dictionary. Into A/B testing, where multiplicity arrives as segments, metrics and peeking all at once, and where §20's question — what does a false discovery actually cost — has a currency answer. Into linear regression and the projection view of least squares, which is §03's decomposition written in the language it actually belongs to.
