02 The obvious thing to do, and exactly how badly it fails

The obvious plan is: run a two-sample t test on every pair of groups and see which come out significant. It is obvious, it is common, and it is wrong — not subtly wrong, but wrong by a factor that is easy to state and easy to measure. This section measures it, because the size of the failure is the entire motivation for everything that follows.

The arithmetic, first as an approximation

with k groups there are C(k, 2) pairwise comparisonsC(k, 2) = k(k − 1)/2 k = 3 → 3 k = 5 → 10 k = 10 → 45 if the comparisons were INDEPENDENT, each at level α, the chance of at least one error would beFWER = 1 − (1 − α)^C the family-wise error rate at α = 0.05 that givesk = 3 C = 3 1 − 0.95³ = 0.1426 k = 5 C = 10 1 − 0.95¹⁰ = 0.4013 k = 10 C = 45 1 − 0.95⁴⁵ = 0.9006
That formula is not the answer, and saying it is would be stating a non-theorem as a theorem. The C(k, 2) pairwise t tests are not independent. They share group means — the comparison 1 vs 2 and the comparison 1 vs 3 both contain ȳ₁ — and if a pooled variance is used they share a denominator as well. The dependence is positive, which makes the errors cluster: when one comparison goes wrong, its neighbours are more likely to go wrong too, so the chance that at least one goes wrong is lower than independence predicts. 1 − (1 − α)^C is an upper bound in practice, not the value, and the only way to know the value is to measure it.

The measured answer

Two hundred thousand simulated experiments per row. Every group is Normal(0, 1) with n = 10, so every null hypothesis is true by construction and any rejection is an error. The pooled standard deviation from all k groups is used, as it would be in practice.

  • At k = 10 it is 61%. Compare ten identical groups pairwise and you will report at least one "significant difference" three times out of five. The independence formula says 90%, which is badly wrong in the direction that makes the problem sound worse than it is — the correct number is bad enough.
  • The omnibus F holds 5% at every k. That single line is the whole argument for the analysis of variance: one test, one error rate, however many groups. The measured column reads 0.0498, 0.0498, 0.0498, 0.0496, 0.0500 against a nominal 0.05, and the small shortfall is Monte Carlo noise at two hundred thousand replicates.
  • The overstatement column is worth staring at. It grows and then stabilises near 50%: the more comparisons, the more overlap between them, and the further independence is from the truth. Any exercise that computes 1 − (1 − α)^C and stops has computed a bound, not a rate.

Three responses, only one of which is the analysis of variance

response 1
Ask one question instead of many
Replace C comparisons by a single omnibus test of "all means equal". One test, one α, no multiplicity at all. That is this page's first half, and it is the response with the best statistical properties and the worst practical usefulness — because a rejection tells you nothing about which groups.
response 2
Keep the comparisons, raise the bar
Do all C comparisons but at a stricter threshold chosen so that the family error rate is 5%. Bonferroni (§17), Holm (§17), Tukey (§18) and Scheffé (§19) are four ways to choose that threshold, with increasing cleverness and decreasing generality.
response 3
Change what you are controlling
When there are ten thousand comparisons, guaranteeing zero false positives leaves no power for anything. Control the proportion of discoveries that are false instead of the probability of any — the false discovery rate, §20.

Those three responses are not competitors so much as answers to three different questions, and the rest of the page is organised around them. The one thing that is never acceptable is the fourth response — do all the comparisons at 5% each and report the ones that came out — which is the 61% row of the table above.

03 Splitting the variation: one observation, three pieces

Everything rests on a single algebraic identity, and the identity rests on a single cancellation. Write each observation as the grand mean plus the group's departure from it plus the observation's departure from its own group mean. Square and sum, and the cross terms vanish — exactly as they did for the regression line in part 6, §11, and for exactly the same reason.

Notation, fixed once

k groups, the jth of size nⱼ, N observations in totalyᵢⱼ = observation i in group j, i = 1, …, nⱼ , j = 1, …, k N = n₁ + n₂ + … + n_k the group mean and the grand meanȳⱼ = (1/nⱼ) Σᵢ yᵢⱼ the mean of group j ȳ̄ = (1/N) Σⱼ Σᵢ yᵢⱼ = (1/N) Σⱼ nⱼ ȳⱼ the grand mean note the second form: the grand mean is the nⱼ-WEIGHTED mean of the group means, not their plain average — the two coincide only when the design is balancedrunning data: (14×47.428571 + 14×49.357143 + 13×45.538462)/41 = 47.487805 unweighted average of the three group means = 47.441392 ← different

The identity

split every observation, by adding and subtracting the two meansyᵢⱼ = ȳ̄ + (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) + (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) grand group effect residual move the grand mean across and square(yᵢⱼ − ȳ̄)² = (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄)² + (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ)² + 2(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄)(yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) sum over i within group j — the cross term dies, and this is the whole contentΣᵢ 2(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄)(yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) = 2(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) = 2(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) · 0 = 0 because deviations from a mean sum to zero, and (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) is CONSTANT within group j so, summing over j as well, EXACTLY:ΣⱼΣᵢ (yᵢⱼ − ȳ̄)² = Σⱼ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄)² + ΣⱼΣᵢ (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ)² SS_total = SS_between + SS_within
A notation collision inside this series, resolved here explicitly. These three quantities have no agreed abbreviations, and two incompatible conventions are both standard.
  • Part 6, §11 wrote SST = SSR + SSE, with SST meaning the total sum of squares, SSR the regression (explained) piece and SSE the error piece. That is the regression convention and it is the more common one in regression texts.
  • Many experimental-design texts write TSS = SST + SSE, with SST meaning the treatment sum of squares and TSS the total. The same three letters, SST, denote the total in one convention and the between-group piece in the other — and the two appear in the same equation.
  • This page uses neither. It writes SS_total, SS_between and SS_within throughout, which cannot be misread. Where part 6's table is quoted, its SST/SSR/SSE are kept and the translation is given: SST → SS_total, SSR → SS_between, SSE → SS_within. §10 shows that this is not merely a translation of labels but a genuine identity — the two decompositions are the same decomposition.
  • When reading anything else, check which SST is meant before trusting a number. The reliable tell is the degrees of freedom: the total always has N − 1, the between-group piece always has k − 1.

Read as geometry: it is Pythagoras

In ℝᴺ, put the centred data y − ȳ̄·1 as one vector. It splits into a vector that is constant within each group — the group-effects vector — and a vector of within-group deviations. Those two are orthogonal: their inner product is exactly the cross term that vanished above. So the identity is the Pythagorean theorem on a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the centred data, and SS_between and SS_within are the squared lengths of the two legs. Compare projections and least squares: the group-effects vector is the orthogonal projection of the data onto the k-dimensional subspace of vectors that are constant within groups, and the residual vector is what is left in the orthogonal complement.

the same statement in projection languageV₀ = span{1} dimension 1 the grand mean lives here V₁ = {vectors constant within each group} dimension k V₀ ⊂ V₁ y = P₀y + (P₁ − P₀)y + (I − P₁)y three orthogonal pieces their squared lengths, and their dimensions, are the ANOVA table‖P₀y‖² = Nȳ̄² dim 1 (the correction for the mean) ‖(P₁ − P₀)y‖² = SS_between dim k − 1 ‖(I − P₁)y‖² = SS_within dim N − k 1 + (k − 1) + (N − k) = N the dimensions add, as they must

Twelve per cent of the total variation in exam scores lies between the three regimes and eighty-eight per cent lies within them. That ratio is the entire raw material of the test; §04 turns it into a statistic by dividing each piece by its degrees of freedom, and §05 supplies the reference distribution. Note already what the boxplots cannot show: 12% is not nothing, and it is also not obviously something.

  • Pull the means apart and only one leg grows. Sliding the separation control moves the group means without touching the within-group scatter, so SS_within is constant to the eye while SS_between and SS_total grow together. The right triangle rotates; its within-group leg does not change length.
  • At separation zero the two kinds of data set behave differently, and the difference is the point. Sliding the fixed exam scores to zero forces the three sample means to coincide, so SS_between is exactly 0 — which is a stronger statement than the null hypothesis ever makes. Sliding a simulated set to zero makes the three populations identical and leaves the noise alone, and there SS_between is small but never zero, because sample means drawn from identical populations never coincide. That leftover is precisely what the F test calibrates against, and it is the reason F is compared to 1 rather than to 0.
  • The squares view is the honest picture of what a p-value is measuring. Not "is the between-group square big" but "is it big relative to the within-group square, allowing for how many degrees of freedom each one had to spend" — which is the next section.

04 Degrees of freedom, mean squares, and why the ratio is a test

Two sums of squares cannot be compared directly: they are built from different numbers of free quantities, so the bigger one is bigger partly for a trivial reason. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom converts both into variance estimates on a common scale — and then the comparison means something, because under the null both estimate the same σ².

Counting the degrees of freedom

SS_between is built from k group means, tied by one constraintthe k deviations (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) satisfy Σⱼ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) = 0 so df = k − 1 SS_within is built from N residuals, tied by one constraint per groupwithin group j: Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ) = 0 ⟹ nⱼ − 1 free residuals df = Σⱼ (nⱼ − 1) = N − k SS_total is built from N deviations tied by one constraintdf = N − 1 and (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1 ✓ the df add, like the SS the two mean squaresMS_between = SS_between/(k − 1) MS_within = SS_within/(N − k) the statisticF = MS_between / MS_within

MS_within is the pooled variance of the two-sample pooled t test extended to k groups: a weighted average of the k sample variances with weights nⱼ − 1.

MS_within is exactly the pooled variance, written two waysMS_within = Σⱼ (nⱼ − 1)sⱼ² / Σⱼ (nⱼ − 1) = SS_within/(N − k) running data(13×4.237457² + 13×4.482567² + 12×4.370648²)/38 = 723.873626/38 = 19.049306 s_pooled = √19.049306 = 4.364551 ← the number every pairwise comparison in §18 uses

What each mean square estimates — the reason the ratio is a test

the within-group mean square is unbiased for σ², null or no nullE[MS_within] = σ² ALWAYS the between-group mean square carries an extra, non-negative termE[MS_between] = σ² + ( Σⱼ nⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)² ) / (k − 1) where μ̄ = Σⱼ nⱼμⱼ/N so under H₀: μ₁ = … = μ_k the extra term is 0 and both estimate the same thingE[MS_between] = E[MS_within] = σ² ⟹ F should be near 1 under H₁ only the NUMERATOR inflates — which is why the test is ONE-SIDEDreject for LARGE F only. A small F means the group means are closer together than chance would predict, which is not evidence of a difference.
The one-sidedness is often misread. The F test is one-sided in the statistic and two-sided — many-sided, really — in the hypothesis. It rejects only for large F, but the alternative it is testing against is "the means are not all equal", which has no direction at all. A conspicuously small F is not evidence for the null; it is a signal that something is wrong with the design — data that were fabricated to look tidy, a blocking variable that was ignored, or observations that are not independent within groups. Fisher used exactly this reasoning to argue that a famous set of genetics data was too good to be true.

The expected-mean-square identity also says something practical: the non-null part of the numerator is Σ nⱼ(μⱼ − μ̄)²/(k − 1), which grows with the group sizes. Doubling every nⱼ doubles the excess while leaving σ² alone, so F grows roughly linearly in n for a fixed pattern of means. That is the same "significance can be bought with sample size" fact that part 8, §18 made about χ² = nφ², and §11 turns it into an effect-size warning.

Derivation of E[MS_between], in four lines

write μⱼ = μ + τⱼ with Σnⱼτⱼ = 0; then ȳⱼ = μ + τⱼ + ε̄ⱼ with Var(ε̄ⱼ) = σ²/nⱼȳⱼ − ȳ̄ = τⱼ + (ε̄ⱼ − ε̄) square, multiply by nⱼ, sum; the cross term has expectation 0 because E[ε̄ⱼ − ε̄] = 0E[SS_between] = Σⱼ nⱼτⱼ² + E[ Σⱼ nⱼ(ε̄ⱼ − ε̄)² ] the second term is the between-group SS of pure noise, which has k − 1 dfE[ Σⱼ nⱼ(ε̄ⱼ − ε̄)² ] = (k − 1)σ² divide by k − 1E[MS_between] = σ² + Σⱼ nⱼτⱼ²/(k − 1)

05 Why the ratio is an F: two independent chi-squares

"The ratio should be about 1" is not a test until there is a distribution for how far from 1 it wanders. That distribution is Fisher's F, and it appears for a reason that part 8, §04 set up and left as a promissory note: chi-squares add, and orthogonal pieces of a normal vector are independent. Both facts are needed, and both are doing real work.

The two ingredients

FACT 1 — additivity, from part 8. Sums of squares of independent normals are chi-squares, and independent chi-squares add, degrees of freedom and allX ~ χ²(a), Y ~ χ²(b), X ⫫ Y ⟹ X + Y ~ χ²(a + b) FACT 2 — the definition of the F distributionU ~ χ²(d₁), V ~ χ²(d₂), U ⫫ V ⟹ (U/d₁)/(V/d₂) ~ F(d₁, d₂) applied to the decomposition, under H₀ and the normal modelSS_between/σ² ~ χ²(k − 1) SS_within/σ² ~ χ²(N − k) and they are INDEPENDENT F = (SS_between/(k−1)) / (SS_within/(N−k)) ~ F(k − 1, N − k) note σ² cancels — which is why the test needs no knowledge of the error variance
Where part 8's promise is cashed. Part 8, §05 noted that "the additivity property … is also what will let part 9 decompose a total sum of squares into independent pieces." Here is the mechanism, and it is worth being precise, because additivity alone is not enough. Additivity says that if two chi-squares are independent their sum is a chi-square. What is needed here is the converse direction: SS_total/σ² ~ χ²(N − 1) is known, and it has been written as a sum of two pieces with (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1 degrees of freedom. Cochran's theorem says that when a chi-square is split into quadratic forms whose ranks add up to its degrees of freedom, the pieces are automatically independent chi-squares. The dimensions adding is not a bookkeeping coincidence — it is the hypothesis of the theorem, and it is what buys independence.

Why independence holds, geometrically

The reason is the orthogonality of §03, plus one special property of the normal distribution. If z is a standard normal vector in ℝᴺ and V₁ ⟂ V₂ are orthogonal subspaces, then the projections P₁z and P₂z are independent, not merely uncorrelated. That is a fact about the multivariate normal specifically: its density is spherically symmetric, so the coordinates in any orthonormal basis are independent, and a projection onto a subspace only involves the coordinates spanning it. Rotate the basis so that the first k − 1 axes span the group-effects space and the remaining N − k span the residual space, and the two sums of squares are sums over disjoint sets of independent squared normals.

the chain, with the load-bearing step markednormal errors ⟹ spherical symmetry ⟹ independent coordinates in ANY orthonormal basis ⟹ orthogonal projections are INDEPENDENT ⟹ the two sums of squares are independent ⟹ their scaled ratio is an F. NORMALITY is the assumption doing this work — not the CLT, not large Nfor non-normal errors, uncorrelated ≠ independent, and the F distribution is only an approximation. §09 measures how good an approximation, and it is surprisingly good.

Reading the distribution

  • The mean of F(d₁, d₂) is d₂/(d₂ − 2) for d₂ > 2 — just above 1, and tending to 1 as the error degrees of freedom grow. "Around 1 under the null" is exactly right and slightly conservative.
  • It is right-skewed and lives on (0, ∞), so the 5% critical value is well above 1: F(2, 38) has F* = 3.2448. Small numerator df give fat tails; the critical value falls as either df grows.
  • F(1, ν) = t(ν)² exactly — the identity part 6, §13 proved. §10 uses it for k = 2.
  • d₁·F(d₁, ∞) = χ²(d₁). When the error variance is effectively known (huge N) the denominator is 1 and the F test collapses into a chi-square test — the same limit that makes part 8's statistic a chi-square rather than an F.
  • 1/F(d₁, d₂) ~ F(d₂, d₁), which is how one-tailed F tables were used before software: the lower tail of one is the upper tail of the other.

06 The ANOVA table — and an audit of a published one

Everything above fits in five columns and three rows, and the layout has been stable for ninety years. It is worth knowing not because the arithmetic is hard but because the table is self-checking: the sums of squares must add, the degrees of freedom must add, each mean square must equal its sum of squares over its own degrees of freedom, and the F must be the ratio of the two mean squares. Four constraints on eleven numbers. This section works the table for the running data and then applies those four checks to a published table, which fails two of them.

That is the general lesson: arithmetic errors in a summary table are usually invisible from the conclusion and visible in seconds from the four internal checks, and the four checks cost nothing.

This is the third such find in this series — part 6 and part 8 each turned up arithmetic errors in the tables they were reproducing. Recomputing every displayed number rather than copying it is not pedantry; it has a hit rate.

07 Why you cannot see it in the boxplots: the square-root law, drawn

The natural first move with k groups is side-by-side boxplots, and the natural next move is to decide from them whether the groups differ. That decision cannot be made by eye, and the reason is not impatience or lack of skill — it is arithmetic. Boxplots show the spread of the data. The test compares the spread of the means. Those two spreads differ by a factor of √n, so a separation that is overwhelming to the test can be genuinely invisible in the picture.

The square-root law, applied to this problem

from part 3: the sampling distribution of a group meanSD(individual observations in group j) = σ SD(ȳⱼ) = σ/√nⱼ ← smaller by √nⱼ so a difference in means of δ is, in the two currencies:measured against the DATA's spread: δ/σ — what a boxplot shows measured against the MEANS' spread: δ√n/σ — what the test uses with n = 30, the test sees a difference √30 = 5.48 times larger than the eye doesδ = 3.5, σ = 10: δ/σ = 0.35 of a boxplot's width — invisible δ√n/σ = 1.92 standard errors — clearly detectable

This is why the analysis of variance exists as a computation rather than as a rule for reading pictures, and it is the one point about the method that everybody who uses it should internalise. It also runs the other way: with n = 4 per group, boxes that barely touch can be entirely consistent with chance. The picture and the test disagree in both directions, and the test is right.

The laboratory: move the groups apart and watch F, p and the decomposition move with them the left panel is what the eye sees · the middle panel is what the test sees

Six experiments to run in it

  1. Start at the defaults and read the two panels against each other. Three groups of thirty, noise σ = 10, means 3.5 apart. The boxes on the left overlap so heavily that no honest reader would call them different; the means in the middle panel, plotted with their standard errors, are visibly separated. The readout reports a significant F. That contradiction is the point of the section, and it is not an illusion in either direction — both panels are drawing the same numbers on different scales.
  2. Now drag n down to 6 without touching anything else. The boxes look about the same — the data's spread has not changed — but the standard errors in the middle panel expand by √5 and the p-value collapses to nothing. Same effect, same noise, five times fewer subjects, and the evidence is gone.
  3. Set the separation to exactly zero. The three means are identical in the population, and the middle panel still shows them scattered. Press "draw a new sample" a dozen times and count how often p < 0.05: it should be about one time in twenty, which is the definition of the significance level and the cheapest demonstration of it available.
  4. Push k to 6 at zero separation. The F test's rejection rate stays at one in twenty even though there are now fifteen pairs of means to compare. This is §02's table, live.
  5. Take the separation up until the boxes visibly separate. On the default settings that needs δ around 12 — by which point p is smaller than 10⁻¹⁰. By the time an effect is visible in a boxplot it is many orders of magnitude past significance. The eye's threshold and the test's threshold are not close.
  6. Load the running data. Three real groups, p = 0.0888, and boxes that overlap almost completely — the honest case, where the picture and the test agree that there is not enough here.
The corollary nobody likes. If a boxplot cannot be trusted to reveal a significant difference, it also cannot be trusted to reveal an important one — and those are different questions. A separation of 0.35σ is invisible and highly significant at n = 30; whether it matters is a question about exam scores, not about statistics. That is §11's subject, and it is the reason a p-value should never be reported without an effect size.
