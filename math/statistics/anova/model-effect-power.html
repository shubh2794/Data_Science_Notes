08 The model behind the table, in two equivalent forms

The table of §06 is arithmetic and needs no assumptions. The p-value in its last column needs a probability model, and that model has two standard parameterisations. They describe exactly the same set of distributions; the second one is worth the extra notation because it makes the null hypothesis a statement about zero rather than about equality, and because it is what generalises to §21's two factors.

Form 1 — cell means

the modelyᵢⱼ = μⱼ + εᵢⱼ , εᵢⱼ independent Normal(0, σ²) k + 1 parameters: μ₁, …, μ_k and σ²H₀ : μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μ_k H₁ : not all equal estimatesμ̂ⱼ = ȳⱼ σ̂² = MS_within = SS_within/(N − k)

Form 2 — effects

split each group mean into a common level and a departure from itτⱼ = μⱼ − μ so yᵢⱼ = μ + τⱼ + εᵢⱼ τⱼ is the TREATMENT EFFECT of group j, and the null becomes a statement about zeroH₀ : τ₁ = τ₂ = … = τ_k = 0 this form has one parameter too many, so it needs a side conditionΣⱼ nⱼ τⱼ = 0 (weighted, the usual choice) or Σⱼ τⱼ = 0 (unweighted) or τ₁ = 0 (corner / reference coding — what regression software uses) estimates under the weighted conditionμ̂ = ȳ̄ τ̂ⱼ = ȳⱼ − ȳ̄ ε̂ᵢⱼ = yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ which is EXACTLY the identity of §03, term for termyᵢⱼ = ȳ̄ + (ȳⱼ − ȳ̄) + (yᵢⱼ − ȳⱼ)
The side condition is not innocuous when the design is unbalanced. The two conventions estimate different μ. Under Σ nⱼτⱼ = 0 the estimate of the common level is the grand mean, 47.487805; under Σ τⱼ = 0 it is the unweighted average of the three group means, 47.441392. The fitted values, the residuals, the sums of squares and the F test are identical under every choice — the constraint only decides how the same fit is described. This is the same non-identifiability that makes dummy-variable coding a choice rather than a fact, and §10 shows the two side by side. It matters as soon as anyone quotes "the effect of treatment j" as a number.

The four assumptions, and what each one is holding up

AssumptionWhat it saysWhat breaks without itWhat survives
A1 · independence within groupsthe nⱼ observations in a group carry no information about each otherEverything. MS_within estimates the wrong thing, usually far too small, so F is far too large and p far too small. Clustered or repeated measurements are the common cause.Nothing. There is no reference distribution and no fix except a model of the dependence (§21).
A2 · independence across groupswhich group a unit is in is unrelated to its outcome except through the treatmentThe causal reading. If students chose their own regime, a significant F measures self-selection as readily as teaching. Randomisation is what buys this, and nothing else does.The test is still a valid test of "these three populations have the same mean"; it is the interpretation that collapses.
A3 · equal variancesVar(εᵢⱼ) = σ², the same in every groupThe level of the test — and by a lot when the group sizes are also unequal. §14 measures a true type I error of 28% at a nominal 5%.Nothing much, but the repair is cheap and complete: Welch's F (§14).
A4 · normal errorsεᵢⱼ ~ Normal(0, σ²)Only the exactness of the F distribution. §09 measures the damage and it is small for balanced designs, even for badly skewed data.Almost everything. This is the least load-bearing of the four, which surprises people who worry about it most.

The ordering matters. The assumption people check is A4, by staring at a normal quantile plot. The assumption that actually destroys the analysis is A1, which no plot of the residuals will reveal, because it is a property of how the data were collected rather than of the numbers. Part 2, §09 is where that is settled, before any data exist.

09 How much the assumptions actually matter, measured

"The F test assumes normal errors with equal variances" is true and almost useless, because it does not say what happens when they fail. This section replaces the warning with numbers. The headline is worth stating in advance: non-normality is nearly harmless when the groups are the same size and dangerous when they are not; unequal variances are the reverse of harmless in both cases.

Non-normality: sixty thousand null experiments per cell

  • With equal group sizes the errors go the safe way. Skew and heavy tails make the test conservative — lognormal data reject at 3.1% instead of 5%, Cauchy data at 1.7%. You lose power, you do not manufacture false positives. That is a much better failure mode than the one people fear.
  • With unequal group sizes the sign flips. The same Cauchy errors that gave 1.7% at (30, 30, 30) give 7.6% at (5, 15, 40). Balance is doing more work here than normality is.
  • Two-valued data are fine once n is moderate. An ANOVA on a 0/1 outcome is legitimate — it is the same arithmetic as comparing k proportions — but at n = 5 with p = 0.1 most groups are all zeros and the statistic is degenerate. That is a small-n problem, not a normality problem; part 8, §10 is the right tool there.
  • The mechanism is the central limit theorem, applied to group means. The F statistic is a function of the k group means and the pooled variance, and by part 3, §06 those means are approximately normal long before the data are. The residual sensitivity is in the variance estimate, which converges more slowly and is what heavy tails damage.

Dependence, which is worse than everything else on this page put together

The table above is reassuring about normality. Nothing on this page should be reassuring about independence, and it is worth putting the two failures on the same scale to see how unequal they are. Suppose the observations within a group are exchangeably correlated — students working in pairs, repeated measurements on the same subject, samples from the same batch — with intraclass correlation ρ. Compare the magnitudes. The worst non-normality in the table above moved the error rate from 5.0% to 7.6%, and in the safe direction for balanced designs. An intraclass correlation of 0.05 — a correlation of one twentieth, which no residual plot will ever reveal — moves it to 13.1%. And Welch does not help: 12.4% at the same ρ, because Welch repairs unequal variances and this is not that. Neither does the permutation F of §13, because dependent observations are not exchangeable. There is no test on this page that survives dependence, and there is no diagnostic on this page that detects it, because it is a fact about how the data were collected rather than about the numbers. That is why §08 ranks A1 first and normality last, and why part 2, §09 — which decides the collection — outranks everything here.

On the running data all four are in reasonable shape. The three group standard deviations are 4.237, 4.483 and 4.371 — a ratio of 1.06, which is as equal as real data ever get. The lowest score, 34 in the study-only group, sits below that group's lower fence (Q1 − 1.5·IQR = 44 − 6 = 38) and is the one point worth a second look. Deleting it moves the study-only mean from 45.538 up to 46.500, which pulls the three groups closer together: SS_between falls from 98.370 to 56.132, F falls from 2.582 to 1.792, and p rises from 0.0888 to 0.1809. A single observation out of forty-one halves the statistic. Note which way it went — the outlier was helping the apparent effect, not creating a spurious one, which is the opposite of what the word "outlier" leads people to expect. Reporting both analyses, as here, is the only defensible move; §23 returns to it.

10 One-way ANOVA is regression on k − 1 indicators

The two methods are taught in different chapters, described in different vocabularies and computed by different software functions, and they are the same procedure. Give the regression of part 6 a set of indicator columns instead of a numeric predictor and its output is the ANOVA table of §06 — not analogous to it, not approximately equal to it, but the identical numbers to machine precision. Making that concrete resolves the notation collision of §03 and buys the whole apparatus of regression diagnostics for free.

The design matrix

the k group means, written as a linear model with k − 1 dummy columns (group 1 = reference)yᵢ = β₀ + β₁·D₂ᵢ + β₂·D₃ᵢ + εᵢ , Dⱼᵢ = 1 if unit i is in group j, else 0 what the coefficients then meanβ₀ = μ₁ the reference group's mean β₀+β₁ = μ₂ so β₁ = μ₂ − μ₁ a DIFFERENCE from the reference β₀+β₂ = μ₃ so β₂ = μ₃ − μ₁ and the null of §08 becomes a null about REGRESSION COEFFICIENTSH₀ : μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ ⟺ H₀ : β₁ = β₂ = 0 which is exactly the null the regression F tests — the overall F of part 6, §13

Why k − 1 and not k? Because a column for every group plus an intercept is one column too many: the k dummies sum to the column of ones, so the design matrix is singular and the coefficients are not identifiable. This is §08's side condition wearing different clothes, and every choice of side condition corresponds to a choice of coding.

Coding is a choice of parameterisation, not of model. What it changes is which comparisons come out of the software as coefficients with standard errors attached — which is why software defaults matter and why "the effect of treatment j" is meaningless until the coding is stated.

What the translation buys, in both directions

  • Every regression diagnostic applies to an ANOVA. Residual plots, quantile plots, leverage, Cook's distance (part 6, §16) — all of them, unchanged. In a one-way layout the leverage of an observation is exactly 1/nⱼ, so small groups are automatically high-leverage, which is a precise version of the warning that unbalanced designs are fragile.
  • Every ANOVA fact applies to a regression. The overall F of a multiple regression is testing exactly what the one-way F tests: "does the model explain more than noise".
  • Continuous and categorical predictors mix freely. Add a numeric covariate to the indicator columns and you have analysis of covariance; the machinery does not notice the difference. That is the whole of the general linear model, and it is why part 2's block and factorial designs are all ordinary regressions.
  • The computation is one least-squares solve. See projections and least squares: the fitted values are P₁y for the projection onto the group-indicator column space, exactly as §03 said.

The mistake this makes obvious: coding groups as a number

If the three regimes are entered as a single numeric column 1, 2, 3, the regression fits a straight line across the group index and spends one degree of freedom instead of two. It tests something much narrower — "do the means increase or decrease linearly across the groups in the order I happened to list them" — and it can be badly wrong when the ordering is arbitrary.

the running data, regressed on the group index 1, 2, 3slope b = −0.908680 SS explained = 22.273740 on 1 df that is 22.6% of SS_between = 98.370276 — the other 77.4% is invisible to it F(1, 39) = 1.085885 p = 0.303802 ← far weaker than the real test why: the true pattern is 47.43, 49.36, 45.54 — up then down. A straight line through an ordering that has no meaning captures almost none of it.

Fitting a line across an ordered factor is a legitimate and powerful thing to do — it is a planned contrast with one degree of freedom, and §19 shows why one-degree-of-freedom questions are more powerful than omnibus ones when they are the right question. Doing it to an unordered factor is an error, and the tell is that relabelling the groups changes the answer.

The special case k = 2, where the F is a t²

Part 6, §13 proved t² = F for a regression slope and verified it to 2 × 10⁻¹⁴. With two groups the design matrix has one dummy column, the slope is the difference in means, and the identity says the one-way F is the square of the pooled two-sample t. It is not re-derived here; only checked, because it is the hinge that makes ANOVA a genuine generalisation rather than a different method.

running data, homework-only against homework-plus-peer-assessmentpooled t = −1.169837533846 t² = 1.368519855596 one-way F = 1.368519855596 |difference| = 6.9 × 10⁻¹⁵ both p-values = 0.252678772931 and over 200 000 random two-group data sets with varying n and σworst relative |t² − F|/F = 3.0 × 10⁻¹¹ i.e. floating-point noise only
Two consequences worth carrying. First, the F test can only ever reproduce a two-sided t: squaring destroys the sign, so if the question is directional the t is the right tool and the F throws information away. Second, t does not generalise and F does — with k groups there are C(k,2) separate t statistics and no single one of them answers "is any of this real", while a single F does, on k − 1 numerator degrees of freedom, with one error rate. That trade — one omnibus question in exchange for one error rate — is the bargain the whole page is built on, and §§16–20 are the bill.

11 Effect size: what the F refuses to tell you

A significant F says the group means are further apart than chance explains. It says nothing whatever about how far apart they are, because the statistic is a ratio in which the sample size appears and the p-value is a monotone function of that ratio. §04 showed the mechanism: the non-null part of E[MS_between] is proportional to n. Report a p-value alone and you have reported something that can be made arbitrarily small by collecting more data from an unchanged world.

Three measures, and what each corrects for

η² (eta squared) — the raw share of variation between groups. Identical to R² (§10).η² = SS_between / SS_total 0 ≤ η² ≤ 1 it is BIASED UPWARD: even under H₀ it has a positive expectationE[η²] under H₀ = (k − 1)/(N − 1) running data: 2/40 = 0.050 ω² (omega squared) — the bias-corrected version, an estimate of the POPULATION shareω² = ( SS_between − (k − 1)·MS_within ) / ( SS_total + MS_within ) it can come out NEGATIVE, and a negative estimate is not a mistake — it means the observed between-group variation is smaller than noise alone would produce. Report 0. Cohen's f — the between-group SD in units of the within-group SDf = √( η²/(1 − η²) ) conventions: 0.10 small, 0.25 medium, 0.40 large partial η² — identical to η² for one factor; differs only when there are several (§21)partial η²(A) = SS_A / (SS_A + SS_error) denominator EXCLUDES other factors

Cohen's f of 0.37 is a LARGE effect by convention. p = 0.089 is not significant.
A large effect and a non-significant test are not a contradiction; they are the normal situation in a small study. The running experiment estimates a large effect very imprecisely. The correct summary is "the regimes may well differ substantially, and forty-one students cannot establish it" — which is a different sentence from "the regimes do not differ", and it points at a different next action: run it again with more students, not abandon the idea. Conflating the two is the single most consequential misreading of a non-significant result, and it is why part 5, §12 insists on reporting an interval or an effect size alongside every p-value.

12 Power, and how many subjects the design needed

The non-significant F of §06 raises an obvious question that §11 half-answered: could this experiment have detected the effect it was looking for? Part 5, §08 owns power in general; this section applies it to k groups, which requires exactly one new object — the non-central F distribution — and then becomes design arithmetic.

The non-central F

under H₁ the numerator chi-square acquires a non-centrality parameterSS_between/σ² ~ non-central χ²(k − 1, λ) with λ = Σⱼ nⱼτⱼ²/σ² so the statistic follows a NON-CENTRAL F, and power is its tail beyond the critical valuepower = P( F(k−1, N−k; λ) > F*₍k−1, N−k; 1−α₎ ) for a balanced design the non-centrality is N times the squared effect sizeλ = N f² where f is Cohen's f of §11 so power depends on the design ONLY through N, k and f — three numbers

The running experiment had about a coin-flip's chance. With roughly fourteen students per group and a true effect of the size actually observed (f = 0.368638), the power is 0.5273. A non-significant result from a design with 53% power is close to uninformative: it is what you would expect about half the time even if the effect were exactly as large as it appears. This is the honest reading of p = 0.089, and it is a far more useful sentence than "no significant difference was found."

  • More groups need fewer subjects each and more in total. Each extra group adds information about the spread of the means, but also a degree of freedom to the numerator, which raises the critical value. The total climbs steadily.
  • Do not compute "observed power" after the fact. Plugging the observed effect size back in gives 0.5160 for the running data (the design is n = 14, 14, 13, so this is not quite the balanced 0.5273 above), and that number is a deterministic, decreasing function of the p-value — it contains no information the p-value did not. It is not evidence that the study was adequately powered and it is not evidence that it was not. Power is a property of a design and a hypothesised effect, computed before the data.
  • Unequal group sizes cost power, and the loss is governed by the harmonic mean of the nⱼ. A design with (5, 15, 40) has the same total as (20, 20, 20) and materially less power, because the smallest group limits every comparison involving it.
