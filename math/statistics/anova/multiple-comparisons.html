16 The multiple testing fallacy, and the look-elsewhere effect

The second half of the page starts here, and it starts with a real failure. A large study set out to test whether living near a power line was associated with adverse health outcomes. It reported a highly significant increase in one of them — childhood leukaemia — at better than the 1% level. A careful follow-up failed to confirm it. Nothing was fabricated and no arithmetic was wrong. The study examined eight hundred health outcomes.

The arithmetic that explains it

from part 5: under a true null the p-value is UNIFORM on (0, 1)P(p < 0.01) = 0.01 exactly, by construction, for every one of the tests so with m = 800 independent tests and NOT ONE REAL EFFECT anywhereE[# results with p < 0.01] = 800 × 0.01 = 8 P(at least one such result) = 1 − 0.99⁸⁰⁰ = 0.99968 P(at least 8) = 0.5477 P(at least 15) = 0.0168 so finding one "highly significant" outcome among 800 is not evidence of anything. Finding EIGHT is the expected yield of pure noise.
This is the look-elsewhere effect, and its formal name is worth having. The p-value answers "how surprising is this result, at this location". What a reader wants to know is "how surprising is the most extreme result anywhere I looked", and those are different questions with different answers whenever the search space is larger than one. The name comes from particle physics, where a bump in a spectrum has to be discounted by the width of the window it could have appeared in; the statistical content is identical. Whenever a result is reported as the most striking of many, the reported p-value is the answer to a question nobody asked.

Data snooping, and why it is worse than counting tests

Counting m is the easy case: the researcher knows the number and can correct for it. Data snooping — also called data dredging — is the case where m is unknown, often to the analyst as much as to the reader.

explicit multiplicity
m is known
Eight hundred outcomes, twenty subgroups, three pairwise comparisons. Correctable, by everything in §§17–20. The researcher can state m and the reader can check it.
implicit multiplicity
m is unknown
Every choice made after seeing the data — which outliers to drop, which transformation, which covariates, where to cut a continuous variable, when to stop collecting — is a fork, and the effective m is the number of paths through the forking tree. Nobody can count it, so nobody can correct it.
the honest response
Fix the analysis before the data
Pre-registration removes implicit multiplicity by making m = 1 true rather than assumed. A held-out validation set (§22) achieves the same thing computationally. There is no post-hoc correction for a search you did not record.

Part 5, §13 is where the critique of this practice lives, together with the garden of forking paths and the incentives that produce it; this page supplies the arithmetic underneath and does not repeat the argument.

17 Controlling the family-wise error rate: Bonferroni, Šidák, Holm

The first thing to control is the probability of making any false claim in the whole family — the family-wise error rate. Three procedures do it with no assumptions worth speaking of, they are ordered by power, and the best of the three is not the famous one.

The target, stated precisely

a family of m hypotheses, of which an unknown subset are trueFWER = P( at least one TRUE null hypothesis is rejected ) two strengths of guarantee, and the difference mattersWEAK control : FWER ≤ α when ALL m nulls are true (the "global null") STRONG control: FWER ≤ α for EVERY configuration of true and false nulls strong control is what you want; weak control is what an omnibus F gives you. Bonferroni, Holm, Šidák, Tukey and Scheffé all control it STRONGLY.
Weak control is why the omnibus F is not enough. The F test guarantees 5% under the complete null and says nothing about the case where two of five groups genuinely differ and the other three do not — which is the realistic case. §18 measures what happens then: "run the F, and if it rejects, do unadjusted pairwise t tests" has a family-wise error rate of 12.0% at k = 4 when one group is genuinely different, against a nominal 5%.

Bonferroni

two equivalent statements of the same rulereject Hᵢ when pᵢ ≤ α/m — lower the threshold or report p̃ᵢ = min(1, m·pᵢ) — inflate the p-value, then read it at α the proof is one line, and it assumes NOTHING about dependenceP(∪ᵢ {pᵢ ≤ α/m}) ≤ Σᵢ P(pᵢ ≤ α/m) = m₀ · α/m ≤ α — Boole's inequality, over the m₀ true nulls. No independence, no normality, no model.
  • Its universality is its selling point. Boole's inequality holds for any dependence structure whatever, which is why Bonferroni is the correction you can always apply and never have to defend.
  • Its conservatism is real and measurable. §02's simulation: at k = 10 with 45 pairwise comparisons, Bonferroni's measured FWER is 0.0353 against a nominal 0.05. It is spending 30% of its error budget on nothing, and that budget is power.
  • The conservatism grows with m and with positive dependence, which is exactly the situation of pairwise comparisons — they overlap heavily, so the union bound is loose.
  • It "guards against having even one false positive", which is the right goal for a regulatory submission and the wrong goal for an exploratory scan of ten thousand genes, where it leaves no power at all. §20 is the alternative.

Šidák — the same idea, assuming independence

if the m tests really were independent, the exact threshold is availableFWER = 1 − (1 − α_each)^m = α ⟹ α_each = 1 − (1 − α)^(1/m) m = 3, α = 0.05Šidák 1 − 0.95^(1/3) = 0.016952 Bonferroni 0.05/3 = 0.016667 m = 45Šidák 1 − 0.95^(1/45) = 0.0011392 Bonferroni 0.05/45 = 0.0011111 so Šidák is very slightly less strict — and it BUYS ALMOST NOTHING

Šidák's threshold is uniformly a shade larger than Bonferroni's, and the gap is a fraction of a per cent even at m = 45. In exchange it requires independence, which pairwise comparisons never have. It is worth knowing and rarely worth using: the correction it improves on is the one that needs no assumptions, and it gives up that property for a rounding error.

Holm's step-down — strictly better than Bonferroni, for free

sort the p-values, then walk DOWN the list with a threshold that relaxes as you go1. order them: p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍ₘ₎ 2. compare p₍ᵢ₎ against α/(m − i + 1) 3. reject p₍₁₎, …, p₍ᵣ₎ where r is the LAST index before the first failure; once a comparison fails, STOP — everything from there on is retained. the sequence of thresholds for m = 5 at α = 0.05α/5 = 0.0100 α/4 = 0.0125 α/3 = 0.0167 α/2 = 0.0250 α/1 = 0.0500 the first step IS Bonferroni; every later step is strictly more generous as adjusted p-values, with the monotonicity enforcedp̃₍ᵢ₎ = max over j ≤ i of min(1, (m − j + 1)·p₍ⱼ₎)
Holm uniformly dominates Bonferroni, and the phrase means something exact. Every hypothesis Bonferroni rejects, Holm also rejects — because Holm's first threshold is α/m — and Holm sometimes rejects more. It controls the FWER strongly, under arbitrary dependence, exactly as Bonferroni does. There is therefore no situation in which Bonferroni is the right choice and Holm is not. The only reasons Bonferroni is more common are that it is one line of arithmetic and that it produces a single fixed threshold, which is convenient to quote. Neither is a statistical reason.
a worked comparison, m = 5, α = 0.05sorted p: 0.008 0.011 0.030 0.041 0.190 Bonferroni threshold 0.010 for all five → rejects ONE (0.008) Holm: 0.008 ≤ 0.0100 ✓ 0.011 ≤ 0.0125 ✓ 0.030 ≤ 0.0167 ✗ → STOP → rejects TWO and note the step-down STOP rule: 0.041 is not tested against α/2 = 0.025 — once the walk halts, everything after it is retained regardless of its value.

A caution about the measured FWER. §02's simulation gives Bonferroni and Holm the identical family-wise error rate at every k — 0.0422, 0.0393, 0.0377, 0.0353. That is not a bug in the simulation and not a contradiction of the dominance claim. Under the complete null, where every hypothesis is true, Holm rejects something if and only if its first step fires, and its first step is Bonferroni's rule. The two procedures can only diverge when some hypotheses are false — which is where Holm's extra rejections are true discoveries. Holm's advantage is in power, not in level, and a global-null simulation is structurally incapable of showing it.

18 Tukey's honest significant difference, and the studentised range

Bonferroni and Holm work for any family of tests, which is their strength and the source of their conservatism: they know nothing about the structure of the comparisons. All pairwise comparisons of k group means have a very specific structure — they are all differences among the same k numbers, sharing one pooled variance estimate — and exploiting it gives an exact answer rather than a bound. The distribution that does it is the studentised range, and the procedure is Tukey's HSD.

The idea

the family-wise error occurs exactly when the LARGEST |difference| is too largeany pairwise error ⟺ maxᵢⱼ |ȳᵢ − ȳⱼ| exceeds the cutoff so control the distribution of the RANGE of the k means, not of each difference the studentised range: the range of k iid normals, divided by an independent sq = ( max_j Zⱼ − min_j Zⱼ ) / (S/σ) Zⱼ ~ N(0, σ²) iid, νS²/σ² ~ χ²(ν), S ⫫ Z written q ~ q(k, ν) two parameters: how many means, and the error df the balanced-design ruledeclare μᵢ ≠ μⱼ when |ȳᵢ − ȳⱼ| > q₍k, N−k; 1−α₎ · √(MS_within/n) the Tukey–Kramer extension, for unequal group sizes|ȳᵢ − ȳⱼ| > q₍k, N−k; 1−α₎ · √( (MS_within/2)(1/nᵢ + 1/nⱼ) ) and the matching simultaneous confidence intervals — the more useful output(ȳᵢ − ȳⱼ) ± q₍k, N−k; 1−α₎ · √( (MS_within/2)(1/nᵢ + 1/nⱼ) )

The distribution, and how it was checked

There is no closed form. The cumulative distribution function is a double integral, and it is computed here numerically rather than looked up.

the range of k iid standard normalsP(W_k ≤ w) = k ∫ φ(z) [ Φ(z) − Φ(z − w) ]^(k−1) dz then mix over the independent scale estimate s = S/σ, whose density isf(s) = ν^(ν/2) / ( Γ(ν/2) 2^(ν/2 − 1) ) · s^(ν−1) e^(−νs²/2) P(q(k, ν) ≤ q) = ∫₀^∞ f(s) · P(W_k ≤ q·s) ds both integrals by Gauss–Legendre quadrature on partitioned intervals; the quantile by bisection on the monotone cdf.
How this page's q was verified, since it is new code rather than a table lookup. Four independent checks, all before it was used for anything.
  • Against an independent implementation, on a grid of 187 combinations spanning k = 2 … 20 and ν = 2 … 500: worst absolute disagreement 1.4 × 10⁻⁸ over the region this page uses (k ≤ 12, ν ≥ 5), and 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ in the extreme corner k = 20, ν = 2, which nothing here goes near. At the 1% level the worst disagreement over twelve cells was 5 × 10⁻¹⁰.
  • Against published critical-value tables. q₀.₉₅(3, 10) = 3.8768 against a tabulated 3.88; (3, 20) = 3.5779 against 3.58; (4, 10) = 4.3266 against 4.33; (5, 20) = 4.2319 against 4.23; (10, 20) = 5.0079 against 5.01; (6, 12) = 4.7502 against 4.75 — agreement to the last tabulated digit throughout.

Read the third row carefully, because it is the whole point of the section. An unadjusted t test on peer-assessment against study-only gives p = 0.0289 — significant at 5%, and a tempting sentence to write. Tukey gives 0.0722, Bonferroni 0.0866, Holm 0.0866. None of them clears the bar, and neither did the omnibus F (p = 0.0888). The unadjusted comparison is significant only because it was chosen after looking — it is the largest of the three differences, and the largest of three is not distributed like one of three. The correction is not a technicality that shrinks a real finding; it is the difference between a result and an artefact of selection.

  • Tukey is exact for balanced designs, and the simulation says so: 0.0496, 0.0492, 0.0501, 0.0495, 0.0499 against a nominal 0.05, with Monte Carlo standard error 0.0005. It uses its entire error budget and no more. That is what a procedure built for this specific family buys over a general-purpose bound.
  • Bonferroni and Holm leave a third of the budget unspent at k = 10 — 0.0353 against 0.05 — and that unspent budget is power that could have been used.
  • Scheffé is enormously conservative for pairwise comparisons: 0.0020 at k = 10, twenty-five times too strict. §19 explains why that is not a defect — it is protecting against a much larger family.
  • Tukey–Kramer, with unequal group sizes, is slightly conservative rather than exact. Measured: (5,10,25) → 0.0487; (5,5,30) → 0.0480; (4,8,12,20) → 0.0472; (3,3,3,3,30) → 0.0469. The exactness proof requires equal nⱼ; the extension is conservative, which is the safe direction, and never by much.

Where Tukey does not apply

  • Only for all pairwise comparisons. The studentised range is the distribution of the largest of all C(k,2) differences. Do a subset and Tukey is conservative; do anything that is not a pairwise difference — "the average of groups 1 and 2 against group 3" — and it does not apply at all. That is §19's territory.
  • Only with a pooled variance. Unequal variances break it exactly as they break the F. The Games–Howell procedure is the Welch-flavoured version, using each pair's own standard error and Welch degrees of freedom with the same q critical value.
  • Dunnett's test is the specialist when there is a control. Comparing k − 1 treatments against one control is k − 1 comparisons, not C(k,2), and Dunnett's distribution exploits that to give shorter intervals than Tukey. If the design has a control group, use it.

19 Contrasts: the conceptual heart of the multiplicity problem

Everything in §§17–18 corrects for the number of comparisons. This section explains why the number matters at all, and the answer is not "because more tests means more chances". It is that the selection is part of the procedure. A comparison chosen before the data and the identical comparison chosen after seeing which one looks biggest are two different statistical acts with two different error rates, and no amount of arithmetic on the data can tell them apart. That is the whole problem, and contrasts are where it becomes visible.

What a contrast is

a CONTRAST is a weighted combination of the group means whose weights sum to zeroL = Σⱼ cⱼ μⱼ with Σⱼ cⱼ = 0 the sum-to-zero condition is what makes it a COMPARISON rather than a level: add a constant to every group mean and L does not move. estimate, standard error, and testL̂ = Σⱼ cⱼ ȳⱼ SE(L̂) = √( MS_within · Σⱼ cⱼ²/nⱼ ) t = L̂/SE(L̂) on N − k df and the contrast's own sum of squares is SS(L) = L̂² / Σⱼ cⱼ²/nⱼ which carries exactly ONE degree of freedom every pairwise comparison is a contrast: cᵢ = +1, cⱼ = −1, the rest 0

A single one-degree-of-freedom question, asked in advance, is more powerful than the omnibus test, because it spends all of its power looking in one direction instead of spreading it over a two-dimensional space of alternatives. If the experiment was designed to answer that question, that is the number to report, with no multiplicity adjustment at all, and the omnibus F is irrelevant to it. If the contrast was chosen after seeing that the study-only group came out lowest, the number is worthless.

Orthogonal contrasts, and the exact partition of SS_between

two contrasts are ORTHOGONAL whenΣⱼ c₁ⱼ c₂ⱼ / nⱼ = 0 — note the division by nⱼ for a BALANCED design this reduces to the familiar Σⱼ c₁ⱼc₂ⱼ = 0; for unequal nⱼ it does NOT, and the balanced criterion gives the wrong answer a complete orthogonal set has k − 1 members and partitions SS_between EXACTLYSS_between = SS(L₁) + SS(L₂) + … + SS(L₍k−1₎) on the running data, with n = (14, 14, 13)L_A = (+1, −1, 0) SS = 26.035714 "PA against plain homework" L_B = (½, ½, −1) SS = 72.334562 "homework against no homework" check orthogonality: Σ c_Aⱼc_Bⱼ/nⱼ = (1)(½)/14 + (−1)(½)/14 + 0 = 0 ✓ check the partition : 26.035714 + 72.334562 = 98.370276 = SS_between ✓ exactly

Two questions, two degrees of freedom, and between them they account for every scrap of the between-group variation. That is what the omnibus test's two degrees of freedom were, decomposed into questions somebody might actually want answered. An orthogonal set of planned contrasts is strictly more informative than the F test, and it costs nothing extra: because the contrasts are orthogonal, their estimates are uncorrelated, and asking both does not distort either.

The orthogonality criterion for unequal group sizes catches people out. With n = (14, 14, 13), the pair (1, −1, 0) and (½, ½, −1) satisfies both criteria, because the only group whose size differs is multiplied by a zero in the first contrast. Now take c₁ = (1, 0, −1) and c₂ = (1, −2, 1) instead. The unweighted criterion says they are orthogonal — 1 − 0 − 1 = 0 exactly — and the weighted one says they are not: 1/14 + 0 − 1/13 = −0.0054945. The consequence is arithmetic and unmissable: SS(L₁) = 24.081400 and SS(L₂) = 76.096536 sum to 100.177936, which overshoots SS_between = 98.370276 by 1.807660. Two questions that overlap have counted part of the between-group variation twice. Use Σ cⱼc′ⱼ/nⱼ = 0; the balanced shortcut is a special case, not the definition.

  • Scheffé is the price of unlimited fishing, and it is a fair price. The F's protection is weak control (§17) and evaporates the moment any null is false — which is the only case in which anyone runs the follow-up.

20 The false discovery rate: a different target, not a better recipe

Everything in §§17–18 controls the probability of making any false claim. With three comparisons that is the right ambition. With ten thousand it is not merely conservative — it is the wrong goal, because guaranteeing zero false positives among ten thousand tests leaves no power to find anything at all. The false discovery rate changes what is being controlled, and the change is conceptual before it is computational.

A worked example: one thousand hypotheses

Too high if the target was 5%, but the question is now "what fraction", not "any at all", and it is answerable.
The shift is about what a "discovery" is for. If each discovery triggers an expensive, irreversible action — a drug approval, a criminal conviction — then one false positive is one too many, and the FWER is the right target however much power it costs. If discoveries are a shortlist for follow-up — the 200 genes worth an experiment, the 50 features worth engineering — then a list of 121 of which 41 are wrong is a perfectly good list, and a procedure that would have returned three names instead is worse, not better. Neither target is more rigorous than the other. They answer different questions, and the design decision is which question the study is asking.

Benjamini–Hochberg

the procedure, in three steps1. sort the p-values ascending: p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍ₘ₎ 2. find the LARGEST k such that p₍ₖ₎ ≤ (k/m)·α 3. reject H₍₁₎, …, H₍ₖ₎ — all of them, including any p above its own line it is a STEP-UP procedure: walk from the largest p-value DOWNWARD looking for the first crossing, then reject everything below it. Contrast Holm (§17), which walks from the smallest UPWARD and stops at the first failure. the theorem: for independent (or positively dependent, PRDS) test statisticsFDR ≤ (m₀/m)·α ≤ α where m₀ is the number of TRUE nulls as adjusted p-values, with monotonicity enforced from the top downp̃₍ᵢ₎ = min over j ≥ i of min(1, (m/j)·p₍ⱼ₎)
Two features of the rule that look like mistakes and are not. First, step 3 rejects hypotheses whose own p-value is above their own threshold, as long as some larger-ranked p-value cleared its line. That is deliberate: BH controls a property of the whole rejected set, not of each member, and the crossing point is what licenses everything below it. Second, the threshold at rank m is α itself — the largest p-value is compared against the uncorrected level. If every p-value is tiny, BH rejects everything, which is correct: when almost all the hypotheses are false, there is almost nothing to protect.

  • BH controls the FDR at π₀·α, not at α, and the simulation reproduces that to three decimal places — 0.0450, 0.0400, 0.0249, 0.0100 against 0.0450, 0.0400, 0.0250, 0.0100. The bound is attained, not merely respected, which means the procedure is not wasting its budget the way Bonferroni does.
  • That also means BH is conservative by exactly the factor π₀, and when few nulls are true it is very conservative. Adaptive versions estimate π₀ from the p-value histogram and use α/π̂₀ instead, recovering the lost power.
  • The power difference is the reason anyone uses this. At π₀ = 0.9, BH finds 50% of the real effects and Bonferroni finds 15%. Holm finds 14.6% — barely more than Bonferroni, because Holm's advantage over Bonferroni is small when m is large and the effects are not overwhelming.
  • Bonferroni's power does not depend on π₀ at all — 0.1454, 0.1455, 0.1454, 0.1457 across the whole table — because its threshold is α/m regardless of how many nulls are true. BH's does, and that responsiveness is the entire design.
  • At π₀ = 1 BH's "FDR" is 0.0470, which is its FWER, as the identity above requires. A procedure aimed at the FDR is automatically a valid FWER procedure when nothing at all is going on.

What BH assumes, and the price of dropping it

the guarantee FDR ≤ π₀α requires independence OR positive regression dependence (PRDS) PRDS is satisfied by, among others, one-sided tests on positively correlated normals — which covers most genomics and most feature-screening problems measured under equicorrelated normal statistics, m = 1000, π₀ = 0.9:ρ = 0.0 FDR = 0.0449 ρ = 0.3 FDR = 0.0405 ρ = 0.7 FDR = 0.0242 so positive dependence makes BH MORE conservative, never less Benjamini–Yekutieli, for ARBITRARY dependence: divide α by the harmonic numberc(m) = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/m ≈ ln m + 0.5772 m = 10 c = 2.9290 effective level 0.01707 m = 100 c = 5.1874 effective level 0.00964 m = 1000 c = 7.4855 effective level 0.00668 m = 10000 c = 9.7876 effective level 0.00511

  • Use BH unless the dependence is genuinely adversarial. The measurements show positive dependence is safe, and negative dependence of the kind that would actually break BH is rare in practice and easy to spot when it is not.
  • The choice is not "FWER or FDR" but "what does a false discovery cost". Part 5, §06's base-rate arithmetic is the same calculation from the Bayesian direction: with π₀ close to 1 and modest power, most rejections are false whatever the nominal level, and BH is the frequentist procedure that faces that fact directly.
