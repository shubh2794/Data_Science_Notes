13 The permutation F: the debt to part 7, paid

Part 7, §11 built the permutation test and §19 promised that its k-group version would appear here. It does, and it needs no new theory at all: shuffle the group labels, recompute F, repeat, and read the p-value off the histogram. The machinery is part 7's; what is new is what it reveals about when the tabulated F can be trusted and when it cannot.

The procedure

the null being tested: the group label carries no information about the outcomeH₀ : the N observations are EXCHANGEABLE — any assignment of labels is as likely as any other the recipe1. compute F on the data as collected F_obs 2. shuffle the N labels, keeping the group SIZES fixed 3. recompute F on the relabelled data F* 4. repeat B times; the p-value is the shuffled proportion at least as large p̂ = (#{F* ≥ F_obs} + 1)/(B + 1) the +1s are part 7, §10 the reference set has size N!/(n₁! n₂! … n_k!) — for the running data41!/(14!·14!·13!) ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁸ far too many to enumerate; sample them a simplification worth knowing: SS_total is INVARIANT under relabelling, soranking shuffles by F ≡ ranking them by SS_between ≡ ranking by Σⱼ nⱼȳⱼ² — the p-value is identical whichever of the three is permuted.

The normal-theory F reproduces the exact randomisation test to within Monte Carlo error, on forty-one integers. This is the same finding part 7, §11 reported for two groups, in the same direction and for the same reason: Fisher presented the randomisation test as what a randomised comparison actually means, and treated the tabulated distribution as a computational shortcut. The shortcut is astonishingly accurate whenever its conditions hold. Which makes the interesting question the other one — what happens when they do not.

When they agree and when they do not

The permutation F does not fix unequal variances, and this is the single most useful thing to know about it. The reason is exactly part 7, §12's: permutation tests the strong null — the groups have the same distribution — and when the variances differ that null is false even though every mean is equal. The test then correctly rejects a null that nobody wanted tested. The 28% figure in the fifth row is the three-group version of part 7's 29.4%, in the same configuration part 7 identified as the dangerous one: a small group with a large spread. That is not an exotic case; it is the shape of an experiment whose treatment arm is small and whose treatment increases variability.

What the permutation F is actually for

  • Non-normal errors with equal variances — and here the gain is not small. Row 3 of the table above is the clean demonstration: with lognormal errors the tabulated F runs at 3.6% against a nominal 5%, and the permutation F at 5.2%. The permutation test is exact under exchangeability, so it recovers the whole of the lost 1.4 percentage points, which is lost power. Welch does not recover it (3.65%), because the problem is not unequal variances. Read rows 3 and 5 together: the permutation F repairs non-normality completely and repairs heteroscedasticity not at all, and the two failures are constantly confused.
  • Statistics with no distribution theory. The recipe never used the fact that the statistic was F. Permute a ratio of trimmed-mean spreads, a median-based statistic, or anything else you can compute, and the p-value is still exact under exchangeability. That freedom is the real reason to own the technique.
  • A diagnostic. When the permutation and tabulated p-values disagree materially, something specific is wrong — heavy tails, a wild point, or unequal spreads with unequal group sizes — and the disagreement tells you to look.
  • Not for unequal variances. Use Welch (§14). If you want a permutation test that survives them, permute a studentised statistic — Welch's F itself — which is part 7, §12's repair applied here unchanged.

14 Unequal variances: Levene, Welch, and Brown–Forsythe

Of the four assumptions in §08, this is the one that actually breaks the test. Part 7, §12 diagnosed the two-group version — the Behrens–Fisher problem — and measured a 29.4% type I error in the worst configuration. This section does not re-diagnose it. It shows the same failure with k groups, gives the two standard repairs, and measures which one to use.

The damage, measured

  • Balanced designs are nearly immune. Even a fourfold spread ratio only takes the classical test from 5.0% to 7.3%. This is the strongest practical argument for equal group sizes that exists, and it is worth more than any test of variance homogeneity.
  • Unbalanced designs are not. Pair a small group with a large variance and the true error rate is 28% at a nominal 5% — five and a half times too many false positives.
  • The failure is direction-dependent, and one direction is invisible. Reverse the pairing — put the large variance in the large group — and the rate drops to 1%. Nothing announces itself; the test simply stops having power, and a real effect goes unreported.
  • Kruskal–Wallis is not a fix. It is a rank test and it inherits the same problem: 11.3% and 13.3% in the two bad rows. Rank-based methods answer the non-normality worry, not this one. §15 develops the point.
  • Welch's F holds the level everywhere, at a small cost: 5.9% when the smallest group has five observations, which is the only visible blemish in the column, and it comes from estimating a denominator degrees of freedom from very little data.

Welch's ANOVA

weight each group by its own precision, not by its sizewⱼ = nⱼ/sⱼ² W = Σⱼ wⱼ ȳ_w = (Σⱼ wⱼȳⱼ)/W the statisticA = ( Σⱼ wⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ_w)² ) / (k − 1) Λ = Σⱼ (1 − wⱼ/W)² / (nⱼ − 1) F_W = A / [ 1 + 2(k − 2)Λ/(k² − 1) ] ~ F(k − 1, ν₂) approximately ν₂ = (k² − 1)/(3Λ) generally NOT an integer for k = 2 this reduces exactly to the square of Welch's two-sample t running dataF_W = 2.445973 on (2, 25.237938) df p = 0.106918 against the classical F = 2.581991 on (2, 38) df, p = 0.088834

The two answers differ mostly because Welch spends degrees of freedom: 25.2 instead of 38. That is the price of not assuming a common variance, and on the running data — where the three standard deviations are 4.24, 4.48 and 4.37 — the assumption was true, so the price is paid for nothing. That is the honest cost accounting: Welch loses a little power when the variances really are equal and prevents a catastrophe when they are not.

Brown–Forsythe's F*

keep the ordinary numerator; replace the denominator with a variance-weighted oneF* = Σⱼ nⱼ(ȳⱼ − ȳ̄)² / Σⱼ (1 − nⱼ/N) sⱼ² with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom for the denominatorν₂ = ( Σⱼ cⱼ )² / Σⱼ cⱼ²/(nⱼ − 1) where cⱼ = (1 − nⱼ/N)sⱼ² running dataF* = 2.581720 on (2, 37.800889) df p = 0.088925 — almost identical to the classical test, because the variances here are almost equal
Two statistics named after Brown and Forsythe, and they are different things. F* above is their test of equal means under unequal variances. Their other contribution, and the more famous one, is the median-centred version of Levene's test for equal variances — a completely different null. Software calls both "Brown–Forsythe" and the confusion is common. The table above uses the first; the paragraph below discusses the second.

Testing for equal variances — and why not to

Levene: run an ordinary one-way ANOVA on the absolute deviations from the centrezᵢⱼ = |yᵢⱼ − centreⱼ| then F-test the zᵢⱼ across groups centre = group MEAN → Levene's original centre = group MEDIAN → Brown–Forsythe's version, far more robust — use this one Bartlett: a likelihood-ratio test, more powerful under normality and very fragile away from it running data — all four agree that there is nothing to seeLevene (mean-centred) W = 0.227647 p = 0.797484 Brown–Forsythe (median) W = 0.127121 p = 0.881000 Bartlett K² = 0.039757 p = 0.980318 Fligner–Killeen X² = 0.492032 p = 0.781910
Do not use these tests to choose your main test. "Run Levene; if it rejects, switch to Welch" is a two-stage procedure whose combined error rate is not the nominal one, and it fails in the worst possible way: variance tests have low power exactly when the group sizes are small, which is exactly when unequal variances do the most damage. The procedure therefore reassures you precisely when it should not. Part 5, §10 made the same point about pre-testing before a t test. The defensible policy is to decide once, in advance, and to decide on Welch — it needs no justification, its cost when the variances are equal is a couple of degrees of freedom, and it removes the entire failure mode. Use the variance tests as description, not as a gate.

15 Kruskal–Wallis: the F test run on ranks

The rank-based alternative to one-way ANOVA is usually presented as a separate method with its own table. It is not. Part 7, §13 showed that the classical rank tests are permutation tests with a statistic chosen so that its null distribution could be tabulated once; Kruskal–Wallis is the one-way ANOVA of that family, and the connection is an identity, not an analogy.

The statistic, and what it really is

replace every observation by its rank among all N, thenH = 12/(N(N+1)) · Σⱼ Rⱼ²/nⱼ − 3(N+1) Rⱼ = sum of ranks in group j reject for large H, comparing against χ²(k − 1) — an approximation, good for nⱼ ≥ 5 the identity that explains the constantsH = (N − 1) · SS_between(ranks) / SS_total(ranks) i.e. H is (N − 1) times the η² OF THE RANKS. The 12 and the 3(N+1) are just SS_total of the ranks 1…N written out, which is N(N² − 1)/12 and needs no data. ties: divide H by 1 − Σ(tᵢ³ − tᵢ)/(N³ − N) over the tie group sizes tᵢ

The last two agree to three decimal places and they are not the same test — H uses a chi-square reference, the rank F uses an F reference on (k−1, N−k) df. The second is generally the better approximation at small N for the same reason the t beats the z: it accounts for the estimated denominator. Both are meaningfully weaker than the F on the raw scores here — 0.18 against 0.089 — because the ranks discard the magnitudes, and exam scores carry real information in their magnitudes.

What it assumes, which is more than people think

  • Its null is "the k distributions are identical", not "the k medians are equal". That is the strong null of part 7, §12, and it means Kruskal–Wallis rejects for a difference in spread or shape just as readily as for a difference in location. §14's table shows the consequence: 11.3% and 13.3% type I error when only the variances differ.
  • Reading it as a test of medians requires an extra assumption — that the distributions differ only by a shift. Under that assumption it is a test of location; without it, a rejection means "these samples do not look like they came from one distribution", which is a weaker conclusion than most reports claim.
  • It is not distribution-free in the sense people want. It is distribution-free under the strong null, which is a real and useful guarantee, and it is not a repair for unequal variances.
  • The efficiency cost is small and the robustness gain is real. Against normal data, the asymptotic relative efficiency is 3/π = 0.955 — a 4.5% loss — and against heavy-tailed data it can be far more efficient than the F. Against a single wild point it is essentially immune, where §09 showed the F is not.

Kruskal–Wallis, the permutation F, and the choice between three tests

The three are the same construction with three different decisions about what to keep.

The decision rule, stated plainly. Unequal variances → Welch (§14), which is the only column in §14's table that holds its level. Heavy tails or an outlier you cannot justify deleting → Kruskal–Wallis, or better, a permutation test on a trimmed statistic. Small samples with an unclear shape → the permutation F, which costs nothing and assumes nothing but exchangeability. Everything else, including mild skew with balanced groups → the ordinary F, which §09 showed is more robust than its reputation. The one combination with no good answer in this list is heavy tails and unequal variances, which needs a robust method beyond this page's scope.
