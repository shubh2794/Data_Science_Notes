Statistics · part 8 of 9

Categorical Data & Chi-Square

Everything so far has been about measurements — heights, times, revenues, numbers you can average. This part is about data that are nothing but counts in boxes: how many first-class passengers survived, how many visitors converted, how many emails were spam. There is no mean to take and no standard deviation to compute, because the categories have no arithmetic. What there is instead is a table, and the only question a table can answer is whether the counts inside it are further from what chance would produce than chance can comfortably explain. Part 5 handed over the whole apparatus of that question — null hypothesis, test statistic, null distribution, p-value, type I and type II error — and this page never re-litigates it; it supplies a new statistic, χ², and a new null distribution to read it against. Part 7 handed over something sharper: it showed that a classical test is usually an approximation to an exact one built by enumeration, and it promised that the exact version for a 2 × 2 table would appear here. It does, in §11, and the promise is kept literally — Fisher's exact test is part 7's permutation test with the table's margins held fixed. Around those two tools sits the part of the subject that actually gets people into trouble: reading a table the right way round, finding out which cell caused a significant result, measuring the size of an association rather than its significance, and knowing why an association can point one way in every subgroup and the other way overall.

01 Counts in boxes: what changes when the data are categorical

A categorical variable records which of a fixed set of classes each unit belongs to. Once you have recorded that, the entire data set is a table of counts — the individual records add nothing. That single fact drives everything on this page: the model is a multinomial, the estimate is a set of proportions, the null hypothesis is a statement about those proportions, and the statistic that measures departure from it compares observed counts to expected counts.

the data
A table of counts
A 2 × 4 table of survivors by ticket class; a 1 × 6 table of sweet colours; a 2 × 2 table of converted-or-not by variant. Part 1 classified these as nominal or ordinal; here they become the whole data set.
the model
Multinomial
n independent units, each landing in category j with probability pⱼ. The counts (O₁, …, O_k) are the sufficient statistic — nothing else about the data matters. §03.
the statistic
χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E
One number summarising the whole table: how far the observed counts sit from the counts the null predicts, each squared difference priced by how big a fluctuation that cell can afford. §04.
the reference
The chi-square distribution
An approximation, valid when the expected counts are not too small — §10 measures exactly how valid — with an exact alternative available by enumeration when they are. §11.

The three questions, which use the same arithmetic and mean different things

The second and third rows produce identical numbers from identical arithmetic. They differ in how the data were collected and in what a rejection licenses you to say. Confusing them is the single most common error in this corner of statistics, and §07 sets the distinction out in full.

One warning that governs the whole page. A χ² test answers exactly one question: is there evidence of an association at all? It says nothing about how strong the association is, nothing about its direction, and nothing about which cells produced it. All three of those are separate calculations — §09 for the cells, §14 for the strength and direction — and a large χ² with a huge n routinely certifies an association far too small to care about. §18 measures that: at a fixed association strength of φ = 0.02, an association nobody would act on, χ² crosses the 0.05 line at n = 10 000 and reaches p = 6 × 10⁻⁵ by n = 40 000.

18 Where it goes wrong: five failures, measured

Everything above assumed the test was the right tool applied to the right table. This section is the catalogue of cases where it is not — and, in keeping with the rest of the page, each failure is quantified rather than merely warned about.

Failure 1 — a huge n makes trivial associations significant

This is the most common misuse of the test in practice, and it follows directly from an identity: at fixed cell proportions, χ² is exactly linear in n.

Failure 2 — ordinal categories thrown away by a nominal test

The χ² test is invariant to permuting rows and columns. Shuffle the four ticket classes into any order and the statistic is unchanged. That invariance is exactly right for nominal categories and it is a waste when the categories are ordered: the test spends its degrees of freedom on alternatives no one believes in, and it ignores the monotone pattern it should be looking for.

the Cochran–Armitage trend test: assign scores to the ordered columns and test a slopez = Σⱼ (O₁ⱼ − Cⱼ p̄) sⱼ / √( p̄(1 − p̄) Σⱼ Cⱼ (sⱼ − s̄)² ) with p̄ = R₁/n and z² is a χ² on ONE degree of freedom, no matter how many ordered columns there are on First / Second / Third only, with scores 1, 2, 3 (crew is not on that scale)nominal test: χ² = 131.0792 on df 2, p = 3.4 × 10⁻²⁹ trend test: z = −11.4281, z² = 130.6022 on df 1, p = 3.0 × 10⁻³⁰ and the leftover, which tests whether anything DEPARTS from a straight trend131.0792 − 130.6022 = 0.4770 on df 1, p = 0.49 no detectable departure
  • The trend test extracts a smaller statistic with a smaller p-value — 130.60 on one degree of freedom beats 131.08 on two. Concentrating the same signal into fewer degrees of freedom is what a well-chosen alternative buys, and here it buys a full order of magnitude in the p-value.
  • The decomposition is the real prize. Splitting χ²(df 2) = z²(df 1) + departure(df 1) says that essentially all of the association is a monotone decline in survival with class number, with nothing left over. That is a much stronger scientific statement than "the classes differ".
  • The scores are a modelling choice. Equally spaced scores 1, 2, 3 are conventional and usually adequate; midpoint scores or ridit scores are alternatives. What you may not do is try several and report the best, which is part 5, §13's problem again.
  • Both variables ordinal? Then a correlation-style statistic is available — the linear-by-linear association test, which is (n − 1)r² with r the correlation of the scores. Again one degree of freedom, again far more power against a monotone alternative.

Failure 3 — structural zeros and impossible cells

  • A sampling zero is a cell that could have been non-empty and happened not to be. The test handles those: χ² is defined, G² uses the convention 0 ln 0 = 0, and the only consequence is that the approximation degrades (§10).
  • A structural zero is a cell that cannot exist — patients who never received a treatment cannot have its side effect; a survey branch that was never shown cannot have answers. Running the ordinary test on such a table is wrong, because the null hypothesis of independence predicts a non-zero expected count for a cell the world forbids, and the difference is charged to the statistic as evidence of association.
  • The fix is a quasi-independence model: fit the independence structure to the admissible cells only, by iterative proportional fitting, and reduce the degrees of freedom by one for each excluded cell. For an r × c table with k structural zeros, df = (r − 1)(c − 1) − k, provided the remaining pattern is connected.
  • A zero margin is different again and is fatal. If an entire row or column is empty then some expected count is zero and the statistic is undefined. The category was not observed; drop it and reduce the table's dimensions, remembering that df falls accordingly.

Failure 4 — dependent observations

  • The multinomial model assumes each unit is counted once and independently. Every violation inflates χ², because clustering makes the counts more variable than a multinomial allows while the denominator E stays the same.
  • The commonest form is repeated units. Counting sessions when the unit of randomisation was users is the classic A/B-testing version; a user with forty sessions contributes forty highly correlated observations. The design effect can easily be 3 or 4, meaning the true variance is three or four times the assumed one and the nominal 5% test is really operating at 15% or more.
  • The second commonest is paired data analysed as unpaired, which §13 covers and which goes in the opposite direction — it usually loses power rather than manufacturing significance.
  • Fixes: aggregate to the independent unit (one row per user); use a cluster-robust or survey-weighted test; or permute at the cluster level, which is part 7, §12's exchangeability argument applied at the right granularity.

Failure 5 — testing many tables, or many cells, and reporting the winner

  • Cross-tabulating an outcome against twenty candidate variables and reporting the significant ones is a 20-test family. At α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive under complete independence is 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64%. §09 measured the within-table version: in a 5 × 5 table with no association at all, some cell exceeds 1.96 two thirds of the time.
  • Feature screening by χ² is exactly this procedure with the reporting automated, and it inherits the problem in full. §19 says what to do about it.
  • Corrections and their costs. Bonferroni is safe and blunt; false-discovery-rate control is the right tool when you are screening rather than confirming; and the cleanest fix of all is pre-registration of which tables you intended to look at.
  • The specific trap for tables: collapsing categories after seeing the data. Merging sparse categories is legitimate if the grouping was decided in advance and is substantively meaningful. Merging the categories that happened to be sparse, or splitting at the point that maximises the statistic, is a search whose result the null distribution knows nothing about.
Beyond the test: what modelling adds. Everything on this page is a test plus a couple of summary numbers. The modelling generalisation is the log-linear model, which writes ln E[Oᵢⱼ] = μ + αᵢ + βⱼ + (αβ)ᵢⱼ and treats independence as the hypothesis that the interaction terms vanish. It buys three things a test cannot: it extends to three-way and higher tables where "independence" splits into several distinct hypotheses (mutual, joint, conditional); it lets nested models be compared by differences of G² (§12); and it produces interpretable parameters instead of a single verdict. For a binary outcome it is the same fitted object as logistic regression, seen from the other side. Not covered in this series; worth knowing it exists as soon as a table has three dimensions.

19 The ML translation: where every one of these tools already lives in your stack

Nothing on this page is a statistics-only technique. Every tool has a machine-learning name, and in most cases the machine-learning version is the same arithmetic with the derivation removed.

On this pageIn machine learningWhat the translation buys
2 × 2 contingency table (§07) The confusion matrix — model evaluation, §02. Rows are true class, columns are predicted class, and TP/FP/FN/TN are a, b, c, d. Precision and recall are conditional distributions of §02 read in the two different directions: recall is a column percentage, precision is a row percentage. The eternal confusion between them is §02's row-versus-column question. A χ² test on a confusion matrix asks whether the classifier is better than one that ignores the input — usually not the question, but the same table.
Odds ratio (§14) Logistic regression coefficients — logistic regression. e^β for a binary predictor is exactly ad/bc. Fitting a logistic model to a 2 × 2 table reproduces §15's estimate and standard error exactly. Going the other way, a table gives you an interpretable check on a coefficient. Remember §17: an adjusted coefficient is a conditional odds ratio and will not match the table's marginal one even with no confounding.
G² = 2n·Î(X;Y) (§12) Information gain, the split criterion in a decision tree. Every candidate split evaluates a contingency table of feature-value against label, and the entropy-based gain is the likelihood-ratio statistic divided by 2n. This explains a known bias directly: high-cardinality features produce tables with more columns and therefore more degrees of freedom, so they look better by chance — which is exactly §18's multiplicity failure and exactly why gain ratio and similar corrections exist.
χ² feature screening (§18) SelectKBest with a chi-square score, and every "top correlated features" table. It tests each feature against the label separately, so it inherits every problem on this page: it is blind to interactions, it favours high-cardinality features, its p-values are uncorrected across hundreds of tests, and with a large n everything is significant. Use the statistic as a ranking if you must, never the p-values as evidence, and validate the selection inside cross-validation rather than before it.
McNemar's test (§13) Comparing two models on the same test set — model evaluation, §09. This is the correct test and it is very widely got wrong. The pairs are examples, and the only informative counts are the disagreements: examples model A got right and B got wrong, and vice versa. Comparing two accuracies with a two-proportion test discards the pairing and is the §13 error verbatim. Part 7, §13's paired permutation test is the resampling equivalent.
Homogeneity across groups (§06) Fairness auditing and drift detection. "Does the model's positive rate differ across groups?" is a homogeneity test on a 2 × k table, and demographic parity is its null hypothesis. "Has the input distribution shifted since training?" is a goodness-of-fit test of this month's category counts against the training distribution — with §18's warning attached in neon, because production n is enormous and every drift is significant. Track φ or a distance, not the p-value.
Fisher's exact test (§11) Small-sample evaluation: rare-class subgroups, red-team results, human-annotation batches. Whenever a comparison rests on a handful of examples — 12 failures out of 200 in one subgroup against 4 out of 200 in another — the approximation is exactly where §10 says it should not be trusted, and the exact test costs one function call.
Simpson's paradox (§17) A/B test segment analysis — A/B testing, §08. The classic production version: an experiment ramped mid-flight, so the arms have different mixes of early and late traffic, and the aggregate lift contradicts every segment. Randomisation prevents it; a broken or changing split reintroduces it. The diagnosis is always the same — check whether the arms have the same composition.
Expected counts and sparsity (§10) Rare-category handling in preprocessing. The same instinct that groups categories with fewer than k occurrences into "other" before one-hot encoding is §10's advice about small expected counts — and it carries the same warning, that the grouping must be decided from the training data alone, never from the outcome.
The one-line summary of this section. A contingency table is what you get whenever two categorical things are compared, and that describes a very large fraction of applied machine-learning evaluation. The statistical apparatus — expected counts, residuals, exact tests, effect sizes, the multiplicity discipline — transfers unchanged, and it is usually more careful than what the evaluation script is doing.

20 Practice

The checklist, before you report anything from a table

  1. Say what a row is. One person, one session, one pair, one impression? If units appear more than once, the test is invalid before it starts (§18, failure 4).
  2. Say which margin was fixed by the design. That decides whether you are testing homogeneity or independence (§07), and whether you are allowed a relative risk (§16).
  3. Percentage in the direction the explanatory variable dictates, and write the sentence out. If the sentence is about composition rather than rate, you have percentaged the wrong way (§02).
  4. Compute the expected counts and look at the smallest. If it is small, compute the exact test too and compare rather than consulting a threshold (§10, §11).
  5. Report the statistic, the df, and the p-value together. χ²(3) = 192.34, p < 0.001 — never a bare p-value, because df is what makes it interpretable.
  6. Report an effect size next to it, always. φ, Cramér's V, a risk difference, or an odds ratio with an interval. A p-value without one is not a finding (§18).
  7. If you reject, look at the adjusted residuals to find the cells responsible, and correct the threshold for how many cells you looked at (§09).
  8. Ask what you did not stratify by. If a plausible third variable is associated with both, the pooled table may be telling you the opposite of the truth (§17).
  9. Say what you were entitled to conclude. Association, not causation, unless the design was randomised (§07).

Worked problems

  1. A die is rolled 120 times and shows each face 15, 25, 18, 22, 19, 21 times. Is it fair? Every expected count is 20. χ² = (25 + 25 + 4 + 4 + 1 + 1)/20 = 60/20 = 3.00 on df = 5, and χ²₅,₀.₉₅ = 11.07, so p = 0.700. No evidence of unfairness. Note the statistic is below its mean of 5 — the die is fitting the null slightly better than a fair die typically would.
  2. A 2 × 2 table has a = 30, b = 20, c = 20, d = 30. Give χ², φ, the odds ratio and its interval. χ² = 100(30·30 − 20·20)²/(50·50·50·50) = 100 × 250000/6250000 = 4.000, p = 0.0455. φ = √(4/100) = 0.20. OR = 900/400 = 2.25; ln OR = 0.81093, SE = √(1/30 + 1/20 + 1/20 + 1/30) = √0.166667 = 0.408248, so the 95% interval for OR is (e^0.01077, e^1.61109) = (1.011, 5.008) — it excludes 1, but only just, which is the same "only just" as p = 0.0455.
  3. The same table with every count multiplied by 4. χ² = 16.000, p = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵, φ = 0.20 unchanged, OR = 2.25 unchanged, and the interval narrows to (1.508, 3.357). The association is identical; only the precision improved. This is §18 in four numbers.
  4. A 2 × 2 table is [[10, 2], [3, 9]]. Should you use χ² or Fisher? Margins 12, 12, 13, 11; expected counts 6.5, 5.5, 6.5, 5.5, so the guideline is satisfied. χ² = 8.2238 gives p = 0.00413; Fisher's exact test gives p = 0.01228. They agree in verdict at 5% but differ threefold in p-value. With twelve observations per group, report the exact one.
  5. Why is the expected count for a cell always row × column / n, in one sentence? Because under independence the fitted cell probability is the product of the fitted marginal probabilities, (Rᵢ/n)(Cⱼ/n), and the expected count is n times that.
  6. A 4 × 5 table gives χ² = 26.1. Significant? df = 3 × 4 = 12, χ²₁₂,₀.₉₅ = 21.03, so yes at 5% — but the statistic is only 2.2 times its mean of 12, so this is a moderate result, not an overwhelming one. p = 0.0104.
  7. You compute a risk ratio of 1.2 and an odds ratio of 1.9 on the same table. Is one of them wrong? Neither. OR = RR(1 − p₀)/(1 − p₁), so a gap that size means the outcome is common. Solving 1.9/1.2 = 1.5833 = (1 − p₀)/(1 − 1.2p₀) gives p₀ = 0.648 and p₁ = 0.778 — an outcome occurring in nearly two thirds of the unexposed. The odds ratio is technically correct and rhetorically misleading; report the risk ratio.
  8. An A/B test on 400 000 users gives χ² = 12.5, p = 0.0004. Should you ship? φ = √(12.5/400000) = 0.0056. Converting: with equal arms and a 5% pooled rate, that is a 4.88% versus 5.12% conversion difference. The test is correct and the effect is almost certainly not worth the shipping risk. Compute the absolute lift and its interval before deciding — the A/B testing page is where that decision belongs.
  9. Two classifiers on 1 000 test examples: A is right on 870, B on 850. A significant difference? Not answerable from those numbers. You need the paired counts: examples where A is right and B wrong (b) and the reverse (c), with b − c = 20. If b = 60, c = 40, McNemar gives χ² = 400/100 = 4.00, p = 0.0455. If b = 25, c = 5, it gives χ² = 400/30 = 13.33, p = 0.00026. Same accuracies, wildly different evidence — because the second case has the models agreeing far more often, so the disagreements are more decisive. §13.
  10. A 2 × 2 table has a zero cell: [[0, 15], [6, 9]]. Report something defensible. The odds ratio is 0 with infinite standard error, so no Wald interval exists. Fisher's exact test gives p = 0.0169. With the Haldane–Anscombe correction OR = (0.5 × 9.5)/(15.5 × 6.5) = 0.04715 and SE = 1.52434, giving a 95% interval of (0.00238, 0.93535) — which excludes 1, agreeing with the exact test. Report the exact p-value, the corrected estimate, and the fact that a correction was used.
  11. A table has expected counts 3.2, 4.1, 12.7 and 15.0. Do you run the test? Two of four cells are below 5, which breaches the common guideline. Run Fisher's exact test as well; if the two agree, report either and say so; if they disagree, report the exact one. Do not refuse to analyse the data, and do not run the approximation with a footnote.
  12. Why does the trend test give a smaller p-value than the omnibus test on the same data? Because it spends one degree of freedom instead of c − 1, and it concentrates the alternative on monotone departures. On the three-class table: 130.60 on 1 df beats 131.08 on 2 df, 3.0 × 10⁻³⁰ against 3.4 × 10⁻²⁹. Choosing a narrower alternative buys power when the alternative is right — and loses it when it is wrong, which is why the scores must be chosen before looking.
  13. You add a strong, perfectly balanced covariate to a logistic model and the treatment odds ratio rises from 1.8 to 2.3. Is the unadjusted estimate confounded? No. Balanced means it cannot be a confounder. This is non-collapsibility (§17): 1.8 is the marginal odds ratio and 2.3 the conditional one, and both are correct estimates of different parameters. A risk-difference model would have shown no movement.
  14. In a 3 × 4 table you find one adjusted residual of 2.4. Report it? With 12 cells, the Bonferroni threshold at 5% is Φ⁻¹(1 − 0.025/12) = 2.87, so 2.4 does not clear it. Measured by simulation, the chance that some cell of a null 3 × 4 table exceeds 1.96 is 0.368. Describe the pattern; do not report the cell as a finding.

Where this goes next. Part 9, ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons asks this page's homogeneity question about a numeric outcome: several groups, one measurement each, and a null that they share a common mean. The statistic is an F rather than a χ², and the family relationship is exact — an F is a ratio of two independent chi-squares divided by their degrees of freedom, so §04's additivity is what makes the whole decomposition of variance possible. Two threads carry straight over. First, the multiplicity problem: §09 measured a two-thirds chance of some cell exceeding 1.96 in a null 5 × 5 table, and part 9's pairwise comparisons after a significant F are the same failure with group means in place of cells — which is why that part is called multiple comparisons and spends half its length on the correction. Second, the exact alternative: part 7 owes part 9 a permutation F, obtained by shuffling the group labels and recomputing F on every shuffle, and it stands to the tabulated F exactly as §11's Fisher's exact test stands to the χ² approximation here. The pattern this page and part 7 have now established twice — the classical test is the large-sample approximation to an enumeration — holds there too.
Where this goes sideways. Into model evaluation, where the confusion matrix is a 2 × 2 contingency table and precision and recall are §02's two conditional distributions read in opposite directions — and into model comparison, where the correct test for two classifiers on one test set is §13's McNemar and almost nobody uses it. Into logistic regression, whose coefficients are the log odds ratios of §14 and whose adjusted estimates differ from a table's marginal ones for the non-confounding reason in §17. Into A/B testing, which is a 2 × 2 table with a design attached, and whose two commonest analytical failures — significance at trivial effect sizes, and segment reversals from unequal ramps — are §18 and §17 respectively. Into decision trees, whose information gain is §12's G² in different units, complete with the high-cardinality bias that §18 explains. Into preprocessing, where rare-category grouping is §10's advice under another name. And back into part 2, since §16's question — which measure the design entitles you to — and §17's — whether you may pool — are both design questions that no amount of arithmetic on the table can settle.
