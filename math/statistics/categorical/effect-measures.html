14 How big is it? Risk difference, relative risk, and the odds ratio

A p-value says an association exists. Three numbers say how large it is, and they are not interchangeable — they can be pushed in different directions by the same data, they behave differently under changes of design, and only one of them survives a case-control study. All three come from the same four counts.

the notation, fixed once: rows are the exposure or treatment, column 1 is the outcome outcome no outcome | total exposed a b | a + b p₁ = a/(a + b) unexposed c d | c + d p₀ = c/(c + d) risk difference — the absolute scale, and the one that determines how many people are affectedRD = p₁ − p₀ range (−1, 1); no association ⟺ RD = 0 relative risk (risk ratio) — the multiplicative scale on PROBABILITIESRR = p₁ / p₀ range (0, ∞); no association ⟺ RR = 1 odds, and the odds ratio — the multiplicative scale on ODDSodds(p) = p/(1 − p) OR = [p₁/(1 − p₁)] / [p₀/(1 − p₀)] = ad/bc range (0, ∞); no association ⟺ OR = 1 number needed to treat — the risk difference inverted, for when the outcome is desirableNNT = 1/|RD| "treat this many to produce one extra good outcome"

The cross-product form OR = ad/bc is worth memorising, because it makes the odds ratio's defining property visible at a glance: it is symmetric in the two variables. The odds ratio of the outcome given exposure equals the odds ratio of exposure given the outcome. Neither the risk difference nor the relative risk has that property, and it is the whole reason §16 exists.

The trap: an odds ratio is not a risk ratio, and the gap grows with the outcome rate

The identity above is the whole story. The multiplier (1 − p₀)/(1 − p₁) is 1 when the outcome is rare and both p's are near zero, and it grows without bound as the outcome becomes common. So an odds ratio always sits further from 1 than the corresponding risk ratio, and how much further depends entirely on the baseline rate.

"The odds ratio was 3.63, so exposed women were 3.63 times more likely to develop the disease." Only if the disease is rare. "Times more likely" is a risk ratio. If the outcome occurs in 30% of the unexposed, an odds ratio of 3.63 corresponds to a risk ratio of about 1.9 — the sentence roughly doubles the effect. This mistranslation appears in published papers, in press releases and in textbooks, and it is the single most common error involving odds ratios. If you want to say "times more likely", compute a risk ratio; if the design only allows an odds ratio (§16), then say "odds" and mean it.

Which measure to report

MeasureReport it whenWeakness
Risk difference
p₁ − p₀		 You are deciding whether an effect is worth acting on. It is the only measure on the scale of how many people are affected, and the only one that converts directly into a count of outcomes prevented or caused. Not portable across populations with different baseline rates. Bounded by the baseline, so a large relative effect on a rare outcome looks like nothing.
Relative risk
p₁ / p₀		 You are describing the strength of an effect and the baseline rate is stated alongside it. It is the measure most people think they are hearing, whatever number was actually reported. Hides the baseline: a rise from 1 in a million to 2 in a million is a doubling. Undefined when p₀ = 0. Not computable from a case-control study (§16).
Odds ratio
ad / bc		 The design fixed the outcome margin (case-control); or you are fitting a logistic model, whose coefficients are log odds ratios; or you need a measure that treats the two variables symmetrically. Overstates the risk ratio whenever the outcome is common, and is routinely reported as though it were one. Non-collapsible (§17), which the other two are not.
φ and Cramér's V
√(χ²/n), √(χ²/(n·min(r−1,c−1)))		 You want a single bounded number in [0, 1] that is comparable across tables of different shapes — a correlation-like summary of the whole table. Signless for tables larger than 2 × 2, hard to interpret substantively, and it answers no decision question. Useful for saying "this association is small" and nothing else.
The odds ratio here, and the odds ratio in logistic regression, are the same object. Logistic regression models ln[p/(1 − p)] = β₀ + β₁x, so a one-unit increase in x multiplies the odds by e^β₁. For a binary predictor coded 0/1 that is exactly this section's odds ratio, and fitting a logistic regression to a 2 × 2 table returns β̂₁ = ln(ad/bc) to machine precision, with the standard error of §15. Which page owns which: that page owns the modelling view — odds as the quantity a linear predictor gets to be linear in, coefficients as log odds ratios, and what happens with several predictors. This page owns the table view — the odds ratio as a descriptive summary of four counts, its interval, and the design question of whether you were entitled to compute it. Read them in either order; the arithmetic is shared.

15 An interval for the odds ratio — and what it really covers

A point estimate of an odds ratio is nearly useless on its own, because its sampling distribution is severely skewed: bounded below by 0, unbounded above, and centred nowhere in particular. The fix is the standard one from part 4 — transform to a scale where the distribution is symmetric, build the interval there, and transform back. For an odds ratio the right scale is the logarithm.

work on the log scale, where the sampling distribution is approximately normalln OR̂ = ln(ad/bc) = ln a + ln d − ln b − ln c the standard error, by the delta method applied to four independent-ish countsSE(ln OR̂) = √( 1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d ) the interval on the log scale, then exponentiated backln OR̂ ± z* · SE then ( exp(lo), exp(hi) ) the interval is MULTIPLICATIVELY symmetric about OR̂, never additivelyOR̂ / exp(z*·SE) to OR̂ × exp(z*·SE) so the upper arm is always the longer one and the corresponding Wald test, which is NOT the same as the chi-square testz = ln OR̂ / SE(ln OR̂) usually close to, but never equal to, √χ²

Where the standard error comes from

the delta method: for a smooth g, Var(g(X)) ≈ g'(E X)² Var(X)treat a as Binomial(n₁, p₁); then Var(ln p̂₁ − ln(1 − p̂₁)) = Var(logit p̂₁) d/dp logit(p) = 1/(p(1 − p)) Var(p̂₁) = p₁(1 − p₁)/n₁ so Var(logit p̂₁) ≈ 1/(n₁p₁(1 − p₁)) = 1/(n₁p₁) + 1/(n₁(1 − p₁)) ≈ 1/a + 1/b the two rows are independent, so the variances add — and the formula is completeVar(ln OR̂) ≈ 1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d the corresponding standard error for a log relative risk, which drops two termsSE(ln RR̂) = √( 1/a − 1/(a+b) + 1/c − 1/(c+d) ) = √( b/(a(a+b)) + d/(c(c+d)) )

The shape of that expression is worth pausing on: the standard error is dominated by the smallest cell. A table with counts 500, 500, 500 and 4 has a log-odds standard error of √(3/500 + 1/4) = 0.506, essentially all of it from the cell with four observations. Doubling the study will not help much unless it doubles that cell.

Zero cells, and the correction that handles them

If any cell is zero the odds ratio is 0 or infinite and the standard error is infinite. This is not an edge case in small studies — it is the common case.

the Haldane–Anscombe correction: add ½ to every cell, then proceed as usualOR̃ = (a + ½)(d + ½) / ( (b + ½)(c + ½) ) SE = √( 1/(a+½) + 1/(b+½) + 1/(c+½) + 1/(d+½) ) on the table [[0, 20], [8, 12]], where the raw estimate is 0 with infinite SEOR̃ = (0.5 × 12.5)/(20.5 × 8.5) = 6.25/174.25 = 0.035868 SE = 1.4988 95% CI: (0.0019, 0.6769) Fisher's exact p on the raw table = 0.00328
  • Add ½ to every cell, not just the empty one. Adding it only where needed introduces a bias that depends on which cell happened to be empty, which is worse than the problem being solved.
  • It is a real bias–variance trade, not a hack. The ½ is the correction that makes the estimator of ln OR approximately median-unbiased for a 2 × 2 table, and it is equivalent to a weak prior. It shrinks the estimate towards 1 — the table above gives 0.036 rather than 0 — which is the honest direction: a single empty cell out of twenty observations is not evidence that the odds ratio is exactly zero.
  • Report the exact test alongside it. The correction fixes the estimate; it does not make the interval trustworthy at these counts. Fisher's exact test gives a p-value that needs no correction at all, and an exact conditional interval for the odds ratio exists too.
  • For the test, prefer Fisher; for the estimate, prefer the correction; and be explicit that you used it. A reported odds ratio of 0.036 from a table containing a zero is meaningless without the note that a continuity correction produced it.

  • With adequate counts the interval is honest — between 94.4% and 95.7% across every row where no cell is threatened. That is as good as anything in part 7, §08 managed for a mean.
  • The failure mode is not under-coverage; it is non-existence. At 20 per arm with risks of 10% and 5%, 44% of studies produce a table with an empty cell and no computable odds ratio at all. Conditioning on the ones that do produce a number gives 99.1% coverage, which sounds excellent and is a selection artefact: those are the samples where the cells happened to be large enough.
  • The correction restores definedness at the cost of conservatism. Every table now yields an interval, and the coverage is 99.3% — the intervals are far too wide. That is a defensible trade at these counts, but it must be stated rather than hidden.
  • Reporting only the studies that produced a finite estimate is a real publication-bias mechanism, not a hypothetical one. In a literature of small studies, the ones that can report an odds ratio are systematically the ones with larger cell counts.

16 Which measure the design lets you compute at all

The three measures of §14 are computable from any four counts — arithmetic never refuses. Whether the resulting number means anything depends on which margin the study fixed, and that is a fact about the design, not about the table. This is the one place where the odds ratio's peculiar algebra earns its keep: it is the only one of the three that survives sampling on the outcome.

The invariance, algebraically

let the population table be a, b, c, d with rows = exposure and columns = case / controla case-control study samples a FRACTION f₁ of the cases and f₀ of the controls the observed table is (f₁a, f₀b ; f₁c, f₀d) — columns scaled, rows untouched the odds ratio, with every sampling fraction cancellingOR_sample = (f₁a · f₀d) / (f₀b · f₁c) = ad/bc = OR_population for ANY f₁, f₀ the relative risk, where the fractions emphatically do not cancelRR_sample = [f₁a/(f₁a + f₀b)] / [f₁c/(f₁c + f₀d)] depends on f₁/f₀ the general reason: the odds ratio is symmetric, so it does not care which margin was fixedOR(outcome | exposure) = ad/bc = OR(exposure | outcome)

The same fact in counts

  • The odds ratio is 4.3333 in both studies — the population value, exactly, whatever the control-to-case ratio. That invariance is the entire reason case-control studies are worth running.
  • The "relative risk" computed from the same tables is 1.91 and 2.43, neither of which is 4, and the two differ from each other. It is not an estimate of anything: it is a function of a design choice the investigator made arbitrarily. Computing it is not a small approximation error; it is a category mistake.
  • The rare-disease approximation. When the outcome is rare in both exposure groups, OR ≈ RR (§14's multiplier tends to 1), so the case-control odds ratio can be read as a risk ratio. That is the historical justification for case-control studies. Here the outcome is not rare — 10% and 2.5% — and the odds ratio of 4.33 already overstates the risk ratio of 4 by 8%.
  • A case-control study cannot estimate absolute risk at all, by construction: the outcome margin was chosen by the investigator, so P(case) in the sample is a design parameter. Any absolute-risk statement requires external information about the population's baseline rate.
The corresponding statement for a cohort study or an experiment. When the exposure margin is fixed, all three measures are estimable and you should generally report the risk difference alongside whichever ratio you prefer, because the ratio alone is unactionable — "halves your risk" means something entirely different at a baseline of 40% than at 4 in a million. In a randomised experiment (part 2, §08) the same three numbers additionally carry a causal reading, which no amount of arithmetic can add to an observational table. This is also exactly the situation in A/B testing: the arm sizes are fixed by the splitter, the conversion counts are random, and the honest headline number is the absolute lift with an interval, not a ratio.

17 Simpson's paradox: every subgroup one way, the total the other

A treatment can beat a control in every subgroup and lose in the pooled table. This is not a rounding artefact, a small-sample effect, or a paradox in any logical sense — it is ordinary arithmetic about weighted averages, and it happens in real data often enough that pooling a table without asking what was pooled is a genuine hazard. This section owns the contingency-table version; part 2, §07 owns the causal machinery that resolves it.

The reversal, with numbers

800 patients, in two strata by severity. A treatment and a control, and the outcome is recovery.

Every number in that table is exact and nothing is hidden. The treatment is better by 10 points among severe cases and by 5 points among mild ones, and worse by 3.75 points overall. The odds ratio reverses too: 1.56 and 1.59 within strata, 0.81 pooled.

The mechanism, which is entirely about the weights

a pooled rate is a weighted average of stratum rates — with weights set by ALLOCATIONp̂(recover | treated) = Σ_s p̂(recover | treated, s) · w_s^treated where w_s^treated = (treated units in stratum s)/(all treated units) the two arms have DIFFERENT weight vectors, and that is the whole paradoxtreated: severe 300/400 = 0.75 mild 100/400 = 0.25 control: severe 100/400 = 0.25 mild 300/400 = 0.75 so the comparison mixes two things that cannot be separated by poolingtreated: 0.75 × 0.70 + 0.25 × 0.90 = 0.525 + 0.225 = 0.750 control: 0.25 × 0.60 + 0.75 × 0.85 = 0.150 + 0.6375 = 0.7875 reweight the control arm to the treated arm's mix and the reversal disappearscontrol at the treated mix: 0.75 × 0.60 + 0.25 × 0.85 = 0.450 + 0.2125 = 0.6625 0.750 vs 0.6625 — the treatment wins by 8.75 points, between its two stratum-specific gains

Three quarters of the treated patients were severe and only a quarter of the controls were. Severe patients recover less often whatever you do. So the treated arm is dragged down by its composition and the control arm is flattered by its own — and the pooled comparison measures the composition difference as much as the treatment.

  • No reversal is possible if the allocation is balanced. Make the weights identical across arms and the pooled difference is a weighted average of the stratum differences, hence between the smallest and the largest of them. A randomised experiment balances the weights in expectation, which is precisely why part 2, §08 insists on randomisation. Simpson's paradox is a symptom of confounded allocation, not an independent phenomenon.
  • The direction of the drag is set by two things at once: the stratum must affect the outcome (severe patients do worse) and it must be associated with the arm (severe patients were preferentially treated). Break either link and the reversal is impossible. Those two conditions are the definition of a confounder.
  • Which table should you act on? Here, the stratified one — because severity was a cause of both the treatment decision and the outcome, so it is a confounder and must be conditioned on. But that is a causal judgement about the world, not something the counts can supply. If the third variable were instead a consequence of the treatment — a side effect, say — then conditioning on it would be exactly the wrong move and the pooled table would be the right one. There is no way to choose from the numbers alone. Part 2, §07 develops this, including the collider case where adjustment actively creates bias.
  • The three-line version. A reversal in the observed conditional probabilities is possible because the two arms average over different mixes. A reversal in the causal quantities is impossible, because those average over the same mix — the population's. Part 2 gives that argument in full.
Slide the allocation and break the aggregate conclusion — while both strata hold firm the within-stratum rates never move; only who gets which treatment does

Non-collapsibility: a different phenomenon that looks like the same one

There is a second way for a pooled measure to disagree with its stratum-specific values, and it is not confounding. It afflicts the odds ratio specifically, it survives perfect randomisation, and conflating it with Simpson's paradox is a genuine error.

The practical consequence, which catches people out. Add an irrelevant but predictive covariate to a logistic regression — one that is balanced across arms and so cannot be a confounder — and the treatment coefficient moves away from zero. Nothing is wrong. The unadjusted model estimates the marginal odds ratio (2.333 in experiment A) and the adjusted model estimates the conditional one (3.000); both are correct, and they are not estimates of the same quantity. A risk-difference or risk-ratio model would show no such movement. If you need adjusted and unadjusted analyses to agree, do not use an odds ratio.
