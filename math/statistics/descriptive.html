Descriptive Statistics

Everything you do to a batch of numbers before you model it: plot its shape, locate its centre, measure its spread, place individual values, decide what to do about the values that do not fit — and stay honest about the fact that every summary is a lossy compression. The last two sections turn the description of a sample into the estimation of a population, which is the hinge the rest of the series swings on.

01 Why you plot before you summarise

A summary statistic is a many-to-one map: it takes n numbers and returns one. Information is destroyed by construction — and the destroyed information is exactly where the interesting failures live. So the order of operations is not negotiable: look at the data, then summarise it, then report both.

A statistic throws away n − 1 dimensions

  • A dataset of n numbers is a point in ℝⁿ. A statistic T collapses that point onto the real line, so every value of T corresponds to an entire (n − 1)-dimensional surface of datasets that are indistinguishable through T.
  • Report three statistics and you have pinned down three coordinates out of n. For n = 11, agreeing on the mean, the standard deviation and the correlation still leaves an eight-dimensional family of datasets free to look like anything at all. §01's second visualization is that fact made concrete.
  • This is not an argument against summaries — you cannot read 10⁶ numbers. It is an argument for always pairing a summary with a picture, because the picture is the cheapest possible check on what the summary threw away.
a statistic is many-to-oneT : ℝⁿ → ℝ T⁻¹(t) = { x ∈ ℝⁿ : T(x) = t } — a surface of dimension n − 1 consequencex̄, s, r all agree ⇏ the datasets agree

The motivating failure: conditioning on the outcome

  • A launch vehicle had flown 23 times. On each flight, engineers recorded the ambient launch temperature and counted how many of six rubber sealing rings showed thermal distress. Seven of the 23 flights had at least one damaged ring; 10 rings were damaged in total.
  • The night before the next launch, with an unprecedented cold front forecast, the analysis that circulated plotted only the flights that had shown damage. Among those seven, temperature looks close to irrelevant: 53 °F produced three damaged rings, but 75 °F produced two, and 70 °F produced one. The correlation over that subset is r = −0.26 — noise.
  • Put the 16 undamaged flights back on the same axes and the picture inverts. Every one of the 4 flights below 65 °F had damage; only 3 of the 19 flights at 66 °F or above did. Over all 23 flights r = −0.56.
  • The error is structural, not arithmetic. Selecting on the outcome — keeping only the flights where the bad thing happened — deletes the comparison group, and a comparison group is the only thing that can turn "cold flights had damage" into "cold flights had more damage than warm ones". The same mistake reappears as survivorship bias, as training on positives only, and as reading a confusion matrix with one column missing.
  • The launch went ahead at 31 °F: 22 °F below the coldest flight in the record, a standardised distance of z = −5.5 from the mean of the 23 previous launches, and −6.6 on the resistant scale of §07. Every summary on the page screamed "you have no data here". The vehicle was lost.

Anscombe's quartet — four datasets, one summary

  • Four bivariate datasets of eleven points each. They share every classical summary — x̄ = 9.00, s²ₓ = 11.00, ȳ = 7.50, s²_y ≈ 4.13, r = 0.816, r² = 0.67, and the fitted line ŷ = 3.00 + 0.500x — with the largest disagreement between any two panels on any of those seven numbers being 0.005, in the variance of y. The means and s²ₓ are equal exactly.
  • They are nothing alike. I is a genuine noisy linear relation. II is a clean parabola — a deterministic curve that a linear summary cannot see. III is an exact straight line plus one contaminated point that tilts the fit. IV has no relationship at all: ten points share a single x, and one leverage point at x = 19 single-handedly manufactures the slope, the correlation and the illusion of evidence.
  • The lesson generalises past four hand-built examples. Given any target values for the means, standard deviations and correlation, you can search the space of datasets — nudging points at random and rejecting moves that change the summaries beyond two decimal places — until the cloud takes any shape you like, including recognisable pictures. The set T⁻¹(t) is enormous, and you can walk around inside it.
  • Dataset IV is the one to remember for modelling. Its correlation is entirely produced by a single point at extreme x. That is leverage, and it is the reason regression diagnostics exist — see linear regression.

02 What kind of variable is it?

Before any summary is legal you have to know what the numbers mean. The variable type decides which arithmetic is meaningful, which plot is honest, and which test is available three parts from now. Getting this wrong is the most common silent error in an analysis, because the arithmetic still runs.

The taxonomy

  • Categorical — the value is a label drawn from a fixed set.
    • Nominal: labels with no order. Species, country, payment method. The only comparison the scale supports is equal / not equal.
    • Ordinal: labels with an order but no defined spacing. Small < medium < large; a 1–5 satisfaction rating; a loan grade A…G. You may rank; you may not assume the step from 1 to 2 equals the step from 4 to 5.
    • Binary is the two-category special case (0/1, yes/no). It is the bridge between the two worlds: the mean of a 0/1 variable is the proportion of 1s, which is why proportions behave like means throughout the rest of the series.
  • Numerical — the value is a quantity.
    • Discrete: counts. Number of defects, children in a household, clicks in a session. Isolated possible values, usually non-negative integers.
    • Continuous: any value in an interval, limited only by measurement precision. Duration, temperature, concentration, price.
  • Cutting across numerical data is the measurement-scale question: does the zero mean "none"?
    • Interval scales have a conventional zero. Differences are meaningful, ratios are not: 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C, and the same two readings in °F are 68 and 50, whose ratio is different again. Mean and standard deviation are fine; the coefficient of variation s/x̄ is meaningless.
    • Ratio scales have a true zero. Length, mass, duration, count, money. Ratios, the coefficient of variation, and the geometric mean all become available.

The traps

  • Numeric-looking nominal data. Postcodes, product IDs, jersey numbers, "1 = north, 2 = south". Every arithmetic operation on them is nonsense, and every library will happily compute it. Cast such columns to a categorical type at load time — see encoding.
  • Averaging ordinal ratings. "Mean satisfaction 3.7 / 5" assumes the five levels are equally spaced, which the scale never claimed. It is done constantly and is sometimes defensible for coarse comparison, but the median and the full bar chart of the five counts are strictly more honest, and a shift of mass from 4 to 5 can move the mean while the distribution's message is unchanged.
  • Binning a continuous variable into "low / medium / high" throws away information you paid to collect, makes every later result depend on the arbitrary cut-points, and typically costs the power of a substantial fraction of your sample. Bin for a picture; keep the raw values for the analysis.
  • Codes hiding inside numeric columns. A −999, a 0 meaning "not measured", a 99 meaning "refused to answer". These show as a spike in the histogram — one more reason to plot before you summarise. See missing data.
  • Cyclic variables. Hour of day, day of week, wind direction, angle. The mean of 23:00 and 01:00 is not 12:00. These need circular statistics or a sine/cosine encoding, never a plain mean.
Direct consequence for modelling: the variable type dictates the encoding (one-hot for nominal, ordered integer or target encoding for ordinal, scaling for continuous), and the encoding dictates what the model can express. Half of "feature engineering" is just taking §02 seriously — see data preprocessing.

03 The shape of a distribution

Shape comes first because it decides whether the later summaries are even meaningful. On a symmetric single-peaked batch the mean and the standard deviation say almost everything; on a skewed or bimodal one they can be actively misleading, and the honest summary is a different pair of numbers — or a picture.

The histogram, stated properly

  • Split the range into bins; the area of each block is proportional to the frequency in that bin. With equal-width bins, height is proportional to count and you can read the plot naively. With unequal widths — sometimes unavoidable, for example an age variable binned 0–1, 1–5, 5–18, 18–65, 65–100 — height must be frequency ÷ width or the plot lies.
  • On the density scale the height is relative frequency per unit of x, so the total area is exactly 1 and the height carries a unit ("proportion per year", "proportion per °C"). This is the scale that lets a histogram be compared with a smooth density on the same axes.
  • Reading it: the height of a block answers "how crowded is it here", and the area of any set of blocks answers "what proportion of the data falls in this range". The second question is the one that matters; the first is a means to it.
the histogram as a density estimate — bin width b, count nⱼ in bin jHₙ(x) = nⱼ / (n·b) for x in bin j ∫ Hₙ(x) dx = Σⱼ (nⱼ/(n·b))·b = 1 reading an areaproportion in a bin = height × width e.g. 20 years × 0.007 per year = 14% of the batch

The bin-width problem, and why there is no right answer

  • Too wide and real structure is averaged away — two modes fuse into one, a gap disappears, a skew flattens. Too narrow and you are plotting sampling noise: every bin holds two or three points and the outline jumps around.
  • This is a bias–variance trade-off in miniature, and it is exactly the same trade-off as in model complexity (bias and variance). Wide bins = high bias, low variance. Narrow bins = low bias, high variance. The optimum shrinks with n, but slowly.
  • Minimising the mean integrated squared error between the histogram and the underlying density gives an optimal width proportional to n^(−1/3). Substituting a normal reference for the unknown density turns that into a usable rule; replacing the standard deviation by a resistant scale gives a rule that survives outliers.
  • The bin origin matters too. Hold the width fixed and slide all the bin edges by half a bin: at a coarse width, the same data can show one mode or two. Any conclusion that survives only one alignment of the bins is not a conclusion.
bin-width / bin-count rulesSturges k = ceil(log₂ n) + 1 (a bin COUNT; near-normal, under-bins large n) Scott b = 3.49·s·n^(−1/3) (normal reference; uses the non-resistant s) Freedman–Diaconis b = 2·IQR·n^(−1/3) (resistant; the safe default for real data) all three share the same rateb* ∝ n^(−1/3) ⟹ quadrupling n narrows the bins by only ≈ 37%

Smoothing it: the kernel density estimate

  • A histogram is a sum of rectangles pinned to a grid. Replace each observation with a small smooth bump centred on the observation itself and add them up: the grid disappears, and so does the dependence on the bin origin.
  • The bandwidth h plays exactly the role of the bin width, with the same trade-off. The kernel K — Gaussian, Epanechnikov, triangular — matters far less than h; it changes the estimate by a few percent, while h changes the story.
  • Boundary bias. A symmetric kernel spreads mass on both sides of every point, so a variable that cannot be negative — a duration, a count, a price — gets a density estimate that is positive at x < 0. On the plot it looks like a soft left tail; in fact it is an artefact. Reflect the data at the boundary, use a boundary kernel, or transform (log) first.
  • A density estimate is always smooth, even when the truth is not. Spikes at round numbers, a point mass at zero, and heaping at multiples of five are invisible to a KDE and glaring in a fine histogram. Plot both.

The median is a rank, so that same value contributes exactly one rank — the same as every other point. That asymmetry is the difference between the two statistics, and §04 makes it concrete.
  • The old rule "mean > median for right-skewed data" is a reliable heuristic, not a theorem: it can be broken by discrete or multimodal batches. Trust the plot, not the rule.

04 Centre: mean, median, mode — and when each is the honest answer

Three candidates for "the typical value", each of which is the optimal answer to a different question. Choosing between them is not a matter of taste; it is a matter of which loss you are willing to pay and how much of the batch you are willing to let one observation control.

The three, as three optimisation problems

  • The mean is the unique minimiser of the total squared distance to the data. Equivalently it is the balance point: the deviations from it sum to exactly zero, so a physical beam with unit weights at each xᵢ balances at x̄.
  • The median is a minimiser of the total absolute distance. For even n, every point between the two middle order statistics minimises it equally; the convention of taking their midpoint is a tie-break, not a derivation.
  • The mode is the most frequent value — the limiting case where you charge a constant penalty for being wrong at all. It is the only centre available for nominal data, and for continuous data it is not a property of the batch but of the smoothing: change the bin width or the bandwidth in §03 and the mode moves, or splits in two.
mean — minimiser of squared errorx̄ = argmin_c Σᵢ (xᵢ − c)² d/dc Σ(xᵢ − c)² = −2Σ(xᵢ − c) = 0 ⟹ c = (1/n)Σxᵢ the balance property that falls outΣᵢ (xᵢ − x̄) = 0 (always, exactly — a useful arithmetic check) median — minimiser of absolute errorMed = argmin_c Σᵢ |xᵢ − c| the derivative is #{xᵢ < c} − #{xᵢ > c}, zero at the middle modeMode = argmax_v #{i : xᵢ = v} (or the argmax of the density estimate)

Two useful hybrids

  • The trimmed mean sorts the data, discards k = floor(n·α) values from each end, and averages the rest. At α = 0 it is the mean; as α → 0.5 it becomes the median; in between it is a dial. A 10% trim is a common default and buys most of the median's protection while still using 80% of the observations. This is why judged sports drop the highest and lowest scores — one hostile judge then cannot move the total.
  • The Winsorised mean is the same idea without discarding: the extreme k values on each side are replaced by the most extreme surviving value, then everything is averaged. It keeps n intact, which matters when you go on to compute a standard error.
  • The weighted mean attaches a weight wᵢ to each observation. Two standard motivations: (i) the observations differ in precision — a noisier sensor gets a smaller weight, and the variance-optimal choice is wᵢ ∝ 1/σᵢ²; (ii) the sample does not represent the population in the right proportions, so under-represented groups are up-weighted. A national rate computed by averaging state rates equally is almost always wrong; it has to be weighted by population.
  • There is a weighted median too: sort by value, accumulate weights, and take the value at which the running weight crosses half the total. Like the median, it is resistant.
trimmed mean — drop the same fraction from each tailx̄_α = ( x₍ₖ₊₁₎ + x₍ₖ₊₂₎ + … + x₍ₙ₋ₖ₎ ) / (n − 2k), k = floor(n·α), x₍₁₎ ≤ … ≤ x₍ₙ₎ weighted mean and its precision-optimal weightsx̄_w = Σᵢ wᵢxᵢ / Σᵢ wᵢ wᵢ ∝ 1/σᵢ² minimises Var(x̄_w) worked — three regions, rates 5.7, 2.4, 4.4 with populations 4.78, 3.57, 37.25 millionunweighted mean = 4.17 population-weighted mean = 4.38

Resistance and the breakdown point

  • An estimator is resistant (or robust) if a small fraction of arbitrarily bad observations cannot move it far. The breakdown point makes this precise: it is the smallest fraction of the sample that, if replaced by adversarially chosen values, can send the estimate to ±∞.
  • The mean has breakdown point 1/n → 0. One observation, made large enough, drags the mean anywhere you like. The median has breakdown point floor((n−1)/2)/n → 1/2, which is the theoretical maximum: past half the data, "the bulk" is no longer well defined and no estimator can survive.
  • The α-trimmed mean has breakdown point exactly α — the dial does what it says.
  • Resistance is not free. Under a clean normal model the mean is the most efficient estimator of the centre, and the median's variance is about π/2 ≈ 1.57 times larger — you would need roughly 57% more data to match it. Resistance buys insurance against contamination, and you pay for insurance whether or not you need it.

The worked case where they disagree: household income

Twenty-three households, annual income in thousands. Right-skewed with a wall at zero and no upper bound — the archetype.

  • "The average household earns $124k" is true and useless. It describes no household; it is a statement about the total divided by 23. If a journalist wants "what is a typical household like", the answer is $45k, and the honest report is both numbers plus the fact that they differ by a factor of three — because that gap is the story.
  • The mean is the right answer whenever the sum is what you care about. n·x̄ = Σxᵢ, exactly — total wage bill, total tax base, total revenue, expected cost per transaction times volume. No other centre carries the total, and the median cannot be aggregated at all (the median of a union is not a function of the medians of the parts).
  • The median is the right answer for "what should I expect for one unit": a typical wait, a typical latency, a typical household. It is also what you want when the tail is real but not the subject — the tail is a separate finding, best reported as a quantile.
  • The mode is the right answer when the values are labels, or when the question is literally "what happens most often" — the most common failure code, the modal basket size.
  • In production monitoring the same logic gives the standard practice of reporting p50, p95 and p99 latency rather than mean latency: the mean of a heavy-tailed latency distribution is a number no request ever experiences.

05 Spread: variance, standard deviation, IQR, MAD — and the n − 1

A centre without a spread is half a summary and can be worse than none: two batches with the same mean can be identical or completely different. Spread is also the quantity the whole rest of the series is built on — every standard error, every confidence interval and every test statistic is a spread in disguise.

Deviations, and why they get squared

  • Start from the deviations dᵢ = xᵢ − x̄. They cannot simply be averaged: by the balance property of §04 they sum to exactly zero, always. The negative ones cancel the positive ones by construction.
  • Two repairs. Take absolute values → the mean absolute deviation. Take squares, average, and take the root → the standard deviation. The first is more intuitive and more resistant; the second is what statistics actually uses, for four reasons worth knowing rather than accepting:
    1. It is differentiable everywhere. |d| has a corner at zero; d² does not, so squared error yields closed-form minimisers (the mean, the least-squares line) while absolute error does not.
    2. Variances of independent quantities add. Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) when X ⟂ Y. Nothing of the sort holds for absolute deviations, and this single additivity property is what makes the 1/√n shrinkage of a standard error — and hence the central limit theorem — work at all.
    3. It is a squared length. Σdᵢ² = ‖x − x̄1‖², so the whole subject inherits Euclidean geometry: orthogonality, projection, the Pythagorean decomposition of variance into "explained" and "residual". See the dot product and least-squares.
    4. It is the natural parameter of the normal distribution, so squared error and maximum likelihood coincide under the model that the central limit theorem keeps producing.
the three deviation-based measuresmean absolute deviation MAD_mean = (1/n) Σᵢ |xᵢ − x̄| sample variance s² = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)² sample standard deviation s = √s² (same units as the data — that is the whole point) the one ordering that always holds — the power-mean (Cauchy–Schwarz) inequality(1/n)Σ|xᵢ − x̄| ≤ √( (1/n)Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ) ≤ s — s is never the smaller one but "s ≥ mean abs dev ≥ MAD" is a rule of thumb, NOT a theoremx = 2, 7, 15: x̄ = 8, |devs| = 6, 1, 7 ⟹ mean abs dev = 14/3 = 4.667 median = 7, |devs| = 5, 0, 8 ⟹ MAD = 5 > 4.667

The median absolute deviation is usually the smallest of the three and is always the most resistant — but, as the counterexample shows, "usually" is the strongest word available. Only the first inequality is a theorem.

The n − 1, answered properly

  • The question is not "which formula is right" but "what are we trying to estimate". If the n numbers are the whole population, divide by n and stop. If they are a sample and you want the population variance σ², the divisor n gives a systematically too-small answer, and here is exactly why.
  • The sum of squared deviations is measured from x̄, which was itself computed from the same data and is therefore pulled toward the sample. Deviations from the true mean μ would be larger. The algebraic identity below quantifies the shortfall as precisely n(x̄ − μ)².
step 1 — an exact identity, true for any batch and any constantΣᵢ (xᵢ − μ)² = Σᵢ ((xᵢ − x̄) + (x̄ − μ))² = Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)² + 2(x̄ − μ)·Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄) + n(x̄ − μ)² = Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)² + n(x̄ − μ)² because Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 step 2 — take expectations over repeated samples, the data independent with mean mu and variance sigma-squaredE[ Σ(Xᵢ − μ)² ] = n·σ² E[ n(X̄ − μ)² ] = n·Var(X̄) = n·(σ²/n) = σ² step 3 — subtractE[ Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)² ] = n·σ² − σ² = (n − 1)·σ² step 4 — so the divisor that makes it unbiased is n − 1, not nE[ s² ] = E[ Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)²/(n − 1) ] = σ² ✓ E[ Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)²/n ] = ((n − 1)/n)·σ² < σ²
  • The geometric reading is the one that sticks. The deviation vector d = x − x̄·1 satisfies 1ᵀd = 0, so it is confined to the hyperplane orthogonal to the all-ones direction. That hyperplane has dimension n − 1. The deviations have n entries but only n − 1 of them are free: fix any n − 1 and the last is determined. That count is the degrees of freedom, and dividing by it is dividing the squared length by the dimension it actually lives in — an average per free direction.
  • The same bookkeeping recurs everywhere: fitting p parameters costs p degrees of freedom, so a regression with an intercept and p slopes divides its residual sum of squares by n − p − 1.
  • Two honest caveats. (i) s² is unbiased for σ², but s is not unbiased for σ — the square root is concave, so by Jensen's inequality E[s] ≤ σ. Nothing standard fixes this and nobody worries about it. (ii) Unbiasedness is not automatically the goal: under a normal model the divisor-n version has smaller mean squared error. The n − 1 convention is a defensible choice, not a law of nature — and for n in the thousands the argument is entirely academic.

Computing it without losing your digits

  • The textbook shortcut Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = Σxᵢ² − n·x̄² is algebraically exact and numerically dangerous: when the data are large and the spread is small it subtracts two nearly equal huge numbers, and floating point can return a negative variance.
  • Use a one-pass updating scheme instead, which keeps the running mean and the running sum of squared deviations and never forms Σxᵢ². Every serious library does this; if you are hand-rolling a streaming metric, do it too.

The resistant measures of spread

  • Range = max − min. Breakdown point zero, and worse: for a fixed distribution the expected range grows with n, so it is not even comparable between samples of different sizes. Report the min and max as facts; never use the range as a measure of spread.
  • Interquartile range IQR = Q₃ − Q₁ — the width of the middle half. Breakdown point 25%, since it takes a quarter of the data on one side to move a quartile past the bulk. This is the default companion to the median.
  • Median absolute deviation: take the median, take the absolute deviation of every point from that median, then take the median of those. Breakdown point 50% — the highest possible, the same as the median itself.
  • The MAD is on a different scale from the standard deviation, so the two are not comparable as printed. Multiplying by 1.4826 fixes that under a normal model: for a normal, the median absolute deviation equals 0.6745σ (because Φ⁻¹(0.75) = 0.6745), and 1/0.6745 = 1.4826. The rescaled version is the standard drop-in replacement for s in any formula that wants a scale.

06 Position: quantiles, the five-number summary, boxplots, z-scores

Centre and spread describe the batch. Position describes one value relative to the batch — by rank, which needs no distributional assumption at all, or by standardised distance, which needs a scale. Both answers are useful and they disagree in instructive ways.

Percentiles and quantiles

  • The pth quantile q(p) is the value with a proportion p of the batch at or below it. The percentile is the same object indexed by 0–100 rather than 0–1: the 90th percentile is the 0.9 quantile. The words are interchangeable; the indexing is not, and mixing them is a common bug.
  • Special cases with names: q(0.25) = Q₁ the lower quartile, q(0.5) the median, q(0.75) = Q₃ the upper quartile, deciles at 0.1, 0.2, …, and — in production monitoring — p95 and p99.
  • Quantiles are the summary of choice for a skewed variable because they are equivariant under any increasing transformation: the median of log x is the log of the median of x. No mean has that property, which is why "the mean of the logs" and "the log of the mean" are different numbers and why geometric means exist.

The uncomfortable truth: there is no single definition

  • For a continuous distribution q(p) = F⁻¹(p) and there is nothing to argue about. For n data points the "rank p·n" generally lands between two order statistics, and every reasonable way of resolving that gives a slightly different number. Statistical packages ship nine named conventions; several are defaults somewhere.
  • Four of them, applied to the eight numbers 1, 2, 3, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, give four different interquartile ranges. This is not an error in any of them — each answers a slightly different question about what "25% of the way through" means.
the batchx₍₁₎ … x₍₈₎ = 1 2 3 3 5 6 7 9 (n = 8, median = 4) convention 1 — linear interpolation on h = (n − 1)p (the default in most array libraries)h = 7(0.25) = 1.75 ⟹ Q₁ = x₍₂₎ + 0.75(x₍₃₎ − x₍₂₎) = 2.75 Q₃ = 6.25 IQR = 3.50 convention 2 — linear interpolation on h = (n + 1)p (x₍ᵢ₎ is treated as the i/(n+1) quantile)h = 9(0.25) = 2.25 ⟹ Q₁ = x₍₂₎ + 0.25(x₍₃₎ − x₍₂₎) = 2.25 Q₃ = 6.75 IQR = 4.50 convention 3 — nearest rank, no interpolation: q(p) is the j-th smallest value with j = ceil(p·n)ceil(0.25·8) = 2 ⟹ Q₁ = x₍₂₎ = 2 ceil(0.75·8) = 6 ⟹ Q₃ = x₍₆₎ = 6 IQR = 4.00 convention 4 — Tukey's hinges: the medians of the lower and upper halveslower half 1 2 3 3 ⟹ Q₁ = 2.5 upper half 5 6 7 9 ⟹ Q₃ = 6.5 IQR = 4.00 same eight numbersIQR ∈ { 3.50, 4.00, 4.50 } depending on the convention alone

  • Practical rule: state the convention when n is small or the number is contractual; otherwise take your library's default and use the same one everywhere in a project. Note that a boxplot function and a quantile function in the same package often disagree — box-drawing code frequently uses hinges while the quantile function interpolates.
  • The nearest-rank convention has a special status: it is exactly Fₙ⁻¹(p) = inf{ x : Fₙ(x) ≥ p }, the plug-in estimator of §08. It is the only one that is a genuine quantile of a genuine distribution — the empirical one.

The five-number summary and the boxplot

  • Five numbers: minimum, Q₁, median, Q₃, maximum. Together they sketch centre, spread, skew and range in five values, and the two halves of the box (median − Q₁ versus Q₃ − median) give a resistant read on asymmetry.
  • Boxplot construction, precisely: the box spans Q₁ to Q₃, so its length is the IQR; a line inside marks the median; the whiskers extend to the most extreme observations still within 1.5 × IQR of the box — not to Q₁ − 1.5·IQR and Q₃ + 1.5·IQR, which are the invisible fences. Anything past a fence is drawn individually.
  • The width of the box carries no information unless you deliberately make it proportional to √n.
boxplot recipebox [Q₁, Q₃] length = IQR fences Q₁ − k·IQR and Q₃ + k·IQR with k = 1.5 by convention whiskers to the smallest / largest observation INSIDE the fences points everything outside the fences, drawn one by one why 1.5 — calibrate it against a normalfor a normal: Q₁ = −0.6745σ, Q₃ = +0.6745σ, IQR = 1.349σ fence = 0.6745σ + 1.5(1.349σ) = 2.698σ P(|Z| > 2.698) = 0.0070 ⟹ ≈0.7% of a clean normal batch is flagged — about 7 points in 1000

  • The boxplot's blind spot is modality. Five numbers cannot represent two peaks, and a strongly bimodal batch can produce a perfectly ordinary-looking box. A violin plot (a mirrored density estimate) or a beeswarm of the raw points fixes this; so does simply plotting the histogram next to it.
  • Where the boxplot is unbeatable is small multiples: eight boxes side by side compare eight groups at a glance in the space one histogram would need, which is why it is the standard display for "distribution of y by level of x".

Move the fence multiplier k and watch points cross from "inside the whisker" to "drawn individually". Switch the quantile convention and watch the box itself move. Switch datasets to see the boxplot's blind spot: the bimodal batch and the symmetric one produce near-identical boxes.

Standardising: the z-score

  • z = (x − x̄)/s answers "how many standard deviations from the centre, and on which side". It is unit-free, which is the entire point: a height and a weight become comparable, and features on wildly different scales stop dominating a distance-based model purely because of their units.
  • The transformed batch has mean exactly 0 and standard deviation exactly 1 — a mechanical consequence, true for any batch whatsoever, and therefore not evidence of anything. Standardising does not make data normal; it does not remove skew; it does not remove outliers. It relocates and rescales, nothing more.
  • Standardising is equivariant: replace x by a·x + b with a > 0 and every z is unchanged. So z-scores are invariant to a change of units — °C to °F, dollars to euros — which is exactly the property you want from a "position" measure.
  • A hard bound most people do not know. In a sample of size n, no observation can have |z| > (n − 1)/√n. For n = 10 the largest achievable |z| is 9/√10 = 2.85, so the popular "flag anything past 3 standard deviations" rule cannot fire at all for n ≤ 10, no matter how absurd the value. The rule only becomes possible from n = 11 (10/√11 = 3.02).
  • The z-score is built from x̄ and s, both of which have breakdown point zero — so the very outlier you are hunting has already inflated the denominator and shrunk its own score. The robust z-score replaces both with resistant versions and does not have that circularity.
standardisingzᵢ = (xᵢ − x̄)/s z̄ = 0, s_z = 1 (identically, for any batch) equivarianceyᵢ = a·xᵢ + b, a > 0 ⟹ z_y,ᵢ = z_x,ᵢ the ceiling on a z-scoremax |zᵢ| ≤ (n − 1)/√n n = 10 ⟹ 2.846 n = 11 ⟹ 3.015 n = 100 ⟹ 9.9 robust z-scorez_rob = (x − median) / (1.4826 · MAD) worked — the 24th launch of §01 against the record of 23classical z = (31 − 69.57)/7.06 = −5.46 robust z = (31 − 70)/(1.4826 × 4) = −6.58
Naming, because the libraries disagree: "standardisation" is (x − x̄)/s and produces mean 0, SD 1, unbounded range. "Normalisation" or "min–max scaling" is (x − min)/(max − min) and produces range [0, 1] with the extremes pinned to the ends — which makes it maximally sensitive to a single outlier. They are different operations with different failure modes: see scaling.

07 Outliers, and why "remove them" is the wrong reflex

An outlier is an observation far from the bulk of the data. That is a description of a position, not a diagnosis of an error — and the gap between those two things is where a great deal of bad analysis lives.

Three quite different things wear the same label

  1. A recording error. A transposed digit, a unit mix-up, a sensor dropout coded as −999, a date parsed as a number. The value does not correspond to any real measurement. Correct it if you can, mark it missing if you cannot — but only after you have identified the mechanism.
  2. A genuine draw from a heavy-tailed process. Income, city population, insurance claims, file sizes, network latency: extreme values are not rare accidents but the normal behaviour of the distribution. Deleting these does not clean the data, it changes the population you are describing — usually into one that does not exist.
  3. Contamination from a different process. A bot in the user data, a calibration run mixed into production readings, one fraudulent transaction among honest ones. The point is real but comes from a different generating mechanism. The right move is usually to identify and model the second mechanism, not to silently delete its evidence.

Only case 1 clearly licenses deletion, and only with a diagnosis attached.

A worked case: the answer was neither "keep" nor "drop"

  • Deleting the two values would have produced a plausible-looking centre (x̄ = 41.8 against the correct 41.45) but a spread a third too small — s = 0.97 against the correct 1.44 — because the deletion removed the only two hours that carried a different measurement regime. Every standard error computed downstream would have been 33% too narrow.
  • The outliers were the evidence that something had changed. Deleting them removed the alarm and kept the fault.
  • The general shape of the lesson: an outlier is a question, and the useful response is an investigation, not a filter. See data cleaning.

Why routine deletion is worse than it looks

  • It biases the spread downward, systematically. The whole point of a tail is that it contributes disproportionately to s. Trim the tail and every downstream standard error, confidence interval and p-value is too narrow — you become more confident by throwing away the evidence that you should not be.
  • It is a decision made on the outcome, which is the same structural error as §01's launch analysis. If you drop the points that disagree with your model, your model will fit.
  • Iterating the rule never terminates sensibly. Remove everything past 3s, recompute s — which has just shrunk — and there are new points past 3s. Repeat and you can delete an arbitrary fraction of a perfectly clean sample.
  • Masking: two or more outliers on the same side inflate x̄ and s so much that neither exceeds 3s. The rule fails exactly when there is most to find. Swamping is the mirror image: one huge value drags the mean so far that legitimate points on the opposite side are flagged.
  • Both failures come from using non-resistant statistics to detect breaches of the assumptions that make those statistics valid. Resistant rules — boxplot fences, robust z-scores — do not have this circularity, which is the entire argument for §05's alternatives.
masking, concretely — 18 readings clustered near 10, plus a contaminating 608.6 9.1 9.4 9.6 9.8 9.9 10.0 10.0 10.1 10.2 10.3 10.5 10.6 10.8 11.0 11.2 11.5 12.0 plus 60 one contaminant — the classical rule firesn = 19 x̄ = 12.87 s = 11.44 z of the 60 = +4.12 > 3 ✓ flagged now make it three contaminants at 60 — and the rule goes blindn = 21 x̄ = 17.36 s = 17.85 z of a 60 = +2.39 < 3 ✗ all three missed the resistant rule on the very same batchmedian = 10.3 1.4826·MAD = 1.04 z_rob of a 60 = +47.9 — unmissable

What to do instead, in order of preference

  1. Diagnose. Find the record, find the process that produced it, and fix the value or the pipeline. This is the only action that improves the data.
  2. Model it. If the tail is real, use a model that expects a tail: work on the log scale, fit a heavy-tailed or count model, or fit a mixture with an explicit component for the second mechanism.
  3. Down-weight it. Winsorise, use a trimmed mean, use a Huber or absolute-error loss instead of squared error. The observation still participates; it just stops being able to dictate the answer.
  4. Summarise resistantly. Median and IQR/MAD instead of mean and SD. Cheap, honest, and requires no decision about individual records.
  5. If you must exclude, then: write the rule down before you look at the results; apply it mechanically; report how many records it removed; and report the analysis with and without them. A result that survives only one of the two is not a result, it is a sensitivity finding — and that is worth reporting too.

Two wrinkles worth knowing

  • Multivariate outliers hide in plain sight. A person 150 cm tall is unremarkable; a person weighing 120 kg is unremarkable; someone who is both is extraordinary — and no one-variable rule will ever find them, because they are inside the fences on every margin. Detecting this needs a joint measure: Mahalanobis distance √((x − x̄)ᵀ S⁻¹ (x − x̄)), which is a z-score that accounts for the correlation structure, or a look at the low-variance principal components (PCA, and the covariance algebra in §18).
  • Sometimes the outliers are the point. In fraud detection, fault monitoring, intrusion detection and quality control, the extreme points are the signal and the bulk exists only to define "normal". The techniques are identical; only the sign of your interest flips.
The one-line policy. Outliers are handled by diagnosis, modelling, or down-weighting — deletion is a last resort that must be pre-specified, counted and reported. If your analysis changes materially when a handful of records is dropped, that fact is the finding.

08 The empirical distribution function and the plug-in principle

Everything so far has described this batch. This section is the bridge to describing the population it came from — and the bridge is a single object: the empirical distribution function, which turns a list of numbers into a genuine probability distribution and thereby makes every descriptive statistic an estimator for free.

Definition and construction

  • Fₙ(x) is the proportion of the batch that is less than or equal to x. Nothing more.
  • Its graph is a staircase: flat everywhere, with a jump of 1/n at each observation (a jump of k/n where k values are tied). It starts at 0 to the left of the minimum and reaches 1 at the maximum, and it is right-continuous by construction.
  • It is a lossless representation of the batch: from the staircase you can read back every observation. Unlike a histogram it involves no binning and no arbitrary choices — no width, no origin, no bandwidth. That is its great virtue and also why it is harder to eyeball than a histogram.
  • Steep stretches are dense regions; flat stretches are gaps. A bimodal batch shows as two steep sections separated by a plateau — the same information a histogram shows as two humps, with no smoothing decision required.
definitionFₙ(x) = (1/n) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ 1{ xᵢ ≤ x } = #{ i : xᵢ ≤ x } / n worked — the batch 4, 3, 9, 1, 7 (n = 5, sorted: 1 3 4 7 9)Fₙ(x) = 0 x < 1 Fₙ(x) = 1/5 1 ≤ x < 3 Fₙ(x) = 2/5 3 ≤ x < 4 Fₙ(x) = 3/5 4 ≤ x < 7 Fₙ(x) = 4/5 7 ≤ x < 9 Fₙ(x) = 1 x ≥ 9 reading a proportion off itproportion in (a, b] = Fₙ(b) − Fₙ(a)

Why this changes the subject

  • Fₙ satisfies every requirement of a distribution function: non-decreasing, right-continuous, limits 0 and 1. So it is the distribution function of a random variable — the one that puts probability 1/n on each observed value.
  • That single observation collapses the distinction between "a dataset" and "a distribution". Anything you can define for a distribution, you can now compute for a dataset by pointing the definition at Fₙ.
  • At any fixed x, n·Fₙ(x) is a count of successes in n independent trials with success probability F(x): a binomial. That immediately gives its mean, its variance, and the fact that its variance vanishes at rate 1/n.
pointwise behaviour — X₁ … Xₙ iid with true cdf Fn·Fₙ(x) ~ Binomial( n, F(x) ) E[Fₙ(x)] = F(x) — unbiased at every x Var(Fₙ(x)) = F(x)(1 − F(x)) / n — → 0, and largest where F(x) = ½ uniformly — the Glivenko–Cantelli theoremsup_x | Fₙ(x) − F(x) | → 0 almost surely, as n → ∞ with a rate — the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequalityP( sup_x | Fₙ(x) − F(x) | > ε ) ≤ 2·e^(−2nε²) for every F and every ε > 0
  • Glivenko–Cantelli is sometimes called the fundamental theorem of statistics, and the reason is the word uniformly. Not "at each point separately" but "the worst gap anywhere on the line shrinks to zero". The empirical distribution eventually knows the true one everywhere at once.
  • DKW turns that into something you can draw. Invert the bound: setting 2e^(−2nε²) = α gives a half-width εₙ that depends only on n and α — not on the unknown F. Adding and subtracting it from the staircase gives a genuinely distribution-free confidence band for the entire curve simultaneously.
  • The band's width shrinks like 1/√n: quadrupling the sample halves it. That rate is the same 1/√n that governs every standard error in parts 3 to 5. It shows up here first, in the most assumption-free setting the subject has.

The plug-in principle

  • A statistical functional is any quantity that is a function of the distribution itself: θ = T(F). The mean, the variance, the median, any quantile, the correlation, the skewness — all of them are functionals.
  • The plug-in estimator is obtained by substituting Fₙ for the unknown F: θ̂ = T(Fₙ). That is the entire idea, and it is the reason the descriptive statistics of §04–§06 are not merely descriptive — each one is already the natural estimator of its population counterpart.
  • For a linear functional T(F) = ∫ r(x) dF(x) the plug-in collapses to an average, because Fₙ puts mass 1/n on each point. This is why so many estimators are "just take the average of something".

  • Where it pays off later. The plug-in estimator is easy to write down but its standard error usually is not — the sample median is the standard example. The fix is to notice that if Fₙ is a good stand-in for F, then drawing new samples from Fₙ is a good stand-in for drawing new samples from F. Sampling from Fₙ means resampling your own data with replacement — which is the bootstrap of part 7, and it is nothing but the plug-in principle applied a second time, to the sampling distribution.
  • Where it does not pay off. The plug-in is not always unbiased (the variance, above), it can be badly behaved in the far tails where Fₙ has no data at all, and it cannot extrapolate: Fₙ assigns probability exactly zero to every value you did not observe, so plug-in estimates of extreme quantiles are structurally optimistic. Tail estimation needs a model, not a staircase.
This is the hinge of the whole series. Part 1 computes numbers about a batch. The empirical distribution function says those numbers are estimates of population quantities — the same formulas, reinterpreted. Part 3 then asks how much those estimates would bounce around across repeated samples, which is the question the rest of statistics answers.

09 Describing two variables at once

Everything above summarises one column. The moment there are two, a new question appears — do they move together? — and the same discipline applies: plot first, then summarise, and know exactly what the summary is blind to.

The scatterplot is not optional

  • Plot the n pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ). Look for: direction (up, down, none), form (straight, curved, stepped), strength (tight or diffuse), clusters, gaps, outliers, and — critically — whether the spread of y is constant across x or fans out (heteroscedasticity, which breaks the assumptions of everything downstream).
  • Nothing in the numerical summaries below can tell you about form, clusters or heteroscedasticity. Anscombe's quartet in §01 exists precisely to prove this.
  • Large n defeats the plain scatterplot: at 10⁵ points you get a solid black blob. The fixes preserve the same reading — hexagonal binning, two-dimensional density contours, or alpha-blending — all of which are just two-dimensional histograms and density estimates, exactly as in §03.
  • Discrete or heaped data defeats it differently: identical pairs overplot into a single dot. Add a small random jitter for the display only, never for the analysis.

Covariance

  • Covariance is the average product of the two deviations. It is positive when the points sit predominantly in the "both above average / both below average" quadrants, and negative when they sit in the mixed quadrants.
  • Its sign is interpretable, its magnitude is not: the units are units-of-x × units-of-y, so switching from metres to centimetres multiplies the covariance by 100 without changing anything about the relationship. This is exactly the defect standardising fixes.
  • Covariance of a variable with itself is its variance, which is why the covariance matrix has the variances on its diagonal — the structure that PCA decomposes.
covariances_xy = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) s_xx = s²ₓ — covariance with itself is the variance not scale-freeCov(a·x + b, c·y + d) = a·c·Cov(x, y)

Correlation: covariance, standardised

  • Divide by both standard deviations and the units cancel. Equivalently: standardise both variables first, then average the products of the z-scores. Both readings are worth carrying.
  • r lies in [−1, +1] — a direct consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality exactly when the points lie on a perfect straight line.
  • The geometric reading. Centre both columns and treat them as vectors in ℝⁿ. Then r is the cosine of the angle between them. Perfectly correlated = parallel (0°); uncorrelated = orthogonal (90°); perfectly anti-correlated = antiparallel (180°). Every intuition you have about angles transfers directly. See the dot product and covariance as a Gram matrix.

Five things correlation is not

  1. It is not a measure of association in general — only of linear association. Take y = x² on a symmetric range of x: the relationship is perfect and deterministic, and r = 0 exactly. A tax-revenue-versus-tax-rate curve that rises and then falls has the same property. r = 0 means "no straight-line trend", never "no relationship".
  2. It is not a slope. r is dimensionless and scale-free; the slope b = r·s_y/sₓ carries the units and the magnitude. A relationship can have r = 0.99 and a slope so small it is practically irrelevant, or r = 0.3 and a slope that matters enormously.
  3. It is not agreement. y = 2x gives r = 1 although y is never equal to x. Measuring whether two instruments agree needs a different tool (differences against means, or a concordance coefficient), not r.
  4. It is not resistant. Both s_xy and the two standard deviations have breakdown point zero, so one point decides the answer. In Anscombe IV a single leverage point manufactures r = 0.816 out of a cloud whose x is otherwise constant — remove it and r is not even defined. In Anscombe III the ten remaining points lie on a straight line with r = 0.99999; the eleventh point alone drags it down to 0.816. Always plot; use a rank correlation if the data may be contaminated.
  5. It is not causation, and the reason is worth stating rather than sloganeering: r is symmetric in x and y, so it cannot possibly encode direction, and it is computed from observational data that may share any number of common causes. Establishing causation requires design — randomisation — which is the subject of part 2.

When the linear measure will not do

  • Spearman's ρ replaces each value by its rank and computes the ordinary correlation of the ranks. It measures monotone association rather than linear, it is resistant (a wild value becomes merely the largest rank), and it is invariant under any increasing transformation of either variable — so it is unchanged by taking logs.
  • Kendall's τ counts concordant minus discordant pairs, normalised. Slower to compute, more interpretable ("the probability that two randomly chosen pairs agree in direction, minus the probability they disagree"), and better behaved for small n and heavy ties.
  • Rules of thumb: r for clean, roughly linear, roughly elliptical clouds. Spearman for monotone-but-curved or contaminated data. Kendall for small samples and ordinal variables. When they disagree sharply, that disagreement is itself diagnostic — go back to the scatterplot.
  • Aggregation inflates correlation. Correlate averages over groups (states, regions, cohorts) and r is typically far larger than the correlation over individuals, because averaging destroys within-group variability while leaving between-group variability intact. An "ecological correlation" of 0.9 can coexist with an individual-level correlation near 0 — and the direction can even reverse, which is Simpson's paradox and belongs to part 8.

What correlation sees, and what it misses drag the orange point · switch the pattern

More than two

  • A correlation matrix holds r for every pair. It is symmetric with ones on the diagonal, so half of it is redundant; plot it as a heatmap and order the rows by a clustering so that blocks of mutually correlated variables become visible.
  • A scatterplot matrix shows every pairwise scatter at once. It scales to roughly a dozen variables before it becomes unreadable, and it catches exactly the non-linearities a correlation matrix hides.
  • Highly correlated predictors are collinear, which destabilises regression coefficients without hurting predictions much — a diagnosis you make here, in the descriptive phase, and act on with regularisation or dimensionality reduction.
  • Two categorical variables get a contingency table of counts, read with row or column percentages depending on which conditional distribution the question is about. That is the entry point to part 8.

10 An exploratory pass, end to end

The sections above are a toolbox. This is the order to reach into it, the traps that catch people anyway, and what the write-up should contain.

The first hour with a dataset

  1. Shape and provenance. How many rows and columns? One row = what, exactly? Where did the data come from, who decided which rows exist, and what would have made a row not exist? (That last question is part 2, and it outranks everything below.)
  2. Type every column — nominal, ordinal, binary, count, continuous — and fix the types that were guessed wrongly at load. Look specifically for numeric-looking identifiers and for ordinal data stored as integers (§02).
  3. Missingness. Count nulls per column; look for sentinel codes masquerading as values (−999, 0, 1900-01-01, 99). Ask whether missingness is related to the outcome — that pattern is information, not a nuisance.
  4. Duplicates and keys. Exact duplicate rows, and duplicates on what should be a key. Both are usually pipeline bugs, and both silently distort every count and every mean.
  5. One variable at a time. For each numeric column: histogram and a density estimate, at two bin widths (§03). Record n, mean, median, SD, IQR, min, max, and the count of zeros. For each categorical column: the full value counts, including the long tail of rare levels and any that differ only by whitespace or case.
  6. Read the shapes. Skew? Bimodality? A spike at zero? A ceiling? A gap? Each of these changes what you should do next, and each has a boring explanation more often than an interesting one.
  7. Position and extremes. Five-number summary and a boxplot per numeric column (§06). List the ten largest and ten smallest values as records, not as numbers — then go and look at those records (§07).
  8. Pairs. Scatterplots of the plausible relationships and of everything against the target; a correlation matrix as a heatmap; boxplots of the numeric variables split by each categorical one (§09).
  9. Time, if there is any. Plot every variable against time. Level shifts, sudden variance changes and gaps almost always mean an upstream change, not a phenomenon.
  10. Write down what you found — including the things you decided not to act on, and why. Every one of them is a decision your model will inherit.

What the write-up should contain

  • n, and what one row is. Without these, no other number can be interpreted.
  • For each key variable: a centre and a spread, matched (§05), plus the shape in one word.
  • The number of missing values and how they were handled; the number of records excluded and by what pre-specified rule.
  • The quantile convention, if any quantile is contractual or is being compared across tools.
  • A picture of every claim. If a relationship is asserted, the scatterplot goes in the appendix at minimum.
