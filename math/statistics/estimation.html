Estimation & Confidence Intervals

Part 3 built the machine: a statistic has a sampling distribution, with a centre, a spread called the standard error, and a shape that the central limit theorem pushes towards normal. This part spends it. First on the question which estimator should I use, and how good can any estimator possibly be? — bias, variance, mean squared error, information. 01 What an estimator is, and what "good" could mean

Part 3 said a statistic is a random variable. An estimator is that idea with a job attached: a rule, fixed in advance, that turns a sample into a guess at a parameter. Once the job exists, so does the possibility of doing it badly — and the whole of this section is the vocabulary for saying how badly, precisely enough to compare two rules and pick one.

The setup, in the notation the rest of the page uses

the data and the thing you wantX₁, …, Xₙ drawn i.i.d. from a distribution F θ = a fixed, unknown number describing F an estimator is a FUNCTION of the data, chosen before the data arriveθ̂ₙ = g(X₁, …, Xₙ) e.g. μ̂ = x̄, p̂ = (#successes)/n, σ̂² = s² so the estimator is random and the parameter is not — the whole subject turns on thisθ is FIXED and unknown. θ̂ₙ is KNOWN and random. three words that are constantly confusedestimator the rule θ̂ₙ = g(X₁,…,Xₙ) a random variable estimate the number it gave θ̂ₙ = 4.02 not random; it is 4.02 estimand the target θ fixed; never observed
  • The parameter need not be a "parameter" in a parametric model. It can be any feature of F at all — a mean, a median, a 99th percentile, a correlation, the area under an ROC curve. Anything of the form θ = T(F) is called a statistical functional, and the first estimator you should think of is always the plug-in one: θ̂ = T(F̂ₙ), the same functional applied to the empirical distribution of the sample. The sample mean is the plug-in estimator of the mean; the sample median of the median; and so on down the list from part 1.
  • The sampling distribution of θ̂ₙ is the object that makes all of this well-defined. Its centre relative to θ is bias; its spread is the standard error; its shape decides which critical value you get to use later. All three were built in part 3, §03.
  • An estimator with no sampling distribution cannot be assessed. If the sample was not produced by a chance mechanism, there is nothing to average over and the words "bias" and "standard error" have no referent — only an unquantifiable error. This is part 2's point, and it is a precondition for everything below.

Three questions you can ask about any estimator

① where is it centred?
Bias
Averaged over all samples that might have been drawn, does it land on θ? bias(θ̂) = E[θ̂] − θ. Zero bias is called unbiased — and is much less important than it sounds.
② how much does it wobble?
Variance
How far does it move when the sample is redrawn? Var(θ̂), whose square root is the standard error. This is the quantity part 3, §09 showed shrinks like 1/√n.
③ how far off is it, typically?
Mean squared error
MSE = E[(θ̂ − θ)²]. The only one of the three that measures what you actually care about, and — as §02 proves — it is exactly bias² plus variance.

The loss function is a choice, not a fact

Nothing forces you to score an estimator by squared error. You could score it by E|θ̂ − θ|, by P(|θ̂ − θ| > δ), or by the length of the interval it eventually produces. Squared error dominates the subject for three reasons, all worth knowing so you notice when they stop applying:

  • It decomposes. Squared error, and essentially only squared error, splits cleanly into a centring term and a spread term (§02). Absolute error does not.
  • It matches the algebra of variances. Variances add for independent things; absolute deviations do not. Every closed form in this series exists because someone squared something.
  • It is the loss the normal model implies. Maximising a normal likelihood is minimising squared error — the reason least squares and maximum likelihood coincide for Gaussian noise (linear regression).
  • But it punishes large errors quadratically, which makes it fragile in exactly the heavy-tailed settings part 3, §08 warned about. When a single observation can dominate the sum, an MSE comparison is measuring the tail, not the estimator.
The sentence to carry through the page. An estimator is a rule you commit to before seeing the data, judged by how it behaves across all the samples you did not draw. Every property below — unbiasedness, consistency, efficiency, and eventually 95% coverage — is a property of the rule, never of the one number it happened to give you. Forgetting this is the single most expensive mistake in applied statistics, and §07 is devoted to it.

02 Bias, variance, MSE — and the identity that links them

Three definitions, one three-line proof, and a set of consequences that quietly govern everything from the n − 1 in the sample variance to why ridge regression works. This identity is the same one machine learning calls the bias–variance decomposition; here it appears in its original form, about an estimator rather than a predictor.

The definitions

bias — a property of the estimator, measured in the units of θbias(θ̂ₙ) = E[θ̂ₙ] − θ unbiased ⟺ E[θ̂ₙ] = θ for every θ ∈ Θ variance and its square root, the standard errorVar(θ̂ₙ) = E[(θ̂ₙ − E[θ̂ₙ])²] SE(θ̂ₙ) = √Var(θ̂ₙ) mean squared error — distance from the TARGET, not from the estimator's own averageMSE(θ̂ₙ) = E[(θ̂ₙ − θ)²]

The decomposition, proved

Write θ̄ = E[θ̂ₙ] for the estimator's own mean, then add and subtract it inside the square:

step 1 — insert a zeroE[(θ̂ₙ − θ)²] = E[( (θ̂ₙ − θ̄) + (θ̄ − θ) )²] step 2 — expand the square into three terms= E[(θ̂ₙ − θ̄)²] + 2(θ̄ − θ)·E[θ̂ₙ − θ̄] + (θ̄ − θ)² step 3 — the cross term dies, because E[θ̂ₙ − θ̄] = 0 by definition of θ̄= Var(θ̂ₙ) + bias²(θ̂ₙ) THE IDENTITYMSE = variance + bias² — exact, no approximation, no assumptions
  • The two terms are in the same units (squared units of θ), which is what makes them tradeable. A rule that pays one unit of squared bias to save two units of variance is a better rule, and the fact that it is now "wrong on average" is irrelevant to its accuracy.
  • Nothing here is asymptotic. The identity holds at n = 3 as exactly as at n = 3 000 000. Only the individual terms depend on n.
  • The cross term dying is the whole trick, and it is the same trick that makes least squares work, that makes Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y for uncorrelated variables, and that gives the Pythagorean flavour to orthogonal projection. Bias is the component along the "error" direction; variance is the component orthogonal to it.

Consistency: the minimum a rule must clear

consistency — convergence in probability to the truthθ̂ₙ →ᵖ θ i.e. for every ε > 0, P(|θ̂ₙ − θ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞ a sufficient condition that is trivial to checkif bias(θ̂ₙ) → 0 and Var(θ̂ₙ) → 0 then MSE → 0 then θ̂ₙ →ᵖ θ why: Chebyshev, exactly as in part 3 §04P(|θ̂ₙ − θ| > ε) ≤ MSE(θ̂ₙ)/ε² → 0
  • Consistency is a floor, not a recommendation. An estimator that is not consistent is broken: more data does not help it. But consistency says nothing about behaviour at the n you actually have. The estimator θ̂ₙ = x̄ + 10⁶/n is consistent and useless.
  • Unbiased does not imply consistent. "Use X₁ and throw the rest away" is exactly unbiased for μ at every n, and its variance is σ² forever. It never converges to anything.
  • Consistent does not imply unbiased. The maximum-likelihood estimator of a normal variance, σ̂² = (1/n)∑(xᵢ − x̄)², is biased at every finite n and consistent.
  • Neither implies the other, and MSE implies both. That is the practical reason to work in MSE and treat unbiasedness as an occasionally convenient special case.

So why does everyone use n − 1? Three honest reasons and one bad one. (1) Unbiasedness composes: sums and differences of unbiased things stay unbiased, which keeps ANOVA and regression tables coherent. (2) The n − 1 version is the one whose ratio to σ² is exactly χ²ₙ₋₁/(n−1), which is what makes the t interval of §08 exact. (3) The optimal divisor n + 1 is optimal only under normality and only for squared-error loss on σ² — it is not optimal for σ, and it is not optimal for other distributions. The bad reason is that people believe unbiasedness is the point. It is not; §03 is about that.

03 When a biased estimator beats an unbiased one

This is the most useful idea on the page for anyone who trains models, and it is stated backwards in most first courses. Unbiasedness is not a goal. It is a constraint, and like every constraint it costs you something — here, variance. Deliberately accepting a little bias to buy a lot of variance reduction is not a compromise or a hack. It is optimal, it is provable, and it is exactly what regularisation does.

The general shape of the argument: shrinkage

Take any unbiased estimator θ̂ with variance v, and shrink it towards a fixed point (take that point to be 0, without loss of generality — shrink towards anything by subtracting first). The shrunk estimator is θ̂_λ = λ·θ̂ with λ ∈ [0, 1].

bias and variance of the shrunk estimator, both elementaryE[θ̂_λ] = λθ ⟹ bias = (λ − 1)θ Var(θ̂_λ) = λ²v so the MSE is a parabola in λMSE(λ) = (λ − 1)²θ² + λ²v differentiate and set to zero2(λ − 1)θ² + 2λv = 0 ⟹ λ* = θ²/(θ² + v) < 1 ALWAYS and the MSE at the optimumMSE(λ*) = θ²v/(θ² + v) = v · θ²/(θ² + v) < v = MSE(1)

Look at what just happened. λ* < 1 whenever the variance is positive, which is always. The derivative of MSE at λ = 1 is 2v > 0, so moving even slightly away from the unbiased estimator strictly decreases the mean squared error. The unbiased estimator is never the MSE optimum in this family. Not sometimes — never.

the honest catch, stated plainlyλ* = θ²/(θ² + v) depends on θ, which is the thing you do not know but the direction is knowable even when the amount is notfor every λ in ( (θ² − v)/(θ² + v) , 1 ) the shrunk estimator beats the unbiased one the gain is largest exactly where estimation is hardestθ² ≫ v (strong signal) → λ* ≈ 1, shrinkage barely helps θ² ≪ v (weak signal) → λ* ≈ 0, shrinkage helps enormously

Two more places bias earns its keep

  • Estimating an exponential rate. Take X₁, …, Xₙ exponential with rate λ. The maximum-likelihood estimator is λ̂ = n/∑xᵢ, and it is biased upward: E[λ̂] = nλ/(n − 1). Working out the whole family a/∑xᵢ gives MSE(a) = λ²[a²/((n−1)(n−2)) − 2a/(n−1) + 1], minimised at a = n − 2. At n = 12: the MLE has MSE 0.1273λ², the unbiased choice a = n − 1 has 0.1000λ², and the biased-low optimum a = n − 2 has 0.0909λ². Simulation over 300 000 samples: 0.1274, 0.1001, 0.0910. Notice that here the unbiased rule beats the MLE but loses to a differently biased rule — the moral is not "bias good", it is "optimise the thing you care about".
  • The bias–variance decomposition of prediction error. The same identity, applied pointwise to a fitted function rather than to a scalar, is the decomposition every ML course opens with: E[(y − f̂(x))²] = bias²(f̂(x)) + Var(f̂(x)) + σ², where the last term is irreducible noise. It is the identity of §02 plus one extra term, and it lives in full at bias–variance trade-off.
The transferable rule. When someone objects that an estimator is biased, the correct reply is a question: biased by how much, and what did the bias buy? An estimator with 5% bias and a third of the variance is a better estimator by any accuracy criterion that matters. Unbiasedness is worth insisting on in exactly two situations: when many estimates will be summed or differenced (errors then cancel only if they are centred), and when the estimator feeds a downstream exactness result — such as the χ² fact that makes the t interval in §08 exact rather than approximate.

04 Efficiency, Fisher information, and how good an estimator can possibly be

§03 said you can trade bias for variance. The natural next question is whether there is a floor — a variance below which no honest estimator can go. There is, it is called the Cramér–Rao lower bound, and the quantity that sets it, Fisher information, has a beautifully concrete meaning: the average sharpness of the peak of the log-likelihood. A sharp peak means the data strongly prefer one value of the parameter; a flat peak means they barely care.

  • Read it as curvature. The log-likelihood is a bump whose peak is the estimate. A tall, narrow, sharply curved bump means the data would have been very unlikely under any nearby parameter value — a lot of information. A wide, flat bump means many values explain the data almost equally well — little information. The second derivative measures exactly that (Taylor, second order).
  • Additivity is why standard errors shrink like 1/√n. Information adds, variance goes like one over information, so variance goes like 1/n and SE like 1/√n. The 1/√n law of part 3, §09 is a corollary of a more general fact.
  • Observed vs expected information. The expected version I(θ) averages over data you did not see; the observed version J(θ̂) uses only your sample. the statement, for an estimator T with E[T] = g(θ)Var(T) ≥ [g′(θ)]² / Iₙ(θ) the case everyone quotes: T unbiased for θ, so g(θ) = θ and g′ = 1Var(T) ≥ 1/Iₙ(θ) = 1/(n·I(θ)) proof, in three lines.   • Equality holds only in a very restricted case. Cauchy–Schwarz is tight only when T − θ is a deterministic multiple of the score, which forces the model into the exponential family with T as its natural sufficient statistic. That is why the bound is attained for Bernoulli, Poisson and the normal mean, and not attained for the exponential rate — same model, different parametrisation, different answer.
  • The bound is about unbiased estimators. Nothing stops a biased estimator from having smaller variance and smaller MSE than 1/Iₙ(θ). The shrinkage estimator of §03 does exactly that. The bound is a statement about the price of insisting on unbiasedness, which is another way of saying §03's point.
  • The regularity conditions are not decoration. They fail whenever the support depends on the parameter, and the Uniform(0, θ) case is the standard demonstration: the MLE X₍ₙ₎ has MSE 2θ²/((n+1)(n+2)), which is of order 1/n² and beats anything of order 1/n for large n.
  • What the bound is good for in practice: a sanity check. If a proposed estimator has variance far above 1/Iₙ(θ), there is room to do better. If someone claims variance far below it and the model is regular and the estimator unbiased, they have made an arithmetic error.

Sufficiency, in outline

a statistic T is SUFFICIENT for θ if the data carry no further information about θ once T is knownthe conditional distribution of X₁,…,Xₙ given T = t does not depend on θ the factorisation criterion — the practical testT is sufficient ⟺ f(x₁,…,xₙ; θ) = h(x₁,…,xₙ) · g(T(x); θ) Rao–Blackwell: conditioning on a sufficient statistic can only helpT′ = E[T | S] has E[T′] = E[T] and Var(T′) ≤ Var(T)
  • The examples are the ones you already use. For Bernoulli, ∑xᵢ is sufficient: once you know 37 successes out of 100, the order in which they arrived is irrelevant to p. For Normal with both parameters unknown, the pair (∑xᵢ, ∑xᵢ²) is sufficient. For Uniform(0, θ), the maximum is.
  • Why it matters here. Rao–Blackwell says any estimator can be improved (weakly) by averaging it over the sufficient statistic, so the search for good estimators can be confined to functions of sufficient statistics. It is also the reason the MLE is almost always a function of one — the likelihood only ever touches the data through g(T(x); θ).
  • The compression reading. A sufficient statistic is a lossless summary with respect to a particular model. Change the model and the summary stops being sufficient — which is exactly why the sample mean is a terrible summary of a bimodal population, and why part 1 insists on plotting.
The chain to hold on to. Log-likelihood curvature → Fisher information → the lower bound on variance → the standard error of the maximum-likelihood estimator → the width of the confidence interval in §06. Every confidence interval built from a likelihood is, ultimately, a statement about how sharply peaked that likelihood was.

05 Three ways to build an estimator, and one worked to the end

So far the estimators have arrived by intuition. There are three systematic recipes: plug-in (make no assumptions), method of moments (match a few averages), and maximum likelihood (ask which parameter makes the data least surprising). The third dominates modern practice — it is the recipe behind logistic regression, behind the cross-entropy loss, and behind almost every model that reports a standard error.

Recipe 1 — the plug-in principle

if the target is a functional of the distribution, apply the functional to the EMPIRICAL distributionθ = T(F) ⟹ θ̂ = T(F̂ₙ) where F̂ₙ puts mass 1/n on each observed point what it gives, mechanicallyμ = ∫x dF → x̄ σ² = ∫(x−μ)²dF → (1/n)∑(xᵢ−x̄)² median → sample median F(t) → F̂ₙ(t) quantiles → sample quantiles ρ → r
  • It assumes nothing about the shape of F, which is its strength: it is the nonparametric default, and it is consistent for any functional that is continuous in the right sense.
  • It is often slightly biased, precisely because F̂ₙ is more concentrated than F: the plug-in variance uses divisor n, not n − 1. §02 already priced that.
  • Standard errors are the hard part. There is no general formula for the SE of a plug-in estimator of an arbitrary functional — which is exactly the gap the bootstrap fills, by resampling from F̂ₙ. That is part 7, and §11 previews it.

Recipe 2 — the method of moments

with k parameters, match the first k theoretical moments to the first k sample momentsαⱼ(θ) = Eθ[Xʲ] α̂ⱼ = (1/n)∑ xᵢʲ solve αⱼ(θ̂) = α̂ⱼ for j = 1…k Normal(μ, σ²): two equations, two unknownsμ̂ = x̄ σ̂² + μ̂² = (1/n)∑xᵢ² ⟹ σ̂² = (1/n)∑(xᵢ − x̄)² Gamma(α, β) with mean αβ and variance αβ²: solve the pairβ̂ = σ̂²/x̄ α̂ = x̄²/σ̂² — closed form, where the MLE needs iteration Uniform(0, θ): mean is θ/2θ̂ = 2x̄ Var(θ̂) = θ²/(3n) — order 1/n, against the MLE's order 1/n²
  • Cheap and usually consistent, asymptotically normal under mild conditions, and often available in closed form when the likelihood is not. It remains the standard way to get a starting value for a numerical likelihood maximisation.
  • Rarely efficient. It uses only the first few moments and throws away the rest of the sample's structure. The Uniform line above is the extreme case: the method-of-moments estimator can even produce an impossible answer — if the sample happens to contain a value above 2x̄, then θ̂ = 2x̄ is smaller than an observation drawn from Uniform(0, θ), which is a contradiction.
  • It needs the moments to exist. For a Cauchy population there is no first moment, and the recipe has nothing to match.

The load-bearing caveat, repeated because it is always forgotten. Every one of those four properties is conditional on the assumed model containing the truth. If f(x; θ) is the wrong family, the MLE still converges — to the member of the family closest in Kullback–Leibler divergence to the real distribution — and its nominal standard error is then a confident measurement of the wrong thing. When the calculus fails, and what to do

  • The maximum is on the boundary. For Uniform(0, θ) the likelihood is θ⁻ⁿ for θ ≥ x₍ₙ₎ and exactly 0 below it — a cliff followed by a decreasing curve. There is no stationary point; the maximum is at the cliff edge, θ̂ = x₍ₙ₎ = max xᵢ. Setting a derivative to zero would have found nothing. Always look at the shape.
  • There is no closed form. The Gamma shape parameter, logistic regression coefficients, and essentially every model with more than two parameters need numerical maximisation — Newton, or the quasi-Newton methods of the optimisation notes. The Hessian at the optimum is the observed information, so the optimiser hands you the standard errors as a by-product.
  • The likelihood is unbounded. Fit a two-component normal mixture and let one component's variance go to zero on a single data point: the likelihood diverges. The global maximum is a degenerate junk solution and the useful answer is a local maximum. This is not a pathology of exotic models; it is why mixture fitting needs constraints or penalties.
  • The log-likelihood is the loss function you already use. Minimising cross-entropy is maximising a Bernoulli or categorical log-likelihood; minimising squared error is maximising a Gaussian one; adding an L2 penalty is multiplying by a normal prior. See logistic regression.

07 What "95% confident" claims — and the four readings that are wrong

This is the most important section on the page and the one most often skimmed. The number 95 is a property of the procedure, measured across the samples you did not draw. It is not a probability about θ, it is not the fraction of the data inside the interval, it is not the chance a future estimate lands inside, and it is not a statement that values in the middle are more likely than values at the edge. Every one of those misreadings appears in published work.

The correct statement, in three equivalent forms

form 1 · the procedure
"The recipe works 95% of the time"
If you drew a fresh sample and rebuilt the interval, over and over, 95% of those intervals would contain the fixed θ. The guarantee attaches to the method, before the data arrive.
form 2 · your career
"95% of my statements will be true"
Build one 95% interval a day, each about a different and unrelated parameter. Over a lifetime, about 95% of those claims are correct. No repetition of the same experiment is needed — which is what makes the guarantee usable at all.
form 3 · compatibility
"These values are compatible with the data"
The interval is exactly the set of θ₀ that a two-sided test at level α would not reject. Reading it as a compatibility range is descriptive, accurate, and hard to abuse.

Form 2 deserves emphasis because form 1 has a fatal practical weakness: you will never repeat this experiment. An interpretation that only applies to an infinite sequence of identical replications is technically correct and operationally empty. Form 2 fixes it. The intervals in the sequence do not have to be about the same quantity, or use the same method, or come from the same field — a 95% procedure applied to a stream of unrelated questions is right about 95% of the time. That is the promise you are buying.

The picture: many intervals, one fixed truth the vertical line never moves — the intervals do

The four wrong readings, each answered

✗ "There is a 95% probability that μ lies between 23.747 and 23.829."
Both endpoints are numbers and μ is a number. Either μ is between them or it is not; the statement is true or false and no probability is involved. The probability lived in the sampling, and the sampling is over. ✓ Say instead: "the procedure that produced this interval captures μ 95% of the time", or, if you genuinely want a probability statement about μ, use a Bayesian credible interval and say so (§12).

The sharpest demonstration. Let θ be unknown and observe Y₁, Y₂ where each Yᵢ = θ ± 1 with equal probability, independently. Define the set C to be the single point (Y₁ + Y₂)/2 when Y₁ ≠ Y₂, and the single point Y₁ − 1 when Y₁ = Y₂. Check the four equally likely cases: C contains θ in three of them, so P(θ ∈ C) = 3/4 for every θ — a bona fide 75% confidence set. Now suppose you observe Y₁ = 15, Y₂ = 17. Then C = {16}, and you know with certainty that θ = 16, because the two observations differed. A "75% confidence" statement that is, conditional on the data you actually saw, 100% certain. The confidence level is a property of the procedure averaged over data; it is not a description of the case in front of you.
✗ "95% of the data fall inside the interval."
No. A confidence interval for a mean has width of order σ/√n and shrinks towards a point as n grows; the data have spread σ and do not shrink at all. At n = 400 the 95% interval for the mean is twenty times narrower than the interval containing 95% of the observations. This is the ①/③ confusion of part 3, §02, and the interval that does contain 95% of the data is a tolerance interval (§12).
✗ "A replication has a 95% chance of landing inside this interval."
It does not, and the true figure is much lower. If you build a 95% interval for μ and then run an independent replication, the probability that the replication's estimate falls inside your original interval is about 83%, not 95% — because two independent estimates each contribute error, so the relevant spread is √2 times larger. The quantity that captures a future observation is a prediction interval (§12), and it is far wider.
✗ "Values near the centre are much more likely than values near the edge — and values just outside are ruled out."
A confidence interval is a set, not a density. The frequentist framework gives it no internal shape. And the boundary is not a cliff: a parameter value one hair outside a 95% interval is only marginally less compatible with the data than one just inside. Treating the endpoint as a decision threshold reproduces every pathology of the 0.05 cut-off in part 5. If you want a graded statement of plausibility across values, that is a likelihood function (§05) or a posterior (§12), not an interval.

Two further traps, both of which void the guarantee

  • Choosing the level, the method, or the endpoint after seeing the data. The 95% is computed over the randomness of the sample under a procedure fixed in advance. If the procedure is allowed to depend on the sample — try 95%, and if that excludes zero, report it; otherwise report 90% — the actual coverage is anybody's guess and it is not 95%. This is the interval-shaped version of the multiplicity problem in part 9.
  • Reading non-overlap as a significant difference. Two 95% intervals that do not overlap do imply a significant difference, but the converse fails badly: intervals can overlap substantially while the difference is significant. The correct object is a single interval for the difference, built from the standard error of the difference (§11). Two independent 95% intervals that just barely touch correspond to a comparison at roughly the 0.5% level — far more conservative than intended.
The one-line version, worth memorising. The confidence is in the recipe, not in the number. Before you look at the data, "this interval will contain μ" has probability 0.95. After you look, it has truth value 0 or 1, and you do not know which. Everything that goes wrong with confidence intervals in practice is a failure to keep those two moments apart.

08 When σ is unknown: the t distribution and its degrees of freedom

The z interval of §06 needed σ, and you never have it. Substituting the sample standard deviation s looks harmless and is not: the pivot stops being normal, because the denominator has become random too. The correct reference distribution is Student's t, the correction is exactly computable, and the size of the correction — negligible at n = 100, enormous at n = 3 — tells you when it is safe to ignore.

Degrees of freedom, explained rather than asserted

Three explanations of the same fact, in increasing order of usefulness.

  • Counting constraints. The n deviations xᵢ − x̄ are not free: they sum to zero by construction, because x̄ was computed from the same data. Fix any n − 1 of them and the last is determined. So n numbers carry only n − 1 independent pieces of information about spread, and dividing the sum of squares by n − 1 is dividing by the number of independent pieces (part 3, §03 proves the unbiasedness this delivers).
  • Geometry. The vector of deviations lives in the (n − 1)-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the all-ones direction — the residual after projecting the data onto that direction. The squared length of a standard normal vector in a d-dimensional subspace is χ²_d. One parameter estimated from the data costs one dimension; that is all "degrees of freedom" ever means, and it is why fitting k regression coefficients leaves n − k (part 6).
  • Information. Each degree of freedom is one independent observation's worth of evidence about σ. With ν = 1 you know almost nothing about the spread, so the interval must be enormous; with ν = 100 you know σ almost exactly, and t* is within 1% of z*. The whole t correction is the price of not knowing σ, and it decays like 1/ν.

Why it fails — three separate defects, not one

  1. The standard error is estimated at the wrong place. The Wald interval uses √(p̂(1−p̂)/n), but the true standard error is √(p(1−p)/n). When p̂ lands below p (and p < ½), the estimated SE is also too small, so the interval is both mis-centred and too short at the same time — the two errors compound instead of cancelling.
  2. It is degenerate at the boundary. If k = 0, then p̂ = 0 and ŜE = 0, so the interval is the single point [0, 0] and covers nothing. Observing zero successes is not rare: at n = 100 and p = 0.01 it happens 36.6% of the time. That single case alone caps coverage at 63.4%, which is essentially the 63.3% in the table.
  3. It ignores the skewness of the binomial. For p away from ½ the sampling distribution of p̂ is skewed, so a symmetric interval is the wrong shape, and it can extend past 0 or 1 — an interval containing impossible values.

Planning for a mean: the awkward σ

  • A pilot study. Twenty to thirty observations give a usable s. Inflate it — the pilot's s is itself noisy, and at ν = 20 its own 95% interval spans roughly 0.77s to 1.44s. Planning with the point estimate under-powers the study about half the time.
  • A previous study, or published values for the same measurement. The most reliable source when it exists.
  • The range rule. For a roughly normal quantity, nearly all values lie within about ±2σ of the centre, so σ ≈ range/4 (or /6 for a wide range). Crude, free, and usually within a factor of 1.5.
  • The t-versus-z circularity. Planning uses z*, but the analysis will use t*, which is larger. For small planned n, iterate once: compute n with z*, look up t* at n − 1, recompute. Two rounds always suffice.
  • The finite-population correction, when you are sampling a large fraction. Required n drops to n₀/(1 + (n₀ − 1)/N), where n₀ is the infinite-population answer. Auditing 1 200 invoices out of a population of 3 000 needs far fewer than 1 200.

The planning conversation, in four questions. (1) What decision will this number drive, and how precise does it have to be to drive it? That fixes m. (2) How costly is a wrong claim? That fixes the confidence level. (3) What is the variability — from a pilot, a previous study, or the worst case? That fixes σ or p₀. (4) What fraction of the data will you actually get? That fixes the inflation for non-response. Anyone who asks "how much data do we need?" without answering the first is asking a question with no answer.

12 Four intervals that look alike and answer different questions

A confidence interval, a prediction interval, a tolerance interval and a credible interval are all written "a to b", all reported at "95%", and all mean different things. Three of them are frequentist and answer questions about different targets; the fourth changes what probability means. Confusing them produces error bars that are off by an order of magnitude, in a direction that always flatters the analyst.

① confidence interval
Where is the parameter?
Captures a fixed unknown number — a mean, a rate, a coefficient. Width ∝ 1/√n. Shrinks to a point as data accumulate. x̄ ± t*·s/√n
② prediction interval
Where will the next observation fall?
Captures one future draw. Carries the population's own spread plus the uncertainty in the centre. Converges to a fixed width, never to a point. x̄ ± t*·s·√(1 + 1/n)
③ tolerance interval
Where do 95% of the population live?
Two percentages, not one: "with 95% confidence, covers 99% of units". Also converges to a fixed width — the population's true 99% range. x̄ ± k·s
④ credible interval
Given this data, where is θ?
A Bayesian posterior region. This one is a probability statement about θ — bought by putting a prior on θ and treating it as random.

13 The machine-learning translation: error bars on everything you report

Every number on a model card is an estimate, computed from a finite sample, with a sampling distribution. Part 3, §11 established that a validation score is a statistic. This section turns that into the three arithmetic habits that follow: put an interval on the score, size the test set on purpose, and never compare two models without an interval on the difference.

Habit 1 — an interval on the metric, not just the metric

accuracy on a held-out set of n examples is a proportion — so §09 applies verbatimuse WILSON, not Wald: the accuracies that matter are near 0.9+, where Wald is worst worked: 912 correct out of 1 000p̂ = 0.9120 Wald: 0.9120 ± 0.0176 = (89.44%, 92.96%) Wilson: = (89.28%, 92.80%) the same accuracy on a small test set — where the difference bitesn = 100, 98 correct: Wald 0.9800 ± 0.0274 = (95.26%, 100.74%) ✗ impossible Wilson = (93.00%, 99.45%) ✓ and the case Wald cannot express at alln = 200, 200 correct: Wald gives the point (100%, 100%) Wilson gives (98.12%, 100%) — the honest answer
  • The 100%-accuracy case is not a corner case. It happens constantly on small evaluation slices, on per-class breakdowns, and on unit-test-style benchmarks. Reporting "100%" without an interval implies a certainty that 200 examples cannot supply: the true accuracy could be 98.2%, which on a million requests is 18 000 failures.
  • Every metric, not just accuracy. Precision, recall and specificity are each proportions with their own denominators — recall's denominator is the number of actual positives, which on an imbalanced set may be a small fraction of n. A model evaluated on 10 000 examples with a 1% positive rate has only 100 positives, so its recall carries the standard error of n = 100, not n = 10 000. See rare-class evaluation.
  • Metrics that are not means need the bootstrap. AUC, F1, mean average precision and calibration error have no simple standard error, because they are not averages of independent per-example quantities. Bootstrap the examples and recompute (part 7).
  • Cross-validation standard errors are not s/√k. The k fold scores share training data and are positively correlated, so the naive formula understates the true variability — often by a factor of two or more. Treat s/√k as a lower bound and prefer a held-out test set for the number you publish (validation strategy).

14 Practice

The checks to run before you print an interval, the traps that produce wrong ones, worked problems with full arithmetic, and the short list of results worth being able to derive from a blank page.

The checklist, before you report any interval

  1. What exactly is being bracketed? A parameter, a future observation, a fraction of the population, or a belief. §12. Get this wrong and nothing else matters.
  2. Was the procedure fixed before the data? Level, method, direction, and the decision to stop collecting. Anything chosen afterwards voids the coverage guarantee.
  3. Which reference distribution, and why? Normal needs a known σ or a large n; t needs approximate symmetry; Wilson needs nothing beyond independence; χ² for a variance needs genuine normality.
  4. Is the standard error a function of the parameter? If yes (proportion, variance, rate), do not plug in the estimate and go symmetric — invert instead (§09).
  5. Is the parameter bounded? If the interval crosses 0, 1, or ∞ it was built on the wrong scale. Transform, build, transform back (§05).
  6. Is n counting the right thing? Independent units, not rows. Clustered, repeated or time-indexed data need an effective sample size.
  7. How skewed is the population? Compute γ₁. The t interval's coverage failure tracks skewness, not sample size (§08).
  8. Is this a rate near 0 or 1? Then np, not n, is the quantity that decides whether the approximation holds.
  9. Is the sampling fraction large? Above 5% of a finite population, apply the correction and get a narrower, correct interval for free.
  10. Have I compared two intervals instead of intervalling the difference? Almost always the wrong move (§07, §11).
  11. How many intervals am I reporting at once? Twenty independent 95% intervals contain, on average, one miss. Multiplicity is part 9.
  12. Is the sampling error even the largest error here? Coverage, non-response and measurement bias are not in the formula and are frequently bigger.

Worked problems

  1. A poll. 1 000 likely voters, 580 approve. Give a 95% interval, and say what it claims.
    Answer. p̂ = 0.580, ŜE = √(0.58×0.42/1000) = 0.015612, m = 1.96×0.015612 = 0.0306, so (54.9%, 61.1%). Wilson gives (54.92%, 61.02%) against Wald's (54.94%, 61.06%) — at n = 1 000 near p = ½ the two agree to within 0.04 points. The claim: the procedure that produced this interval captures the true approval rate 95% of the time. It does not claim a 95% chance the rate is in this interval, and it prices only the sampling gap — not non-response, not question wording.
  2. The same poll, one-sided. Give a 95% lower bound on approval.
    Answer. All of α in one tail: z = 1.64485, m = 0.02568, bound = 0.5543. "With 95% confidence, approval is at least 55.4%." Tighter than the two-sided lower end of 54.9% — because you gave up the upper bound to get it.
  3. A tiny sample of measurements. Five readings: 4.1, 3.8, 4.6, 3.9, 4.4. Give a 95% interval for the mean.
    Answer. x̄ = 4.16; deviations −0.06, −0.36, 0.44, −0.26, 0.24; ∑d² = 0.452; s² = 0.452/4 = 0.113, s = 0.33615. SE = 0.33615/√5 = 0.15033. ν = 4, t* = 2.77645, m = 0.41738. Interval (3.743, 4.577). Using 1.96 instead would give (3.865, 4.455) — 29% narrower and only 88% confident.
  4. Zero failures. You test 300 units and none fails. What is the failure rate?
    Answer. Not zero. The exact one-sided 95% upper bound is 1 − 0.05^(1/300) = 0.00994, essentially the rule of three, 3/300 = 1%. The Wald interval would say [0, 0], which asserts certainty from an experiment that could not have detected a 0.5% rate: at p = 0.005, the chance of seeing zero failures in 300 trials is 0.995³⁰⁰ = 22%.
  5. The Wald trap, quantified. A monitoring system flags 1 of 100 requests. Give both intervals.
    Answer. Wald: 0.01 ± 1.96√(0.01×0.99/100) = 0.01 ± 0.0195 = (−0.95%, 2.95%) — a negative rate. Wilson: (0.18%, 5.45%). The upper ends differ by nearly a factor of two, and at p near 0.01 with n = 100 the Wald interval's actual coverage is 63%.
  6. Sample size for an evaluation set. You need to distinguish 90.0% from 91.0% accuracy. How many test examples?
    Answer. Paired, with a plausible disagreement rate of 5%: for a 2-SE separation, n ≈ 4(0.05 − 0.01²)/0.01² ≈ 2 000. Unpaired it is n ≈ 4(0.9×0.1 + 0.91×0.09)/0.01² ≈ 6 876 per model. Pairing is worth a factor of three in data.
  7. Biased beats unbiased. With n = 6 normal observations, compare the three variance estimators of §02.
    Answer. In units of σ⁴: unbiased 2/5 = 0.400, MLE 11/36 = 0.306, optimal 2/7 = 0.286. The MSE-optimal estimator is 29% better than the unbiased one, and it under-estimates σ² by 2σ²/7 on average. Both statements are true simultaneously.
  8. Reading a likelihood. Twelve exponential lifetimes sum to 48 hours. Give the MLE of the rate, its standard error, and a 95% interval; then give an interval for the median lifetime.
    Answer. λ̂ = 12/48 = 0.25/h. ℓ″ = −n/λ² so J = 12/0.0625 = 192 and ŜE = 1/√192 = 0.07217. Interval (0.1086, 0.3914). Median = log2/λ, so by invariance m̂ = 4log2 = 2.773 h; by the delta method ŜE = log2 × 1.1547 = 0.8004, giving (1.204, 4.341) h. Check against the exact chi-square interval for λ: (0.1292, 0.4100) — the asymptotic version is 3% too narrow at this n.
  9. Two proportions. Variant A converts 120 of 2 000; variant B, 156 of 2 000. Is B better, and by how much?
    Answer. p̂_A = 6.00%, p̂_B = 7.80%, difference 1.80 points. SE = √(0.06×0.94/2000 + 0.078×0.922/2000) = 0.008010, m = 0.0157, interval (0.23, 3.37) points. Statistically clear; practically ambiguous. If the change is only worth shipping above a 2-point lift, this experiment has not settled it, and the correct next step is more data, not a decision.
  10. A misread interval. A study reports "mean recovery time 12.4 days, 95% CI (11.8, 13.0)". A colleague concludes patients typically recover in 12 to 13 days. What is wrong?
    Answer. That is a confidence interval for the mean, not a range for a patient. If n = 200, then s ≈ 0.3×√200 ≈ 4.3 days, so a 95% prediction interval for one patient is roughly 12.4 ± 1.97×4.3×√(1.005) = (3.9, 20.9) days — about fourteen times wider. §12.
