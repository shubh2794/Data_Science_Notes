06 From a point to an interval: the pivot

A point estimate answers "what is your best guess?" and refuses to answer "how sure are you?". An interval answers both. The construction is one algebraic move — take a quantity whose distribution you know, bracket it, and rearrange — and the move is worth doing slowly, because everything that goes wrong later goes wrong in the rearranging.

The pivot: the one construction behind every interval in this series

A pivot is a function of the data and the parameter whose distribution does not depend on the parameter. Find one, and an interval falls out mechanically.

step 1 — a pivot. For a mean with σ known, the CLT (part 3, §06) supplies oneZ = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ≈ Normal(0, 1) — its distribution mentions neither μ nor σ step 2 — bracket the pivot with the quantiles of its own known distributionP( −z* < Z < z* ) = 1 − α z* = Φ⁻¹(1 − α/2) step 3 — rearrange the inequality to isolate μ. Pure algebra; no new idea enters−z* < (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) < z* −z*·σ/√n < x̄ − μ < z*·σ/√n x̄ − z*·σ/√n < μ < x̄ + z*·σ/√n step 4 — the intervalCₙ = x̄ ± z*·σ/√n the z INTERVAL for a mean, σ known
Watch step 3 closely, because this is where the misinterpretation is born. Before the rearrangement, the random thing is x̄ and the statement is "the estimator lands near the parameter". After the rearrangement, the same statement is written with μ in the middle — but μ did not become random by being moved. The randomness stayed with x̄, which is now sitting in both endpoints. "If I am near Paris, then Paris is near me" is a true and useful rephrasing; it does not make Paris move.

  • Confidence and width are the trade. Going from 95% to 99% costs 31% more width; going down to 80% saves 35%. A 100% interval is (−∞, ∞) and is perfectly honest and completely useless. The level is a decision about how often you are willing to be wrong, and it should be made before the data are seen — choosing it afterwards silently destroys the guarantee.
  • 95% is convention, not mathematics. Nothing in the theory prefers it. Particle physics uses roughly 5σ (a level around 1 − 3×10⁻⁷); many engineering standards use 90%. Use whatever matches the cost of being wrong, and say which you used.
  • Splitting α evenly between the tails is also a choice. It gives the shortest interval when the pivot's distribution is symmetric and unimodal — which is why everyone does it — but any split with α₁ + α₂ = α is a valid interval, and §11 uses a completely lopsided split to build one-sided intervals.

A first concrete interval

Twenty-three standardised laboratory measurements of the heat content of a coal shipment, in megajoules per kilogram, have mean 23.788. The measurement procedure is documented to have σ = 0.100.

the arithmetic, in fullSE = σ/√n = 0.100/√23 = 0.020851 m = 1.95996 × 0.020851 = 0.040866 CI = 23.788 ± 0.0409 = (23.747, 23.829) MJ/kg the same data at three levels, to see the price of confidence90%: (23.754, 23.822) width 0.0686 95%: (23.747, 23.829) width 0.0817 99%: (23.734, 23.842) width 0.1074

Three things were needed and only one came from the data: the estimate. The standard error came from an external calibration, the critical value from the normal table, and the level from a convention. §08 removes the external calibration — which is where the t distribution enters — and §09 handles the case where the estimate is a proportion, where the standard error is a function of the very thing being estimated.

The duality with testing, stated now and used in part 5. A two-sided test of H₀: θ = θ₀ at level α rejects exactly when θ₀ falls outside the 1 − α interval. They are the same computation read in two directions, and the interval is the more informative direction: it reports which values are compatible with the data, not merely a verdict on one of them.

07 What "95% confident" claims — and the four readings that are wrong

This is the most important section on the page and the one most often skimmed. The number 95 is a property of the procedure, measured across the samples you did not draw. It is not a probability about θ, it is not the fraction of the data inside the interval, it is not the chance a future estimate lands inside, and it is not a statement that values in the middle are more likely than values at the edge. Every one of those misreadings appears in published work.

The correct statement, in three equivalent forms

form 1 · the procedure
"The recipe works 95% of the time"
If you drew a fresh sample and rebuilt the interval, over and over, 95% of those intervals would contain the fixed θ. The guarantee attaches to the method, before the data arrive.
form 2 · your career
"95% of my statements will be true"
Build one 95% interval a day, each about a different and unrelated parameter. Over a lifetime, about 95% of those claims are correct. No repetition of the same experiment is needed — which is what makes the guarantee usable at all.
form 3 · compatibility
"These values are compatible with the data"
The interval is exactly the set of θ₀ that a two-sided test at level α would not reject. Reading it as a compatibility range is descriptive, accurate, and hard to abuse.

Form 2 deserves emphasis because form 1 has a fatal practical weakness: you will never repeat this experiment. An interpretation that only applies to an infinite sequence of identical replications is technically correct and operationally empty. Form 2 fixes it. The intervals in the sequence do not have to be about the same quantity, or use the same method, or come from the same field — a 95% procedure applied to a stream of unrelated questions is right about 95% of the time. That is the promise you are buying.

The picture: many intervals, one fixed truth the vertical line never moves — the intervals do

The four wrong readings, each answered

✗ "There is a 95% probability that μ lies between 23.747 and 23.829."
Both endpoints are numbers and μ is a number. Either μ is between them or it is not; the statement is true or false and no probability is involved. The probability lived in the sampling, and the sampling is over. ✓ Say instead: "the procedure that produced this interval captures μ 95% of the time", or, if you genuinely want a probability statement about μ, use a Bayesian credible interval and say so (§12).

The sharpest demonstration. Let θ be unknown and observe Y₁, Y₂ where each Yᵢ = θ ± 1 with equal probability, independently. Define the set C to be the single point (Y₁ + Y₂)/2 when Y₁ ≠ Y₂, and the single point Y₁ − 1 when Y₁ = Y₂. Check the four equally likely cases: C contains θ in three of them, so P(θ ∈ C) = 3/4 for every θ — a bona fide 75% confidence set. Now suppose you observe Y₁ = 15, Y₂ = 17. Then C = {16}, and you know with certainty that θ = 16, because the two observations differed. A "75% confidence" statement that is, conditional on the data you actually saw, 100% certain. The confidence level is a property of the procedure averaged over data; it is not a description of the case in front of you.
✗ "95% of the data fall inside the interval."
No. A confidence interval for a mean has width of order σ/√n and shrinks towards a point as n grows; the data have spread σ and do not shrink at all. At n = 400 the 95% interval for the mean is twenty times narrower than the interval containing 95% of the observations. This is the ①/③ confusion of part 3, §02, and the interval that does contain 95% of the data is a tolerance interval (§12).
✗ "A replication has a 95% chance of landing inside this interval."
It does not, and the true figure is much lower. If you build a 95% interval for μ and then run an independent replication, the probability that the replication's estimate falls inside your original interval is about 83%, not 95% — because two independent estimates each contribute error, so the relevant spread is √2 times larger. The quantity that captures a future observation is a prediction interval (§12), and it is far wider.
✗ "Values near the centre are much more likely than values near the edge — and values just outside are ruled out."
A confidence interval is a set, not a density. The frequentist framework gives it no internal shape. And the boundary is not a cliff: a parameter value one hair outside a 95% interval is only marginally less compatible with the data than one just inside. Treating the endpoint as a decision threshold reproduces every pathology of the 0.05 cut-off in part 5. If you want a graded statement of plausibility across values, that is a likelihood function (§05) or a posterior (§12), not an interval.

Two further traps, both of which void the guarantee

  • Choosing the level, the method, or the endpoint after seeing the data. The 95% is computed over the randomness of the sample under a procedure fixed in advance. If the procedure is allowed to depend on the sample — try 95%, and if that excludes zero, report it; otherwise report 90% — the actual coverage is anybody's guess and it is not 95%. This is the interval-shaped version of the multiplicity problem in part 9.
  • Reading non-overlap as a significant difference. Two 95% intervals that do not overlap do imply a significant difference, but the converse fails badly: intervals can overlap substantially while the difference is significant. The correct object is a single interval for the difference, built from the standard error of the difference (§11). Two independent 95% intervals that just barely touch correspond to a comparison at roughly the 0.5% level — far more conservative than intended.
The one-line version, worth memorising. The confidence is in the recipe, not in the number. Before you look at the data, "this interval will contain μ" has probability 0.95. After you look, it has truth value 0 or 1, and you do not know which. Everything that goes wrong with confidence intervals in practice is a failure to keep those two moments apart.

08 When σ is unknown: the t distribution and its degrees of freedom

The z interval of §06 needed σ, and you never have it. Substituting the sample standard deviation s looks harmless and is not: the pivot stops being normal, because the denominator has become random too. The correct reference distribution is Student's t, the correction is exactly computable, and the size of the correction — negligible at n = 100, enormous at n = 3 — tells you when it is safe to ignore.

Why substituting s changes the distribution

the pivot you want, and the one you can actually computeZ = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ~ Normal(0,1) T = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) ~ ? the difference: TWO random quantities, not oneZ has a fixed denominator. T has a random denominator that is itself an estimate. the exact decomposition, for a NORMAL populationT = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ÷ √( [(n−1)s²/σ²] / (n−1) ) = Z / √(V/ν) with Z ~ Normal(0,1), V = (n−1)s²/σ² ~ χ²ν, ν = n − 1, and Z ⊥ V THAT ratio is the definition of Student's tT ~ t(ν) ν = n − 1 degrees of freedom
  • Where the extra spread comes from. Half the time s underestimates σ, which inflates T; half the time it overestimates, which deflates it. But inflation and deflation are not symmetric in their effect — dividing by a number that is too small blows the ratio up without limit, while dividing by one that is too large can only shrink it towards zero. The result is a distribution with the same centre, heavier tails, and variance ν/(ν − 2) > 1.
  • The independence of x̄ and s² is special to the normal. It is what makes the t result exact rather than approximate, and it is the reason the normal assumption is stated for the t interval even though the CLT is doing the work at large n.
  • The cost of pretending otherwise is measurable. For normal data with n = 5, the interval x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n — the z critical value with an estimated σ — has actual coverage 87.8%, not 95%. At n = 10 it is 91.8%; at n = 30, 94.0%; at n = 100, 94.7%. Those numbers are exact, computed as P(|T_{n−1}| < 1.95996).

Degrees of freedom, explained rather than asserted

Three explanations of the same fact, in increasing order of usefulness.

  • Counting constraints. The n deviations xᵢ − x̄ are not free: they sum to zero by construction, because x̄ was computed from the same data. Fix any n − 1 of them and the last is determined. So n numbers carry only n − 1 independent pieces of information about spread, and dividing the sum of squares by n − 1 is dividing by the number of independent pieces (part 3, §03 proves the unbiasedness this delivers).
  • Geometry. The vector of deviations lives in the (n − 1)-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the all-ones direction — the residual after projecting the data onto that direction. The squared length of a standard normal vector in a d-dimensional subspace is χ²_d. One parameter estimated from the data costs one dimension; that is all "degrees of freedom" ever means, and it is why fitting k regression coefficients leaves n − k (part 6).
  • Information. Each degree of freedom is one independent observation's worth of evidence about σ. With ν = 1 you know almost nothing about the spread, so the interval must be enormous; with ν = 100 you know σ almost exactly, and t* is within 1% of z*. The whole t correction is the price of not knowing σ, and it decays like 1/ν.

A worked t interval, and a comparison worth staring at

Twenty-two measurements of heat content on a second coal shipment, where the calibration is not trusted: x̄ = 31.012, s = 0.1294, n = 22, so ν = 21 and t* = 2.0796.

the arithmeticSE = s/√n = 0.1294/√22 = 0.027589 m = 2.0796 × 0.027589 = 0.057378 CI = 31.012 ± 0.0574 = (30.954, 31.069) MJ/kg against §06's interval on the FIRST shipment, with almost the same nshipment 1: n = 23, σ known = 0.100, z* = 1.960 → width 0.0817 shipment 2: n = 22, s = 0.1294, t* = 2.0796 → width 0.1148 +40%

Two separate effects and it is worth separating them. The estimated spread is larger than the calibrated one (0.1294 against 0.100), which accounts for a factor of 1.294. Not knowing σ costs a further factor of 1.061 through the critical value. Together, 1.373 — and the observed ratio of widths is 0.1148/0.0817 = 1.404, the small remainder being the different n. Knowing your measurement error in advance is worth real money.

  • Symmetry, not normality, is what the t interval really needs. The uniform population is nothing like a bell and the interval is fine at n = 10. The exponential is unimodal and smooth and still 5 points short at n = 10. Skewness is the variable that matters — exactly the conclusion of part 3, §07, and the reason that page recommends computing 44γ₁² instead of chanting "n ≥ 30".
  • The Bernoulli row is a catastrophe and it is the most common case in practice. A rare 0/1 event at n = 10: with probability 0.95¹⁰ = 0.599 the sample is all zeros, so x̄ = 0, s = 0, and the interval is the single point 0, which misses p = 0.05 with certainty. Nominal 95%, actual 40%. Never put a 0/1 variable through the t interval; §09 exists for this.
  • Skewness makes the interval mis-centred, not merely narrow. The two tails fail at different rates: for a right-skewed population the interval sits too low too often, so the misses pile up on one side. A symmetric interval is the wrong shape for an asymmetric sampling distribution, which is one of the arguments for the bootstrap of §11.
