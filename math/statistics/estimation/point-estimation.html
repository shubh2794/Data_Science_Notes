02 Bias, variance, MSE — and the identity that links them

Three definitions, one three-line proof, and a set of consequences that quietly govern everything from the n − 1 in the sample variance to why ridge regression works. This identity is the same one machine learning calls the bias–variance decomposition; here it appears in its original form, about an estimator rather than a predictor.

The definitions

bias — a property of the estimator, measured in the units of θbias(θ̂ₙ) = E[θ̂ₙ] − θ unbiased ⟺ E[θ̂ₙ] = θ for every θ ∈ Θ variance and its square root, the standard errorVar(θ̂ₙ) = E[(θ̂ₙ − E[θ̂ₙ])²] SE(θ̂ₙ) = √Var(θ̂ₙ) mean squared error — distance from the TARGET, not from the estimator's own averageMSE(θ̂ₙ) = E[(θ̂ₙ − θ)²]

The decomposition, proved

Write θ̄ = E[θ̂ₙ] for the estimator's own mean, then add and subtract it inside the square:

step 1 — insert a zeroE[(θ̂ₙ − θ)²] = E[( (θ̂ₙ − θ̄) + (θ̄ − θ) )²] step 2 — expand the square into three terms= E[(θ̂ₙ − θ̄)²] + 2(θ̄ − θ)·E[θ̂ₙ − θ̄] + (θ̄ − θ)² step 3 — the cross term dies, because E[θ̂ₙ − θ̄] = 0 by definition of θ̄= Var(θ̂ₙ) + bias²(θ̂ₙ) THE IDENTITYMSE = variance + bias² — exact, no approximation, no assumptions
  • The two terms are in the same units (squared units of θ), which is what makes them tradeable. A rule that pays one unit of squared bias to save two units of variance is a better rule, and the fact that it is now "wrong on average" is irrelevant to its accuracy.
  • Nothing here is asymptotic. The identity holds at n = 3 as exactly as at n = 3 000 000. Only the individual terms depend on n.
  • The cross term dying is the whole trick, and it is the same trick that makes least squares work, that makes Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y for uncorrelated variables, and that gives the Pythagorean flavour to orthogonal projection. Bias is the component along the "error" direction; variance is the component orthogonal to it.

Consistency: the minimum a rule must clear

consistency — convergence in probability to the truthθ̂ₙ →ᵖ θ i.e. for every ε > 0, P(|θ̂ₙ − θ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞ a sufficient condition that is trivial to checkif bias(θ̂ₙ) → 0 and Var(θ̂ₙ) → 0 then MSE → 0 then θ̂ₙ →ᵖ θ why: Chebyshev, exactly as in part 3 §04P(|θ̂ₙ − θ| > ε) ≤ MSE(θ̂ₙ)/ε² → 0
  • Consistency is a floor, not a recommendation. An estimator that is not consistent is broken: more data does not help it. But consistency says nothing about behaviour at the n you actually have. The estimator θ̂ₙ = x̄ + 10⁶/n is consistent and useless.
  • Unbiased does not imply consistent. "Use X₁ and throw the rest away" is exactly unbiased for μ at every n, and its variance is σ² forever. It never converges to anything.
  • Consistent does not imply unbiased. The maximum-likelihood estimator of a normal variance, σ̂² = (1/n)∑(xᵢ − x̄)², is biased at every finite n and consistent.
  • Neither implies the other, and MSE implies both. That is the practical reason to work in MSE and treat unbiasedness as an occasionally convenient special case.

A worked case: three estimators of a variance

The classic and most useful example, because you can compute every term exactly. Take X₁, …, Xₙ normal with variance σ², write SS = ∑(xᵢ − x̄)², and consider the whole family T_c = c·SS. The chi-square fact SS/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁ gives the two moments you need:

It is closer to the truth, on average, at every sample size — and it is wrong on average, by construction. Monte-Carlo confirms the algebra: at n = 5 over 400 000 normal samples the measured MSEs are 0.4962, 0.3581 and 0.3324 against theoretical 0.5000, 0.3600 and 0.3333.

So why does everyone use n − 1? Three honest reasons and one bad one. (1) Unbiasedness composes: sums and differences of unbiased things stay unbiased, which keeps ANOVA and regression tables coherent. (2) The n − 1 version is the one whose ratio to σ² is exactly χ²ₙ₋₁/(n−1), which is what makes the t interval of §08 exact. (3) The optimal divisor n + 1 is optimal only under normality and only for squared-error loss on σ² — it is not optimal for σ, and it is not optimal for other distributions. The bad reason is that people believe unbiasedness is the point. It is not; §03 is about that.

03 When a biased estimator beats an unbiased one

This is the most useful idea on the page for anyone who trains models, and it is stated backwards in most first courses. Unbiasedness is not a goal. It is a constraint, and like every constraint it costs you something — here, variance. Deliberately accepting a little bias to buy a lot of variance reduction is not a compromise or a hack. It is optimal, it is provable, and it is exactly what regularisation does.

The general shape of the argument: shrinkage

Take any unbiased estimator θ̂ with variance v, and shrink it towards a fixed point (take that point to be 0, without loss of generality — shrink towards anything by subtracting first). The shrunk estimator is θ̂_λ = λ·θ̂ with λ ∈ [0, 1].

bias and variance of the shrunk estimator, both elementaryE[θ̂_λ] = λθ ⟹ bias = (λ − 1)θ Var(θ̂_λ) = λ²v so the MSE is a parabola in λMSE(λ) = (λ − 1)²θ² + λ²v differentiate and set to zero2(λ − 1)θ² + 2λv = 0 ⟹ λ* = θ²/(θ² + v) < 1 ALWAYS and the MSE at the optimumMSE(λ*) = θ²v/(θ² + v) = v · θ²/(θ² + v) < v = MSE(1)

Look at what just happened. λ* < 1 whenever the variance is positive, which is always. The derivative of MSE at λ = 1 is 2v > 0, so moving even slightly away from the unbiased estimator strictly decreases the mean squared error. The unbiased estimator is never the MSE optimum in this family. Not sometimes — never.

the honest catch, stated plainlyλ* = θ²/(θ² + v) depends on θ, which is the thing you do not know but the direction is knowable even when the amount is notfor every λ in ( (θ² − v)/(θ² + v) , 1 ) the shrunk estimator beats the unbiased one the gain is largest exactly where estimation is hardestθ² ≫ v (strong signal) → λ* ≈ 1, shrinkage barely helps θ² ≪ v (weak signal) → λ* ≈ 0, shrinkage helps enormously

Two more places bias earns its keep

  • Estimating an exponential rate. Take X₁, …, Xₙ exponential with rate λ. The maximum-likelihood estimator is λ̂ = n/∑xᵢ, and it is biased upward: E[λ̂] = nλ/(n − 1). Working out the whole family a/∑xᵢ gives MSE(a) = λ²[a²/((n−1)(n−2)) − 2a/(n−1) + 1], minimised at a = n − 2. At n = 12: the MLE has MSE 0.1273λ², the unbiased choice a = n − 1 has 0.1000λ², and the biased-low optimum a = n − 2 has 0.0909λ². Simulation over 300 000 samples: 0.1274, 0.1001, 0.0910. Notice that here the unbiased rule beats the MLE but loses to a differently biased rule — the moral is not "bias good", it is "optimise the thing you care about".
  • The bias–variance decomposition of prediction error. The same identity, applied pointwise to a fitted function rather than to a scalar, is the decomposition every ML course opens with: E[(y − f̂(x))²] = bias²(f̂(x)) + Var(f̂(x)) + σ², where the last term is irreducible noise. It is the identity of §02 plus one extra term, and it lives in full at bias–variance trade-off.
The transferable rule. When someone objects that an estimator is biased, the correct reply is a question: biased by how much, and what did the bias buy? An estimator with 5% bias and a third of the variance is a better estimator by any accuracy criterion that matters. Unbiasedness is worth insisting on in exactly two situations: when many estimates will be summed or differenced (errors then cancel only if they are centred), and when the estimator feeds a downstream exactness result — such as the χ² fact that makes the t interval in §08 exact rather than approximate.

04 Efficiency, Fisher information, and how good an estimator can possibly be

§03 said you can trade bias for variance. The natural next question is whether there is a floor — a variance below which no honest estimator can go. There is, it is called the Cramér–Rao lower bound, and the quantity that sets it, Fisher information, has a beautifully concrete meaning: the average sharpness of the peak of the log-likelihood. A sharp peak means the data strongly prefer one value of the parameter; a flat peak means they barely care.

Fisher information: two formulas, one meaning

definition — the variance of the scoreI(θ) = Var(s(X;θ)) = E[ s(X;θ)² ] (the second equality because E[s] = 0) the equivalent curvature form, usually far easier to computeI(θ) = −E[ ∂² log f(X;θ) / ∂θ² ] the EXPECTED CURVATURE of the log-density information is ADDITIVE over independent observationsIₙ(θ) = n·I(θ) — n independent looks carry n times the information the OBSERVED information: the same curvature, evaluated on YOUR data, no expectationJ(θ̂) = −ℓ″(θ̂) where ℓ(θ) = ∑ log f(xᵢ;θ)
  • Read it as curvature. The log-likelihood is a bump whose peak is the estimate. A tall, narrow, sharply curved bump means the data would have been very unlikely under any nearby parameter value — a lot of information. A wide, flat bump means many values explain the data almost equally well — little information. The second derivative measures exactly that (Taylor, second order).
  • Additivity is why standard errors shrink like 1/√n. Information adds, variance goes like one over information, so variance goes like 1/n and SE like 1/√n. The 1/√n law of part 3, §09 is a corollary of a more general fact.
  • Observed vs expected information. The expected version I(θ) averages over data you did not see; the observed version J(θ̂) uses only your sample. Both are valid for standard errors; the observed one is usually preferred in practice because it reflects the actual shape of the likelihood you have, and it is what numerical optimisers hand you for free as the Hessian (Hessian).

The Cramér–Rao lower bound, stated and proved

the statement, for an estimator T with E[T] = g(θ)Var(T) ≥ [g′(θ)]² / Iₙ(θ) the case everyone quotes: T unbiased for θ, so g(θ) = θ and g′ = 1Var(T) ≥ 1/Iₙ(θ) = 1/(n·I(θ)) proof, in three lines. Let S = ∑ s(Xᵢ;θ) be the total score, so E[S] = 0, Var(S) = Iₙ(θ)g′(θ) = ∂/∂θ ∫ T(x)·f(x;θ)dx = ∫ T(x)·s(x;θ)·f(x;θ)dx = E[T·S] = Cov(T, S) Cauchy–Schwarz: Cov(T, S)² ≤ Var(T)·Var(S) ⟹ [g′(θ)]² ≤ Var(T)·Iₙ(θ) ⟹ Var(T) ≥ [g′(θ)]²/Iₙ(θ) ∎ efficiency of an unbiased estimatore(T) = [1/Iₙ(θ)] / Var(T) ∈ (0, 1] e(T) = 1 ⟺ T is EFFICIENT
  • Equality holds only in a very restricted case. Cauchy–Schwarz is tight only when T − θ is a deterministic multiple of the score, which forces the model into the exponential family with T as its natural sufficient statistic. That is why the bound is attained for Bernoulli, Poisson and the normal mean, and not attained for the exponential rate — same model, different parametrisation, different answer.
  • The bound is about unbiased estimators. Nothing stops a biased estimator from having smaller variance and smaller MSE than 1/Iₙ(θ). The shrinkage estimator of §03 does exactly that. The bound is a statement about the price of insisting on unbiasedness, which is another way of saying §03's point.
  • The regularity conditions are not decoration. They fail whenever the support depends on the parameter, and the Uniform(0, θ) case is the standard demonstration: the MLE X₍ₙ₎ has MSE 2θ²/((n+1)(n+2)), which is of order 1/n² and beats anything of order 1/n for large n.
  • What the bound is good for in practice: a sanity check. If a proposed estimator has variance far above 1/Iₙ(θ), there is room to do better. If someone claims variance far below it and the model is regular and the estimator unbiased, they have made an arithmetic error.

Sufficiency, in outline

a statistic T is SUFFICIENT for θ if the data carry no further information about θ once T is knownthe conditional distribution of X₁,…,Xₙ given T = t does not depend on θ the factorisation criterion — the practical testT is sufficient ⟺ f(x₁,…,xₙ; θ) = h(x₁,…,xₙ) · g(T(x); θ) Rao–Blackwell: conditioning on a sufficient statistic can only helpT′ = E[T | S] has E[T′] = E[T] and Var(T′) ≤ Var(T)
  • The examples are the ones you already use. For Bernoulli, ∑xᵢ is sufficient: once you know 37 successes out of 100, the order in which they arrived is irrelevant to p. For Normal with both parameters unknown, the pair (∑xᵢ, ∑xᵢ²) is sufficient. For Uniform(0, θ), the maximum is.
  • Why it matters here. Rao–Blackwell says any estimator can be improved (weakly) by averaging it over the sufficient statistic, so the search for good estimators can be confined to functions of sufficient statistics. It is also the reason the MLE is almost always a function of one — the likelihood only ever touches the data through g(T(x); θ).
  • The compression reading. A sufficient statistic is a lossless summary with respect to a particular model. Change the model and the summary stops being sufficient — which is exactly why the sample mean is a terrible summary of a bimodal population, and why part 1 insists on plotting.
The chain to hold on to. Log-likelihood curvature → Fisher information → the lower bound on variance → the standard error of the maximum-likelihood estimator → the width of the confidence interval in §06. Every confidence interval built from a likelihood is, ultimately, a statement about how sharply peaked that likelihood was.

05 Three ways to build an estimator, and one worked to the end

So far the estimators have arrived by intuition. There are three systematic recipes: plug-in (make no assumptions), method of moments (match a few averages), and maximum likelihood (ask which parameter makes the data least surprising). The third dominates modern practice — it is the recipe behind logistic regression, behind the cross-entropy loss, and behind almost every model that reports a standard error.

Recipe 1 — the plug-in principle

if the target is a functional of the distribution, apply the functional to the EMPIRICAL distributionθ = T(F) ⟹ θ̂ = T(F̂ₙ) where F̂ₙ puts mass 1/n on each observed point what it gives, mechanicallyμ = ∫x dF → x̄ σ² = ∫(x−μ)²dF → (1/n)∑(xᵢ−x̄)² median → sample median F(t) → F̂ₙ(t) quantiles → sample quantiles ρ → r
  • It assumes nothing about the shape of F, which is its strength: it is the nonparametric default, and it is consistent for any functional that is continuous in the right sense.
  • It is often slightly biased, precisely because F̂ₙ is more concentrated than F: the plug-in variance uses divisor n, not n − 1. §02 already priced that.
  • Standard errors are the hard part. There is no general formula for the SE of a plug-in estimator of an arbitrary functional — which is exactly the gap the bootstrap fills, by resampling from F̂ₙ. That is part 7, and §11 previews it.

Recipe 2 — the method of moments

with k parameters, match the first k theoretical moments to the first k sample momentsαⱼ(θ) = Eθ[Xʲ] α̂ⱼ = (1/n)∑ xᵢʲ solve αⱼ(θ̂) = α̂ⱼ for j = 1…k Normal(μ, σ²): two equations, two unknownsμ̂ = x̄ σ̂² + μ̂² = (1/n)∑xᵢ² ⟹ σ̂² = (1/n)∑(xᵢ − x̄)² Gamma(α, β) with mean αβ and variance αβ²: solve the pairβ̂ = σ̂²/x̄ α̂ = x̄²/σ̂² — closed form, where the MLE needs iteration Uniform(0, θ): mean is θ/2θ̂ = 2x̄ Var(θ̂) = θ²/(3n) — order 1/n, against the MLE's order 1/n²
  • Cheap and usually consistent, asymptotically normal under mild conditions, and often available in closed form when the likelihood is not. It remains the standard way to get a starting value for a numerical likelihood maximisation.
  • Rarely efficient. It uses only the first few moments and throws away the rest of the sample's structure. The Uniform line above is the extreme case: the method-of-moments estimator can even produce an impossible answer — if the sample happens to contain a value above 2x̄, then θ̂ = 2x̄ is smaller than an observation drawn from Uniform(0, θ), which is a contradiction.
  • It needs the moments to exist. For a Cauchy population there is no first moment, and the recipe has nothing to match.

The load-bearing caveat, repeated because it is always forgotten. Every one of those four properties is conditional on the assumed model containing the truth. If f(x; θ) is the wrong family, the MLE still converges — to the member of the family closest in Kullback–Leibler divergence to the real distribution — and its nominal standard error is then a confident measurement of the wrong thing. Optimality inside a wrong model is worth nothing.

The likelihood machine: hunt the maximum, watch the fit drag the handle on the log-likelihood curve

When the calculus fails, and what to do

  • The maximum is on the boundary. For Uniform(0, θ) the likelihood is θ⁻ⁿ for θ ≥ x₍ₙ₎ and exactly 0 below it — a cliff followed by a decreasing curve. There is no stationary point; the maximum is at the cliff edge, θ̂ = x₍ₙ₎ = max xᵢ. Setting a derivative to zero would have found nothing. Always look at the shape.
  • There is no closed form. The Gamma shape parameter, logistic regression coefficients, and essentially every model with more than two parameters need numerical maximisation — Newton, or the quasi-Newton methods of the optimisation notes. The Hessian at the optimum is the observed information, so the optimiser hands you the standard errors as a by-product.
  • The likelihood is unbounded. Fit a two-component normal mixture and let one component's variance go to zero on a single data point: the likelihood diverges. The global maximum is a degenerate junk solution and the useful answer is a local maximum. This is not a pathology of exotic models; it is why mixture fitting needs constraints or penalties.
  • The log-likelihood is the loss function you already use. Minimising cross-entropy is maximising a Bernoulli or categorical log-likelihood; minimising squared error is maximising a Gaussian one; adding an L2 penalty is multiplying by a normal prior. See logistic regression.
