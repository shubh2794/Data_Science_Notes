09 Intervals for a proportion, and the interval that does not work

A proportion is the most common parameter in applied work — a click rate, a conversion, a defect rate, a model's accuracy. It is also the case where the textbook interval is genuinely broken: the Wald interval, the one taught first and used everywhere, can have 63% coverage when it claims 95%, and its coverage oscillates wildly as p moves. This section shows the failure, explains it, and gives three intervals that work.

The Wald interval, and why it looks fine

the obvious construction: plug p̂ into the standard error and use the CLTp̂ = X/n ŜE = √( p̂(1 − p̂)/n ) CI = p̂ ± z*·√( p̂(1 − p̂)/n ) the conditions everyone quotesnp̂ ≥ 10 and n(1 − p̂) ≥ 10 (some texts say 5) worked: 58 of 1 000 sampled voters approvep̂ = 0.580 ŜE = √(0.58×0.42/1000) = 0.015612 CI = 0.580 ± 0.0306 = (54.9%, 61.1%)

Nothing about that looks suspicious, and at p = 0.58 with n = 1 000 it is in fact fine. The trouble starts as soon as p moves away from ½ or n gets small — and the trouble is much worse than "slightly too narrow".

  • Averaged over p in the sane range [0.05, 0.95], Wald coverage is 89.9% at n = 20, 92.6% at n = 40, 94.0% at n = 100, 94.8% at n = 400. Wilson is 95.4%, 95.2%, 95.1%, 95.0% at the same sample sizes. Wilson is right; Wald is systematically short.
  • The oscillation is the strangest part. At n = 100, Wald coverage is 95.2% at p = 0.106 and 91.1% at p = 0.107. A change in the fourth decimal place of a parameter you do not know moves your actual coverage by four points. Coverage is not a smooth function of p, because it is a sum over a lattice: as p creeps upward, one value of k abruptly stops covering and its whole binomial probability drops out of the sum.

Why it fails — three separate defects, not one

  1. The standard error is estimated at the wrong place. The Wald interval uses √(p̂(1−p̂)/n), but the true standard error is √(p(1−p)/n). When p̂ lands below p (and p < ½), the estimated SE is also too small, so the interval is both mis-centred and too short at the same time — the two errors compound instead of cancelling.
  2. It is degenerate at the boundary. If k = 0, then p̂ = 0 and ŜE = 0, so the interval is the single point [0, 0] and covers nothing. Observing zero successes is not rare: at n = 100 and p = 0.01 it happens 36.6% of the time. That single case alone caps coverage at 63.4%, which is essentially the 63.3% in the table.
  3. It ignores the skewness of the binomial. For p away from ½ the sampling distribution of p̂ is skewed, so a symmetric interval is the wrong shape, and it can extend past 0 or 1 — an interval containing impossible values.

Three intervals that work

Wilson (score) interval — the default. Do not plug p̂ into the standard error; instead solve for the values of p that the test would not reject, using the true standard error at each candidate p:

start from the correct pivot and solve for p instead of approximating it| p̂ − p | / √( p(1 − p)/n ) = z* ⟹ (p̂ − p)² = z*²·p(1 − p)/n a quadratic in pp²(n + z*²) − p(2np̂ + z*²) + np̂² = 0 whose two roots are the endpointscentre = (k + z*²/2)/(n + z*²) half = z*/(n + z*²) · √( k(n − k)/n + z*²/4 ) with k = np̂ the equivalent, more memorable formCI = [ p̂ + z*²/(2n) ± z*√( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z*²/(4n²) ) ] / (1 + z*²/n)
  • It never leaves [0, 1], it is never degenerate — at k = 0 and n = 50 it gives (0, 0.0713), which is informative rather than empty — and it is asymmetric, pulled towards ½ exactly as the skewness of the binomial demands.
  • Read the centre as a shrunk estimate. (k + z*²/2)/(n + z*²) is p̂ pulled towards ½ by an amount that vanishes as n grows. Section 03's shrinkage, arriving here without being invited.
  • It is the interval you get by inverting the score test, which is why it is sometimes called the score interval, and why the χ² tests of part 8 agree with it rather than with Wald.

Agresti–Coull — the version you can do in your head. Add two successes and two failures, then run the Wald formula:

the "add two and two" interval, at the 95% level where z*² = 3.84 ≈ 4ñ = n + z*² p̃ = (k + z*²/2)/ñ CI = p̃ ± z*·√( p̃(1 − p̃)/ñ ) at 95%, in wordspretend you saw k + 2 successes out of n + 4 trials, then do the naive thing
  • Same centre as Wilson, slightly wider, and therefore slightly conservative — which is the right direction to be wrong. It is the easiest correct interval to remember, and there is no excuse for teaching Wald instead.
  • The mean coverage at n = 20 over p ∈ [0.05, 0.95] is 96.0%, against Wilson's 95.4% and Wald's 89.9%.

Clopper–Pearson — the exact interval, and its price. Invert the exact binomial test rather than any normal approximation:

the endpoints are beta quantiles (equivalently, roots of the exact binomial tail equations)lower = Beta⁻¹(α/2; k, n − k + 1) (0 when k = 0) upper = Beta⁻¹(1 − α/2; k + 1, n − k) (1 when k = n) the guarantee it buys and the price it chargescoverage ≥ 1 − α for EVERY p and every n — never under-covers but typically covers 97–99% when you asked for 95%: wider than necessary the special case worth memorising: no successes in n trialsone-sided 95% upper bound = 1 − 0.05^(1/n) ≈ 3/n "the rule of three"

The rule of three is the answer to "we tested 300 units and saw no failures — what is the failure rate?" It is not zero; a 95% upper bound is about 3/300 = 1%. Exactly: 1 − 0.05^(1/300) = 0.00994.

The general lesson, which is not about proportions. Whenever the standard error depends on the parameter, plugging the estimate into the standard error and building a symmetric interval is the wrong move. Solving for the parameter values consistent with the data — inverting a test — is the right one. The same distinction reappears for a variance, for a Poisson rate, for an odds ratio, and for every model whose likelihood is not quadratic. Wald is the lazy approximation; the score or likelihood-ratio interval is the honest one.

10 Margin of error and planning the sample size

Run the interval formula backwards and it becomes a budget. "How much data do I need?" has an exact answer as soon as you commit to three numbers: the confidence level, the precision you want, and a guess at the variability. The third is the awkward one — and for a proportion, there is a trick that removes it entirely.

The margin of error, and solving for n

  • 1 068 is the number behind every "±3 points" you have ever read, and it does not depend on the size of the population — a thousand people give the same margin in a country of 300 million as in a town of 3 000 (part 3, §09).
  • The conservative choice costs surprisingly little. Planning for p = ½ when the truth is 0.30 over-samples by only 19%. Planning for ½ when the truth is 0.02 over-samples by a factor of 12.7 — so for genuinely rare events, get a pilot estimate. The function p(1−p) is flat near its maximum: it is 0.25 at p = 0.5 and still 0.21 at p = 0.3.
  • Always round up, and always add for non-response. If you expect a 60% response rate and need 1 068 completed responses, you must contact ceil(1068/0.6) = 1 780 people — and the non-responders are not a random subset, which is the non-sampling bias the margin of error says nothing about.
  • The margin of error is only the sampling gap. It is silent on coverage error, non-response, question wording, and measurement error. In a real survey those are usually larger than the ±3 points printed next to the result.

The quadratic price, restated as a budget line

precision is bought at a quadratic price — the exchange ratehalve the margin → 4× the data divide by 3 → 9× divide by 10 → 100× a 10% improvement in marginn → n/0.81 = 1.235n +23.5% more data for a 10% tighter interval the other lever: buy precision by giving up confidence95% → 90%: margin × 0.839, so n × 0.704 for the SAME margin 95% → 80%: margin × 0.654, so n × 0.428

Two levers, and they are not equally priced. Dropping from 95% to 90% confidence buys a 16% narrower interval for free — or lets you cut the sample by 30%. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on the cost of being wrong, and is a decision, not a calculation.

Planning for a mean: the awkward σ

  • A pilot study. Twenty to thirty observations give a usable s. Inflate it — the pilot's s is itself noisy, and at ν = 20 its own 95% interval spans roughly 0.77s to 1.44s. Planning with the point estimate under-powers the study about half the time.
  • A previous study, or published values for the same measurement. The most reliable source when it exists.
  • The range rule. For a roughly normal quantity, nearly all values lie within about ±2σ of the centre, so σ ≈ range/4 (or /6 for a wide range). Crude, free, and usually within a factor of 1.5.
  • The t-versus-z circularity. Planning uses z*, but the analysis will use t*, which is larger. For small planned n, iterate once: compute n with z*, look up t* at n − 1, recompute. Two rounds always suffice.
  • The finite-population correction, when you are sampling a large fraction. Required n drops to n₀/(1 + (n₀ − 1)/N), where n₀ is the infinite-population answer. Auditing 1 200 invoices out of a population of 3 000 needs far fewer than 1 200.
Sample-size planner: margin of error against n set the target margin; read off the price
The planning conversation, in four questions. (1) What decision will this number drive, and how precise does it have to be to drive it? That fixes m. (2) How costly is a wrong claim? That fixes the confidence level. (3) What is the variability — from a pilot, a previous study, or the worst case? That fixes σ or p₀. (4) What fraction of the data will you actually get? That fixes the inflation for non-response. Anyone who asks "how much data do we need?" without answering the first is asking a question with no answer.

11 One-sided intervals, differences, variances, and the bootstrap

The same four ingredients, recombined. Everything here is the pivot construction of §06 with a different estimate, a different standard error, or a different reference distribution — and the last item, the bootstrap, is what you reach for when no formula for the standard error exists at all.

One-sided intervals: put all of α in one tail

the two-sided split is a choice; here is the other choiceupper bound only: (−∞, x̄ + z*α·σ/√n) lower bound only: (x̄ − z*α·σ/√n, ∞) note the critical value changes: α in ONE tail, not α/2 in each95% one-sided: z = 1.64485 95% two-sided: z = 1.95996 99% one-sided: z = 2.32635 99% two-sided: z = 2.57583 so a one-sided bound is 16% tighter at 95%, for the same confidence1.64485/1.95996 = 0.839
  • Use one when only one direction is a decision. "The impurity level is at most 0.4 ppm with 95% confidence" is a one-sided claim, and paying for a lower bound you will never use makes the upper bound needlessly loose.
  • Decide before you look. Choosing the direction after seeing which way the estimate went converts a 95% procedure into a 90% one. This is the most common way a one-sided interval is abused.
  • The rule of three (§09) is exactly a one-sided interval — no successes in n trials, all of α pushed to the upper end.

The difference of two means

the estimate and its standard error — variances of independent things addestimate = x̄₁ − x̄₂ SE = √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) WELCH (unequal variances) — the default, and what software gives youCI = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t*ν · √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) ν = ( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )² / [ (s₁²/n₁)²/(n₁−1) + (s₂²/n₂)²/(n₂−1) ] Welch–Satterthwaite POOLED (equal variances assumed) — narrower, and wrong if the assumption failss²ₚ = [ (n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂² ] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2) CI = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t*ₙ₁₊ₙ₂₋₂ · sₚ·√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) PAIRED — a completely different design, and almost always the better oneform dᵢ = x₁ᵢ − x₂ᵢ, then it is a ONE-sample t interval on the d's with ν = n − 1
  • Welch by default. The equal-variance assumption buys a couple of degrees of freedom and costs correctness when it fails; Welch is nearly as powerful when variances are equal and much safer when they are not. There is no good reason to run the pooled version except to reproduce someone else's table.
  • Pairing is the single largest free win available. Measuring the same units under both conditions cancels the between-unit variation entirely, which is often most of the noise. Part 3, §11 works this out for model comparison: on the same 800-example validation set, a paired comparison had a standard error nearly half the unpaired one.
  • The degrees of freedom are fractional under Welch, which is fine — t* is defined for any positive ν. Rounding down is conservative.

The difference of two proportions

the naive interval, with the same Wald pathologies as §09(p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± z*·√( p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂ ) the "add one success and one failure to each arm" fix, which works remarkably wellp̃ᵢ = (kᵢ + 1)/(nᵢ + 2) then the same formula with ñᵢ = nᵢ + 2 a worked A/B test: 120/2 000 vs 156/2 000 conversionsp̂₁ = 0.0600 p̂₂ = 0.0780 difference = −0.0180 SE = √(0.06·0.94/2000 + 0.078·0.922/2000) = √(0.0000282 + 0.00003596) = 0.008010 CI = −0.0180 ± 0.0157 = (−3.37, −0.23) percentage points

That interval excludes zero, so the difference is real at the 5% level — but read the width, not just the sign. The data are consistent with anything from a quarter of a point to three and a third. A decision that only makes sense if the effect exceeds 2 points is not supported by this experiment, even though the result is "significant". This is the argument for reporting intervals rather than verdicts, and it is developed further in online experimentation.

An interval for a variance or a standard deviation

the pivot is chi-square, and it is NOT symmetric — so the interval is not either(n − 1)s²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁ bracket it and invert, remembering that inverting flips the inequalityCI for σ² = ( (n−1)s² / χ²ₙ₋₁,₁₋α/₂ , (n−1)s² / χ²ₙ₋₁,α/₂ ) for σ, take square roots of both ends — invariance againthe interval is asymmetric about s², with a much longer right tail
Handle with extreme care. Unlike the t interval for a mean, the chi-square interval for a variance is not protected by the central limit theorem: it is exact for normal data and can be badly wrong for anything else, at any sample size. A population with heavier-than-normal tails will produce far more extreme s² values than the chi-square allows, and the interval's coverage collapses. If you need an interval for a spread on non-normal data, bootstrap it.

The bootstrap interval, in outline

the principle: if you cannot compute the sampling distribution, simulate it from the data you have1. resample n points from your sample WITH replacement 2. recompute the statistic on the resample 3. repeat B times (B = 2 000 or more for an interval) 4. read the interval off the spread of those B values the percentile interval — the simplest, and the one to start withCI = ( the α/2 quantile of the bootstrap values , the 1 − α/2 quantile ) the studentised (bootstrap-t) interval — better coverage, more workbootstrap T* = (θ̂* − θ̂)/ŜE*, take its quantiles c*ₗ, c*ᵤ, then CI = ( θ̂ − c*ᵤ·ŜE , θ̂ − c*ₗ·ŜE ) — note the endpoints SWAP
  • What it buys. No formula for the standard error is needed, so it works for a median, a trimmed mean, a correlation, an AUC, an R², a ratio of two estimates — anything you can compute. And it adapts to skewness: the resulting interval is asymmetric when the sampling distribution is, which the t interval can never be.
  • What it does not buy. It does not rescue a biased sample, it does not create information that is not in the data, and it degrades badly at very small n and for statistics that depend on the extreme tail — a bootstrap interval for a maximum is meaningless, because a resample can never exceed the original maximum.
  • A concrete comparison. For 135 heavily right-skewed inter-failure times with x̄ = 656.9 and s = 1 037.3, the 90% t interval is (509.0, 804.7) — symmetric about the mean by construction. The 90% bootstrap-t interval is (532.9, 845.0): shifted right and asymmetric, because the underlying distribution is. Given a skewness this large, the second is the more trustworthy of the two.
  • The full treatment is part 7, Resampling — percentile, bootstrap-t, BCa, the bootstrap's failure modes, and permutation tests.
