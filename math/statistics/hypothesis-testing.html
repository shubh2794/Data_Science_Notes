Hypothesis Testing

Part 4 answered "how far off is my number likely to be?" with an interval. This part answers a narrower question — is this particular value still on the table? — and answers it with a single number that almost everybody misreads. The machinery is small: assume the boring explanation, compute how surprising the data would be under it, and reject only if the surprise clears a bar you fixed before you looked. The difficulty is entirely in the interpretation, so a fifth of this page is spent on what a p-value is not, with the arithmetic that proves each misreading wrong.

01 The logic: proof by contradiction, with a probabilistic contradiction

A significance test is one argument, reused everywhere. You are not asked to believe the boring explanation; you are asked to assume it, work out what the world would look like if it were true, and check whether the world you actually got is one it could plausibly have produced. If it is not, you discard the assumption. That is all. Everything else on this page — the tables, the Greek letters, the tests with names — is bookkeeping for that one move.

The move, in five lines

the argument1. Assume H₀: "nothing extraordinary is going on". 2. Under that assumption, derive the distribution of some summary T of the data. 3. Compute the observed value t of T. 4. Ask: how probable is a value of T at least as extreme as t, IF H₀ holds? → the p-value. 5. If that probability is below a threshold fixed IN ADVANCE, discard H₀.

Why it is not quite proof by contradiction

Classical modus tollens is airtight: if H implies D, and D is false, then H is false. A significance test is the probabilistic imitation of that, and the imitation leaks.

the airtight versionH ⟹ D not D ──────── not H the version a test actually usesH₀ makes D very improbable D happened ──────── H₀ is doubtful and this is INVALID as deduction — the standard counterexampleH₀: this person is a member of parliament. D: this person is the Prime Minister. P(D | H₀) ≈ 1/650 — very small. Observe D. Conclude "H₀ is doubtful"? D actually IMPLIES H₀.

So the test's conclusion is not a deduction; it is a decision rule with a controlled long-run error rate. That reframing is the whole contribution of the Neyman–Pearson school (§11): stop pretending the p-value measures how false H₀ is, and instead build a rule whose probability of wrongly rejecting a true H₀ is a number you chose. The rule can be justified even though the underlying inference cannot.

Why the counterexample bites, and when it does not. The Prime Minister case fails because "improbable" was computed for a single arbitrary outcome, not for a tail ordered by how much it favours an alternative. A real test compares H₀ against a specific alternative and calls "extreme" only those outcomes the alternative makes more likely. That is exactly what the Neyman–Pearson lemma formalises, and it is the reason a well-chosen test statistic is not an arbitrary choice.

Failing to reject is not evidence for the null

This is the single most consequential asymmetry in the subject, and it survives no amount of repetition. A large p-value has two completely different causes and the test cannot tell them apart:

cause 1
H₀ really is true
There is no effect, the data look like noise because they are noise, and the p-value is a draw from a uniform distribution that happened to land high.
cause 2
There is an effect and the test could not see it
The sample was too small, the measurement too noisy, or the effect too subtle. The test had low power (§07). "Not significant" here means "not measured", not "not there".
the two claims, in symbols — the second does not follow from the firstobserved: P(data at least this extreme | H₀) = 0.31 ← a large p-value NOT shown: P(H₀ | data) is large ← a different conditional what a non-rejection actually licenses"θ₀ is among the values still compatible with these data" — and so are many others. the honest report is the interval, which names the whole compatible set"the effect is between −0.4 and +2.9" says far more than "p = 0.31".
  • If you want to argue for a null, you need a different tool. Report a confidence interval and show it is tight around zero — an interval of (−0.02, +0.03) on a scale where anything under 0.5 is uninteresting is a genuine argument that the effect is negligible. That is an equivalence test: reverse the burden of proof by making "the effect is bigger than Δ" the null, and reject that. Two one-sided tests at level α against ±Δ is the standard construction.
  • "We found no significant difference" is a statement about the study, not the world. Two studies of the same effect, one with n = 20 and one with n = 2000, will routinely disagree on significance while agreeing perfectly on the estimate.
  • A test is designed to be conservative in one direction only. The framework spends all its protection on avoiding false rejections and leaves false retentions uncontrolled — the type II rate is whatever it happens to be, and nobody ever reports it. §07 and §08 are about paying that debt.
The one-sentence version. A significance test does not tell you whether the null is true. It tells you whether the data would have been surprising if it were — and "surprising" is measured on a scale you fixed before you looked, so that the rate at which you are fooled is a number you own rather than a number you discover.

12 Statistical significance is not importance, and n is why

A significance test answers "is the effect distinguishable from zero?" Nobody has ever cared about that question for its own sake. What they care about is "is the effect big enough to matter?" — and the test is structurally incapable of answering it, because the same statistic that measures the effect also measures how much data you collected.

The theorem nobody states as a theorem

for any effect δ ≠ 0, however small, the power tends to 1power(δ, n) = Φ( δ√n/σ − z₍1−α/2₎ ) + … → 1 as n → ∞ contrapositive: given enough data, EVERY point null is rejected, because every point null is false. Nothing in nature is exactly zero to infinitely many decimal places."is δ exactly 0?" is a question whose answer is always no and never interesting. the smallest effect that a study of size n will call significantδ_min = z₍1−α/2₎ · σ/√n → 0 as n grows

  • Every row of that table produces the same sentence in a paper. "The intervention had a statistically significant effect on test scores (p < 0.05)." Only the first row describes something a school would change its timetable for.
  • At industrial scale the problem inverts entirely. With ten million users, a change of 0.001% in click-through is significant at p < 0.001. The question stops being "is it real?" — it is real — and becomes "does 0.001% pay for the engineering, the latency, and the complexity?" That is a cost-benefit calculation, and no p-value participates in it.
  • The dual failure is just as common and less noticed. A small study finds a large, important effect with p = 0.20, and reports "no effect". The effect may be entirely real and the study simply too small. Both failures come from reading a threshold crossing as a statement about magnitude.

The remedy: name the smallest effect that matters, before you look

the minimum effect of interest, fixed in advanceΔ = the smallest effect that would change a decision now the confidence interval answers all four questions at onceCI entirely above Δ → the effect is real AND it matters CI excludes 0 but sits below Δ → real, but too small to act on CI contains 0 and is narrow → any effect is smaller than Δ: practical equivalence CI contains 0 and is wide → the study was uninformative. Say so. a p-value distinguishes only rows {1,2} from rows {3,4}, which is the wrong cutthe interesting boundary is between 1 and 2, and p cannot see it.

"Session length rose by 4.2 seconds (95% CI 0.9 to 7.5), p = 0.013" cannot be misread as importance, because the number a reader sees first is the importance. "The increase was significant (p = 0.013)" invites exactly the misreading, and it invites it in the abstract, which is the only part most people read.

13 How 5% becomes 60%: p-hacking, forking paths, and the replication crisis

Everything so far assumed one analysis, fixed in advance, run once. Relax that assumption in any of a dozen ordinary-looking ways and the advertised 5% error rate becomes fiction. This section quantifies the damage — with simulations rather than assertions — describes the mechanisms, and reviews what the profession has proposed. The aim is to be fair rather than preachy: several of the proposals have real costs, and one of the most cited critiques has a well-argued rebuttal.

The mechanism, in one sentence

the guaranteeP( p ≤ α | H₀ ) = α — for ONE test, specified BEFORE the data. what any flexibility doesP( MIN over k analyses of p ≤ α | H₀ ) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵏ for k independent looks so the error rate is set not by α but by how many analyses you COULD have runk = 1: 0.050 k = 5: 0.226 k = 10: 0.401 k = 20: 0.642 k = 50: 0.923 and the flexibility need not be exercised consciously to do the damageit is enough that you WOULD have looked elsewhere had the first look failed.

All of the first, third, fourth and sixth together: 0.05 → 0.607. A published simulation of exactly these four researcher degrees of freedom, each individually defensible, found a false-positive rate of 60.7% — a researcher analysing pure noise is more likely than not to find something significant.

Optional stopping, simulated

The most common and most innocent-looking of the moves. Data arrive over time; you test as you go and stop when p < 0.05. Every individual test is correct. The procedure is not.

simulation: standard normal data, H₀ TRUE (δ = 0), two-sided z at 0.05, K equally spaced looks up to n = 1000, 40 000 replications eachK = 1 looks → type I 0.0500 K = 10 looks → 0.1956 K = 2 looks → 0.0849 K = 20 looks → 0.2495 K = 5 looks → 0.1434 K = 50 looks → 0.3214 and testing after EVERY observation from n = 10 to n = 1000type I error = 0.4572 — nine times the advertised rate the limit, if you never stop lookingP(reject eventually | H₀) = 1. The random walk |∑xᵢ|/√n is recurrent: it crosses ANY fixed boundary with probability one if you wait long enough.
  • The last line is the crucial one and it is a theorem, not a simulation artefact. "Run the A/B test until it reaches significance" is a procedure that reaches significance with probability 1, whether or not there is any effect. The stopping rule is the analysis.
  • The legitimate version exists and is called group sequential testing. Fix the number and timing of looks in advance, and spend the α across them — O'Brien–Fleming and Pocock boundaries are the standard schemes, requiring a much smaller nominal α at each look so that the overall rate stays at 0.05. Sequential probability ratio tests and "always-valid" e-value methods go further and allow continuous monitoring with a genuine guarantee. A/B testing, §sequential covers the practical versions.
  • The cost of peeking honestly is small. Five pre-planned O'Brien–Fleming looks cost roughly 2–3% extra sample size relative to a single fixed-n test, in exchange for the option to stop early. There is no good reason to peek dishonestly instead.

The garden of forking paths: p-hacking without any hacking

The word "hacking" implies intent, and the most important version of the problem requires none. A researcher who runs exactly one analysis, on data they see once, honestly reporting a single p-value, can still be producing an error rate far above 5% — if the analysis they chose was contingent on the data.

the fishing expedition
Many analyses run, best reported
Explicit multiplicity. Correctable in principle, by Bonferroni or by the false discovery rate (part 9). The researcher knows k.
the garden of forking paths
One analysis run, but chosen after seeing the data
Implicit multiplicity. The researcher genuinely ran one test — but would have run a different one on different data. k is the number of paths that could have been taken, and it is unknown even to them. Not correctable after the fact.

The forking-paths version is worse because it is invisible. No amount of scrutinising the reported analysis reveals it; the p-value is correct for the test performed, and the test was performed once. What is wrong is the reference class — the p-value is computed against "this test, repeated" when the honest reference is "this researcher, on fresh data". The only fixes are structural: pre-registration, or a genuinely held-out sample.

Publication bias and the winner's curse

Two effects compound the above, and both are properties of the literature rather than of any individual study.

1. PUBLICATION BIAS: only significant results are published, so the literature IS the "discoveries" column of §06's table. With π = 0.10 and power 0.35published findings: 35 true, 45 false → PPV = 0.4375 most of the published literature in that regime is false, with no fraud anywhere. 2. THE WINNER'S CURSE: conditioning on significance selects for LARGE estimates, so the published effect is biased upward. Exact E[δ̂ | significant] for a normal estimate:power 0.17 → published effect is 2.45× the truth power 0.35 → 1.66× power 0.52 → 1.39× power 0.80 → 1.12× power 0.95 → 1.03× so exaggeration is a direct function of how underpowered the study wasand it explains the replication literature's central finding without any misconduct.
These two combine into a prediction, and the prediction was tested. If the literature is filtered for significance and its studies are underpowered, then replications should (a) frequently fail and (b) find systematically smaller effects. A large coordinated replication of 100 psychology studies found exactly that: 97% of the originals reported significant results, 36% of the replications did, and the replication effect sizes were on average about half the originals. The winner's curse table above predicts a ratio near 2 at typical power levels, which is what was observed. A similar picture emerged from industrial attempts to reproduce preclinical biomedical findings, where reproduction rates in the 10–25% range were reported.

  • The honest summary of the debate. Nearly everyone agrees that thresholded p-values are over-weighted and that intervals and effect sizes should lead. Beyond that, the field splits: one camp wants a stricter threshold, another wants no threshold, and both have coherent arguments. What both agree on is that the underlying problem is incentives — a system that rewards novel positive findings will produce them — and that no change to the statistics can fix an incentive problem by itself.
  • The critique is about the practice, not the mathematics. Nothing in §01–§11 is wrong. A p-value computed correctly means exactly what §04 says it means. Every failure in this section is a failure to run the procedure the theory describes: one pre-specified analysis, one threshold, all results reported.
  • Multiple testing has a full treatment in part 9, "ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons". The explicit-multiplicity half of this problem — k known in advance — has proper machinery: family-wise error rate control by Bonferroni, Holm and Tukey, and false-discovery-rate control by Benjamini–Hochberg, which is the right tool when k is in the thousands and controlling every false positive would leave you with no power at all.
The practical defence, for one analyst working alone. Write down the analysis before you look. Record every analysis you run, including the ones that failed, and report the count. Split the data and keep a holdout you touch exactly once. Report intervals. Treat any result that required a choice made after seeing the data as exploratory and label it so — an exploratory finding is not worthless, it is a hypothesis, and the honest next step is to test it on data that did not generate it.

14 The machine-learning translation: comparing two models honestly

"Model B scores 88.0% and model A scores 84.0%, so B is better" is a hypothesis test with the test omitted. The right analysis is nearly always a paired one, because both models were evaluated on the same examples, and the paired version can turn a hopeless comparison into a decisive one — or expose a difference as noise. Everything in §10 and §13 transfers directly; only the vocabulary changes.

  • The information the unpaired test throws away is the correlation between the two models. Two models trained on the same data usually agree on the easy cases and disagree on the hard ones. That shared component of the score is common to both and cancels in a paired comparison — exactly the husbands-and-wives argument of §10, with test examples playing the role of couples.
  • Row 3 is instructive: pairing does not always help. When the models disagree a great deal, the paired test's answer converges on the unpaired one. Pairing helps in proportion to the correlation, and two genuinely unrelated models get no benefit from it.
  • Use the exact binomial version of McNemar when b + c is small. The normal approximation to c ~ Binomial(b+c, ½) is the same approximation that failed at n = 10 in §03, and here b + c = 10. The exact column above is 1.5–2× larger than the normal one — again in the anti-conservative direction.
  • For continuous metrics, use the paired t on the per-example differences. Compute the loss of each model on every test example, take the differences, and run a one-sample t test on them. This is the same test as McNemar in spirit and it uses the magnitudes, which McNemar discards for the same reason the sign test does (§10).

Sizing the test set

a paired comparison of accuracies. δ = the accuracy gain worth shipping, p_d = P(the two models disagree on an example). Only disagreements countn ≈ ( z₍1−α/2₎ + z₍1−β₎ )² · p_d / δ² worked: detect a 1-point accuracy gain, models disagree on 10% of examples, 80% power at α = 0.05n = 7.84888 × 0.10 / 0.01² = 7 849 examples the same target with models that disagree on 30% of examplesn = 7.84888 × 0.30 / 0.01² = 23 547 examples and the unpaired version of the same comparison, for contrastn per model ≈ 2 × 7.84888 × 0.86 × 0.14 / 0.01² = 18 901 — and it wastes the pairing

The point of this arithmetic is that a 1 000-example test set cannot resolve a one-point accuracy difference, no matter how carefully you compute the p-value afterwards. Leaderboard positions separated by a few tenths of a point on test sets of a few thousand examples are, in the overwhelming majority of cases, noise. Part 4's §13 makes the same point with intervals: put an interval on the metric and the resolution of your evaluation becomes visible immediately.

Repeated evaluation on the same test set is p-hacking

the standard workflow, stated as a statistical proceduretrain a model → evaluate on the test set → adjust → evaluate again → ... → report the best this is EXACTLY "try k analyses, report the smallest p" from §13after k evaluations, P(some model beats the baseline by chance | no real gain) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵏ k = 20 evaluations → 0.642. k = 100 → 0.994. and the reported gain is inflated by the winner's curse of §13the best of k noisy scores has expectation well above the truth, so the held-out number you publish will not reproduce in production.
  • This is the entire reason for the three-way split. Train / validation / test exists so that the set you tune against is not the set you report on. Every hyperparameter search, every architecture comparison, every early-stopping decision is a look at the data — and all of those looks belong to the validation set. The test set gets used once, at the end, and reports a number that means what it says. See model evaluation, §validation.
  • Public benchmarks are a shared test set used thousands of times. When a whole community tunes against one leaderboard, the community collectively p-hacks even though each participant looked once. Reported gains on a long-lived benchmark should be discounted, and the discount grows with the benchmark's age. This is the garden of forking paths at the scale of a field.
  • Cross-validation makes the comparison more precise but not independent. The k fold-level scores share training data, so they are correlated, and treating them as k independent observations understates the standard error — the naive paired t test across folds has a type I rate well above its nominal level. Repeat the whole cross-validation with different splits, or use a corrected variance estimate, and always report the fold-to-fold spread. Model comparison covers the practice.

A/B testing: the same test with the same traps

trap 1
Peeking at the dashboard
The live metric updates continuously and everyone watches it. §13's simulation applies verbatim: 0.05 becomes 0.46 with continuous monitoring. Fix the sample size in advance, or use a sequential method built for it.
trap 2
Many metrics, one winner
Clicks, revenue, retention, session length, and six guardrails. Twenty metrics at 0.05 gives a 64% chance one moves "significantly" under a null change. Name one primary metric in advance; everything else is a guardrail or an exploration.
trap 3
Significant but worthless
§12 at industrial scale. Ten million users make a 0.01% change significant. Define the minimum effect worth shipping before you start, and compare the interval to that, not to zero.
trap 4
Broken independence
Users appear many times; sessions within a user are correlated; network effects leak between arms. The formula's standard error assumes independent observations, and when they are not it is too small — so the test is anti-conservative in a way no amount of data fixes.

The full treatment, including sequential designs and the practical machinery, is in A/B testing; §significance and §pitfalls are the direct continuations of this section.

The three habits that carry all of this. Pair whenever both systems saw the same data — it costs nothing and often changes the verdict. Split so that the set you tune against is never the set you report on, and touch the reporting set once. Report an interval on the difference, not two accuracies side by side — the difference is the quantity you are deciding on, and it is the only one whose uncertainty you can state honestly.

15 Practice

The checklist, before you report any test

  1. Were H₀, H₁, the level and the analysis fixed before the data? If not, the result is exploratory. Say so.
  2. Is the test statistic's null distribution actually right here? Check the assumption that binds: normality at small n, independence always, cell counts for proportions.
  3. Is the sample independent? Clustered, repeated or time-ordered data break every standard error on this page.
  4. Should this be paired? If the same units generated both numbers, yes.
  5. One-sided or two-sided, and was that decided in advance?
  6. How many analyses did you run, or could you have run? Report the count.
  7. What is the effect size, in units a reader understands?
  8. What is the confidence interval? Report it before the p-value.
  9. What power did the study have against the smallest effect that matters? Computed in advance, not from the observed effect.
  10. If you failed to reject: is the interval tight enough to support "no effect", or is the study simply uninformative?

Worked problems

  1. A coin gives 60 heads in 100 tosses. Test fairness. SE = √(100 × ¼) = 5, z = (60 − 50)/5 = 2.00, two-sided p = 0.0455. With continuity correction, z = 9.5/5 = 1.90, p = 0.0574. The exact binomial two-sided p-value is 0.0569. Three answers straddling 0.05 — report the exact one and note the fragility.
  2. Same coin, 600 heads in 1 000 tosses. SE = √(1000 × ¼) = 15.81, z = 100/15.81 = 6.32, p = 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁰. The proportion is identical to problem 1; the p-value moved by eight orders of magnitude. This is §12 in two lines.
  3. A drug lowers blood pressure by 0.4 mmHg in a trial of 40 000, σ = 10. SE = 10/200 = 0.05, z = 8.0, p ≈ 10⁻¹⁵, 95% CI (0.302, 0.498). Overwhelmingly significant, clinically negligible — clinical guidelines treat differences under about 2 mmHg as immaterial. Report the interval and let it speak.
  4. Design: detect a 0.3σ difference between two groups with 90% power at α = 0.05. n = 2 × 10.50742/0.09 = 234 per group by the normal formula; the exact t answer is 235. Halve the effect to 0.15σ and it becomes 934 per group — a fourfold cost for a halved target.
  5. Twelve paired differences: mean 3.1, SD 5.4. SE = 5.4/√12 = 1.5588, t = 1.9887, df = 11, two-sided p = 0.0722. 95% CI 3.1 ± 2.20099 × 1.5588 = (−0.331, 6.531). Not significant, and the interval says why: this study cannot distinguish "no effect" from "an effect of 6", so it has settled nothing.
  6. You test 40 features for association with the outcome, all null, at α = 0.05. Expected false positives = 40 × 0.05 = 2. P(at least one) = 1 − 0.95⁴⁰ = 0.871. You will almost certainly find something, and it will almost certainly be nothing.
  7. Model A is right on 82 of 100 test cases, model B on 88; they disagree on 12, with B right on 9 of those. McNemar: b = 3, c = 9, z = 6/√12 = 1.7321, p = 0.0833; exact p = 2·P(X ≥ 9 | Bin(12, ½)) = 0.1460. Use the exact version at these counts. Not significant — a six-point gap on 100 examples is within noise.
  8. A 95% CI for a difference is (0.4, 3.6). What is the two-sided p-value at H₀: δ = 0? By duality the interval excludes 0, so p < 0.05. More precisely: the centre is 2.0, the half-width 1.6, so SE = 1.6/1.95996 = 0.81634 and z = 2.0/0.81634 = 2.4500, giving p = 0.0143. The interval contained the test all along.

Things to be able to derive from a blank page

  • The p-value's three definitions, and why they are the same thing.
  • That the p-value is Uniform(0,1) under a continuous null, by the probability integral transform — and that this is why the level is honoured.
  • The four-cell table, and that α + β ≠ 1.
  • The power function β(μ) = 1 − Φ(√n(c − μ)/σ), and that its supremum over Θ₀ is attained at the boundary.
  • The two-sided power formula, and that it equals α at δ = 0.
  • n = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)²σ²/δ², and the factor 2 for two independent groups.
  • The duality theorem, from |θ̂ − θ₀| > c·SE in three lines.
  • The Neyman–Pearson lemma's proof, from the pointwise inequality.
  • That the likelihood ratio for a normal mean reduces to x̄, hence the z test is UMP one-sided.
  • The base-rate formula for PPV, and that low power lowers PPV as surely as a high α does.
  • 1 − (1 − α)ᵏ, and that optional stopping rejects with probability 1.
  • Why McNemar and the paired t use only the within-pair variation, and what that buys.
