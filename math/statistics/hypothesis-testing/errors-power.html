07 Two ways to be wrong, and the function that measures both

A test makes a binary decision about a binary state of the world, so there are four outcomes and two of them are mistakes. The framework controls one of the two mistakes exactly, by construction, and leaves the other to whatever the design happens to deliver. Understanding which is which — and why the asymmetry is deliberate rather than an oversight — is most of what "power" means.

The power function: β is not one number

"The" type II rate does not exist, because H₁ is usually composite — "the drug works" covers effects of every size. What exists is a function of the true parameter, and both error rates are values read off it.

  • The second term is almost always negligible and occasionally is not. It is the probability of rejecting in the wrong direction — concluding the effect is negative when it is really positive, a "type III" or sign error. At δ√n/σ = 3 it is Φ(−4.96) ≈ 3.5×10⁻⁷. But at δ√n/σ = 0.5, power is only 0.079 and the wrong-sign term contributes Φ(−2.46) = 0.0069, which is 8.8% of all the rejections that test will ever make. In a badly underpowered study, roughly one significant result in eleven points the wrong way — the study finds a real effect, declares it significant, and gets the sign backwards.
  • Power at the boundary equals α. Put δ = 0 in the formula: power = 2Φ(−z₁₋α/₂) = α. A test has no ability to detect an effect of zero, and the floor of the power function is exactly the significance level. Any claimed power below α is an arithmetic mistake.
  • Power is a pre-data quantity. It depends on α, n, σ and a hypothetical δ — none of which is the data you collected. This is why the "post-hoc power" of §08 is meaningless: it substitutes the observed effect for the hypothetical one and thereby stops being power.

The trade-off, made visible

The two error rates are pushed in opposite directions by the same lever. Move the critical value out and you reject less often: fewer false positives, more false negatives. There is no setting that makes both small; the only thing that shrinks both at once is more data, which narrows both curves.

Why the asymmetry is deliberate

controlled exactly
Type I, at rate α
Because H₀ is a specific distribution, the probability of rejecting under it is computable, and the critical value is chosen to make it come out at α. This works for every n, without knowing the effect size.
not controlled at all
Type II, at whatever rate
H₁ is a family, so β depends on which alternative is true — a number you do not know. You cannot set β in advance without first committing to an effect size you care about. Almost nobody does, which is why so many studies are underpowered.
  • The framework is built to protect the status quo. Its guarantee is "I will not overturn a true null more than 100α% of the time". It offers no guarantee at all about missing real effects. If your costs run the other way — a missed detection is catastrophic, a false alarm is cheap — the standard machinery is protecting the wrong thing, and you should either swap the hypotheses or raise α deliberately.
  • Screening tests get this right and hypothesis tests usually do not. A medical screen is tuned for high sensitivity (power) and tolerates false positives, because the follow-up test is cheap and the missed cancer is not. That is a conscious choice of α and β against costs; a study run at "α = 0.05, power = whatever" is the same choice made by default.
  • The four cells are the confusion matrix. Type I error is 1 − specificity; power is sensitivity, also called recall; PPV from §06 is precision; the prior π is prevalence. Moving the critical value traces out a curve of (false-positive rate, true-positive rate) pairs — which is the ROC curve, exactly. A significance test is one operating point on it, chosen by fixing the false-positive rate at α.
The dictionary, worth memorising once. α = type I rate = false positive rate = 1 − specificity = the significance level = the size. 1 − β = power = sensitivity = recall = the true positive rate. PPV = precision = 1 − FDR. Statistics, medicine and machine learning each named these independently, and the only thing that differs between the three vocabularies is which quantity is treated as the headline.

08 Power: what drives it, how to plan for it, and why post-hoc power is empty

Power is the probability the study will succeed if the effect you are looking for is really there. It is the only design quantity that connects "how much data should I collect?" to "what would I consider worth finding?", and it is the one number that is systematically not computed. Four things move it, three of which you control.

The four levers

everything enters power through ONE combination — the noncentralityΔ = δ√n / σ power = Φ(Δ − z₍1−α/2₎) + Φ(−Δ − z₍1−α/2₎) so the four levers are1. effect size δ bigger effects are easier power ↑ with δ (not yours to choose) 2. sample size n power ↑ with √n yours, and expensive 3. noise σ power ↑ as σ falls yours, via measurement and design 4. level α power ↑ with α yours, but it buys power with false positives note the √n. Quadrupling the data does NOT quadruple Δ — it doubles itthis is part 3's 1/√n law again, now in its most expensive form.

The third lever is the underused one. Halving the measurement noise does exactly what quadrupling the sample does, and is often far cheaper: better instruments, blocking, stratification, and above all pairing, which replaces the variance of two groups with the variance of their differences (§10). A paired design on the same budget routinely delivers what an unpaired design would need several times the sample to match.

The exact column comes from the noncentral t distribution — the distribution of (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) when the true mean is not μ₀. At δ = 0.2σ the correction is 1%; at δ = 1σ it is 25%.   • Two independent groups cost four times a paired design at the same δ. Two per group, times two groups, is 4× the one-sample total. That factor is the price of not pairing, and §10 shows how to avoid paying it when the design allows.

Post-hoc power, and why it is empty

After a study returns a non-significant result, a referee asks for "the power of the test". The natural move is to plug the observed effect into the power formula. This computation is not wrong so much as vacuous: it is a deterministic, decreasing function of the p-value and therefore contains not one bit of information the p-value did not already carry.

  • Every non-significant result has observed power below 0.5, necessarily. The row above says so: p > 0.05 ⟹ power_obs < 0.50, always, whatever the study, whatever the sample size. So "the study was underpowered" is guaranteed by the non-significance, not discovered by the calculation. It is a restatement dressed as a diagnosis.
  • The genuinely informative version is the confidence interval. If you want to know whether the study could have detected an effect worth caring about, look at whether the interval excludes that effect. An interval of (−0.02, 0.03) rules out anything meaningful; an interval of (−1.4, 1.6) rules out nothing. That distinction is exactly what observed power cannot make, because both can give p = 0.5.
  • Power is for design, intervals are for interpretation. Compute power before the data exist, using an effect size you specified in advance; interpret afterwards with the interval. Crossing the two is the error.
The one design question worth asking out loud. What is the smallest effect that would change what I do? Answer it, put it in the formula, get an n, and compare that n to your budget. If the budget is smaller, you have learned something valuable before spending anything: this study cannot answer this question, and running it anyway produces a result whose "not significant" is uninterpretable and whose "significant" is, by §13's winner's curse, an overestimate.

09 The duality: a confidence interval is the set of nulls you would not reject

Part 4's interval and this part's test are not two methods that happen to agree. They are one computation read in two directions, and the equivalence is an identity, not an empirical fact. Once you see it, the argument for reporting the interval instead of the p-value becomes hard to resist: the interval answers the test's question for every null value at once, and carries an effect size while doing it.

The statement

the duality theorem, two-sided versionThe level-α two-sided test of H₀: θ = θ₀ REJECTS ⟺ θ₀ ∉ C₍1−α₎, the 1 − α confidence interval. equivalently, read right to left — this is the DEFINITION of a confidence set by inversionC₍1−α₎ = { θ₀ : the level-α test of H₀: θ = θ₀ does NOT reject } one-sided versionreject H₀: θ ≤ θ₀ in favour of θ > θ₀ at level α ⟺ θ₀ < the 1 − α lower confidence bound

The proof, which is three lines of algebra

take the Wald form: estimate θ̂, standard error SE, critical value c = z₍1−α/2₎the test rejects ⟺ | (θ̂ − θ₀)/SE | > c multiply through by SE > 0 — the inequality direction is untouched ⟺ | θ̂ − θ₀ | > c·SE unfold the absolute value ⟺ θ₀ < θ̂ − c·SE or θ₀ > θ̂ + c·SE which says exactly ⟺ θ₀ ∉ ( θ̂ − c·SE , θ̂ + c·SE ) = C₍1−α₎ ∎ and the coverage guarantee falls out of the size guarantee, for freePθ( θ ∈ C ) = 1 − Pθ( the level-α test of the TRUE value rejects ) = 1 − α

Nothing in that argument used normality, or a mean, or even a symmetric interval. Any family of level-α tests, one for each candidate θ₀, can be inverted into a confidence set by collecting the values that survive — and the set inherits the coverage automatically. This is how the Clopper–Pearson interval of part 4, §09 is built (invert the exact binomial test), and how confidence sets are constructed in settings where no pivot exists.

The p-value function, which is the interval's missing dimension

Plotting the p-value as a function of the hypothesised value — sweeping θ₀ across the axis and recording p(θ₀) — produces a curve that contains every interval at every level simultaneously. It is worth knowing that this object exists, because it dissolves several of §05's confusions on sight.

the p-value function (confidence curve, compatibility curve)p(θ₀) = 2 · [ 1 − Φ( |θ̂ − θ₀| / SE ) ] for the Wald test its propertiesp(θ̂) = 1 the estimate is maximally compatible with itself p(θ₀) → 0 as |θ₀ − θ̂| → ∞ { θ₀ : p(θ₀) > α } = the 1 − α interval, for EVERY α at once so slicing the curve at different heights gives the nested family of intervalscut at 0.05 → the 95% interval. Cut at 0.01 → the 99% interval. Same curve.
  • The curve makes the arbitrariness of 0.05 visible. There is no feature of the curve at height 0.05 — no kink, no cliff. The 95% interval's endpoints are where a horizontal line you drew happens to cross a smooth function. §05's misreading 7 is a statement about that line, not about the data.
  • It also kills the "values inside are more likely" misreading from part 4's §07, by showing what a graded statement would actually look like. The p-value function is not a probability density over θ — it does not integrate to 1 and it is not a posterior — but it does grade compatibility continuously, which is the thing people want an interval to do and it cannot.

The caveat that keeps the p-value in the toolkit. Duality is exact only when the interval and the test are built from the same pivot. Compare a t-interval with a sign test and they will disagree, legitimately. And for a genuinely composite or multi-parameter null — "these five coefficients are all zero" — there is no interval to invert, and a likelihood ratio test (§11) is the natural object. The interval wins for a single scalar parameter, which is most, but not all, of practice.
