Regression & Correlation

Part 5 handed over a complete machine for one number at a time: an estimate, a standard error, a t statistic on the ratio of the two, a p-value and a confidence interval that are two readings of the same fact. This part points that machine at two variables measured on the same units, and almost nothing new is needed — the slope of a fitted line is an estimate, it has a standard error, and dividing one by the other gives a t on n − 2 degrees of freedom. What is new is everything around the arithmetic: a single number that summarises how tightly two variables move together and the half-dozen ways it misleads, a line that predicts less extremely than the data it was fitted to and the fallacy that has been built on top of that fact for a century and a half, two different bands around the same line that answer two different questions, and a set of diagnostic pictures whose job is to tell you when the standard error you just computed is fiction.

01 Two variables, one question — and what this page does not cover

Every technique on this page is an answer to one question: knowing x, what should I say about y, and how wrong am I likely to be? The first clause is description and prediction; the second is inference, and it is the half that the word "regression" usually gets used without. Both halves rest on a single object that exists whether or not anybody fits a line to it.

The thing being estimated

Suppose (X, Y) is a pair of random variables with some joint distribution. For each value x that X can take, the conditional distribution of Y given X = x has a mean. Collect those means as x varies and you have a function — and that function is what "regression" means before any line, any least squares, any software.

the regression function — the population object, defined without reference to any modelr(x) = E[ Y | X = x ] = ∫ y · f(y | x) dy it is the best predictor of Y from X under squared error, and this is a theorem, not a conventionfor any function g, E[ (Y − g(X))² ] = E[ (Y − r(X))² ] + E[ (r(X) − g(X))² ] ≥ E[ (Y − r(X))² ] so argmin₍g₎ E[ (Y − g(X))² ] = r with equality only when g = r almost everywhere SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION is the assumption that this function happens to be a straight liner(x) = β₀ + β₁x and then all that remains is to estimate two numbers

The decomposition above is worth doing once by hand, because it is the reason squared error and conditional means are welded together everywhere in statistics and machine learning. Write Y − g(X) = (Y − r(X)) + (r(X) − g(X)) and square. The cross term is 2·E[(Y − r(X))(r(X) − g(X))]; condition on X, and the inner factor r(X) − g(X) is a constant that comes outside, leaving E[Y − r(X) | X] = 0. The cross term vanishes for every g, so the mean squared error of any predictor splits cleanly into irreducible noise plus the squared distance from the truth. That is the bias–variance decomposition in its original habitat.

02 The scatterplot: direction, form, strength — in that order

Before any number is computed, plot the pair. The scatterplot is the only step in this whole subject that cannot be skipped, because every summary below it — r, the slope, R², the p-value — is a projection of the picture onto one axis, and each of them can take an identical value on pictures that mean completely different things. Read the plot in three passes.

The three questions, and what each one licenses

pass 1
Direction
Does the cloud slope up or down? Positive association: large x tends to go with large y. Negative: large with small. This is the sign of r and the sign of the slope, and it is the only feature of the plot that survives every summary intact.
pass 2
Form
Straight, curved, or in separate lumps? Form comes before strength, because r and the least-squares line are only summaries of a linear form. If the form is a parabola, the strength of the linear part is a description of nothing.
pass 3
Strength
How tightly do the points hug that form? Only once the form is agreed to be linear does a single number — r — mean anything, and even then it measures scatter about a line, not the steepness of one.
pass 4
And then: departures
Isolated points far from the pattern, gaps, a second cloud, a hard ceiling or floor from a measurement limit. Each of these breaks a different downstream assumption, and §15 and §16 are about them.

The mistake this ordering prevents

Strength is the number people report and form is the property that decides whether the number means anything, so the two are constantly transposed. A cloud with r = 0.2 that is a clean parabola contains more usable structure than a cloud with r = 0.8 that is two separate blobs, but the reported numbers say the opposite. The habit that fixes this is trivially cheap: draw the plot, decide the form, and only then let yourself compute r.

What a scatterplot cannot show you either. Two things are invisible in the plot and fatal downstream. The first is the order the points arrived in: if the units are consecutive days, weeks or production batches, they are not independent and every standard error on this page is wrong. Plot the residuals against time as well as against x (§15). The second is a third variable. Every pattern here is compatible with an unmeasured cause driving both axes, and no amount of staring at two dimensions will reveal it (§05).
Set the correlation and watch the cloud — plus what r is actually averaging turn on the quadrants: r is the mean of the shaded products

Reading the picture as an average of products

The dashed cross in the figure is at (x̄, ȳ). A point in the upper-right quadrant is above average on both variables, so both its z-scores are positive and their product is positive; a point in the lower-left is below average on both, and the product of two negatives is again positive. The other two quadrants contribute negatives. The correlation is the average of those signed products, and that single sentence explains its sign, its scale-freedom and its blind spots all at once. A cloud that fills the two green quadrants gets a large positive average; a cloud that fills all four evenly averages to zero — whatever shape it has. That last clause is §04.

03 The correlation coefficient, defined four ways

One number, four equivalent formulas, and each of the four makes a different property obvious. Learn all four: the z-score form explains why it is unit-free, the covariance form is how it is computed, the vector form explains why it is bounded, and the population form is what it estimates.

Definition 1 — the mean product of z-scores

standardise each variable separately, then average the productsr = (1/(n − 1)) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ [ (xᵢ − x̄)/s_x ] · [ (yᵢ − ȳ)/s_y ] = (1/(n−1)) Σ z_x,ᵢ · z_y,ᵢ the convention that must match — and this is the one place the textbooks differdivide by n − 1 ⟺ s_x, s_y were computed with n − 1 (the sample convention) divide by n ⟺ s_x, s_y were computed with n (the population convention) EITHER pairing gives the SAME r. Mixing them does not. on the running data, both pairings agree to the last digit(1/11)Σ z_x z_y with n−1 SDs = 0.9589921 (1/12)Σ z_x z_y with n SDs = 0.9589921 MIXED — n−1 SDs but divided by n — gives 0.8790761, which is r × (n−1)/n. Wrong by 8%.

The two conventions cancel because the factor n − 1 appears once in the denominator of the average and twice inside s_x s_y — (n−1)/(√(n−1)·√(n−1)) = 1 — and identically for n. The consequence is that the choice never matters as long as it is made once, which is why software never asks. It matters only when you compute s_x in one place, the sum of products in another, and divide by whichever number came to hand.

the covariance measures joint variation but carries the product of both unitss_xy = (1/(n−1)) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) units: [x]·[y] dividing by both SDs strips the units offr = s_xy / (s_x · s_y) units: [x][y] / ([x][y]) = none the computational form, which needs one pass and no stored meansr = ( nΣxy − ΣxΣy ) / √( [ nΣx² − (Σx)² ] · [ nΣy² − (Σy)² ] ) and the three sums of squares this page will use constantlyS_xx = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² S_yy = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² S_xy = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) r = S_xy / √(S_xx · S_yy) on the running data: S_xx = 318, S_yy = 3112, S_xy = 954 r = 954/√(318 × 3112) = 954/994.79 = 0.95899 ✓

The one-pass form is numerically treacherous — it subtracts two large nearly-equal numbers — and should not be used on real data with a large mean and small spread. It is here because it is the form that appears in textbooks and in the guts of old libraries, and because it makes visible that r depends on the data only through five running sums: n, Σx, Σy, Σx², Σy², Σxy. Those six numbers are a sufficient summary of a bivariate data set for every purpose on this page except the diagnostics.

Definition 3 — a cosine, which is why |r| ≤ 1

centre both variables and read them as vectors in ℝⁿu = (x₁ − x̄, …, xₙ − x̄) v = (y₁ − ȳ, …, yₙ − ȳ) then r is exactly the cosine of the angle between themr = (u · v) / (‖u‖ ‖v‖) = cos θ and the bound is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, nothing more|u · v| ≤ ‖u‖ ‖v‖ ⟹ −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 with equality exactly when the vectors are parallel — i.e. the points are exactly collinearr = +1 ⟺ yᵢ = c + dxᵢ for every i, with d > 0 r = −1 ⟺ yᵢ = c + dxᵢ for every i, with d < 0 r = 0 ⟺ u ⊥ v — the centred variables are ORTHOGONAL, which is not the same as independent
  • A self-contained proof of the bound, if you would rather not invoke Cauchy–Schwarz. For any real t, Σ(z_x,ᵢ + t·z_y,ᵢ)² ≥ 0. Expand: Σz_x² + 2tΣz_x z_y + t²Σz_y² ≥ 0. Both sums of squares equal n − 1 by construction, so the expression is (n−1)(1 + 2tr + t²) ≥ 0 for all t. A quadratic in t that is never negative has a non-positive discriminant: 4r² − 4 ≤ 0, hence r² ≤ 1. Equality needs the quadratic to touch zero, i.e. some t makes every term z_x,ᵢ + t·z_y,ᵢ vanish — exact collinearity.
  • The cosine reading is the bridge to linear algebra. Quadratic Forms & PCA develops the covariance matrix whose diagonal is the variances and whose normalised version is the correlation matrix; the angle interpretation is what makes a correlation matrix positive semi-definite and its eigenvectors the principal directions.
  • r = 0 means orthogonal, not independent. Independence implies r = 0 (when both variances exist); the converse is false, and §04's curved cloud is the standard counterexample. The one important exception: for a bivariate normal pair, ρ = 0 does imply independence, which is why so much regression teaching quietly assumes normality.

Definition 4 — the population parameter it estimates

for random variables with finite non-zero variancesρ = Corr(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y) / (σ_X σ_Y) = E[ (X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y) ] / (σ_X σ_Y) r is the plug-in estimator of ρ: replace every expectation by its sample averager is consistent (r → ρ) but BIASED in finite samples; the bias is about −ρ(1 − ρ²)/(2n) its sampling distribution is badly skewed near ±1, which is why intervals use a transformFisher's z: ζ = ½ ln((1 + r)/(1 − r)) = artanh(r) ≈ Normal( artanh(ρ), 1/(n − 3) ) interval for ρ: tanh( artanh(r) ± z* /√(n − 3) ) — asymmetric in r, by construction on the running data: r = 0.95899, n = 12artanh(0.95899) = 1.93321 1/√9 = 0.33333 1.93321 ± 1.95996 × 0.33333 = (1.27989, 2.58652) → tanh → 95% CI for ρ: (0.8565, 0.9887)

The interval is visibly lopsided: 0.103 below r and only 0.030 above it. That asymmetry is not a defect, it is the correct answer — r is squeezed against a hard ceiling at 1, so the sampling distribution piles up on the low side and a symmetric interval would be wrong. The n − 3 rather than n − 1 is the standard variance-stabilising result for this transform and is worth remembering only as "three, not one".

And it is a summary of the sample you happened to draw, not of the relationship — which is why the next three sections are all about ways the sample and the relationship disagree.

04 What r cannot see

The correlation coefficient answers exactly one question — how close are these points to some straight line? — and it answers it with a single number, which means it must be blind to everything that number does not encode. The blindness is total and it is not a matter of degree: there are data sets with strong, obvious, deterministic structure and r of exactly zero.

Blindness 1 — non-linear form

take an exact parabola, symmetric about zero — no noise at allx = −3, −2.99, …, 2.99, 3 y = x² y is a DETERMINISTIC function of x yet the centred vectors are orthogonalΣ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = Σ xᵢ(xᵢ² − x̄²) = Σxᵢ³ = 0 by symmetry, since x̄ = 0 r = 0 (2.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ in floating point) R² = 0 "no linear association" and the honest description of the same datay is perfectly predictable from x. Knowing x removes ALL uncertainty about y.

The lesson is not "r is unreliable"; it is "r answers a narrower question than the one you asked". r = 0 means "no linear component", never "no relationship". The remedy is the same as always: look at the plot, and if the form is curved, either transform (§15) or use a method that does not assume a line.

Blindness 2 — a single point

r is a sum over n terms, and one term can dominate it. Two symmetric failures, both common:

  • One point manufactures a correlation. Take a round, structureless blob with r ≈ 0 and add one far-away point on the diagonal. Because that point's z-scores are both huge, its product dwarfs the other n − 1 terms and r can be dragged past 0.8 by a single observation. Nothing about the other points changed.
  • One point destroys a correlation. Take a tight line with r = 0.99 and add one point far off it in the perpendicular direction; r collapses. In both cases the number moved a long way and the relationship did not move at all.
  • The diagnostic is not a number, it is a re-run. Compute r with and without the suspicious point. If the two differ materially, report both, and say which points are driving the claim. §16 turns this into a systematic procedure.

Blindness 3 — clusters and mixtures

Two clouds with no internal structure at all, offset from each other along a diagonal, produce a large r that describes only the offset between the groups. Within either group the correlation is zero. Worse, the two effects can point in opposite directions: a strong negative correlation inside every subgroup can appear as a positive correlation overall if the subgroups are arranged the right way. That is Simpson's paradox in continuous clothing, and part 8 develops the categorical version.

Blindness 4 — everything except the marginal five numbers

The strongest statement of the problem: x̄, s_x, ȳ, s_y, r — five numbers — determine the least-squares line exactly and determine nothing else at all. Data sets can be constructed to agree on all five (and hence on the fitted line, the residual standard deviation, R², the slope's standard error and the p-value) while looking utterly unlike each other: one linear with honest scatter, one an exact parabola, one a perfect line plus a single outlier, one a vertical stack of points at a single x plus one far-off point that fixes the slope by itself. Every regression number is identical; every correct conclusion is different.

The rank-based alternative: Spearman's ρ

When the form is monotone but not straight — y rises with x throughout, but along a curve — Pearson's r understates the association because it is measuring the wrong thing. Replacing each variable by its ranks and correlating those instead fixes exactly this case and nothing else.

  • Use it when the form is monotone and the scale is arbitrary — ordinal ratings, ranks, heavily skewed quantities, or anything where you would have been happy to take logs but do not want to argue about which transform.
  • It is resistant to outliers by construction, because a wildly extreme value becomes merely "rank n". This is the same robustness argument that makes the median resistant (part 1, §04), applied one dimension up.
  • It does not rescue a non-monotone form. The parabola above has ρ_S ≈ 0 too. Ranks preserve order, and a U-shape has no consistent order.
  • It changes what "linear" means, so it no longer matches the fitted line. ρ_S is a description of association, not a slope; there is no "Spearman regression line" and ρ_S² is not a fraction of variance explained.
  • Kendall's τ is the other standard rank measure — the proportion of concordant minus discordant pairs. It has a cleaner probabilistic meaning and smaller variance in small samples; Spearman is more common because it is computed the same way as r.
The rule that covers all four blindnesses. Never report r without the plot that produced it, and never let r be the reason you believe the form is linear — the plot decides the form, and r only then measures strength. Every failure in this section is a case of using the number to answer a question it was not computed to answer.

05 Correlation is not causation — the four explanations of any association

Suppose the plot is clean, the form is linear, r is large and the p-value of §13 is tiny. You have established that x and y move together in this sample, and you have established nothing whatever about whether changing x would change y. The slogan is famous and still under-applied, because the interesting content is not the slogan but the list of what else it could have been.

The four explanations, all of which fit the same scatterplot

1
x causes y
The one everybody assumes. Intervening on x would move y. This is the only explanation under which the fitted slope is a policy-relevant number.
2
y causes x — reverse causation
The scatterplot is symmetric; nothing in it distinguishes the direction. Hospitals with more staff have more infections; police numbers rise with crime. In both, the arrow points the other way, or both ways.
3
A third variable drives both — confounding
The lurking variable. Neither x nor y affects the other; both are downstream of something unmeasured. This is the default explanation for almost every surprising correlation.
4
Coincidence, or selection
With enough variable pairs, large correlations appear by chance (part 5, §13). And if the sample was selected on a variable related to both, the association can be manufactured by the selection itself.

The lurking variable, worked

Among primary-school children, shoe size correlates strongly with reading score. The association is real, reproducible, and would survive any significance test you cared to run. Buying a child larger shoes does not make them read better. Both quantities are driven by age: seven-year-olds have bigger feet and read better than five-year-olds, and pooling across ages creates the correlation out of nothing.

the structure — a common cause, with no arrow between x and y at all age ╱ ╲ ↓ ↓ shoe size reading score r(shoe, reading) is LARGE and MEANINGLESS the test that exposes it: hold the lurking variable fixed and look againwithin seven-year-olds only: r(shoe, reading) ≈ 0 within six-year-olds only: r(shoe, reading) ≈ 0 ← the association was age, all along this is exactly the aggregation structure of §06, and the same algebra explains bothbetween-group variation carries the whole correlation; within-group variation carries none.
  • "Control for it" means condition on it, and you can only condition on what you measured. Adding age as a second predictor makes the shoe-size coefficient collapse towards zero — that is what multiple regression is for. But it works only for confounders you thought of, measured, and measured accurately. An unmeasured confounder leaves no trace in the data at all.
  • Adjusting for the wrong variable makes things worse, not better. Conditioning on something that lies downstream of both x and y — a collider — creates an association where none existed. "Throw every available variable into the model" is not a safe default.
  • Randomisation is the only general fix, and it is a design fix, not an analysis one. If x is assigned by a coin flip, it cannot be downstream of anything, so explanations 2, 3 and part of 4 are eliminated by construction. Part 2, §08 is the full argument, and it is worth re-reading here: nothing computed on this page can substitute for it.

What an observational slope is allowed to mean

Wrong: "each extra component replaced causes 3 more minutes on site."
Right: "service calls with one more component replaced took, on average, 3 minutes longer" — a statement about the calls that were observed, with no claim about what would happen if somebody replaced an extra component on purpose.
Wrong: "the association is highly significant, so it is unlikely to be a coincidence, so x probably causes y."
Right: a small p-value rules out explanation 4 and only explanation 4. It has nothing to say about reverse causation or confounding, and those are the live possibilities. Significance is evidence against chance, never evidence for a mechanism.
Wrong: "we controlled for the obvious confounders, so the remaining association is causal."
Right: "the association survives adjustment for the confounders we measured." The set of confounders you did not measure is not empty and its size is unknown.
Prediction does not need causation; decisions do. If all you want is to guess y from x in a population that behaves like this one, a confounded association predicts perfectly well — shoe size really does forecast reading score. The moment you propose to act on x, the confounding becomes fatal, because acting breaks the link to the lurking variable that was doing all the work. This is the sharpest available statement of why "the model has high R²" and "the model tells us what to do" are unrelated claims.

06 Three ways r understates or overstates the thing it does measure

Section 04 was about r answering a different question from the one you asked. This section is worse: here r answers the right question and still gives the wrong number, because the sample's spread is not the population's spread. All three mechanisms have exact formulas, all three are verifiable by simulation, and all three are routinely mistaken for substantive findings.

Distortion 1 — measurement error attenuates r towards zero

Suppose the true predictor is X but what you recorded is X* = X + U, where U is measurement noise independent of X and of Y. Nothing about the underlying relationship changed; only the recording did. Both the correlation and the slope shrink, by different amounts, and the amounts are computable.

setup: noise added to the PREDICTOR only, independent of everythingX* = X + U U ⊥ X, U ⊥ Y, Var(U) = σ_u² λ = σ_x²/(σ_x² + σ_u²) ← the RELIABILITY the covariance is untouched; only the variance of the predictor inflatesCov(X*, Y) = Cov(X, Y) Var(X*) = σ_x² + σ_u² = σ_x²/λ so both quantities shrink, and by DIFFERENT powers of the reliabilitycorrelation: ρ* = ρ · √λ slope: β* = β · λ simulation check, σ_x = 1, β = 2, σ_ε = 1.5, so ρ = 0.8 — two million drawsσ_u = 0.5 λ = 0.800 r observed 0.71566 vs ρ√λ = 0.71560 b 1.6004 vs βλ = 1.600 σ_u = 1.0 λ = 0.500 r observed 0.56604 vs ρ√λ = 0.56573 b 1.0003 vs βλ = 1.000 σ_u = 2.0 λ = 0.200 r observed 0.35872 vs ρ√λ = 0.35780 b 0.4008 vs βλ = 0.400 noise in the RESPONSE behaves completely differently — it leaves the slope aloneY* = Y + V, V ⊥ everything: β* = β (unbiased) ρ* = ρ·√(σ_y²/(σ_y² + σ_v²)) simulated: σ_v = 2 gives b = 2.0003 (true 2) and r = 0.62482 vs predicted 0.62464
  • Attenuation is a bias, and it always points the same way — towards zero. A noisily measured predictor produces a slope that is too small and a correlation that is too weak, systematically, no matter how large the sample. More data does not help; only better measurement does.
  • The asymmetry between the two kinds of noise is the practical headline. Noise in y costs you precision — a wider interval — but leaves the estimated slope centred on the truth. Noise in x costs you the truth itself. When resources are limited, spend them on measuring the predictor well.
  • The correction is division by the reliability, if you happen to know it: β = β*/λ, ρ = ρ*/√λ. Reliability is usually estimated from repeated measurements on the same units. Applying the correction without a defensible λ is worse than not correcting.
  • Two noisy predictors in a multiple regression do something nastier than attenuate. The bias is no longer towards zero and can even change sign, because the noise redistributes explanatory credit among the correlated predictors. Simple attenuation is a single-predictor result.

  • The formula assumes the model still holds outside the slice — same line, same residual SD. Truncating on y instead of on x, or selecting on some combination of the two, biases the slope as well, and there is no simple correction.
  • Deliberate range expansion is the mirror image. Sampling extra points at extreme x inflates r and shrinks the slope's standard error — SE(b) = s_e/√S_xx and S_xx grows fast at the ends (§13). It is a legitimate and powerful design choice, provided the linear form really does extend that far.

07 The least-squares line, derived from the criterion

Among all the straight lines that could be drawn through a cloud, one is singled out by a rule: minimise the total squared vertical distance from the points to the line. Two derivatives and one substitution turn that rule into two formulas, and the formulas turn out to be the correlation in disguise. Everything downstream — the fitted values, the residuals, R², the standard errors — is a consequence of the two equations the derivatives produce.

The criterion

a candidate line assigns each xᵢ a predicted valueŷᵢ = a + b·xᵢ residual eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ (vertical, signed) choose a and b to minimise the sum of SQUARED residualsQ(a, b) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ ( yᵢ − a − b·xᵢ )² note what is being measured: VERTICAL gaps, not perpendicular onesthe criterion is asymmetric in x and y by construction — and §09 is the consequence

The derivation, in full

set the two partial derivatives to zero — the NORMAL EQUATIONS∂Q/∂a = −2 Σ( yᵢ − a − b xᵢ ) = 0 ⟹ Σ eᵢ = 0 ∂Q/∂b = −2 Σ xᵢ( yᵢ − a − b xᵢ ) = 0 ⟹ Σ xᵢ eᵢ = 0 the first equation, divided by n, gives the intercept immediatelyȳ − a − b x̄ = 0 ⟹ a = ȳ − b x̄ ⟹ the line passes through (x̄, ȳ) substitute that into the second and the intercept disappearsΣ xᵢ( yᵢ − ȳ + b x̄ − b xᵢ ) = 0 Σ xᵢ( yᵢ − ȳ ) − b Σ xᵢ( xᵢ − x̄ ) = 0 and both sums may be re-centred for free, because Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0Σ xᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ) = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = S_xy Σ xᵢ(xᵢ − x̄) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = S_xx giving the slopeb = S_xy / S_xx = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² and, dividing numerator and denominator by (n − 1), the form worth memorisingb = s_xy / s_x² = r · s_y / s_x a = ȳ − b x̄

The rewrite b = r·s_y/s_x is the single most useful identity on this page. It says the slope is the correlation, converted from unit-free to real units by multiplying by the ratio of the spreads. Two clouds with the same r and different spreads have different slopes; two clouds with the same slope and different scatter have different r. Slope and correlation are not the same quantity and neither determines the other without s_x and s_y.

Is it really a minimum?

The five algebraic consequences you should be able to recite

  • The residuals sum to zero, Σeᵢ = 0 — that is the first normal equation. So the mean residual is zero automatically, for any data, and a residual plot centred on zero is not evidence of anything.
  • The residuals are orthogonal to the predictor, Σxᵢeᵢ = 0 — the second normal equation. So r(x, e) = 0 always: any leftover linear structure has already been removed. A residual plot can therefore never show a tilt, and if it appears to, you have a bug.
  • The line passes through the point of averages (x̄, ȳ), so ŷ = ȳ whenever x = x̄. Predicting at the mean returns the mean.
  • The fitted values have the same mean as the data, Σŷᵢ = Σyᵢ, and are also orthogonal to the residuals, Σŷᵢeᵢ = a Σeᵢ + b Σxᵢeᵢ = 0. That orthogonality is what makes the variance decomposition of §11 exact rather than approximate.
  • In standard units the line is ẑ_y = r · z_x, slope r, intercept 0. Every regression is the same regression once both variables are standardised — which is the whole content of §08.

The line as a graph of averages

There is a second reading of the fitted line that requires no calculus and explains why it predicts well. Slice the scatterplot into narrow vertical strips. If the underlying relationship is linear, the strip averages fall close to the least-squares line — and they do so because §01's theorem says the conditional mean is the best squared-error predictor, and least squares is estimating exactly that conditional mean under a straight-line constraint.

08 Regression to the mean, and the fallacy named after it

The word "regression" does not mean "fitting a line". It means going back, and it entered statistics as the name of a phenomenon: predictions are less extreme, in standard units, than the values they are predicted from. This is a two-line algebraic consequence of |r| < 1, it is unavoidable, it has no mechanism behind it, and roughly a century and a half of confident causal explanations have been offered for it anyway.

The two lines of algebra

write the fitted line in standard units — substitute a = ȳ − bx̄ and b = r·s_y/s_xŷ = ȳ + r (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) ⟹ (ŷ − ȳ)/s_y = r · (x − x̄)/s_x that is, in z-scoresẑ_y = r · z_x with |r| ≤ 1 so the prediction is ALWAYS pulled towards the mean, in standard units|ẑ_y| = |r|·|z_x| ≤ |z_x| with equality only in the degenerate case |r| = 1 in words"x is two SDs above average" ⟹ "predict y to be 2r SDs above average", not 2.

This is not a claim about a mechanism, a tendency, a force, or a drift. It is what the least-squares line is. Any prediction rule that is a linear function of x and gets the mean right must shrink towards the mean whenever the correlation is imperfect, because shrinking is what minimising squared error requires when the predictor is noisy.

Why it happens: a heuristic that is actually correct

Decompose each score into a stable part and a transient part — preparation plus luck, skill plus noise, signal plus measurement error. To land in the very top group on the midterm, a student almost certainly needed both a high stable part and a favourable transient part. The stable part persists to the final; the transient part is redrawn. So the group's average transient contribution, which was positive on the midterm by selection, is about zero on the final, and the group falls back. The same argument in reverse lifts the bottom group.

The regression fallacy

The fallacy is to observe the fall-back and attribute it to a cause. It is one of the most reliably reproducible errors in applied statistics, because the effect is real, visible, and always available to be explained.

ObservationThe causal story that gets toldWhat regression to the mean already predicts
The top midterm scorers do worse on the final"They got complacent and stopped working."Their final z-scores should average r times their midterm z-scores. No behaviour change needed.

The diagnostic question, and the fix. Were the units selected for study because of an extreme value of the very variable being re-measured? If yes, expect regression to the mean and do not attribute it to anything. The fix is not statistical, it is a design: a control group selected the same way. Both groups regress; the difference between them is what the intervention did. This is the single most important reason part 2, §09 insists on a comparison group even when the before/after change looks overwhelming.
  • The effect is strongest when r is small — that is, when the measurement is noisy. A perfectly reliable measurement (r = 1) shows no regression at all; a pure-noise measurement (r = 0) regresses all the way to the mean, and every selected group's follow-up average is simply ȳ.
  • It is not a claim that the spread shrinks over time. The predictions are less spread out than the data — SD(ŷ) = |r|·s_y — but the actual final scores have the same spread as the midterm scores in standard units. Nothing is converging. If the whole distribution were really collapsing towards its mean, exam scores would have become identical generations ago.
  • It applies to any pair of imperfectly correlated measurements, in either time order, with no requirement that one causes the other or even that they are measured at different times.

09 Two regression lines, and the third line that is neither

Regression is not symmetric. Predicting y from x and predicting x from y are two different problems with two different answers, and the second answer is not obtained by solving the first for x. Getting this wrong is the most common purely mechanical error in the subject, and it is easy to avoid with one rule.

Why there are two lines

Least squares minimises vertical distances — gaps in the direction of the variable being predicted. Turn the plot on its side and "vertical" means something else, so a different line minimises it. Both lines pass through (x̄, ȳ); they differ in slope, and the angle between them is a direct visual reading of how far r is from 1.

line 1 — regress y on x: minimise Σ(yᵢ − a − bxᵢ)²ŷ = ȳ + r (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) slope in the (x, y) plot: b = r·s_y/s_x line 2 — regress x on y: minimise Σ(xᵢ − a′ − b′yᵢ)²x̂ = x̄ + r (s_x/s_y)(y − ȳ) slope in the (y, x) plot: b′ = r·s_x/s_y drawn on the SAME (x, y) axes, line 2 has slope 1/b′, which is steeper than b1/b′ = s_y/(r·s_x) vs b = r·s_y/s_x ratio = 1/r² ≥ 1 the product of the two slopes is r², which is the neatest statement of the whole sectionb · b′ = r² they coincide ⟺ r² = 1 ⟺ the points are exactly collinear and the line lying between them, slope s_y/s_x, is the SD LINESD line: y = ȳ + (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) — it passes through the "corner" of the cloud, is what the eye wants to draw, and predicts NOTHING optimally. The rule, and the geometry behind it

  • The rule: always put the variable you are given on the horizontal axis, and refit. Never algebraically invert a fitted line. If you are given y and want x, the regression you need is the regression of x on y, and its coefficients are b′ = r·s_x/s_y, a′ = x̄ − b′ȳ.
  • The two lines pinch together as |r| → 1 and open to a right angle as r → 0. At r = 0 the y-on-x line is horizontal at ȳ (best guess: the mean, ignore x) and the x-on-y line is vertical at x̄. The angle between them is a live display of the correlation.
  • Applying both lines in succession does not return you to where you started. Predict y from x, then x from that ŷ, and the composition has slope b·b′ = r² — you end up shrunk towards x̄ by a factor r². Two regressions to the mean, compounded.
  • The SD line is what the eye draws and it is not an estimator of anything. It has slope s_y/s_x, passes through (x̄, ȳ), and hits the corners of the elliptical cloud. Since b = r·s_y/s_x, the regression line is always flatter than the SD line by exactly the factor r. Confusing the two is the regression fallacy in geometric form: the SD line is the line along which "no regression to the mean" would happen.
  • Nor is the SD line the major axis of the cloud. It is the first principal direction of the standardised cloud carried back into the original units. The genuine major axis — the perpendicular-distance fit of §07 — is a third line again, and it moves if you change the units of either variable, which is exactly why it is not used for prediction.
Three lines, three jobs. y on x: the best guess for y when x is known. x on y: the best guess for x when y is known. The SD line: a description of the cloud's orientation in standard units, and the reference against which the regression effect is measured. Only the first two are predictions, and each answers exactly one of the two questions.

11 R² = r², and the exact splitting of the variance

The total variation in y divides, exactly and without remainder, into a part the line accounts for and a part it does not. The identity is a Pythagorean theorem in disguise — it holds because least squares makes the residual vector orthogonal to the fitted one — and the ratio it defines is the most reported and most over-read number in applied statistics.

What R² means, said carefully

correct
"92% of the variation in y is accounted for by the linear fit on x"
A statement about a sum of squares in this sample. It is arithmetic, not inference, and it makes no claim about mechanism.
correct
"the residual spread is √(1 − R²) times the original spread"
The version worth quoting, because it is on the scale of the data. R² = 0.92 means the scatter fell to √0.08 = 0.28 of what it was: a genuinely large reduction.
wrong
"92% of y is caused by x"
No causal content whatever (§05). R² is as large for a confounded association as for a mechanism.
wrong
"R² is high, so the model is right"
A curved relationship can have R² = 0.95 against a straight-line fit while the residual plot screams. R² measures tightness, not correctness of form.
wrong
"R² is low, so there is nothing here"
A small, real, precisely estimated slope can have a tiny R². In noisy domains an R² of 0.02 with a tight confidence interval is a finding; in a physics calibration it would be a broken instrument.
careful
"R² is comparable across studies"
It is not. R² depends on the spread of x in the sample exactly as r does (§06), so a narrower sample of the same relationship reports a smaller R² with no change in the world.

Six further facts, each of which stops a misuse

  • R² never decreases when a predictor is added, even a column of random numbers, because the larger model contains the smaller one and the minimum of a sum of squares over a bigger set cannot be larger. This is why R² cannot be used to choose between models of different sizes; adjusted R² = 1 − (SSE/(n − k − 1))/(SST/(n − 1)) penalises the extra parameters and can go down. Neither is a substitute for held-out error (model evaluation).
  • R² = r² only for simple regression with an intercept. With several predictors, √R² is the multiple correlation — the correlation between y and the fitted values ŷ — which is a different and always non-negative quantity. Fit without an intercept and SST = SSR + SSE itself breaks, which is why software reports a different, non-comparable R² for through-the-origin models.
  • R² is a sample quantity and is optimistically biased. Even when x is pure noise, the expected R² from fitting k predictors to n points is about k/(n − 1). With n = 11 and ten random predictors it reaches 1 exactly, having explained nothing.
  • 1 − R² is the number to quote when the audience is optimistic. "R² = 0.7" sounds like most of the job is done; "30% of the variation is unexplained, and the residual SD is 55% of the original" is the same fact and gives a truer impression.
  • The regression F statistic of §13 is a monotone function of R²: F = R²/(1 − R²) × (n − 2)/1. So the test of "is there any linear association at all" is literally a test on R², rescaled by the sample size — which is why a tiny R² can be overwhelmingly significant at large n.
  • R² is not the fraction of y explained, it is the fraction of y's variance explained. The two get conflated constantly, and the distinction is what makes the √(1 − R²) version above so much more informative.

12 The probability model, and what each assumption is holding up

Everything so far was arithmetic on twelve points and needed no assumptions at all. The moment you want a standard error, a p-value or an interval, you have to say what the twelve points are a sample of. That statement is the regression model, it has four parts, and each part is load-bearing for a specific downstream number — which means each has a specific failure mode.

Teaching order tends to be the reverse of this, because normality is the one with a picture attached.

Fixed x or random x?

  • Fixed-x (designed) regression: the xᵢ are chosen by the experimenter — doses, temperatures, batch sizes — and only y is random. All the distribution theory below is exact under A1–A4 with no further conditions, and S_xx is a design constant you control.
  • Random-x (observational) regression: both variables are sampled together. The same formulas remain valid conditionally on the observed x's, which is what every piece of software reports. This is why the standard errors below never contain a term for the randomness of x.
  • The practical difference is not in the arithmetic, it is in what r means. With fixed x, the correlation is not estimating any population parameter at all — you set the spread of x yourself, so §06's range-restriction argument says r is whatever you designed it to be. Report the slope. With random x, r estimates ρ and is meaningful.

14 Two bands around one line: confidence and prediction

Software draws a shaded region around a fitted line and calls it "the confidence band", and almost everyone reads it as "where the next observation will fall". It is not. There are two bands, they answer two different questions, the wider one is usually the one you wanted, and at moderate n the wider one is several times the width of the narrower.

The two questions

question 1
Where is the line?
"For all units with x = 14, what is their average y?" The target is E[Y | X = 14] = β₀ + 14β₁ — a fixed unknown number. The only uncertainty is in the two estimated coefficients. → the confidence band.
question 2
Where is the next point?
"The next unit has x = 14. What will its y be?" The target is a random variable, not a parameter. The uncertainty is the line's uncertainty plus that unit's own error term. → the prediction band.

Three properties that follow from the formulas alone

  • The prediction band is always wider, at every x. The radicand differs by exactly 1, so the ratio of half-widths is √(1 + v)/√v with v = 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx. At x_p = x̄ this is √(n + 1) — a factor of 3.6 at n = 12, 5.6 at n = 30, 10.0 at n = 100. The gap grows with the sample size, because the confidence band shrinks and the prediction band does not.
  • Both are narrowest at x̄ and widen as |x_p − x̄| grows, and both widen hyperbolically — the boundary is a hyperbola, not a pair of straight lines. The mechanism is visible in the re-parameterised form: the fitted line pivots about (x̄, ȳ), so uncertainty in the slope has no effect at the pivot and a leverage-arm effect proportional to the distance from it.
  • As n → ∞ the confidence band collapses onto the true line; the prediction band does not collapse at all. It converges to ±t*σ, roughly ±1.96σ. Infinite data tells you the line exactly and still leaves you ignorant about the next individual. This is the single most useful sentence in the section, and it is the reason "our model has a very tight confidence band" is not a claim about predictive accuracy.

Four traps

Wrong: "95% of the data points fall inside the shaded band."
Right: that is a property of the prediction band, and even then only approximately — the band was built from these same points, so it is slightly optimistic in-sample. The confidence band typically contains a small minority of the data; at n = 100 it contains roughly a tenth of them, and that is correct behaviour, not a defect.
Wrong: "the band is 95% confident along its whole length."
Right: both formulas are pointwise. Each x separately has 95% coverage; the probability that the true line lies inside the band everywhere at once is much less. A simultaneous band needs a bigger multiplier — the Working–Hotelling one, √(2·F₍₂,ₙ₋₂;1−α₎), which for n = 12 is √(2 × 4.10282) = 2.8645 instead of t* = 2.2281, so the band is 29% wider. Software almost always draws the pointwise one.
Wrong: "with enough data the prediction interval will get tight."
Right: it converges to ±t*σ and stops. If that is too wide to be useful, the answer is a better model or a less noisy measurement — never more rows.
Wrong: "the prediction interval is as trustworthy as the confidence interval."
Right: it is much less so, and for a specific reason. The confidence band is protected by the central limit theorem — a and b are averages, so they are near-normal whatever the errors look like. The prediction band's leading σ² is the distribution of one error term, and no averaging happens to it. If the errors are skewed or heavy-tailed, the prediction interval is wrong in a way that more data cannot fix, and the honest alternatives are a quantile-based interval or the resampling methods of part 7.

16 Outliers, leverage, influence — and the point the residual plot cannot find

Three different things get called "a bad point", and conflating them is the reason a well-known failure mode survives every diagnostic. An outlier is far from the line.   • The mechanism, in one sentence: a high-leverage point drags the line towards itself, and once the line has arrived the residual is small — so the diagnostic that measures disagreement with the line reports success precisely because the point won.
  • The procedure that actually works is not a statistic, it is a habit: identify the points with extreme x, refit without each of them, and report the slope both ways. If the conclusion changes, the conclusion was never about the data set, it was about one row of it.
  • Deleting points is a last resort and must be disclosed. Delete when you can show the value is wrong — a transcription error, a broken sensor, a unit mix-up. Do not delete because a point is inconvenient. The honest report is both analyses and the reason for preferring one.
  • An outlier is often the most informative point in the data. The county whose vote count sits far above a log–log fit is not noise to be trimmed; it is the finding.
  • Robust regression makes this automatic. Least absolute deviations and Huber regression bound the influence any single point can have, so the fit does not need protecting by hand. The cost is the loss of the closed-form standard errors of §13.

  • Linear relationships are almost always local. Fertiliser increases yield until it poisons the soil; dose increases response until it saturates; a growth curve is exponential until the resource runs out. The line was an approximation over the observed range and there is no reason for the approximation to survive outside it.

  • The boundary is not just the range of x. With several predictors, an extrapolation can be a combination of values each of which is individually ordinary — inside every marginal range and outside the observed cloud. Leverage hᵢ is the general measure of "far from the data" and is what to check.
  • Deliberate extrapolation is sometimes the whole point — calibration, forecasting, dose-finding. It is legitimate when the functional form comes from theory rather than from the scatterplot. What is never legitimate is quoting the interval as if it covered the risk.

17 The ML translation

Every object on this page reappears in machine learning under a different name and with a different emphasis. The vocabulary shift is small; the shift in what is being optimised is not, and the mismatch is where most of the confusion between the two traditions lives.

Statistics puts its effort into the uncertainty of a parameter and assumes the model form; machine learning puts its effort into the honesty of an error estimate on unseen data and treats the model form as something to be searched over. The techniques on this page are the first kind and stop being appropriate the moment the model was chosen by looking at the same data — at which point the p-values are meaningless (part 5, §13) and a held-out set is the only remaining honest measurement.

18 Practice

The checklist, before you report any regression

  1. Did you plot the two variables before computing anything? Direction, then form, then strength (§02).
  2. Is the form actually linear? If not, r, the slope and R² are all summaries of a fiction (§04).
  3. Which variable is being predicted? Put it on the vertical axis and never invert a fitted line (§09).
  4. Are the units independent? Clustered, repeated or time-ordered rows break every standard error on this page (§12, §15).
  5. Is the residual plot an unstructured horizontal band? Against x and against observation order (§15).
  6. Any point with extreme x? Refit without it and report both slopes (§16).
  7. Are you predicting inside the observed range of x? If not, say so and do not quote the interval as if it covered the risk (§16).
  8. Which band did you draw — mean or individual? Say which question it answers (§14).
  9. Did you report the slope with its interval and units? Before, or instead of, the p-value (§13).
  10. Is the sample's spread of x representative? A restricted range crushes r and R² without touching the slope (§06).
  11. How well is x measured? Noise in the predictor biases the slope towards zero, permanently (§06).
  12. Is the unit of analysis the unit you want to make claims about? Correlations between averages are not correlations between individuals (§06).
  13. Were the units selected on an extreme value of the outcome? Then expect regression to the mean and do not explain it (§08).
  14. Is anything causal being claimed? If yes, what randomised the predictor (§05)?

Worked problems

  1. Given x̄ = 50, s_x = 10, ȳ = 200, s_y = 40, r = 0.6, predict y at x = 65. b = 0.6 × 40/10 = 2.4, a = 200 − 2.4 × 50 = 80, ŷ = 80 + 2.4 × 65 = 236. Check with z-scores: z_x = 1.5, ẑ_y = 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9, ŷ = 200 + 0.9 × 40 = 236 ✓. The input was 1.5 SDs above average and the prediction is only 0.9 SDs above — the regression effect in one line.
  2. Same five numbers: predict x for a unit with y = 280. Refit the other way: b′ = 0.6 × 10/40 = 0.15, a′ = 50 − 0.15 × 200 = 20, x̂ = 20 + 0.15 × 280 = 62. The wrong answer, from inverting the first line, is (280 − 80)/2.4 = 83.3. Sanity check: y = 280 is z_y = 2, so x̂ must be at z_x = 0.6 × 2 = 1.2, i.e. 50 + 12 = 62 ✓, whereas 83.3 sits at z = 3.33 — more extreme than the value it was predicted from, which is impossible.
  3. What fraction of the variation is left unexplained when r = 0.6? 1 − r² = 0.64. And the residual spread is √0.64 = 0.8 of the original — a correlation most people would call substantial removes one fifth of the scatter.
  4. n = 25, b = 4.2, s_e = 6, s_x = 3. Test H₀: β₁ = 0 and give a 95% interval. S_xx = (n−1)s_x² = 24 × 9 = 216, SE(b) = 6/√216 = 0.40825, t = 4.2/0.40825 = 10.288 on 23 df, p = 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁰. t*₍₂₃₎ = 2.06866, so the interval is 4.2 ± 0.8445 = (3.356, 5.045).

  6. A study reports r = 0.30 among applicants who were admitted; the admitted group's s_x is 40% of the full applicant pool's.   7. A predictor is measured with reliability λ = 0.7. The fitted slope is 1.4 and the observed correlation 0.5. What are the underlying values? Slope: β = 1.4/0.7 = 2.0. Correlation: ρ = 0.5/√0.7 = 0.598. Note the two corrections differ — the slope is divided by λ, the correlation by √λ.
  8. n = 12, x̄ = 10, S_xx = 318, s_e = 5. How much wider is the prediction interval than the confidence interval at x = 10, and at x = 18? At x̄: √(1 + 1/12)/√(1/12) = √13 = 3.606. At x = 18: v = 1/12 + 64/318 = 0.28459, ratio √1.28459/√0.28459 = 2.125. The two bands converge as you move away from x̄, because the line's own uncertainty grows while the individual noise term does not.
  9. Thirteen points; the thirteenth has h = 0.886 and a standardised residual of −1.80. Is it influential? Cook's D = (r²/(k+1))·h/(1−h) = (3.24/2) × 0.886/0.114 = 12.6, more than ten times the "investigate" threshold of 1 — despite a residual that no plot would flag. This is the fourth row of §16's table: removing it moves the slope by 14%. The residual is small because the point is influential.
  10. Two studies of the same relationship report r = 0.8 and r = 0.4. Name four explanations before concluding they disagree. Different spread of x (§06); different measurement reliability (§06); different units of analysis, e.g. one aggregated (§06); different ranges over which a non-linear relationship was approximated by a line (§04). Only after those are excluded is there a substantive disagreement — and the slopes may agree perfectly all along.
  11. A residual plot fans out to the right and the data span three orders of magnitude in both variables. What is the first thing to try? Log both axes. If the underlying structure is y = c·x^p·η with multiplicative error, log–log linearises the form and stabilises the variance in one step, and the slope becomes an elasticity.
  12. Fitting on log y gives an intercept 2.1 and slope 0.35. Interpret the slope, and predict y at x = 4. Each unit of x multiplies y by e^0.35 = 1.419, i.e. about +42%. Things to be able to derive from a blank page

  • The regression function r(x) = E[Y|X = x] as the minimiser of squared error, with the cross term vanishing.
  • r as the mean product of z-scores, and why the n / n − 1 conventions cancel.
  • |r| ≤ 1 from the non-negativity of Σ(z_x + t·z_y)², in three lines.
  • The two normal equations, and b = S_xy/S_xx = r·s_y/s_x, a = ȳ − bx̄ from them.
  • That the least-squares residuals satisfy Σeᵢ = 0 and Σxᵢeᵢ = 0, and that everything else follows from those two.
  • ẑ_y = r·z_x, and the regression effect as its immediate consequence.
  • Why the x-on-y line has slope r·s_x/s_y, and that the two slopes multiply to r².

Where this goes next. Part 7, Resampling removes the assumptions this page rests on: bootstrap the pairs, refit the line on each resample, and read SE(b) off the spread of the bootstrapped slopes — no normality, no homoscedasticity, and a standard error that is honest even when §15's residual plot is not flat. It also gives a permutation test of H₀: β₁ = 0 by shuffling the y's against the x's. Part 8, Categorical Data is this page's association question when both variables are counts rather than measurements, with χ² in place of r. Part 9, ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons is §13's F ratio generalised: replace the single predictor by a grouping factor, and MSR/MSE becomes between-group over within-group variance on (k − 1, n − k) degrees of freedom — the same table, the same logic, and then the multiplicity problem that comes with testing many slopes at once.
