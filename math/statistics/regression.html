Regression & Correlation

Part 5 handed over a complete machine for one number at a time: an estimate, a standard error, a t statistic on the ratio of the two, a p-value and a confidence interval that are two readings of the same fact. This part points that machine at two variables measured on the same units, and almost nothing new is needed — the slope of a fitted line is an estimate, it has a standard error, and dividing one by the other gives a t on n − 2 degrees of freedom. What is new is everything around the arithmetic: a single number that summarises how tightly two variables move together and the half-dozen ways it misleads, a line that predicts less extremely than the data it was fitted to and the fallacy that has been built on top of that fact for a century and a half, two different bands around the same line that answer two different questions, and a set of diagnostic pictures whose job is to tell you when the standard error you just computed is fiction.

01 Two variables, one question — and what this page does not cover

Every technique on this page is an answer to one question: knowing x, what should I say about y, and how wrong am I likely to be? The first clause is description and prediction; the second is inference, and it is the half that the word "regression" usually gets used without. Both halves rest on a single object that exists whether or not anybody fits a line to it.

The thing being estimated

Suppose (X, Y) is a pair of random variables with some joint distribution. For each value x that X can take, the conditional distribution of Y given X = x has a mean. Collect those means as x varies and you have a function — and that function is what "regression" means before any line, any least squares, any software.

the regression function — the population object, defined without reference to any modelr(x) = E[ Y | X = x ] = ∫ y · f(y | x) dy it is the best predictor of Y from X under squared error, and this is a theorem, not a conventionfor any function g, E[ (Y − g(X))² ] = E[ (Y − r(X))² ] + E[ (r(X) − g(X))² ] ≥ E[ (Y − r(X))² ] so argmin₍g₎ E[ (Y − g(X))² ] = r with equality only when g = r almost everywhere SIMPLE LINEAR REGRESSION is the assumption that this function happens to be a straight liner(x) = β₀ + β₁x and then all that remains is to estimate two numbers

The decomposition above is worth doing once by hand, because it is the reason squared error and conditional means are welded together everywhere in statistics and machine learning. Write Y − g(X) = (Y − r(X)) + (r(X) − g(X)) and square. The cross term is 2·E[(Y − r(X))(r(X) − g(X))]; condition on X, and the inner factor r(X) − g(X) is a constant that comes outside, leaving E[Y − r(X) | X] = 0. The cross term vanishes for every g, so the mean squared error of any predictor splits cleanly into irreducible noise plus the squared distance from the truth. That is the bias–variance decomposition in its original habitat.

The choice of which variable goes on which axis is not cosmetic and not symmetric. Correlation treats the two variables even-handedly and returns the same number either way. Regression does not: the line that predicts y from x is a different line from the one that predicts x from y, and §09 is entirely about that asymmetry and the mistakes it causes.

The order of the argument

plot the pair→summarise the strength with r→distrust r six ways→fit the line→read the line honestly→add a probability model→put a standard error on the slope→put a band around the prediction→check the residuals before believing any of it
  • Sections 02–06 need no probability model at all. They are descriptive statistics for two variables: what r is, what it hides, and how sampling and measurement distort it. Everything there is computable from the data with no assumption whatever, which is exactly why the conclusions are so limited.
  • Sections 07–11 add a line but still no model. Least squares is a definition — the minimiser of a sum of squares — and the regression effect, the two lines, the conditional spread and R² are all algebra. No p-value can appear yet, and none does.
  • Sections 12–16 add the probability model, and only then do standard errors, t statistics, intervals and bands become meaningful. The last two sections are the diagnostics that decide whether the model was entitled to be there.

The running example

One data set recurs from §07 to §16, so that the same twelve points carry the least-squares fit, the ANOVA table, the slope test, both bands and every diagnostic. Twelve service calls were logged; x is the number of components replaced and y is the time on site in minutes.

the running data — twelve pairsx: 2 3 5 6 8 9 11 12 14 15 17 18 components replaced y: 10 14 26 22 26 37 31 48 52 57 53 56 minutes on site everything this page computes from it, once, exactlyx̄ = 10 s_x = 5.37672 Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = 318 ȳ = 36 s_y = 16.81990 Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² = 3112 r = 0.95899 b = 3 exactly a = 6 exactly Σeᵢ² = 250 s_e = 5 exactly

The numbers are contrived so that the fit comes out at exactly ŷ = 6 + 3x with a residual standard deviation of exactly 5 minutes — the residuals were chosen to satisfy Σeᵢ = 0 and Σxᵢeᵢ = 0 with Σeᵢ² = 250, which are precisely the least-squares conditions of §07. Nothing else about the example is contrived: the leverages, the bands, the ANOVA table and the diagnostics all fall out of those twelve pairs as they would from any real ones.

17 The ML translation

Every object on this page reappears in machine learning under a different name and with a different emphasis. The vocabulary shift is small; the shift in what is being optimised is not, and the mismatch is where most of the confusion between the two traditions lives.

This pageThe ML pageWhat changes, and what does not
Least-squares criterion Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²Mean squared error loss, minimised by gradient descentIdentical objective, different solver. The closed form exists and is exact; descent is used because it survives millions of features and non-linear models. Same minimum.
β₀, β₁ — parameters to be estimated, with standard errorsWeights — parameters to be learned, usually reported without anyThe whole difference in outlook. Inference asks "how precisely do I know this coefficient"; prediction asks "how well does the whole function generalise".
s_e = √(SSE/(n − 2))Training RMSE, √(SSE/n)The same sum of squares with a different divisor. The n − 2 is there to make it unbiased for σ; ML divides by n because the number is being compared across models, not used in an interval.
R² in-sampleHeld-out R², which can be negativeThe essential fix. In-sample R² cannot decrease when you add anything; out-of-sample R² can be worse than predicting ȳ, and that is the signal you wanted.
The four assumptions A1–A4 (§12)The i.i.d. assumption and a train/test splitML drops A2 and A4 entirely and replaces them with empirical validation. It keeps A3 — and violates it constantly, because leakage is exactly the failure of independence between training and test rows.
Slope t test, §13Feature importance, ablation, permutation importanceRelated questions with different answers. A t statistic is about a coefficient in a fixed model; permutation importance is about predictive contribution and survives non-linear models. Neither is a causal claim (§05).
Prediction band, §14Prediction interval / conformal predictionThe same target with weaker assumptions. §14's band needs A1–A4; conformal methods give a distribution-free coverage guarantee from held-out residuals, at the cost of being wider and only marginally valid.
Leverage hᵢ, §16Out-of-distribution detectionLiterally the same computation in the linear case: hᵢ is a Mahalanobis distance from the training x's. "High leverage" and "this input is unlike anything I was trained on" are one concept.
Extrapolation, §16Covariate shift, distribution driftSame failure, larger scale. A model deployed on inputs whose distribution has moved is extrapolating, and its confidence estimates are as uninformative as the band at x = 40.
Gauss–Markov: least squares is the best unbiased linear estimatorRidge and lasso — deliberately biased, lower total errorThe theorem is not violated, its premise is abandoned. Accepting bias buys a variance reduction that can more than pay for it (part 4, §03).
Attenuation from noisy x, §06Noisy features / label noiseUnder-appreciated in practice: a noisy feature does not merely add variance, it systematically shrinks the coefficient towards zero and can make a genuinely important feature look useless.
Regression to the mean, §08Shrinkage — ridge, James–Stein, empirical Bayes, early stoppingThe same mathematics, embraced rather than mistaken. Optimal prediction shrinks extreme estimates towards the mean by a factor governed by the reliability, which is exactly ẑ_y = r·z_x.
Ecological fallacy, §06Choosing the unit of analysis: per-user vs per-session vs per-request metricsAn aggregated metric can move in the opposite direction from every individual unit. Deciding the grain before computing the correlation is the whole fix.
The one asymmetry worth internalising. Statistics puts its effort into the uncertainty of a parameter and assumes the model form; machine learning puts its effort into the honesty of an error estimate on unseen data and treats the model form as something to be searched over. The techniques on this page are the first kind and stop being appropriate the moment the model was chosen by looking at the same data — at which point the p-values are meaningless (part 5, §13) and a held-out set is the only remaining honest measurement.

18 Practice

The checklist, before you report any regression

  1. Did you plot the two variables before computing anything? Direction, then form, then strength (§02).
  2. Is the form actually linear? If not, r, the slope and R² are all summaries of a fiction (§04).
  3. Which variable is being predicted? Put it on the vertical axis and never invert a fitted line (§09).
  4. Are the units independent? Clustered, repeated or time-ordered rows break every standard error on this page (§12, §15).
  5. Is the residual plot an unstructured horizontal band? Against x and against observation order (§15).
  6. Any point with extreme x? Refit without it and report both slopes (§16).
  7. Are you predicting inside the observed range of x? If not, say so and do not quote the interval as if it covered the risk (§16).
  8. Which band did you draw — mean or individual? Say which question it answers (§14).
  9. Did you report the slope with its interval and units? Before, or instead of, the p-value (§13).
  10. Is the sample's spread of x representative? A restricted range crushes r and R² without touching the slope (§06).
  11. How well is x measured? Noise in the predictor biases the slope towards zero, permanently (§06).
  12. Is the unit of analysis the unit you want to make claims about? Correlations between averages are not correlations between individuals (§06).
  13. Were the units selected on an extreme value of the outcome? Then expect regression to the mean and do not explain it (§08).
  14. Is anything causal being claimed? If yes, what randomised the predictor (§05)?

Worked problems

  1. Given x̄ = 50, s_x = 10, ȳ = 200, s_y = 40, r = 0.6, predict y at x = 65. b = 0.6 × 40/10 = 2.4, a = 200 − 2.4 × 50 = 80, ŷ = 80 + 2.4 × 65 = 236. Check with z-scores: z_x = 1.5, ẑ_y = 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9, ŷ = 200 + 0.9 × 40 = 236 ✓. The input was 1.5 SDs above average and the prediction is only 0.9 SDs above — the regression effect in one line.
  2. Same five numbers: predict x for a unit with y = 280. Refit the other way: b′ = 0.6 × 10/40 = 0.15, a′ = 50 − 0.15 × 200 = 20, x̂ = 20 + 0.15 × 280 = 62. The wrong answer, from inverting the first line, is (280 − 80)/2.4 = 83.3. Sanity check: y = 280 is z_y = 2, so x̂ must be at z_x = 0.6 × 2 = 1.2, i.e. 50 + 12 = 62 ✓, whereas 83.3 sits at z = 3.33 — more extreme than the value it was predicted from, which is impossible.
  3. What fraction of the variation is left unexplained when r = 0.6? 1 − r² = 0.64. And the residual spread is √0.64 = 0.8 of the original — a correlation most people would call substantial removes one fifth of the scatter.
  4. n = 25, b = 4.2, s_e = 6, s_x = 3. Test H₀: β₁ = 0 and give a 95% interval. S_xx = (n−1)s_x² = 24 × 9 = 216, SE(b) = 6/√216 = 0.40825, t = 4.2/0.40825 = 10.288 on 23 df, p = 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁰. t*₍₂₃₎ = 2.06866, so the interval is 4.2 ± 0.8445 = (3.356, 5.045).
  5. From that fit, what is R²? SSE = (n−2)s_e² = 23 × 36 = 828; SSR = b²S_xx = 17.64 × 216 = 3810.24; SST = 4638.24; R² = 0.8215, so r = +0.9064. Cross-check with the identity F = R²/(1−R²) × (n−2): 0.8215/0.1785 × 23 = 105.85 = t² ✓ (10.288² = 105.84).
  6. A study reports r = 0.30 among applicants who were admitted; the admitted group's s_x is 40% of the full applicant pool's. What is the full-pool correlation? u = 0.4, so ρ = (r/u)/√(1 + r²(1/u² − 1)) = 0.75/√(1 + 0.09 × 5.25) = 0.75/1.21347 = 0.6181. A "weak" correlation of 0.30 in a selected group is a substantial 0.62 in the population it was selected from.
  7. A predictor is measured with reliability λ = 0.7. The fitted slope is 1.4 and the observed correlation 0.5. What are the underlying values? Slope: β = 1.4/0.7 = 2.0. Correlation: ρ = 0.5/√0.7 = 0.598. Note the two corrections differ — the slope is divided by λ, the correlation by √λ.
  8. n = 12, x̄ = 10, S_xx = 318, s_e = 5. How much wider is the prediction interval than the confidence interval at x = 10, and at x = 18? At x̄: √(1 + 1/12)/√(1/12) = √13 = 3.606. At x = 18: v = 1/12 + 64/318 = 0.28459, ratio √1.28459/√0.28459 = 2.125. The two bands converge as you move away from x̄, because the line's own uncertainty grows while the individual noise term does not.
  9. Thirteen points; the thirteenth has h = 0.886 and a standardised residual of −1.80. Is it influential? Cook's D = (r²/(k+1))·h/(1−h) = (3.24/2) × 0.886/0.114 = 12.6, more than ten times the "investigate" threshold of 1 — despite a residual that no plot would flag. This is the fourth row of §16's table: removing it moves the slope by 14%. The residual is small because the point is influential.
  10. Two studies of the same relationship report r = 0.8 and r = 0.4. Name four explanations before concluding they disagree. Different spread of x (§06); different measurement reliability (§06); different units of analysis, e.g. one aggregated (§06); different ranges over which a non-linear relationship was approximated by a line (§04). Only after those are excluded is there a substantive disagreement — and the slopes may agree perfectly all along.
  11. A residual plot fans out to the right and the data span three orders of magnitude in both variables. What is the first thing to try? Log both axes. If the underlying structure is y = c·x^p·η with multiplicative error, log–log linearises the form and stabilises the variance in one step, and the slope becomes an elasticity.
  12. Fitting on log y gives an intercept 2.1 and slope 0.35. Interpret the slope, and predict y at x = 4. Each unit of x multiplies y by e^0.35 = 1.419, i.e. about +42%. At x = 4, log ŷ = 2.1 + 1.4 = 3.5, so e^3.5 = 33.1 — but that is an estimate of the geometric mean of y, which sits below E[Y|x = 4]. Back-transforming a log-scale fit under-predicts the mean.

Things to be able to derive from a blank page

  • The regression function r(x) = E[Y|X = x] as the minimiser of squared error, with the cross term vanishing.
  • r as the mean product of z-scores, and why the n / n − 1 conventions cancel.
  • |r| ≤ 1 from the non-negativity of Σ(z_x + t·z_y)², in three lines.
  • The two normal equations, and b = S_xy/S_xx = r·s_y/s_x, a = ȳ − bx̄ from them.
  • That the least-squares residuals satisfy Σeᵢ = 0 and Σxᵢeᵢ = 0, and that everything else follows from those two.
  • ẑ_y = r·z_x, and the regression effect as its immediate consequence.
  • Why the x-on-y line has slope r·s_x/s_y, and that the two slopes multiply to r².
  • SST = SSR + SSE, with the cross term killed by the normal equations, and hence R² = r².
  • SSE = S_yy(1 − r²), and the conditional spread √(1 − r²)·s_y.
  • b = β₁ + Σcᵢεᵢ with cᵢ = (xᵢ − x̄)/S_xx, and hence E[b] = β₁, Var(b) = σ²/S_xx.
  • SE(b) = s_e/√S_xx = s_e/(s_x√(n − 1)), and the three levers that move it.
  • Why the divisor is n − 2, by counting the constraints on the residuals.
  • The ANOVA table, E[MSR] = σ² + β₁²S_xx, and t² = F on (1, n − 2).
  • Var(ŷ(x_p)) = σ²(1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx), from ŷ = ȳ + b(x_p − x̄) and Cov(ȳ, b) = 0.
  • The extra σ² in the prediction interval, and that the ratio at x̄ is √(n + 1).
  • hᵢ = 1/n + (xᵢ − x̄)²/S_xx, Σhᵢ = 2, and Var(eᵢ) = σ²(1 − hᵢ).
  • Cook's D as residual × leverage, and why a small residual at high leverage is the dangerous case.
  • The attenuation results ρ* = ρ√λ and β* = βλ, from Cov(X*, Y) = Cov(X, Y).
two variables→a scatterplot→a form→r, distrusted six ways→a least-squares line→a model→SE(b), a t, an interval→two bands→residuals, leverage, influence→a claim you can defend
Where this goes next. Part 7, Resampling removes the assumptions this page rests on: bootstrap the pairs, refit the line on each resample, and read SE(b) off the spread of the bootstrapped slopes — no normality, no homoscedasticity, and a standard error that is honest even when §15's residual plot is not flat. It also gives a permutation test of H₀: β₁ = 0 by shuffling the y's against the x's. Part 8, Categorical Data is this page's association question when both variables are counts rather than measurements, with χ² in place of r. Part 9, ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons is §13's F ratio generalised: replace the single predictor by a grouping factor, and MSR/MSE becomes between-group over within-group variance on (k − 1, n − k) degrees of freedom — the same table, the same logic, and then the multiplicity problem that comes with testing many slopes at once.
Where this goes sideways. Into linear regression, which is this page with many predictors, a solver instead of a formula, and generalisation error instead of a p-value; into projections and least squares, where the normal equations become XᵀXβ̂ = Xᵀy and the residual's orthogonality of §11 becomes a picture; into covariance matrices, where r is a cosine and the whole correlation structure is one matrix; into regularisation, which trades §12's unbiasedness for variance and is §08's shrinkage used deliberately; into model evaluation, where R² is recomputed out of sample and allowed to be negative; into the bias–variance decomposition, which is §01's cross-term argument again; and back into part 5's duality, which has been quietly doing all the inferential work on this page from §13 onward.
