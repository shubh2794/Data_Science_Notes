12 The probability model, and what each assumption is holding up

Everything so far was arithmetic on twelve points and needed no assumptions at all. The moment you want a standard error, a p-value or an interval, you have to say what the twelve points are a sample of. That statement is the regression model, it has four parts, and each part is load-bearing for a specific downstream number — which means each has a specific failure mode.

The model

one equation, four assumptionsyᵢ = β₀ + β₁xᵢ + εᵢ , i = 1, …, n A1 · LINEARITY — the conditional mean really is a straight lineE[εᵢ] = 0 for every i ⟺ E[Y | X = x] = β₀ + β₁x A2 · CONSTANT VARIANCE (homoscedasticity) — the same spread at every xVar(εᵢ) = σ² for every i, not depending on xᵢ A3 · INDEPENDENCE — the errors carry no information about each otherCov(εᵢ, εⱼ) = 0 for i ≠ j A4 · NORMALITY — needed only for EXACT small-sample t and Fεᵢ ~ Normal(0, σ²) the three parameters, and what estimates eachβ₀ ← a = ȳ − bx̄ β₁ ← b = S_xy/S_xx σ ← s_e = √( SSE/(n − 2) )

The assumption ranking, which is not the order they are usually taught in. Independence is the most important and the least checked — it is the only one whose violation can be invisible in every plot of y against x. Linearity is next, and it is checkable. Constant variance is third and is usually fixable by a transformation. Normality is a distant fourth and matters mainly at very small n. Teaching order tends to be the reverse of this, because normality is the one with a picture attached.

Fixed x or random x?

  • Fixed-x (designed) regression: the xᵢ are chosen by the experimenter — doses, temperatures, batch sizes — and only y is random. All the distribution theory below is exact under A1–A4 with no further conditions, and S_xx is a design constant you control.
  • Random-x (observational) regression: both variables are sampled together. The same formulas remain valid conditionally on the observed x's, which is what every piece of software reports. This is why the standard errors below never contain a term for the randomness of x.
  • The practical difference is not in the arithmetic, it is in what r means. With fixed x, the correlation is not estimating any population parameter at all — you set the spread of x yourself, so §06's range-restriction argument says r is whatever you designed it to be. Report the slope. With random x, r estimates ρ and is meaningful.

Gauss–Markov, stated because it explains why anyone bothers with least squares. Under A1, A2 and A3 alone — with no normality assumption whatever — the least-squares estimators have the smallest variance among all estimators that are (i) linear functions of the y's and (ii) unbiased. "Best Linear Unbiased Estimator". Drop constant variance and the theorem drops with it: weighted least squares becomes the best linear unbiased estimator instead. Drop linearity of the estimator or unbiasedness and better estimators exist — which is exactly what ridge regression is, a biased estimator with lower total error (part 4, §03).

13 Inference on the slope: a standard error, a t, an interval — and the F that repeats them

This is where part 5's machine gets pointed at a coefficient, and almost nothing new is needed. The slope is an estimate; §12 gave it a standard deviation; replacing the unknown σ by s_e costs the same degrees-of-freedom tax as everywhere else and produces a t on n − 2. The one genuinely new thing is the ANOVA table, which reports the identical test in a different currency and is the bridge to part 9.

The third lever deserves a moment. S_xx grows like the square of the range of x, so doubling the spread of the predictor halves the slope's standard error — the same gain as quadrupling the sample size, at no extra cost in observations. In a designed experiment this is the cheapest precision available. The catch is stated in §16: pushing x to extremes only helps if the straight line is still the right model out there.

Simulation check on the formula. Two hundred thousand data sets were generated from y = 6 + 3x + ε with ε ~ Normal(0, 5²) at the twelve x-values of the running example. The mean of the 200 000 slopes was 2.99941 against a true 3, confirming unbiasedness; their standard deviation was 0.280379 against the formula's σ/√S_xx = 5/√318 = 0.280386 — agreeing to within the simulation's own error of 0.00044. The intercepts' standard deviation was 3.15277 against a predicted 3.15356. The t statistics passed a Kolmogorov–Smirnov comparison against t₁₀ with a distance of 0.0022, and the t interval for β₁ covered the truth in 94.98% of the runs.

The test and the interval

the pivot — an estimate minus its null value, over its standard error (part 5, §10)t = ( b − β₁⁰ ) / SE(b) ~ t on n − 2 degrees of freedom, under H₀ and A1–A4 the default test, which every software package prints without being askedH₀: β₁ = 0 ("x carries no linear information about y") H₁: β₁ ≠ 0 t = b/SE(b) two-sided p = 2·P( T₍ₙ₋₂₎ > |t| ) the interval, which says more and should be reported firstb ± t*₍ₙ₋₂, 1−α/2₎ · SE(b) the running data, computed to the endt = 3/0.2803861 = 10.69953 df = 10 p = 8.52 × 10⁻⁷ t*₍₁₀, 0.975₎ = 2.228139 95% CI: 3 ± 2.228139 × 0.2803861 = 3 ± 0.624739 = (2.37526, 3.62474) and the sentence it licenses"each additional component replaced is associated with between 2.4 and 3.6 more minutes on site, with 95% confidence." — an effect size, not a verdict.
  • The same t tests the correlation. Testing H₀: ρ = 0 uses t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²), and it is numerically identical to the slope t — on the running data both are 10.699533. They must be: b = 0 exactly when r = 0, so any sensible test of one is a test of the other. Reporting them as two findings is reporting one finding twice.
  • The interval and the test are the same statement (part 5, §09). The 95% interval excludes 0, so p < 0.05; and the exact p can be recovered from the interval alone. Prefer the interval, always.
  • The null β₁ = 0 is a default, not a law. If theory predicts a slope of 1 — a calibration curve, a unit-elasticity hypothesis — test H₀: β₁ = 1 with t = (b − 1)/SE(b). The interval answers every such question at once, which is the third reason to prefer it.
  • Rejecting β₁ = 0 establishes almost nothing. It says the linear component is distinguishable from zero at this sample size. It does not say the relationship is linear, that it is large, that it is causal, or that the model is adequate. At n = 10 000 a slope of no practical size at all will clear the bar (part 5, §12).
  • A confidence interval for the intercept is usually meaningless, because x = 0 is usually outside the data — extrapolation (§16). Here it is borderline defensible: zero components replaced is a real scenario, and the interval 6 ± 2.228139 × 3.153564 = (−1.03, 13.03) honestly reports that twelve visits cannot pin down the fixed cost of a call.

  • Why bother with F at all, if it is t²? Because it generalises and t does not. With k predictors there are k separate t statistics and no single one of them tests "is any of this useful"; the F does, on k numerator degrees of freedom, in one test with one error rate.   • t is two-sided; F has no sides. Squaring destroys the sign, so the F can only ever reproduce a two-sided t. If you genuinely want a one-sided slope test, use the t.
  • The identity t² = F is exact, not asymptotic. A t on ν df squared is an F on (1, ν) df, by the definition of both as ratios involving the same chi-squares. Two hundred thousand simulated data sets agreed to 2 × 10⁻¹⁴ — floating-point noise — and the simulated rejection rate at the 5% level under a true null was 5.02%.
  • The table's F tests only what it says. A large F means "not all of this variation is noise"; it does not validate the straight-line form, and §15's residual plot can be catastrophic while the F is enormous.

14 Two bands around one line: confidence and prediction

Software draws a shaded region around a fitted line and calls it "the confidence band", and almost everyone reads it as "where the next observation will fall". It is not. There are two bands, they answer two different questions, the wider one is usually the one you wanted, and at moderate n the wider one is several times the width of the narrower.

The two questions

question 1
Where is the line?
"For all units with x = 14, what is their average y?" The target is E[Y | X = 14] = β₀ + 14β₁ — a fixed unknown number. The only uncertainty is in the two estimated coefficients. → the confidence band.
question 2
Where is the next point?
"The next unit has x = 14. What will its y be?" The target is a random variable, not a parameter. The uncertainty is the line's uncertainty plus that unit's own error term. → the prediction band.

The two standard errors, derived

first, a re-parameterisation that makes the algebra fall out: centre the line at x̄ŷ(x_p) = a + b x_p = ȳ + b(x_p − x̄) since a = ȳ − bx̄ and ȳ and b are UNCORRELATED, which is the fact that kills every cross termCov(ȳ, b) = Cov( (1/n)Σyᵢ , Σcᵢyᵢ ) = (σ²/n)Σcᵢ = 0 (§12: Σcᵢ = 0) so the variance of the fitted value is a clean sum of two termsVar( ŷ(x_p) ) = Var(ȳ) + (x_p − x̄)² Var(b) = σ²/n + σ²(x_p − x̄)²/S_xx SE( ŷ(x_p) ) = s_e √( 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx ) ← the CONFIDENCE band's SE a NEW observation at x_p adds its own independent error, and nothing elsey_new − ŷ(x_p) = ε_new − [ ŷ(x_p) − E(Y|x_p) ] the two pieces are independent Var( y_new − ŷ(x_p) ) = σ² + σ²/n + σ²(x_p − x̄)²/S_xx SE( prediction ) = s_e √( 1 + 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx ) ← the PREDICTION band's SE both use the same multiplier, on n − 2 degrees of freedomconfidence: ŷ(x_p) ± t*₍ₙ₋₂₎ · s_e √( 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx ) prediction: ŷ(x_p) ± t*₍ₙ₋₂₎ · s_e √( 1 + 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx ) the ONLY difference is a single leading 1 under the root — and that 1 is the whole pointit is the irreducible noise of an individual observation, which no amount of data removes.

Three properties that follow from the formulas alone

  • The prediction band is always wider, at every x. The radicand differs by exactly 1, so the ratio of half-widths is √(1 + v)/√v with v = 1/n + (x_p − x̄)²/S_xx. At x_p = x̄ this is √(n + 1) — a factor of 3.6 at n = 12, 5.6 at n = 30, 10.0 at n = 100. The gap grows with the sample size, because the confidence band shrinks and the prediction band does not.
  • Both are narrowest at x̄ and widen as |x_p − x̄| grows, and both widen hyperbolically — the boundary is a hyperbola, not a pair of straight lines. The mechanism is visible in the re-parameterised form: the fitted line pivots about (x̄, ȳ), so uncertainty in the slope has no effect at the pivot and a leverage-arm effect proportional to the distance from it.
  • As n → ∞ the confidence band collapses onto the true line; the prediction band does not collapse at all. It converges to ±t*σ, roughly ±1.96σ. Infinite data tells you the line exactly and still leaves you ignorant about the next individual. This is the single most useful sentence in the section, and it is the reason "our model has a very tight confidence band" is not a claim about predictive accuracy.

Four traps

Wrong: "95% of the data points fall inside the shaded band."
Right: that is a property of the prediction band, and even then only approximately — the band was built from these same points, so it is slightly optimistic in-sample. The confidence band typically contains a small minority of the data; at n = 100 it contains roughly a tenth of them, and that is correct behaviour, not a defect.
Wrong: "the band is 95% confident along its whole length."
Right: both formulas are pointwise. Each x separately has 95% coverage; the probability that the true line lies inside the band everywhere at once is much less. A simultaneous band needs a bigger multiplier — the Working–Hotelling one, √(2·F₍₂,ₙ₋₂;1−α₎), which for n = 12 is √(2 × 4.10282) = 2.8645 instead of t* = 2.2281, so the band is 29% wider. Software almost always draws the pointwise one.
Wrong: "with enough data the prediction interval will get tight."
Right: it converges to ±t*σ and stops. If that is too wide to be useful, the answer is a better model or a less noisy measurement — never more rows.
Wrong: "the prediction interval is as trustworthy as the confidence interval."
Right: it is much less so, and for a specific reason. The confidence band is protected by the central limit theorem — a and b are averages, so they are near-normal whatever the errors look like. The prediction band's leading σ² is the distribution of one error term, and no averaging happens to it. If the errors are skewed or heavy-tailed, the prediction interval is wrong in a way that more data cannot fix, and the honest alternatives are a quantile-based interval or the resampling methods of part 7.

15 Residual plots: the picture that decides whether §13 and §14 were fiction

Every standard error, interval and p-value on this page was computed under A1–A4, and none of them contains any information about whether those assumptions hold. The residuals do. A residual plot is not an optional extra after the analysis; it is the step that decides whether the analysis existed.

Transformations: the ladder, and what each rung is for

A curved or fan-shaped plot does not mean regression is unusable. It usually means regression is being applied on the wrong scale. Re-expressing one or both variables can straighten the form and equalise the spread simultaneously, because the two problems very often have the same cause: a quantity that behaves multiplicatively being modelled additively.

  • Transform y to fix the spread; transform x to fix the shape. A useful first rule, because y's transform is the one that changes the error structure and x's is the one that changes the form. Often you need both, and the second is chosen after the first has been applied.
  • Transforming y changes what the model is about, and this is under-reported. Least squares on log y estimates the mean of log y, and exp of that is the geometric mean of y — a median-like quantity, not E[Y|x]. Back-transforming a fitted value therefore under-predicts the mean, systematically. Say which quantity you are predicting, or use a model that works on the original scale.
  • Logs need positive data. Zeros and negatives force a shift (log(y + c)) whose constant c is arbitrary and materially changes the fit. If zeros are common and meaningful, a transformation is the wrong tool.
  • Robust standard errors are the alternative when you want to keep the original scale. Heteroscedasticity-consistent (sandwich) standard errors leave b alone and repair only its standard error. They fix A2 without touching A1, they need a decent n, and they are the standard move when the untransformed slope is the quantity of interest.
  • Weighted least squares is the principled fix when the variance pattern is known. If Var(εᵢ) = σ²/wᵢ, minimising Σwᵢeᵢ² restores the Gauss–Markov optimality of §12. The difficulty is that wᵢ is almost never known and estimating it from the same residuals is circular.

16 Outliers, leverage, influence — and the point the residual plot cannot find

Three different things get called "a bad point", and conflating them is the reason a well-known failure mode survives every diagnostic. An outlier is far from the line. A high-leverage point is far from x̄. An influential point is one whose removal moves the fit. They are independent properties: a point can be any combination of the three, and the most dangerous combination is high leverage with a small residual.

Measuring influence: delete it and look

Only refitting finds it.
  • The mechanism, in one sentence: a high-leverage point drags the line towards itself, and once the line has arrived the residual is small — so the diagnostic that measures disagreement with the line reports success precisely because the point won.
  • The procedure that actually works is not a statistic, it is a habit: identify the points with extreme x, refit without each of them, and report the slope both ways. If the conclusion changes, the conclusion was never about the data set, it was about one row of it.
  • Deleting points is a last resort and must be disclosed. Delete when you can show the value is wrong — a transcription error, a broken sensor, a unit mix-up. Do not delete because a point is inconvenient. The honest report is both analyses and the reason for preferring one.
  • An outlier is often the most informative point in the data. The county whose vote count sits far above a log–log fit is not noise to be trimmed; it is the finding.
  • Robust regression makes this automatic. Least absolute deviations and Huber regression bound the influence any single point can have, so the fit does not need protecting by hand. The cost is the loss of the closed-form standard errors of §13.

Extrapolation

the arithmetic never objects — that is the problemrunning data: x ranges from 2 to 18. Predict at x = 40: ŷ = 6 + 3 × 40 = 126 minutes 95% prediction interval at x = 40: (103.96, 148.04) confidently, precisely, absurd what the interval is actually claiming, and why it cannot deliver itthe band is computed under A1 — that the line holds at x = 40. There is no evidence for that anywhere in the data, and the band cannot express uncertainty about A1 itself. the honest statement"the model was fitted for 2 ≤ x ≤ 18 and is not evidence about x outside that range."
  • Linear relationships are almost always local. Fertiliser increases yield until it poisons the soil; dose increases response until it saturates; a growth curve is exponential until the resource runs out. The line was an approximation over the observed range and there is no reason for the approximation to survive outside it.
  • The band widens outside the data, which lulls rather than warns. At x = 25 the running example's prediction interval is (66.1, 95.9) — 29% wider than at x̄, which looks like appropriate caution. It is not: it is the uncertainty given that the line is right, and it says nothing about the risk that it is not. Model uncertainty is the dominant term out there and appears in no formula on this page.
  • The boundary is not just the range of x. With several predictors, an extrapolation can be a combination of values each of which is individually ordinary — inside every marginal range and outside the observed cloud. Leverage hᵢ is the general measure of "far from the data" and is what to check.
  • Deliberate extrapolation is sometimes the whole point — calibration, forecasting, dose-finding. It is legitimate when the functional form comes from theory rather than from the scatterplot. What is never legitimate is quoting the interval as if it covered the risk.
