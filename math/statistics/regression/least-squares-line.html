07 The least-squares line, derived from the criterion

Among all the straight lines that could be drawn through a cloud, one is singled out by a rule: minimise the total squared vertical distance from the points to the line. Two derivatives and one substitution turn that rule into two formulas, and the formulas turn out to be the correlation in disguise. Everything downstream — the fitted values, the residuals, R², the standard errors — is a consequence of the two equations the derivatives produce.

The criterion

a candidate line assigns each xᵢ a predicted valueŷᵢ = a + b·xᵢ residual eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ (vertical, signed) choose a and b to minimise the sum of SQUARED residualsQ(a, b) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ ( yᵢ − a − b·xᵢ )² note what is being measured: VERTICAL gaps, not perpendicular onesthe criterion is asymmetric in x and y by construction — and §09 is the consequence

The derivation, in full

set the two partial derivatives to zero — the NORMAL EQUATIONS∂Q/∂a = −2 Σ( yᵢ − a − b xᵢ ) = 0 ⟹ Σ eᵢ = 0 ∂Q/∂b = −2 Σ xᵢ( yᵢ − a − b xᵢ ) = 0 ⟹ Σ xᵢ eᵢ = 0 the first equation, divided by n, gives the intercept immediatelyȳ − a − b x̄ = 0 ⟹ a = ȳ − b x̄ ⟹ the line passes through (x̄, ȳ) substitute that into the second and the intercept disappearsΣ xᵢ( yᵢ − ȳ + b x̄ − b xᵢ ) = 0 Σ xᵢ( yᵢ − ȳ ) − b Σ xᵢ( xᵢ − x̄ ) = 0 and both sums may be re-centred for free, because Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0Σ xᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ) = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = S_xy Σ xᵢ(xᵢ − x̄) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = S_xx giving the slopeb = S_xy / S_xx = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² and, dividing numerator and denominator by (n − 1), the form worth memorisingb = s_xy / s_x² = r · s_y / s_x a = ȳ − b x̄

The rewrite b = r·s_y/s_x is the single most useful identity on this page. It says the slope is the correlation, converted from unit-free to real units by multiplying by the ratio of the spreads. Two clouds with the same r and different spreads have different slopes; two clouds with the same slope and different scatter have different r. Slope and correlation are not the same quantity and neither determines the other without s_x and s_y.

Is it really a minimum?

Q is a quadratic in (a, b), so the Hessian is constant∂²Q/∂a² = 2n ∂²Q/∂a∂b = 2Σxᵢ ∂²Q/∂b² = 2Σxᵢ² its determinant4[ nΣxᵢ² − (Σxᵢ)² ] = 4n Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = 4n · S_xx so the Hessian is positive definite exactly when the x's are not all identicalS_xx > 0 ⟹ strictly convex ⟹ the stationary point is the UNIQUE global minimum S_xx = 0 ⟹ every xᵢ equal; no line is determined, and b is undefined (division by zero)

The five algebraic consequences you should be able to recite

  • The residuals sum to zero, Σeᵢ = 0 — that is the first normal equation. So the mean residual is zero automatically, for any data, and a residual plot centred on zero is not evidence of anything.
  • The residuals are orthogonal to the predictor, Σxᵢeᵢ = 0 — the second normal equation. So r(x, e) = 0 always: any leftover linear structure has already been removed. A residual plot can therefore never show a tilt, and if it appears to, you have a bug.
  • The line passes through the point of averages (x̄, ȳ), so ŷ = ȳ whenever x = x̄. Predicting at the mean returns the mean.
  • The fitted values have the same mean as the data, Σŷᵢ = Σyᵢ, and are also orthogonal to the residuals, Σŷᵢeᵢ = a Σeᵢ + b Σxᵢeᵢ = 0. That orthogonality is what makes the variance decomposition of §11 exact rather than approximate.
  • In standard units the line is ẑ_y = r · z_x, slope r, intercept 0. Every regression is the same regression once both variables are standardised — which is the whole content of §08.

Why squares, and what it costs

CriterionWhat it fitsBehaviour
Sum of squared residuals Σeᵢ²The conditional mean of y given xDifferentiable, unique closed-form solution, decomposes the variance exactly. Highly sensitive to outliers: a residual of 10 counts 100 times a residual of 1.
Sum of absolute residuals Σ|eᵢ|The conditional medianResistant to outliers; no closed form, solved by linear programming; the fit can be non-unique.
Huber loss — squared near zero, linear in the tailsA compromiseThe standard robust default. Keeps the smooth optimisation and caps the influence of a single point.
Perpendicular distance to the lineThe major axis of the cloudA different problem entirely: symmetric in x and y, not scale-invariant, and the solution is the first principal component. See PCA.
Sum of squares of horizontal gapsThe regression of x on yYet another line. §09.
  • Squares are not a mathematical necessity, they are three coincidences that happen to line up. They give a closed form; they estimate the conditional mean, which §01 showed is the optimal squared-error predictor; and under normal errors they are simultaneously the maximum-likelihood estimator and — by the Gauss–Markov theorem, with no normality needed — the minimum-variance estimator among all linear unbiased ones.
  • The price is paid entirely in the tails. Squaring means one badly mismeasured point can move the line more than fifty good ones; §16 quantifies exactly how much.
  • The three criteria in the table are three different lines, not three estimates of one line — except in the special case of a symmetric, homoscedastic, outlier-free cloud, where they all nearly coincide. It is the cases where they disagree that matter.

The line as a graph of averages

There is a second reading of the fitted line that requires no calculus and explains why it predicts well. Slice the scatterplot into narrow vertical strips. In each strip, take the average of the y-values of the points it contains. Plot those averages against the strip centres. If the underlying relationship is linear, the strip averages fall close to the least-squares line — and they do so because §01's theorem says the conditional mean is the best squared-error predictor, and least squares is estimating exactly that conditional mean under a straight-line constraint.

the two readings of ŷ = a + bx, which are the same statementMECHANICAL: the line that minimises Σ(yᵢ − a − bxᵢ)² STATISTICAL: an estimate of the AVERAGE y among all units whose x is near this x and why an average is a good prediction at allamong all constants c, the one minimising Σ(yᵢ − c)² is c = ȳ (part 1, §04) regression does the same thing, conditionally: ȳ within the strip at x, forced onto a line
Where this leaves the fitted line. It is a description of the average y at each x, computed by a rule that is convenient, optimal under conditions that have not yet been stated, and fragile in ways §16 will make precise. It is not yet an estimate of anything, because no population has been mentioned — §12 supplies that. Everything in §08 to §11 is still pure algebra on the twelve points.

08 Regression to the mean, and the fallacy named after it

The word "regression" does not mean "fitting a line". It means going back, and it entered statistics as the name of a phenomenon: predictions are less extreme, in standard units, than the values they are predicted from. This is a two-line algebraic consequence of |r| < 1, it is unavoidable, it has no mechanism behind it, and roughly a century and a half of confident causal explanations have been offered for it anyway.

The two lines of algebra

write the fitted line in standard units — substitute a = ȳ − bx̄ and b = r·s_y/s_xŷ = ȳ + r (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) ⟹ (ŷ − ȳ)/s_y = r · (x − x̄)/s_x that is, in z-scoresẑ_y = r · z_x with |r| ≤ 1 so the prediction is ALWAYS pulled towards the mean, in standard units|ẑ_y| = |r|·|z_x| ≤ |z_x| with equality only in the degenerate case |r| = 1 in words"x is two SDs above average" ⟹ "predict y to be 2r SDs above average", not 2.

This is not a claim about a mechanism, a tendency, a force, or a drift. It is what the least-squares line is. Any prediction rule that is a linear function of x and gets the mean right must shrink towards the mean whenever the correlation is imperfect, because shrinking is what minimising squared error requires when the predictor is noisy.

The worked example

A class sits a midterm and a final. The two scores are correlated but not perfectly, and the cloud is football-shaped.

the five numbers that determine everythingmidterm: x̄ = 49.5 s_x = 10.2 final: ȳ = 69.1 s_y = 11.8 r = 0.67 the line, by hand — no software requiredb = r · s_y/s_x = 0.67 × 11.8/10.2 = 0.67 × 1.156863 = 0.7750980 a = ȳ − b x̄ = 69.1 − 0.7750980 × 49.5 = 69.1 − 38.367353 = 30.7326471 ŷ = 30.7326 + 0.77510 x predict the final score of a student who scored 41 on the midtermŷ = 30.7326 + 0.77510 × 41 = 62.5117 ≈ 62.5 the same answer via z-scores, which shows the regression effect directlyz_x = (41 − 49.5)/10.2 = −0.83333 ẑ_y = 0.67 × (−0.83333) = −0.55833 ŷ = 69.1 + (−0.55833)(11.8) = 69.1 − 6.5883 = 62.5117 ✓ same to the last digit read the two z-scores side by side — this is the whole phenomenonthe student was 0.833 SDs BELOW average on the midterm and is predicted only 0.558 SDs below average on the final 0.558 = 0.67 × 0.833

Notice which way this cuts. The prediction for a weak midterm score is above the corresponding position on the final — the student is predicted to improve, in relative terms. The prediction for a strong midterm score is below its corresponding position. The shrinkage is symmetric, towards the middle from both ends, and it happens with no assumption about the students at all.

Test and retest: the top group falls back, the bottom group climbs drag the shaded midterm band and read the two averages

Why it happens: a heuristic that is actually correct

Decompose each score into a stable part and a transient part — preparation plus luck, skill plus noise, signal plus measurement error. To land in the very top group on the midterm, a student almost certainly needed both a high stable part and a favourable transient part. The stable part persists to the final; the transient part is redrawn. So the group's average transient contribution, which was positive on the midterm by selection, is about zero on the final, and the group falls back. The same argument in reverse lifts the bottom group.

the decomposition that makes the heuristic exactx = S + U₁ y = S + U₂ S the stable part, U₁ U₂ independent transient parts then the correlation is exactly the fraction of variance that is stabler = Var(S) / ( Var(S) + Var(U) ) assuming Var(U₁) = Var(U₂) = Var(U) so r is the RELIABILITY of the measurement — the same λ as in §06 selecting on x selects partly on U₁, which does not carry overE[ z_y | z_x = c ] = r·c the fraction r of the selection survives; the rest was luck simulated: 400 000 students, r = 0.67, take the top decile of the MIDTERMtheir mean midterm z = +1.752 their mean final z = +1.179 ratio 0.673 ≈ r ✓ take the bottom decile: mean midterm z = −1.754 mean final z = −1.174 they IMPROVED and — the detail that kills every causal story — it works backwards tootake the top decile of the FINAL: mean final z = +1.756, mean MIDTERM z = +1.180 the best finalists had less extreme midterms. Time's arrow is irrelevant to the algebra.

The regression fallacy

The fallacy is to observe the fall-back and attribute it to a cause. It is one of the most reliably reproducible errors in applied statistics, because the effect is real, visible, and always available to be explained.

ObservationThe causal story that gets toldWhat regression to the mean already predicts
The top midterm scorers do worse on the final"They got complacent and stopped working."Their final z-scores should average r times their midterm z-scores. No behaviour change needed.
The bottom scorers improve"The extra tutoring worked."Same algebra, opposite sign. Any intervention aimed at the worst performers will appear to work.
Accidents fall after a camera is installed at a blackspot"The camera reduced accidents."Sites are chosen because they had an extreme year. The next year is less extreme regardless.
Pilots praised after an excellent landing do worse next time; pilots criticised after a bad one improve"Criticism works, praise backfires."Landings are noisy. Extreme performances are followed by less extreme ones in both directions.
The sickest patients improve most under the new treatment"The treatment is most effective in severe cases."Selection on an extreme measured value; the next measurement regresses. This is why single-arm before/after studies are uninterpretable.
An outstanding first season is followed by a mediocre second"Second-season slump — the pressure got to them."The first season was selected on for being outstanding.
The diagnostic question, and the fix. Were the units selected for study because of an extreme value of the very variable being re-measured? If yes, expect regression to the mean and do not attribute it to anything. The fix is not statistical, it is a design: a control group selected the same way. Both groups regress; the difference between them is what the intervention did. This is the single most important reason part 2, §09 insists on a comparison group even when the before/after change looks overwhelming.
  • The effect is strongest when r is small — that is, when the measurement is noisy. A perfectly reliable measurement (r = 1) shows no regression at all; a pure-noise measurement (r = 0) regresses all the way to the mean, and every selected group's follow-up average is simply ȳ.
  • It is not a claim that the spread shrinks over time. The predictions are less spread out than the data — SD(ŷ) = |r|·s_y — but the actual final scores have the same spread as the midterm scores in standard units. Nothing is converging. If the whole distribution were really collapsing towards its mean, exam scores would have become identical generations ago.
  • It applies to any pair of imperfectly correlated measurements, in either time order, with no requirement that one causes the other or even that they are measured at different times.

09 Two regression lines, and the third line that is neither

Regression is not symmetric. Predicting y from x and predicting x from y are two different problems with two different answers, and the second answer is not obtained by solving the first for x. Getting this wrong is the most common purely mechanical error in the subject, and it is easy to avoid with one rule.

Why there are two lines

Least squares minimises vertical distances — gaps in the direction of the variable being predicted. Turn the plot on its side and "vertical" means something else, so a different line minimises it. Both lines pass through (x̄, ȳ); they differ in slope, and the angle between them is a direct visual reading of how far r is from 1.

line 1 — regress y on x: minimise Σ(yᵢ − a − bxᵢ)²ŷ = ȳ + r (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) slope in the (x, y) plot: b = r·s_y/s_x line 2 — regress x on y: minimise Σ(xᵢ − a′ − b′yᵢ)²x̂ = x̄ + r (s_x/s_y)(y − ȳ) slope in the (y, x) plot: b′ = r·s_x/s_y drawn on the SAME (x, y) axes, line 2 has slope 1/b′, which is steeper than b1/b′ = s_y/(r·s_x) vs b = r·s_y/s_x ratio = 1/r² ≥ 1 the product of the two slopes is r², which is the neatest statement of the whole sectionb · b′ = r² they coincide ⟺ r² = 1 ⟺ the points are exactly collinear and the line lying between them, slope s_y/s_x, is the SD LINESD line: y = ȳ + (s_y/s_x)(x − x̄) — it passes through the "corner" of the cloud, is what the eye wants to draw, and predicts NOTHING optimally. It is neither regression line.

Both regression lines and the SD line on one cloud slide r to zero and watch the two lines swing to perpendicular

The rule, and the geometry behind it

  • The rule: always put the variable you are given on the horizontal axis, and refit. Never algebraically invert a fitted line. If you are given y and want x, the regression you need is the regression of x on y, and its coefficients are b′ = r·s_x/s_y, a′ = x̄ − b′ȳ.
  • The two lines pinch together as |r| → 1 and open to a right angle as r → 0. At r = 0 the y-on-x line is horizontal at ȳ (best guess: the mean, ignore x) and the x-on-y line is vertical at x̄. The angle between them is a live display of the correlation.
  • Applying both lines in succession does not return you to where you started. Predict y from x, then x from that ŷ, and the composition has slope b·b′ = r² — you end up shrunk towards x̄ by a factor r². Two regressions to the mean, compounded.
  • The SD line is what the eye draws and it is not an estimator of anything. It has slope s_y/s_x, passes through (x̄, ȳ), and hits the corners of the elliptical cloud. Since b = r·s_y/s_x, the regression line is always flatter than the SD line by exactly the factor r. Confusing the two is the regression fallacy in geometric form: the SD line is the line along which "no regression to the mean" would happen.
  • Nor is the SD line the major axis of the cloud. It is the first principal direction of the standardised cloud carried back into the original units. The genuine major axis — the perpendicular-distance fit of §07 — is a third line again, and it moves if you change the units of either variable, which is exactly why it is not used for prediction.
Three lines, three jobs. y on x: the best guess for y when x is known. x on y: the best guess for x when y is known. The SD line: a description of the cloud's orientation in standard units, and the reference against which the regression effect is measured. Only the first two are predictions, and each answers exactly one of the two questions.

10 The normal approximation inside a vertical strip

The fitted line gives the centre of the y-values at a given x. To answer a question like "what fraction of these units exceed 60?" you need the spread as well — and if the cloud is football-shaped, that spread is a single number, the same at every x, and it is s_y shrunk by a factor that depends only on r.

The conditional spread

for a bivariate normal pair, the conditional distribution of Y given X = x is normalY | X = x ~ Normal( μ_y + ρ(σ_y/σ_x)(x − μ_x) , σ_y²(1 − ρ²) ) the mean is the regression line, and the SD does not depend on x at allcentre: ŷ = a + bx spread: σ_y√(1 − ρ²) ← the same in every strip so to standardise a y-value INSIDE a strip, use the line and that shrunken SDz = ( y − ŷ ) / ( √(1 − r²) · s_y ) then read the normal table the shrinkage factor, tabulated — it is unforgivingly slow at firstr = 0.30 0.50 0.67 0.80 0.90 0.95 0.99 √(1−r²) = 0.954 0.866 0.742 0.600 0.436 0.312 0.141 a correlation of 0.5 removes only 13% of the spread; 0.9 removes 56%.

The table is worth staring at. Knowing x shrinks the uncertainty about y by the factor √(1 − r²), and that factor is close to 1 for every correlation people usually describe as "moderate". A correlation of 0.5 — respectable in most fields — leaves 87% of the original spread. This is the honest counterweight to the impressiveness of a scatterplot: an association can be unmistakable to the eye and still buy almost no predictive precision.

Where the shrunken SD comes from without assuming normality

the residual sum of squares always factors this way — pure algebra, proved in §11SSE = Σeᵢ² = S_yy(1 − r²) so the root-mean-square residual, with the n − 1 divisor, is exactly the shrunken SD√( SSE/(n − 1) ) = √( S_yy(1 − r²)/(n − 1) ) = s_y √(1 − r²) the INFERENTIAL estimate of σ uses n − 2 instead, because two parameters were fitted (§12)s_e = √( SSE/(n − 2) ) = s_y √(1 − r²) · √( (n − 1)/(n − 2) ) on the running data the two differ by 5%, which at n = 12 is not negligibles_y√(1 − r²) = 16.81990 × 0.283433 = 4.76733 s_e = 5 exactly ratio = √(11/10) = 1.048809 5/4.76733 = 1.048805 ✓

So √(1 − r²)·s_y is a descriptive quantity — the RMS vertical scatter about the fitted line in this sample — while s_e is an estimate of a population parameter and pays a degrees-of-freedom tax for it. Use the first for normal-approximation questions about the data in hand, and the second for every standard error from §13 onward. At n = 100 they differ by half a percent and nobody distinguishes them; at n = 12 they differ by 5%.

  • Two different questions, two different spreads — and this is the seed of §14. "Where is the average final score for students at 41?" is a question about the line, and its uncertainty is small. "Where is one particular student's final score?" is a question about an individual, and its uncertainty includes the whole conditional spread. This section answered the second kind, assuming the line is known exactly; §14 removes that assumption and gets the two bands.
  • The approximation needs the football shape, and says so out loud. Three things must hold: the conditional means fall on a line, the conditional spread is the same in every strip (homoscedasticity), and the conditional distribution is roughly normal. The residual plot of §15 is the tool for checking the first two; a normal quantile plot of the residuals checks the third.
  • Where it fails loudest. A fan-shaped cloud has a different spread in every strip, so one number cannot describe them all, and the answer will be badly wrong at both ends. A curved cloud has the centre wrong as well as the spread. Both are transformable (§15).
  • Conditioning is what removes the uncertainty, and it removes only r² of it. The variance falls from σ_y² to σ_y²(1 − r²) — a fraction r² removed. That sentence is §11 stated in advance.

11 R² = r², and the exact splitting of the variance

The total variation in y divides, exactly and without remainder, into a part the line accounts for and a part it does not. The identity is a Pythagorean theorem in disguise — it holds because least squares makes the residual vector orthogonal to the fitted one — and the ratio it defines is the most reported and most over-read number in applied statistics.

The decomposition

split each deviation from the mean at the fitted valueyᵢ − ȳ = ( ŷᵢ − ȳ ) + ( yᵢ − ŷᵢ ) = explained part + residual part square and sum — the cross term vanishes, which is the whole contentΣ(yᵢ − ȳ)² = Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)² + Σeᵢ² + 2Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)eᵢ and Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)eᵢ = Σ(a + bxᵢ − ȳ)eᵢ = (a − ȳ)Σeᵢ + bΣxᵢeᵢ = 0 + 0 = 0 — by the two normal equations of §07, and by nothing else. Hence, EXACTLY:SST = SSR + SSE Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² = Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)² + Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² total regression error the coefficient of determination is the fraction on the leftR² = SSR/SST = 1 − SSE/SST 0 ≤ R² ≤ 1

The cancellation of the cross term is the reason people say the decomposition is "Pythagorean": in ℝⁿ, the centred data vector is the hypotenuse and the fitted and residual vectors are the two perpendicular legs. That orthogonality is not a property of the data — it is forced by the least-squares criterion. Fit the line by any other rule and the identity fails.

Why R² equals r² in simple regression

the fitted values are an affine function of x, so their deviations are b times x'sŷᵢ − ȳ = b(xᵢ − x̄) ⟹ SSR = b² Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = b² S_xx substitute b = S_xy/S_xxSSR = (S_xy/S_xx)² S_xx = S_xy²/S_xx divide by SST = S_yyR² = S_xy² / (S_xx S_yy) = [ S_xy/√(S_xx S_yy) ]² = r² and therefore, rearranged, the identity §10 usedSSE = SST(1 − r²) = S_yy(1 − r²) on the running data, every number exactlySST = 3112 SSR = b²S_xx = 9 × 318 = 2862 SSE = 3112 − 2862 = 250 R² = 2862/3112 = 0.9196658 r² = 0.9589921² = 0.9196658 ✓ 1 − R² = 250/3112 = 0.0803342 ← the fraction of the variation left in the residuals

What R² means, said carefully

correct
"92% of the variation in y is accounted for by the linear fit on x"
A statement about a sum of squares in this sample. It is arithmetic, not inference, and it makes no claim about mechanism.
correct
"the residual spread is √(1 − R²) times the original spread"
The version worth quoting, because it is on the scale of the data. R² = 0.92 means the scatter fell to √0.08 = 0.28 of what it was: a genuinely large reduction.
wrong
"92% of y is caused by x"
No causal content whatever (§05). R² is as large for a confounded association as for a mechanism.
wrong
"R² is high, so the model is right"
A curved relationship can have R² = 0.95 against a straight-line fit while the residual plot screams. R² measures tightness, not correctness of form.
wrong
"R² is low, so there is nothing here"
A small, real, precisely estimated slope can have a tiny R². In noisy domains an R² of 0.02 with a tight confidence interval is a finding; in a physics calibration it would be a broken instrument.
careful
"R² is comparable across studies"
It is not. R² depends on the spread of x in the sample exactly as r does (§06), so a narrower sample of the same relationship reports a smaller R² with no change in the world.

Six further facts, each of which stops a misuse

  • R² never decreases when a predictor is added, even a column of random numbers, because the larger model contains the smaller one and the minimum of a sum of squares over a bigger set cannot be larger. This is why R² cannot be used to choose between models of different sizes; adjusted R² = 1 − (SSE/(n − k − 1))/(SST/(n − 1)) penalises the extra parameters and can go down. Neither is a substitute for held-out error (model evaluation).
  • R² = r² only for simple regression with an intercept. With several predictors, √R² is the multiple correlation — the correlation between y and the fitted values ŷ — which is a different and always non-negative quantity. Fit without an intercept and SST = SSR + SSE itself breaks, which is why software reports a different, non-comparable R² for through-the-origin models.
  • R² is a sample quantity and is optimistically biased. Even when x is pure noise, the expected R² from fitting k predictors to n points is about k/(n − 1). With n = 11 and ten random predictors it reaches 1 exactly, having explained nothing.
  • 1 − R² is the number to quote when the audience is optimistic. "R² = 0.7" sounds like most of the job is done; "30% of the variation is unexplained, and the residual SD is 55% of the original" is the same fact and gives a truer impression.
  • The regression F statistic of §13 is a monotone function of R²: F = R²/(1 − R²) × (n − 2)/1. So the test of "is there any linear association at all" is literally a test on R², rescaled by the sample size — which is why a tiny R² can be overwhelmingly significant at large n.
  • R² is not the fraction of y explained, it is the fraction of y's variance explained. The two get conflated constantly, and the distinction is what makes the √(1 − R²) version above so much more informative.
