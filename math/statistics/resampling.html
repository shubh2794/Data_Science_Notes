Resampling

Parts 4, 5 and 6 handed over a complete inferential machine and one hidden dependency. Part 4 gave an estimate with a standard error and turned the pair into a confidence interval; part 5 gave the same pair a null value and turned it into a p-value; part 6 pointed both at the slope of a line. Every one of those results needed a formula for the standard error and a named distribution for the statistic — s/√n and Student's t, s_e/√S_xx and t on n − 2. Most statistics have neither. There is no formula for the standard error of a median, a trimmed mean, a ratio of two means, a correlation, a 90th percentile, or the output of any model with a search in it; and where a formula exists it is usually an asymptotic approximation whose small-print you cannot check. This part replaces both dependencies with computation. The bootstrap manufactures the sampling distribution of anything you can compute, by resampling the data with replacement. The permutation test manufactures the null distribution of anything you can compute, by shuffling the labels. Between them they cover most of parts 4–6 with almost no assumptions — and they have their own failure modes, which §16 makes as concrete as the successes.

01 Two questions, one idea: resample the data instead of trusting a formula

Everything on this page follows from a single move — when you cannot compute a probability, simulate it — applied twice, to two different questions. The two applications look almost identical in code and are routinely confused. They resample differently, they estimate different objects, and they answer different questions.

the bootstrap
How variable is my estimate?
Resample the data with replacement, recompute θ̂ on each resample. The spread of the θ̂* approximates the sampling distribution of θ̂. Output: a standard error, a bias estimate, a confidence interval. Replaces part 4.
the permutation test
Could chance alone have done this?
Shuffle the group labels without replacement, recompute the statistic on each shuffle. The spread of the T* is the null distribution, exactly, by construction. Output: a p-value. Replaces part 5.
the shared engine
Monte Carlo
Both are the law of large numbers used as a computational device: an unknown quantity is written as an expectation, and the expectation is replaced by an average over many simulated draws. §02.
the shared licence
The plug-in principle
The population is inaccessible, so its empirical stand-in F̂ₙ — the data set itself, with mass 1/n on each point — is used in its place. §03.

One honest warning before anything else. Resampling removes assumptions about shape. It removes nothing at all about sampling. If the data are a convenience sample, if the units are not independent, if there is selection on the outcome, or if the measurement is biased, the bootstrap will faithfully reproduce the sampling distribution of an estimate of the wrong thing, and it will do so with a reassuringly tight interval. 02 The Monte Carlo method: computing a number by averaging random ones

Before any resampling happens there is a more basic idea, and it is the only piece of theory the rest of the page needs. A fixed unknown number is written as the expected value of a random variable you know how to draw; then you draw a lot of them and average. The law of large numbers does the rest, and the error can be made as small as you like by drawing more.

The pattern

the target is a fixed number written as an expectationθ = E[ h(Y) ] for some random variable Y you can simulate draw Y₁, …, Y_B independently from the same distribution and averageθ̂_B = (1/B) Σ_{j=1..B} h(Yⱼ) the law of large numbers (part 3) says this converges — in probability, and in fact almost surelyθ̂_B ⟶ θ as B ⟶ ∞, provided E|h(Y)| < ∞ and the central limit theorem prices the error, which is the practically useful halfSE(θ̂_B) = σ_h/√B where σ_h² = Var(h(Y)) so the error shrinks like 1/√B: 100× more computation buys 10× more accuracy.

Three things about that last line deserve emphasis, because they are the whole economics of the method. First, 1/√B is slow — four extra digits of accuracy costs a hundred million times the work. Second, it does not depend on the dimension of the problem, which is why Monte Carlo beats every deterministic quadrature rule above a handful of dimensions. Third, and most importantly for this page, B is yours to choose. Unlike n, the number of observations, which is fixed by whatever data you were able to collect, B costs only electricity. That asymmetry is the subject of §10.

Three instances of the pattern, in increasing order of subtlety

1 · a population mean — h is the identity, and the "simulation" is sampling peopleθ = E[X] = average height of everyone in the country θ̂ = (1/n) Σ Xᵢ from n people drawn at random ← an ordinary sample mean IS Monte Carlo 2 · a probability — h is an indicator, and the average is a proportionp = P(A) = E[ 1{A} ] p̂_B = (#{A occurred})/B SE(p̂_B) = √( p(1 − p)/B ) ≤ 1/(2√B) 3 · a standard error — h is a squared deviation, and here the pattern strainsSE(θ̂) = √( E[ (θ̂ − Eθ̂)² ] ) draw B independent data sets, compute θ̂₁, …, θ̂_B, and take their sample SD: s(θ̂₁, …, θ̂_B) = √( (1/(B−1)) Σ (θ̂ⱼ − θ̄)² ) ≈ SE(θ̂)
The caveat in case 3, stated properly — because it is usually waved through. A sample standard deviation is not an average of independent random variables: every term (θ̂ⱼ − θ̄)² involves θ̄, which involves all B of them, so the summands are dependent and the law of large numbers does not apply to the expression as written. The repair is one line of algebra, and it is worth doing once. Write the variance in its computational form:
the identity that rescues the argument(1/B)Σ(θ̂ⱼ − θ̄)² = [ (1/B)Σ θ̂ⱼ² ] − [ (1/B)Σ θ̂ⱼ ]² now BOTH bracketed terms ARE averages of i.i.d. variables, so the LLN applies to each(1/B)Σ θ̂ⱼ² ⟶ E[θ̂²] and (1/B)Σ θ̂ⱼ ⟶ E[θ̂] and the continuous mapping theorem carries the limit through the subtraction and the square roots(θ̂₁, …, θ̂_B) ⟶ √( E[θ̂²] − (E[θ̂])² ) = SE(θ̂) ✓ (needs E[θ̂²] < ∞)
So Monte Carlo does work for a standard error, but not for the reason the one-line version suggests, and the finite-second-moment condition it quietly needs is exactly the condition that fails in §16's heavy-tailed example. The B−1 versus B divisor is irrelevant in the limit and irrelevant in practice at any B worth using.

What Monte Carlo cannot do on its own

Case 3 needs B independent data sets — B fresh samples of n observations each, drawn from the population. If you had those, you would not need any of this: you would have B·n observations and could simply use them. In reality you have one sample of size n and no access to the population at all. The Monte Carlo machine is fully general and completely unusable, because its input is unavailable. §03 supplies a substitute input, and that substitution is the bootstrap.

03 The plug-in principle: use the sample where the population should go

Every population quantity worth estimating is a function of the population's distribution. The plug-in principle says: since you cannot evaluate that function at the true distribution F, evaluate it at the best available stand-in, the empirical distribution F̂ₙ of the data. Most familiar estimators turn out to be plug-in estimators that nobody described that way.

Why plugging in works

The justification is entirely visual, and the lecture's version of it is the right one: the sample histogram is close to the population histogram, so a number computed from the sample histogram is close to the same number computed from the population histogram. The formal statement of "close" is the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem, and it is stronger than one might guess — the ECDF converges to F not just at every point but uniformly, and at a rate that does not depend on F at all.

Glivenko–Cantelli — the "fundamental theorem of statistics"sup_x | F̂ₙ(x) − F(x) | ⟶ 0 almost surely, as n ⟶ ∞ and the DKW inequality makes it quantitative and distribution-free (part 1, §07)P( sup_x |F̂ₙ(x) − F(x)| > ε ) ≤ 2 e^(−2nε²) so a 95% simultaneous band has half-width ε = √( ln(2/0.05) / (2n) ) = √(1.84444/n) n = 20 → ε = 0.3037 n = 100 → ε = 0.1358 n = 1000 → ε = 0.0429 the honest reading of those numbersat n = 20 the whole ECDF is only pinned down to ±0.30 in probability. The plug-in principle is not "F̂ₙ ≈ F"; it is "F̂ₙ ≈ F well enough for THIS functional, at THIS n".
  • Continuity is the condition that matters. If T is a smooth functional — one that does not change much when F is perturbed slightly — then F̂ₙ → F forces T(F̂ₙ) → T(F), and the plug-in estimate is consistent. The mean, the variance, the correlation and the trimmed mean are all comfortably smooth. The maximum is not: T(F) = sup{x : F(x) < 1} depends on an arbitrarily thin sliver of the right tail, exactly where F̂ₙ carries the least information. That single sentence is the whole of §16's headline failure.
  • The plug-in estimate is not automatically unbiased, and nobody claims it is. The plug-in variance is biased low by (n−1)/n; the plug-in maximum is biased low by θ/(n+1); the plug-in correlation is biased towards zero. Bias is a separate question from consistency, and part 4, §03 already argued that a little bias is often a bargain.
  • Two very different objects are called "the bootstrap distribution" and it pays to keep them apart. There is the ideal bootstrap distribution — the exact distribution of θ̂* when the data are drawn from F̂ₙ, a finite object with at most C(2n−1, n) atoms — and there is the Monte-Carlo approximation to it built from B resamples. §10 shows that the second is a controllable nuisance and the first is where the real error lives.

04 The bootstrap: plug-in, then Monte Carlo

§02 gave a method that needs many samples from the population. §03 gave a stand-in for the population. Put them together and the method becomes executable: simulate from the sample, because the population is not available. That sentence is the entire bootstrap; everything after it is bookkeeping.

The reasoning, in the order it has to be read

  1. If the population were accessible, the standard error of θ̂ could be found by Monte Carlo: draw B samples of size n, compute θ̂ on each, take the SD of the B results (§02, case 3).
  2. It is not accessible. There is one sample and no way to get another.
  3. Replace the population by F̂ₙ — the plug-in move of §03. Now "drawing a sample from the population" becomes "drawing a sample from the data set".
  4. Drawing one observation from F̂ₙ is drawing one data point at random, since F̂ₙ puts mass 1/n on each. Drawing a whole sample of size n from F̂ₙ is therefore drawing n times with replacement from X₁, …, Xₙ. Without replacement you would get the original data set back every time, in a different order, and every statistic that ignores order would be constant.
  5. Run Monte Carlo on that: B bootstrap samples, B values of θ̂*, and read whatever you need off their distribution.

The bootstrap standard error of the mean — where the answer is known exactly

For the mean and only the mean, the ideal bootstrap has a closed form, which makes it the one case where the method can be checked against algebra rather than against another simulation. Conditional on the data, each X*ᵢ is a draw from a distribution putting 1/n on each observed value, so its mean is x̄ and its variance is the plug-in variance σ̂².

conditional on the data, the X*ᵢ are i.i.d. with these two momentsE[X*ᵢ | data] = (1/n)Σxⱼ = x̄ Var(X*ᵢ | data) = (1/n)Σ(xⱼ − x̄)² = σ̂² so the bootstrapped mean has an exactly computable mean and varianceE[ x̄* | data ] = x̄ Var( x̄* | data ) = σ̂²/n the ideal bootstrap SE, before any simulation is run — B = ∞SE_boot(x̄) = σ̂/√n = √(9790/20)/√20 = 22.124647/4.472136 = 4.947221 against part 4's formula, and the exact ratio between thems/√n = 5.075742 SE_boot/(s/√n) = √((n−1)/n) = √(19/20) = 0.974679 The bootstrap SE of a mean is ALWAYS 2.5% low at n = 20, 0.5% low at n = 100, and 0.05% low at n = 1000 — a known, exactly quantified, and negligible bias. and what 200 000 actual resamples givesimulated SE = 4.9437 against the exact 4.947221 — 0.07% apart, consistent with the Monte-Carlo error of 1/√(2B) = 0.16% (§10).
Read that comparison the right way round. The bootstrap did not fail to reproduce s/√n; it reproduced σ̂/√n, which is the correct answer to the question the bootstrap asks. The n − 1 in the sample variance is a bias correction for estimating σ²; the bootstrap world's variance is σ̂² and there is nothing to correct. The gap is a definitional artefact of size 1 − √(1 − 1/n), it is the only discrepancy of its kind on this page, and knowing it exists is what lets you rule it out as an explanation when a bootstrap SE disagrees with a formula by more than a percent or two.

05 What is actually inside a bootstrap sample — and where 0.632 comes from

A bootstrap resample is not a copy of the data. It is a random reweighting of it: some observations appear twice or three times, and about a third do not appear at all. The arithmetic of that reweighting is short, completely exact, and it explains a constant that turns up unannounced in machine learning.

  • That 0.632 is the "out-of-bag" fraction. In bagging and random forests, each tree is grown on a bootstrap resample of the rows; the 36.8% of rows it never saw are its out-of-bag set and give a free held-out error estimate with no separate validation split. The number is not a heuristic — it is e⁻¹, and it comes from exactly the calculation above.
  • The number of distinct resamples is C(2n−1, n), not nⁿ. There are nⁿ ordered draws, but a statistic that ignores order only sees the multiplicity vector, and the number of ways to put n indistinguishable draws into n labelled buckets is C(2n−1, n) by the stars-and-bars argument. At n = 20 that is 6.9 × 10¹⁰ — already far beyond exhaustive enumeration, which is why the bootstrap is always Monte Carlo and never exact, unlike the permutation test of §13.
  • Not all resamples are equally likely. The all-distinct resample (every Mᵢ = 1) has probability n!/nⁿ, which at n = 20 is 2.3 × 10⁻⁸. The most likely multiplicity vectors are the ragged ones. This is why the bootstrap distribution of an order statistic is so lumpy (§16).
  • An m-out-of-n bootstrap changes these numbers deliberately. Drawing m < n points with replacement leaves (1 − 1/n)^m → e^(−m/n) of the data out, and it is one of the few repairs that rescues the failures of §16 — at the cost of having to choose m.

06 Four bootstraps: nonparametric, parametric, smoothed, block

Step 3 of §04's reasoning said "replace the population by F̂ₙ". That was one choice among several. Every bootstrap variant is a different answer to what stands in for the population, and choosing well is worth more than choosing a large B.

variant 1 — the default
Nonparametric
Stand-in: F̂ₙ, the data itself. Resample n points with replacement. Assumes only that the observations are i.i.d. draws from something. Use this unless you have a specific reason not to.
variant 2
Parametric
Stand-in: F(·; θ̂), a fitted model. Simulate fresh samples from the model. More efficient when the model is right; wrong in a way you cannot detect when it is not.
variant 3
Smoothed
Stand-in: a kernel density estimate — resample, then add a small jitter. Repairs the lumpiness that wrecks quantile statistics (§16), at the price of a bandwidth choice.
variant 4 — for dependent data
Block
Stand-in: the empirical distribution of blocks of consecutive observations. Resampling single points destroys serial dependence; resampling runs of length ℓ preserves it up to lag ℓ.

  • Here the two agree, and that is informative. A parametric bootstrap that disagrees with the nonparametric one is telling you the model does not fit; agreement is a weak but real goodness-of-fit check that costs nothing once both are coded.
  • The parametric bootstrap can produce values the data never contained, which is precisely why it helps in the tail. The nonparametric interval for the 90th percentile could not exceed 91 (§04); the parametric one can, because the fitted lognormal has a right tail. If the model is right, that is extra information; if it is wrong, it is confident fiction.
  • It is also the only bootstrap available when the data are not a plain i.i.d. sample — for generalised linear models, mixed models, state-space models and time series with a fitted structure, the parametric bootstrap simulates from the fitted mechanism and is often the standard tool.
  • Rule of thumb. Parametric when the model is a scientific commitment you would defend anyway; nonparametric when the model is a convenience. The bootstrap does not repair a wrong model — it inherits it.

The block bootstrap, and how badly the naive one fails without it

The nonparametric bootstrap resamples observations independently. If the data are a time series, that destroys the very thing that makes them a time series. The consequence is not subtle: an autocorrelated series has a far larger sampling variance for its mean than an independent one of the same length, and the i.i.d. bootstrap misses the whole of the difference.

Two more variants worth knowing by name

  • The smoothed bootstrap. Resample as usual, then add independent noise hZ with Z standard normal and h a bandwidth — equivalently, sample from a kernel density estimate rather than from the spiky F̂ₙ. This removes the atoms that make bootstrap distributions of medians and quantiles lumpy (§08, §16) and can restore consistency in some of the cases where the plain bootstrap fails. The cost is a bandwidth, and a bad bandwidth is a new source of error.
  • The wild bootstrap, for regression with non-constant error variance: instead of resampling residuals, multiply each residual in place by an independent random sign or a mean-zero, unit-variance random variable, e*ᵢ = êᵢ·vᵢ. Each observation keeps its own residual magnitude, so heteroscedasticity is preserved rather than pooled away. §15 uses it.

07 Three intervals from one histogram — and why they are different

Once θ̂*₁, …, θ̂*_B exist there are three standard ways to turn them into a confidence interval. On symmetric data all three coincide and the choice does not matter.   • It cannot escape the data. The endpoints are order statistics of the θ̂*, and for many statistics that traps them inside the observed range — harmless for a mean, fatal for a maximum (§16).

08 What the three intervals actually cover — measured, not claimed

Every interval on this page is labelled 95%. Part 4, §07 made the distinction that matters: nominal coverage is what the label says, actual coverage is the fraction of intervals that contain the parameter when the whole procedure is repeated. They are not the same number, nobody's software tells you the difference, and the only way to find out is to simulate a population you know the answer for. This section does that.

The experiment

Twenty thousand replications at each cell. Each replication draws a fresh sample of size n from a known population, runs B = 999 bootstrap resamples, builds all four intervals, and checks whether each contains the true parameter. Quantiles use the linear-interpolation convention throughout. The Monte-Carlo standard error on a figure near 95% is √(0.95 × 0.05/20000) = 0.15 percentage points, so differences below about half a point should be ignored.

10 Choosing B: two errors, and only one of them is yours

This is the single most misunderstood point about the bootstrap, and it takes one sentence to state. There are two sources of error, they are governed by two different numbers, and increasing B only touches one of them. Everything else in the section is arithmetic in support of that sentence.

error 1 · yours to control
Monte-Carlo error
The gap between your B resamples and the ideal bootstrap answer (B = ∞). Shrinks like 1/√B. Costs only compute. Reproducibility depends on it: two analysts with the same data and different seeds differ by exactly this much.
error 2 · not yours to control
Statistical error
The gap between the ideal bootstrap answer and the truth. Governed by n. Completely unaffected by B. This is the left-hand gap in §03's diagram, and it is almost always the larger of the two.

11 Permutation tests: build the null distribution instead of looking it up

Part 5's machine needed a named reference distribution for the test statistic under H₀ — a t, a z, an F — and every one of those names is an approximation that comes with conditions. A permutation test needs no name and no conditions. It constructs the null distribution from the data, by doing to the labels exactly what the null hypothesis says was irrelevant about them.

The argument, in four sentences

  1. Suppose the null hypothesis is that the group labels carry no information — that the observation "17.3 seconds, group A" would have been "17.3 seconds, group B" had the coin landed the other way.
  2. Then every reassignment of the labels to the same observations is equally likely. This is the assumption of exchangeability, and it is the only assumption the test makes. It is guaranteed by design in a randomised experiment, and it is an assumption about the population in an observational study.
  3. So enumerate the reassignments — all of them if there are few, a large random sample of them otherwise — and compute the test statistic for each. That collection is the null distribution of the statistic. Not an approximation to it: it is exactly the distribution of the statistic over the randomisation that generated the data.
  4. Compare the observed value to that collection. The fraction of reassignments giving something at least as extreme is the p-value, and it means exactly what part 5, §04 says a p-value means.
the recipe for a two-sample comparison, n₁ and n₂ observations1. compute the observed statistic T_obs = t(group A, group B) 2. POOL all n = n₁ + n₂ observations (this is where H₀ enters) 3. shuffle, deal out n₁ to A* and n₂ to B*, recompute T* — WITHOUT replacement 4. repeat B times, or enumerate all C(n, n₁) splits 5. p = the fraction at least as extreme as T_obs the two-sided p-value, with the correction of the next subsectionp = ( 1 + #{ b : |T*_b| ≥ |T_obs| } ) / ( B + 1 ) and the one-sided version, when the direction was fixed in advancep = ( 1 + #{ b : T*_b ≥ T_obs } ) / ( B + 1 ) the statistic t(·,·) is COMPLETELY free — any of these works, with no new theoryx̄_A − x̄_B median_A − median_B trimmed mean difference a Welch t a rank sum a correlation an AUC an F ratio a fitted model's loss

Step 5 is the only place the choice of statistic matters, and it matters only for power, never for validity. Under exchangeability the null distribution is correct for whatever t you chose, so the test cannot have the wrong type I error because you picked an unusual statistic. That is a genuinely unusual freedom, and it is why permutation tests are the natural inference tool for quantities like "the difference in AUC between two models" that have no distributional theory at all.

The +1, and why the naive p-value is not merely inelegant

Write c = #{b : |T*_b| ≥ |T_obs|}. The obvious estimator is c/B. It has two defects, one cosmetic and one real.

  • The cosmetic one: c/B can be 0. A p-value of exactly zero is a claim that the observed result is impossible under the null, which is false — the observed labelling is itself one of the permutations, so at least one permutation is always at least as extreme as the observed one. Reporting p = 0 from a resampling procedure is reporting a number the procedure cannot produce.
  • The real one: c/B is anti-conservative. It rejects slightly too often, because it does not count the observed labelling among the reference set. The corrected form (1 + c)/(B + 1) does, and the resulting test is exactly valid: P(p ≤ α) ≤ α under the null, for every B and every α.

12 What a permutation test actually tests — and the one every write-up gets wrong

A permutation test is routinely described as "an assumption-free test of equal means". It is not. It is an exact test of exchangeability, which for two samples means the two distributions are identical in every respect — same centre, same spread, same shape, same tails. Under that null the test is exact, always, for any statistic. Under the weaker null "the means are equal", it is not exact, and the failure can be enormous.

The two nulls

what the shuffling licenses
H₀: F_A = F_B
The strong null: the label is irrelevant, so any relabelling is as probable as the observed one. This is exactly the condition needed for the permutation distribution to be the null distribution. Guaranteed by randomisation in an experiment.
what people report
H₀: μ_A = μ_B
The weak null. It permits F_A ≠ F_B — different variances, different shapes — with the same mean. Under it the observations are not exchangeable, and the permutation distribution is the wrong reference. §12's table shows what "wrong" costs.

With five observations in the wide group and twenty-five in the narrow one, and identical population means, a permutation test on the difference of means declares a difference nearly six times as often as it should. In the mirror-image design it declares one 0.09% of the time — fifty-five times too rarely, which destroys power without announcing itself. This is the Behrens–Fisher problem, and it is not a small-print caveat: the very configuration that breaks it — a small group with large spread against a large group with small spread — is the normal shape of an A/B test where the treatment arm is small and the treatment increases variance.

The mechanism is worth seeing directly. Pooling and reshuffling gives every permuted group the same mixed spread — a blend of the two populations. The observed statistic, however, was computed with the wide-spread values concentrated in the small group, where they inflate x̄_A's variance far more than the pooled reference distribution allows for. The reference distribution is too narrow, so the observed difference looks too extreme. When the small group is the narrow one, the reference distribution is too wide and everything looks tame. Balanced designs are nearly immune, which is the first practical lesson: balance the groups.

The two mistakes this table prevents

Wrong: using the bootstrap distribution of x̄_A − x̄_B to compute a p-value by asking what fraction of it falls beyond 0.
Right: that distribution is centred at the observed difference, not at 0. The tail area beyond 0 is a statement about the estimate, not about the null. The bootstrap can be made to give a p-value — by inverting the interval, or by shifting each group to a common mean before resampling — but the naive tail-area reading is not it. The permutation test does the job directly and exactly.
Wrong: using the spread of a permutation distribution as a standard error for the difference.
Right: the permutation distribution is built under H₀, where the two groups are the same population. Its spread is the standard error the difference would have if the null were true, which is not the standard error of the observed difference unless the null is true. If the treatment changes the variance — which treatments routinely do — the two differ substantially.
Wrong: "the permutation test showed the groups are different, so the bootstrap interval should exclude zero" (or vice versa), treated as a consistency check.
Right: they usually do agree, and their disagreement is informative rather than contradictory. On the running two-sample data, the permutation p-value is 0.103 and the bootstrap percentile interval for the difference is (−0.072, 2.463) — both say "not distinguishable at 5%", and they say it in the two currencies of part 5, §09's duality. Where they disagree, §12's exchangeability question is the first place to look.
A third procedure exists and is worth naming. The bootstrap permutation test pools the two groups and then draws the two resamples with replacement rather than shuffling. This models two random elements at once — the assignment of treatment to subject and the selection of subjects from a population — rather than only the first. The distinction matters philosophically, matters little numerically, and is worth knowing mostly so that you recognise the term. If in doubt, shuffle.

On the twelve points, zero of 99 999 shuffles produced a slope as large as 3 in absolute value, so p = 1/(99 999 + 1) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ — the floor of §11 — against the t test's 8.52 × 10⁻⁷. Both say "overwhelming"; only the parametric one can put a number on how overwhelming, and only by extrapolating a normal tail.
  • An interval for anything downstream of the fit — R², a ratio of two coefficients, the x at which the fitted curve peaks, the predicted value at a new point, the difference in R² between two models. None of these has a usable standard error formula; all of them are one line inside an existing bootstrap loop. This is where resampling stops being a substitute for part 6 and starts doing things part 6 cannot.
  • Clustered data need clustered resampling. If rows come in groups — repeated measurements per subject, students within schools — resample whole clusters with replacement, never rows. Row-level resampling on clustered data understates standard errors for exactly the same reason §06's i.i.d. bootstrap did on the AR(1) series, and by a similar magnitude.

16 Where the bootstrap fails — with the failures measured

The bootstrap is consistent under conditions, and the conditions are not decorative. When they break, the method does not degrade gracefully and it does not warn you: it returns a tidy interval with the wrong coverage, and the interval often gets no better as n grows. This section catalogues five failures, gives the mechanism for each, and measures the damage.

Failure 1 — the sample maximum. The canonical case, and the cleanest.

The general condition is that θ̂ must be a smooth (Hadamard-differentiable) functional of F with a non-degenerate derivative, and boundaries violate the second half of that.

Failure 4 — small n

Repeating §08's table, because it belongs in the catalogue: mean of a Normal(0, 1) sample, nominal 95% percentile interval, coverage 74.5% at n = 3, 83.5% at n = 5, 88.5% at n = 8. Nothing exotic is happening. There are only C(2n−1, n) distinct resamples — 10 at n = 3, 126 at n = 5 — and the empirical distribution of three points is a poor stand-in for anything. All bootstrap theory is asymptotic. The bootstrap does not create data, and the widespread belief that it rescues small samples is exactly backwards.

Failure 5 — dependence

Covered with numbers in §06: an AR(1) series with φ = 0.8 gives an i.i.d.-bootstrap interval with 48.45% coverage. The same failure appears for clustered data, spatial data, repeated measures, and any data set with a hidden grouping. The general rule is that you must resample the exchangeable unit, and identifying it is a design question, not a computational one.

A short checklist for whether the bootstrap applies

  1. Is n at least 20–30? Below that, expect meaningful undercoverage and say so.
  2. Is the statistic a smooth function of the data? Means, variances, correlations, regression coefficients, ratios away from zero denominators: yes. Maxima, minima, ranges, modes, counts of distinct values, and anything defined by an extreme: no.
  3. Is the parameter in the interior of its space? A variance that might be 0, a proportion that might be 0 or 1, a constrained coefficient at its bound: expect trouble.
  4. Does the population plausibly have a finite variance? Financial returns, network traffic, city sizes and insurance claims frequently do not.
  5. Are the observations independent? If not, what is the exchangeable unit, and are you resampling it?
  6. Does the bootstrap distribution look pathological? A large atom, visible spikes, or a hard edge at θ̂ is the diagnostic. Always plot the histogram of the θ̂* before quoting an interval from it — it is free, and it is the only warning you get.

The rule that comes out of this is the same one §16 arrived at from the other direction: the jackknife needs the statistic to be smooth, and it needs more smoothness than the bootstrap does. A one-point deletion is an infinitesimal perturbation of F̂ₙ, so the jackknife is essentially estimating a derivative; if the statistic is not differentiable in the right sense, there is no derivative to estimate. The bootstrap perturbs F̂ₙ by a finite amount and survives the median comfortably.

  • Use the jackknife for: a quick, deterministic, seed-free standard error for a smooth statistic; a bias estimate; BCa's acceleration; a diagnostic scan for influential observations (a leave-one-out value far from the rest names the point).
  • Do not use it for: medians, quantiles, or anything with a kink. And do not use it for a confidence interval directly — it gives a standard error, not a shape, so an interval from it is a normal interval with all of §07's limitations.
  • Cost. n refits against the bootstrap's B. Cheaper for n < 1000, and free of Monte-Carlo error entirely — two different analysts get the identical number, which is occasionally worth a lot.
  • The delete-d jackknife — leave out d > 1 observations at a time — restores consistency for the median and other non-smooth statistics, at the cost of C(n, d) subsets (usually sampled rather than enumerated). 18 The ML translation: cross-validation, bagging, and the rest of the family

Machine learning uses this page's ideas constantly and almost never calls them resampling. The vocabulary changed because the purpose changed: statistics resamples to price the uncertainty of a parameter, ML resamples to price the error of a prediction on data it has not seen. The mechanics are the same and several of the constants are literally the same.

This pageThe ML pageWhat is the same, and what is not
Bootstrap resample of the rowsBagging — bootstrap aggregatingIdentical resampling, opposite use. The bootstrap looks at how much θ̂* varies; bagging averages the θ̂* away and keeps the average. Same variance, one method measures it and the other cancels it.

The one thing to carry across. Bagging works because the bootstrap distribution has real spread: averaging many high-variance fits reduces variance without much increasing bias (the bias–variance decomposition). The bootstrap's inferential use measures exactly the quantity bagging exploits. If the bootstrap distribution of a fitted model is wide, bagging will help a lot and your single fitted model should not be trusted; if it is narrow, bagging is a waste of compute and the fit is stable. Running a bootstrap before deciding whether to bag is a five-minute experiment that answers the question directly.

19 Practice

The checklist, before you report any resampled number

  1. Which question are you answering? A test needs a permutation; an interval needs a bootstrap. Using the wrong one is §14's first two traps (§14).
  2. What is the exchangeable unit? Rows, subjects, clusters, sessions, blocks of time. Resample that, not whatever happens to be a row in the file (§06, §12, §15).
  3. Is n big enough? Below 20, quote the coverage caveat with the interval; below 10, do not quote the interval (§08, §16).
  4. Is the statistic smooth? Maxima, minima, ranges, modes and anything at a boundary will fail silently (§16).
  5. Did you plot the histogram of the θ̂*? Atoms, spikes and a hard edge at θ̂ are the only warning you get (§16).
  6. Which interval did you use, and did you say? "A 95% bootstrap interval" is not a specification. Percentile, pivotal, BCa and bootstrap-t give different answers on the same resamples (§07, §09).
  7. Is B large enough for what you computed? 1 000 for a standard error, 10 000 for interval endpoints, 10 000+ for BCa (§10).
  8. Did you fix and report the seed? Without it the analysis is not reproducible (§10).
  9. For a permutation test: did you use (1 + c)/(B + 1)? And is B ≥ 1/α − 1, so rejection is even possible (§11)?
  10. Can your test reach the p-value you need? The floor is 1/(B+1) for sampled tests and 1/C(n, n₁) for exhaustive ones (§11, §13).
  11. Are the group sizes and spreads both unequal? Then do not permute a raw difference of means (§12).
  12. In a regression, which scheme? Cases or wild if you doubt constant variance; residuals only if you are committed to the model, and then rescale by √(1 − hᵢ) (§15).
  13. Is the sampling itself sound? No resampling repairs selection bias, a bad frame, or confounding (§01, part 2).

Worked problems

  1. A sample is 2, 5, 9, 14. Give the ideal bootstrap standard error of the mean without simulating anything. x̄ = 7.5, Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = 30.25 + 6.25 + 2.25 + 42.25 = 81, so σ̂² = 81/4 = 20.25 and SE_boot = σ̂/√n = 4.5/2 = 2.25. The formula answer is s/√n = √27/2 = 2.598. The ratio is √(3/4) = 0.866 exactly, as §04 requires. Also: there are only C(7, 4) = 35 distinct resamples, so at n = 4 the whole bootstrap distribution could be listed by hand.
  2. A bootstrap gives θ̂ = 0.72, θ̂*₍₀.₀₂₅₎ = 0.61, θ̂*₍₀.₉₇₅₎ = 0.79. Write down the percentile and pivotal intervals and say which you would report if θ is a proportion. Percentile (0.61, 0.79); pivotal (2×0.72 − 0.79, 2×0.72 − 0.61) = (0.65, 0.83). Report the percentile one: it is range-respecting, and the bootstrap distribution is right-skewed here (θ̂ sits 0.11 above its lower endpoint and 0.07 below its upper one), which is exactly the situation §08 measured the pivotal interval failing in.
  3. How many resamples for a bootstrap standard error accurate to 2% relative? B ≈ 1/(2ε²) = 1/(2 × 0.0004) = 1 250. For interval endpoints this is not enough — the first table in §10 shows the 97.5% endpoint still wobbling by ±0.53 at B = 1 000 against the SE's ±0.11.
  4. A permutation test with B = 999 finds 29 shuffles at least as extreme. Give the p-value and its Monte-Carlo standard error. p = (1 + 29)/1000 = 0.030. SE = √(0.03 × 0.97/999) = 0.0054, so a 95% Monte-Carlo interval for the exhaustive p-value is roughly 0.030 ± 0.011. If the decision hinges on whether p < 0.05 that is fine; if it hinges on p < 0.025, raise B.
  5. Two groups of five. What is the smallest two-sided p-value a permutation test can produce? C(10, 5) = 252 splits, and the two most extreme are the observed one and its mirror, so p_min = 2/252 = 0.0079. A one-sided test reaches 1/252 = 0.0040. Adequate for α = 0.05, hopeless for a Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.001 across many tests.
  6. You bootstrap a regression by resampling residuals with n = 200 and 5 predictors (plus intercept). Your SE(b̂) is 0.412. What should it be? Divide out the deflation: the raw-residual bootstrap is low by √((n − p)/n) = √(194/200) = 0.98487, so the corrected value is 0.412/0.98487 = 0.4183 — a 1.5% correction here, negligible. Repeat with n = 50 and 20 parameters and the factor is √(30/50) = 0.7746, a 29% correction. The correction matters when p/n is not small.
  7. A series has AR(1) autocorrelation 0.6. By how much does the i.i.d. bootstrap understate the standard error of the mean? The variance inflation factor is (1 + φ)/(1 − φ) = 1.6/0.4 = 4, so the true SE is √4 = 2 times the i.i.d. one. A nominal 95% interval is half the width it should be, and its coverage will be far below 95% — §06 measured 73% at φ = 0.5, where the inflation is only 3×.
  8. A bootstrap puts 55% of the θ̂* below θ̂, and the jackknife acceleration is â = 0.03. What are the BCa percentage points at 95%? The bias correction is ẑ₀ = Φ⁻¹(0.55) = 0.12566. Then u = ẑ₀ + z₍₀.₀₂₅₎ = 0.12566 − 1.95996 = −1.83430 and α₁ = Φ(0.12566 + (−1.83430)/(1 + 0.03×1.83430)) = 0.0534; similarly u = 2.08563 gives α₂ = 0.9906. So instead of the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles you read off the 5.3rd and 99.1st — the whole interval shifts right, correcting for the fact that the bootstrap distribution sits below θ̂. Note α₂ = 0.9906 needs B in the tens of thousands to be resolvable.
  9. What fraction of the data appears in a bootstrap resample when n = 8? When n = 25? 1 − (7/8)⁸ = 0.6564 and 1 − (24/25)²⁵ = 0.6396. Both are already within four points of the limiting 0.6321, which is why out-of-bag error estimates behave the same way at almost any sample size.
  10. Someone reports a bootstrap 95% interval for the largest claim their insurer will see next year, computed from 500 historical claims. What is wrong? Two things, either fatal. The parameter is an extreme, and §16 shows the percentile interval for a maximum has coverage exactly zero — the interval cannot exceed the largest historical claim, and next year's maximum very probably will. And insurance claims are a canonical heavy-tailed population where even the mean's bootstrap misbehaves. The right tools are extreme-value theory or a parametric tail model, not resampling.
  11. An A/B test has 40 000 users per arm and the metric is revenue per user, which is zero for 97% of users and heavily skewed for the rest. Bootstrap or t test? Either works and they will agree, because n = 40 000 puts the mean firmly in central-limit territory. Use the permutation test if you want an exact answer for a metric like the 95th percentile or the conversion-weighted mean, and the bootstrap if you want an interval for the lift. Do not use the t test on a subgroup with 200 users and 97% zeros — that is part 4, §08's Bernoulli(0.05) row, where the t interval covered 22% of the time at n = 5.
  12. Design question: you can afford either 10× more bootstrap resamples or 25% more data. Which? The data, essentially always. §10 measured a hundredfold increase in B removing 5% of the total uncertainty; 25% more data cuts the statistical error by 1 − 1/√1.25 = 10.6%, which is twice as much for a fraction of the effort. The only case for more resamples is reproducibility of a published figure.

Where this goes next. Part 8, Categorical Data replaces measurements by counts and the t by χ². Its central tool, Fisher's exact test, is §13's permutation test applied to a 2 × 2 table — the reference set is all tables with the same margins — and the χ² test is the large-sample approximation to it, standing in the same relation as the pooled t did to the permutation test in §11. When the expected counts are small and the χ² approximation is warned against, the permutation test is what you fall back to. Part 9, ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons generalises part 6's F to k groups, and the same substitution is available: permute the group labels, recompute F on every shuffle, and you have an F test that needs neither normality nor equal variances — subject to §12's caution, which applies with k groups exactly as it does with two. The general statement is worth keeping: a permutation test is the assumption-free version of essentially every test in parts 5, 8 and 9, obtained by replacing the tabulated null distribution with a constructed one, and it agrees with the classical test whenever the classical test's conditions hold.
Where this goes sideways. Into cross-validation, which is §17's jackknife with a prediction loss and a block of held-out rows instead of one; into bagging and random forests, which resample exactly as §04 does and then average rather than measure — and whose out-of-bag error is §05's e⁻¹; into the bias–variance decomposition, which is the reason averaging bootstrap fits helps at all; into model comparison, where the honest test of "is model A better than model B on this test set" is a paired permutation over the per-example score differences; into A/B testing, where randomisation makes §12's exchangeability a fact rather than an assumption and the permutation test is therefore exact; into hyperparameter search, whose repeated evaluation on the same validation set is part 5's multiplicity problem under another name; and back into part 3, since every statement on this page about when resampling works is a statement about whether a central limit theorem applies to the statistic in question.
