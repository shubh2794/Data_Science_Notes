11 Permutation tests: build the null distribution instead of looking it up

Part 5's machine needed a named reference distribution for the test statistic under H₀ — a t, a z, an F — and every one of those names is an approximation that comes with conditions. A permutation test needs no name and no conditions. It constructs the null distribution from the data, by doing to the labels exactly what the null hypothesis says was irrelevant about them.

The argument, in four sentences

  1. Suppose the null hypothesis is that the group labels carry no information — that the observation "17.3 seconds, group A" would have been "17.3 seconds, group B" had the coin landed the other way.
  2. Then every reassignment of the labels to the same observations is equally likely. This is the assumption of exchangeability, and it is the only assumption the test makes. It is guaranteed by design in a randomised experiment, and it is an assumption about the population in an observational study.
  3. So enumerate the reassignments — all of them if there are few, a large random sample of them otherwise — and compute the test statistic for each. That collection is the null distribution of the statistic. Not an approximation to it: it is exactly the distribution of the statistic over the randomisation that generated the data.
  4. Compare the observed value to that collection. The fraction of reassignments giving something at least as extreme is the p-value, and it means exactly what part 5, §04 says a p-value means.
the recipe for a two-sample comparison, n₁ and n₂ observations1. compute the observed statistic T_obs = t(group A, group B) 2. POOL all n = n₁ + n₂ observations (this is where H₀ enters) 3. shuffle, deal out n₁ to A* and n₂ to B*, recompute T* — WITHOUT replacement 4. repeat B times, or enumerate all C(n, n₁) splits 5. p = the fraction at least as extreme as T_obs the two-sided p-value, with the correction of the next subsectionp = ( 1 + #{ b : |T*_b| ≥ |T_obs| } ) / ( B + 1 ) and the one-sided version, when the direction was fixed in advancep = ( 1 + #{ b : T*_b ≥ T_obs } ) / ( B + 1 ) the statistic t(·,·) is COMPLETELY free — any of these works, with no new theoryx̄_A − x̄_B median_A − median_B trimmed mean difference a Welch t a rank sum a correlation an AUC an F ratio a fitted model's loss

Step 5 is the only place the choice of statistic matters, and it matters only for power, never for validity. Under exchangeability the null distribution is correct for whatever t you chose, so the test cannot have the wrong type I error because you picked an unusual statistic. That is a genuinely unusual freedom, and it is why permutation tests are the natural inference tool for quantities like "the difference in AUC between two models" that have no distributional theory at all.

The +1, and why the naive p-value is not merely inelegant

Write c = #{b : |T*_b| ≥ |T_obs|}. The obvious estimator is c/B. It has two defects, one cosmetic and one real.

  • The cosmetic one: c/B can be 0. A p-value of exactly zero is a claim that the observed result is impossible under the null, which is false — the observed labelling is itself one of the permutations, so at least one permutation is always at least as extreme as the observed one. Reporting p = 0 from a resampling procedure is reporting a number the procedure cannot produce.
  • The real one: c/B is anti-conservative. It rejects slightly too often, because it does not count the observed labelling among the reference set. The corrected form (1 + c)/(B + 1) does, and the resulting test is exactly valid: P(p ≤ α) ≤ α under the null, for every B and every α.
Measured. 200 000 null data sets, two groups of 12 and 15 both drawn from Normal(0, 1), statistic x̄_A − x̄_B, nominal α = 0.05:

Three things to read off. (a) The corrected p-value holds its level: 5.01%, 5.05%, 4.98% and 5.00% against a Monte-Carlo error of ±0.05 percentage points at B = 19, 99, 199 and 999 — exact, to within the resolution of the experiment — and 4.04%, conservative, at B = 49. (b) At B = 49 the naive version rejects at 6.03%, a 21% inflation of the error rate, purely from the missing +1. (c) At B = 19 one null data set in twenty produces the literally impossible report p = 0. The reason the two columns agree at B = 19, 99, 199, 999 and disagree at 49 is pure integer arithmetic: (1+c)/(B+1) ≤ 0.05 and c/B ≤ 0.05 define the same set of counts whenever B + 1 is a multiple of 1/α. Choosing B of the form k/α − 1 — 19, 99, 199, 999, 9 999 — is not superstition; it makes the discreteness land exactly on the threshold.

That floor is a real constraint and it is easy to trip over. Part 6's slope has a t-test p-value of 8.52 × 10⁻⁷. Permuting the twelve y-values against the x-values 99 999 times produced zero shuffles with a slope as large as the observed one, so the permutation test reports p = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ and can say nothing more precise. Reaching the t-test's answer would need B around ten million. When a p-value is tiny, a permutation test tells you it is tiny and stops; if you need the exponent, you need the parametric model.

The pooled t test reproduces the exhaustive permutation test to within 0.0007. That is not a coincidence and it is the historically correct way round: Fisher presented the randomisation test as what the comparison really means, and treated the normal-theory t as a computational shortcut that happens to reproduce it — a shortcut worth having when the alternative was seventeen million hand calculations. The shortcut is astonishingly good — here it is right to three decimal places on n = 27 mildly non-normal observations. Two consequences worth carrying away. First, if a permutation test and a pooled t test disagree materially, something specific is wrong (heavy tails, a wild point, or unequal spreads with unequal group sizes — §12), and the disagreement is diagnostic. Second, Welch's test is not approximating this permutation test: it targets a different null and gives 0.088 here, which is the subject of the next section.

12 What a permutation test actually tests — and the one every write-up gets wrong

A permutation test is routinely described as "an assumption-free test of equal means". It is not. It is an exact test of exchangeability, which for two samples means the two distributions are identical in every respect — same centre, same spread, same shape, same tails. Under that null the test is exact, always, for any statistic. Under the weaker null "the means are equal", it is not exact, and the failure can be enormous.

The two nulls

what the shuffling licenses
H₀: F_A = F_B
The strong null: the label is irrelevant, so any relabelling is as probable as the observed one. This is exactly the condition needed for the permutation distribution to be the null distribution. Guaranteed by randomisation in an experiment.
what people report
H₀: μ_A = μ_B
The weak null. It permits F_A ≠ F_B — different variances, different shapes — with the same mean. Under it the observations are not exchangeable, and the permutation distribution is the wrong reference. §12's table shows what "wrong" costs.

The demonstration

Forty thousand null data sets, both groups normal with mean exactly 0, so the weak null is true by construction. Statistic x̄_A − x̄_B, B = 199 shuffles, corrected p-value, nominal α = 0.05. Welch's t test, which was designed for exactly this situation, is shown alongside.

29.4% at a nominal 5%. With five observations in the wide group and twenty-five in the narrow one, and identical population means, a permutation test on the difference of means declares a difference nearly six times as often as it should. In the mirror-image design it declares one 0.09% of the time — fifty-five times too rarely, which destroys power without announcing itself. This is the Behrens–Fisher problem, and it is not a small-print caveat: the very configuration that breaks it — a small group with large spread against a large group with small spread — is the normal shape of an A/B test where the treatment arm is small and the treatment increases variance.

The mechanism is worth seeing directly. Pooling and reshuffling gives every permuted group the same mixed spread — a blend of the two populations. The observed statistic, however, was computed with the wide-spread values concentrated in the small group, where they inflate x̄_A's variance far more than the pooled reference distribution allows for. The reference distribution is too narrow, so the observed difference looks too extreme. When the small group is the narrow one, the reference distribution is too wide and everything looks tame. Balanced designs are nearly immune, which is the first practical lesson: balance the groups.

The repair: studentise the statistic

Permute a statistic that already divides out the group variances, and much of the damage disappears. Using the Welch t itself as the permuted statistic is the standard fix — same shuffles, same code, a different function of each shuffle.

29.4% becomes 7.6% — a large improvement and still not 5%. The theoretical statement matching this is: permuting a studentised statistic gives a test that is asymptotically correct for the weak null even when variances differ, while remaining exactly correct for the strong null. "Asymptotically" is doing real work at n₁ = 5. The practical ranking under suspected heteroscedasticity is therefore: use Welch's t test if the data are anywhere near normal; use a permuted studentised statistic if they are not; never permute a raw difference of means with both unequal spreads and unequal group sizes.

Exchangeability, broken on purpose both groups always have mean 0 — every rejection here is a false positive

  • Shuffling across blocks is the commonest implementation bug. If subjects are matched or measured in batches, a global shuffle mixes strata and destroys the very structure the design created — the same error as running an unpaired test on paired data, and it fails the same way: usually a catastrophic loss of power, occasionally an inflated error rate.
  • Randomisation makes exchangeability a fact, not an assumption. In a randomised experiment the null distribution generated by shuffling is literally the distribution induced by the coin flips the experimenter made. There is no population, no sampling model, and no approximation. This is why permutation tests are sometimes called randomisation tests and why they are the natural analysis of an A/B test.
  • In an observational study, exchangeability is an assumption about the population, and it is exactly as strong as any other. The test is exact given it, and useless without it.
  • Order-dependent data cannot be permuted naively. There is no plain permutation test for a time series; the analogue is a block permutation or a phase randomisation, and both are approximations. Compare §06's block bootstrap — the same problem, the same shape of solution.

13 Enumerate it by hand: exact tests, and the classical rank tests unmasked

When the sample is small enough, "sample B shuffles" becomes "list all of them", and the p-value stops being an estimate. Doing that once by hand is the fastest way to make the whole idea concrete — and it reveals that several of the named tests in part 5 are permutation tests that were tabulated before computers existed.

A four-against-four comparison, enumerated completely

The t test and the exact test land on opposite sides of 0.05 on eight numbers. The exact test is right — it makes no distributional claim and its type I error is guaranteed — and the t test's p = 0.0415 is a normal-theory extrapolation into a tail that eight observations cannot support. Note also the resolution floor: with four against four there are only 70 splits, so the smallest attainable two-sided p-value is 2/70 = 0.0286. No arrangement of these group sizes can ever produce p < 0.028, however dramatic the separation. Knowing your test's floor before you run it is the same design discipline that part 5, §11 applied to the sign test.

Part 5's paired example, redone by sign-flipping

Five couples; the age differences are d = 2, 1, 1, 2, 1. Under H₀ "the pairing carries no information about direction", the exchangeable objects are the two members within each couple, so the reference set is the 2⁵ = 32 ways of assigning signs.

  • The exact test agrees with the sign test here, and both are thirteen times the paired t's p-value. That is not a defect of the permutation test; it is the floor again. Five pairs give 32 arrangements, so 2/32 = 0.0625 is the smallest two-sided p-value physically obtainable, and no statistic — d̄, the t, the signed rank — can go below it. The paired t's 0.00464 is available only because the normal model supplies information the design does not.
  • Which to believe depends on what you are willing to assume. If age differences within couples are plausibly normal, the t answer is a real gain from a real assumption. If they are not, 0.0625 is what five pairs can honestly support. Reporting both, as here, is the fully honest move.
  • The sign-flip test is the paired analogue and needs symmetry, not exchangeability of everything. Under H₀ the difference dᵢ is symmetric about 0, so −dᵢ is as likely as dᵢ — which is exactly what flipping the sign simulates. Note this is a slightly weaker null than "the two members of a pair are exchangeable", and slightly stronger than "the median difference is 0".

The historical reading, which is worth having. The nonparametric tests are not a separate tradition from the permutation test; they are the permutation test made computable in 1945. Once shuffling a million labelled arrays takes a second, the reason for the rank transform largely disappears — you can permute the raw values, or any statistic you actually care about, and get an exact test with no efficiency loss. Rank tests remain worth using when you genuinely want robustness to outliers or only have ordinal data, which are real reasons, rather than because the arithmetic is easier, which is no longer one.

14 Permutation against bootstrap: two procedures, one keyboard

The code differs by one argument — replace = True or replace = False — and everything else about the two methods differs. They are confused constantly, including in published work, and the confusion produces answers that are not wrong by a little.

The two mistakes this table prevents

Wrong: using the bootstrap distribution of x̄_A − x̄_B to compute a p-value by asking what fraction of it falls beyond 0.
Right: that distribution is centred at the observed difference, not at 0. The tail area beyond 0 is a statement about the estimate, not about the null. The bootstrap can be made to give a p-value — by inverting the interval, or by shifting each group to a common mean before resampling — but the naive tail-area reading is not it. The permutation test does the job directly and exactly.
Wrong: using the spread of a permutation distribution as a standard error for the difference.
Right: the permutation distribution is built under H₀, where the two groups are the same population. Its spread is the standard error the difference would have if the null were true, which is not the standard error of the observed difference unless the null is true. If the treatment changes the variance — which treatments routinely do — the two differ substantially.
Wrong: "the permutation test showed the groups are different, so the bootstrap interval should exclude zero" (or vice versa), treated as a consistency check.
Right: they usually do agree, and their disagreement is informative rather than contradictory. On the running two-sample data, the permutation p-value is 0.103 and the bootstrap percentile interval for the difference is (−0.072, 2.463) — both say "not distinguishable at 5%", and they say it in the two currencies of part 5, §09's duality. Where they disagree, §12's exchangeability question is the first place to look.
A third procedure exists and is worth naming. The bootstrap permutation test pools the two groups and then draws the two resamples with replacement rather than shuffling. This models two random elements at once — the assignment of treatment to subject and the selection of subjects from a population — rather than only the first. The distinction matters philosophically, matters little numerically, and is worth knowing mostly so that you recognise the term. If in doubt, shuffle.
