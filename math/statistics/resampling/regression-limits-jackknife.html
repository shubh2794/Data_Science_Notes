15 Resampling a regression: residuals, cases, and which one to trust

Part 6, §13 got SE(b) = s_e/√S_xx out of assumptions A1–A4: linearity, independence, constant error variance and — for the t — normality. Resampling can drop the last two, and the choice of what to resample decides which ones you keep. There are two natural answers and they are not interchangeable.

Scheme 1 — resample the residuals (fixed design)

the recipe1. fit once: ê ᵢ = yᵢ − â − b̂xᵢ 2. resample: e*₁, …, e*ₙ drawn WITH replacement from ê₁, …, êₙ 3. rebuild: y*ᵢ = â + b̂xᵢ + e*ᵢ — the xᵢ never move 4. refit: (xᵢ, y*ᵢ) ⟶ â*, b̂* 5. repeat B times; the SD of the b̂* is SE(b)

This is the lecture's scheme, and it is exactly the right one when the model is right. Every bootstrap data set has the same x-values, so S_xx is constant across resamples and the only thing varying is the noise — which is precisely the thought experiment the formula standard error describes. Run it on the twelve points with B = 200 000 and it comes out 8.6% too small. The reason is exact and worth deriving, because it turns a puzzle into a one-line correction.

  • The √((n−2)/n) deflation is not a small-sample curiosity — it is √((n − p)/n) in general, so a model with 20 predictors fitted to 50 rows produces a residual bootstrap SE that is √(30/50) = 0.775 of the truth. A 22% understatement of every standard error, silently. Always rescale.
  • Why the corrected version is still 1.4% low rather than exact: rescaling by √(1 − hᵢ) equalises the residual variances, but the least-squares residuals are also mutually correlated, with Cov(êᵢ, êⱼ) = −σ²hᵢⱼ. Resampling them independently cannot reproduce that. The correction removes the first-order error and leaves a term of order 1/n.
  • Residual resampling assumes the model. Step 3 rebuilds y* from the fitted line, so a wrong functional form is baked into every resample; and step 2 pools all the residuals into one common pool, so it assumes the error variance is the same at every x. Both assumptions are invisible in the output.

Scheme 2 — resample the cases (random design)

the recipe — resample WHOLE ROWS1. draw indices i*₁, …, i*ₙ with replacement from {1, …, n} 2. the resampled data set is (x_{i*₁}, y_{i*₁}), …, (x_{i*ₙ}, y_{i*ₙ}) 3. refit from scratch; repeat B times on the twelve points, B = 200 000SE(b*) = 0.246526 percentile CI (2.5773, 3.5656) pivotal CI (2.4344, 3.4227) against the formula's 0.280386 and its t interval (2.3753, 3.6247)

Case resampling assumes far less: no model form, no constant variance, not even that x is fixed. It treats (xᵢ, yᵢ) as one exchangeable unit and asks what the fit would look like on a different draw of units. The price is that S_xx now varies from resample to resample — some resamples happen to contain the extreme x-values twice and are unusually informative, others miss them and are not — and at n = 12 that extra variability, combined with the bootstrap's usual small-sample downward bias, leaves the SE about 12% below the formula — and the figure in this section, which uses a smaller B, will report a number a point or two either side of that. Both effects shrink as n grows.

When A4 holds, all four agree to within a percent and the choice is irrelevant. When it fails, the residual bootstrap fails in exactly the same way and by exactly the same amount as the formula it was supposed to replace — both understate the slope's uncertainty by about a fifth — while case and wild resampling recover the truth to within a few per cent. This is the whole argument for case resampling as the default: it is the only one of the three that does not quietly re-import the assumption you were trying to escape. Resampling residuals is not "the nonparametric alternative to the formula"; it is the same analysis with the normality assumption removed and nothing else.

Scheme 3 — the wild bootstrap

keep each residual attached to its own observation; randomise only its SIGN and sizee*ᵢ = ê ᵢ/√(1 − hᵢ) · vᵢ with vᵢ i.i.d., E[vᵢ] = 0, Var(vᵢ) = 1 y*ᵢ = â + b̂xᵢ + e*ᵢ the design stays fixed, as in scheme 1 two standard choices for vRademacher: vᵢ = ±1 with probability ½ each — the usual default Mammen: a two-point distribution matching the third moment as well why it survives heteroscedasticityobservation i's error keeps its OWN magnitude |ê ᵢ|; nothing is pooled across i. A point in a noisy region stays noisy in every resample.

The wild bootstrap is the fixed-design analogue of case resampling: it keeps the advantages of scheme 1 — a stable design, no degenerate resamples, works when x is genuinely fixed by the experimenter — while dropping the constant-variance assumption. It is the standard tool for heteroscedasticity-robust inference in regression, and it is the resampling counterpart of the sandwich (Huber–White) standard error.

Two more things resampling gives a regression

  • A permutation test of H₀: β₁ = 0. Under that null, y carries no information about x, so the pairing is exchangeable: shuffle the y-values against the x-values, refit, collect the b*. On the twelve points, zero of 99 999 shuffles produced a slope as large as 3 in absolute value, so p = 1/(99 999 + 1) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ — the floor of §11 — against the t test's 8.52 × 10⁻⁷. Both say "overwhelming"; only the parametric one can put a number on how overwhelming, and only by extrapolating a normal tail.
  • An interval for anything downstream of the fit — R², a ratio of two coefficients, the x at which the fitted curve peaks, the predicted value at a new point, the difference in R² between two models. None of these has a usable standard error formula; all of them are one line inside an existing bootstrap loop. This is where resampling stops being a substitute for part 6 and starts doing things part 6 cannot.
  • Clustered data need clustered resampling. If rows come in groups — repeated measurements per subject, students within schools — resample whole clusters with replacement, never rows. Row-level resampling on clustered data understates standard errors for exactly the same reason §06's i.i.d. bootstrap did on the AR(1) series, and by a similar magnitude.

16 Where the bootstrap fails — with the failures measured

The bootstrap is consistent under conditions, and the conditions are not decorative. When they break, the method does not degrade gracefully and it does not warn you: it returns a tidy interval with the wrong coverage, and the interval often gets no better as n grows. This section catalogues five failures, gives the mechanism for each, and measures the damage.

Failure 1 — the sample maximum. The canonical case, and the cleanest.

the setting: X₁, …, Xₙ ~ Uniform(0, θ), estimate θ by the sample maximumθ̂ = X₍ₙ₎ = max Xᵢ P(θ̂ ≤ t) = (t/θ)ⁿ E[θ̂] = θ·n/(n+1) the TRUE sampling distribution of the error, suitably scaled, is smooth and continuousn(θ − θ̂)/θ ⟶ Exponential(1) P( θ̂ = θ ) = 0 the BOOTSTRAP distribution has a huge atom exactly where the truth has noneP( θ̂* = θ̂ | data ) = P( the largest point is drawn at least once ) = 1 − (1 − 1/n)ⁿ ⟶ 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.632121 measured, 200 000 resamples per rown = 10 simulated 0.6506 exact 0.6513 n = 20 simulated 0.6401 exact 0.6415 n = 50 simulated 0.6367 exact 0.6358 n = 200 simulated 0.6358 exact 0.6330 → the atom does NOT shrink

Nearly two thirds of the bootstrap distribution sits on a single point. Whatever that is, it is not a copy of a continuous exponential. The bootstrap is inconsistent here — it converges, but to the wrong thing — and no increase in n or B repairs it. The consequence for intervals is total.

Zero per cent, and it is not a simulation artefact — it is a theorem. Every bootstrap resample is drawn from the observed data, so θ̂* ≤ θ̂ always. Every percentile of the bootstrap distribution is therefore at most θ̂, so the interval's upper endpoint is at most θ̂, and θ̂ < θ with probability 1. The percentile interval for a maximum cannot contain the parameter, ever. The pivotal interval escapes total failure only because reflecting sends its upper endpoint above θ̂ — it is right for the wrong reason, and it plateaus around 88% rather than converging to 95%. The whole failure was predicted in §03: the maximum is a non-smooth functional of F that depends on the extreme right tail, which is exactly where F̂ₙ carries no information.

Failure 2 — non-smooth functionals, of which the maximum is only the most obvious

estimate θ = μ² by θ̂ = x̄², with X ~ Normal(μ, 1). The map μ ⟼ μ² has zero derivative at 0.at μ ≠ 0: √n(θ̂ − θ) ⟶ Normal(0, 4μ²) the delta method works, bootstrap fine at μ = 0: n·θ̂ ⟶ χ²₁ the rate and the shape both change measured percentile coverage, nominal 95%μ = 2.0 n = 25 → 92.53% n = 100 → 94.33% ✓ fine μ = 0.5 n = 25 → 93.50% n = 100 → 94.33% ✓ fine μ = 0.0 n = 25 → 0.00% n = 100 → 0.00% ✗ total

Again zero, again for a reason that is arithmetic rather than luck: θ̂* = x̄*² is non-negative and its 2.5% quantile is strictly positive, so the interval is a strictly positive interval and cannot contain θ = 0. The same phenomenon appears wherever a parameter sits on a boundary or at a kink — a variance component at zero, a mixing weight at 0 or 1, a constrained coefficient at its limit, a maximum-likelihood estimate on the edge of its parameter space. The general condition is that θ̂ must be a smooth (Hadamard-differentiable) functional of F with a non-degenerate derivative, and boundaries violate the second half of that.

Failure 3 — infinite variance

§02's Monte-Carlo argument for the standard error needed a finite second moment. Without one, the sample standard deviation of the θ̂* converges to nothing, and the interval is built on sand. Pareto populations with tail index α, where the mean exists for α > 1 and the variance only for α > 2:

At α = 1.5 a nominal 95% interval covers two thirds of the time at n = 30 and three quarters at n = 200. It is creeping upward — the bootstrap for the mean is technically consistent whenever the variance is finite and, for 1 < α < 2, needs an m-out-of-n scheme to be consistent at all — but "creeping upward" is not a usable guarantee. Heavy tails are also the case where a single wild observation dominates the resample: about 63% of resamples contain the largest point, and in a heavy-tailed sample the largest point is most of the mean.

Failure 4 — small n

Repeating §08's table, because it belongs in the catalogue: mean of a Normal(0, 1) sample, nominal 95% percentile interval, coverage 74.5% at n = 3, 83.5% at n = 5, 88.5% at n = 8. Nothing exotic is happening. There are only C(2n−1, n) distinct resamples — 10 at n = 3, 126 at n = 5 — and the empirical distribution of three points is a poor stand-in for anything. All bootstrap theory is asymptotic. The bootstrap does not create data, and the widespread belief that it rescues small samples is exactly backwards.

Failure 5 — dependence

Covered with numbers in §06: an AR(1) series with φ = 0.8 gives an i.i.d.-bootstrap interval with 48.45% coverage. The same failure appears for clustered data, spatial data, repeated measures, and any data set with a hidden grouping. The general rule is that you must resample the exchangeable unit, and identifying it is a design question, not a computational one.

A short checklist for whether the bootstrap applies

  1. Is n at least 20–30? Below that, expect meaningful undercoverage and say so.
  2. Is the statistic a smooth function of the data? Means, variances, correlations, regression coefficients, ratios away from zero denominators: yes. Maxima, minima, ranges, modes, counts of distinct values, and anything defined by an extreme: no.
  3. Is the parameter in the interior of its space? A variance that might be 0, a proportion that might be 0 or 1, a constrained coefficient at its bound: expect trouble.
  4. Does the population plausibly have a finite variance? Financial returns, network traffic, city sizes and insurance claims frequently do not.
  5. Are the observations independent? If not, what is the exchangeable unit, and are you resampling it?
  6. Does the bootstrap distribution look pathological? A large atom, visible spikes, or a hard edge at θ̂ is the diagnostic. Always plot the histogram of the θ̂* before quoting an interval from it — it is free, and it is the only warning you get.

17 The jackknife: the bootstrap's predecessor, and part 6's influence in disguise

Thirty years before the bootstrap there was the jackknife: leave out one observation, recompute, repeat n times, and read the variability off the n answers. It is deterministic, cheap, and less general — and its leave-one-out values are exactly part 6, §16's influence calculation, which is why it deserves a section rather than a footnote.

The (n − 1) looks arbitrary and is not. Deleting one point from n barely moves an estimate, so the leave-one-out values are far less spread out than genuinely resampled estimates would be — by a factor of roughly n − 1 in variance. The multiplier restores the scale, and for the mean it does so exactly.

Where the jackknife breaks, and why that is instructive

the same twenty values, the MEDIANbootstrap SE = 3.500 jackknife SE = 2.179 38% too small the mechanism, which is completely visible on a small data setWith n even, deleting one point moves the median to one of only TWO values — whichever side you deleted from. The n leave-one-out medians take at most two distinct values, so their spread measures nothing about the median's real sampling variability. The jackknife is INCONSISTENT for sample quantiles.

The rule that comes out of this is the same one §16 arrived at from the other direction: the jackknife needs the statistic to be smooth, and it needs more smoothness than the bootstrap does. A one-point deletion is an infinitesimal perturbation of F̂ₙ, so the jackknife is essentially estimating a derivative; if the statistic is not differentiable in the right sense, there is no derivative to estimate. The bootstrap perturbs F̂ₙ by a finite amount and survives the median comfortably.

The link to leverage and influence

  • Use the jackknife for: a quick, deterministic, seed-free standard error for a smooth statistic; a bias estimate; BCa's acceleration; a diagnostic scan for influential observations (a leave-one-out value far from the rest names the point).
  • Do not use it for: medians, quantiles, or anything with a kink. And do not use it for a confidence interval directly — it gives a standard error, not a shape, so an interval from it is a normal interval with all of §07's limitations.
  • Cost. n refits against the bootstrap's B. Cheaper for n < 1000, and free of Monte-Carlo error entirely — two different analysts get the identical number, which is occasionally worth a lot.
  • The delete-d jackknife — leave out d > 1 observations at a time — restores consistency for the median and other non-smooth statistics, at the cost of C(n, d) subsets (usually sampled rather than enumerated). It is the jackknife's version of §16's m-out-of-n repair, and the resemblance is not superficial: both work by making the perturbation big enough to see past the non-smoothness.
