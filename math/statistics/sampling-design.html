Producing Data & Sampling

The one part of statistics that happens before there are any numbers. How the data came into existence — who could have been in the sample and who could not, who chose the treatment and why — fixes what the data are able to prove. Everything downstream is arithmetic on that decision. No test repairs a broken design, and this is the page that explains why not.

01 What the data can prove is decided before you analyse them

Part 1 took a batch of numbers as given and asked what it looks like. This part asks the prior question — where did these numbers come from? — and it is the more important of the two, because it is the only one whose answer cannot be changed later. Analysis is reversible; you can always refit. Data production is not.

The one equation the whole part hangs on

  • Every estimate you will ever compute differs from the thing you wanted to know for exactly two kinds of reason: a systematic one that would repeat if you collected the data again the same way, and a random one that would come out differently next time.
  • The random part is called chance error or sampling error. It is the price of looking at a sample instead of the whole population, it is calculable, and it shrinks like 1/√n. Parts 3 to 5 of this series are, essentially, one long study of it.
  • The systematic part is called bias. It is not calculable from the data — nothing in the sample reveals it, because the sample is the thing that is skewed — and it does not shrink with n. Collecting more data the same broken way reproduces the same error at greater expense and with more apparent authority.
  • That asymmetry is the whole subject. It is why a statistician's first question about a dataset is never "how many rows?" but "how did a row get in here, and what would have kept one out?"

The ladder of claims, and what each rung costs

  • Description — "in this dataset, 63% did X." Needs nothing but honest arithmetic. It is also almost never what anyone wants.
  • Generalisation — "in the population, about 63% do X." Needs the sample to have been drawn from that population by a known chance mechanism. This is what §02–§06 buy you.
  • Causation — "doing X would change the rate of Y." Needs the treatment to have been assigned, ideally at random, not chosen by the subjects. This is what §07–§09 buy you.
  • Each rung requires a design feature that must be in place at collection time. You cannot promote a claim up the ladder afterwards with a cleverer model. You can only demote it, honestly, in the write-up.

The rule that governs the rest of the page. Randomness is not a nuisance you tolerate — it is a resource you deliberately spend. Randomising who is selected is what licenses generalisation to a population. Randomising who is treated is what licenses a causal claim. Where you spend randomness determines which of the two you get, and if you spend none you get neither.

02 Population, frame, sample — and the gap that eats surveys

Four words that beginners use interchangeably and that professionals keep rigidly apart. Most real-world bias does not enter at the moment of sampling; it enters earlier, in the list you sampled from, and it is invisible to every diagnostic you can run on the data you ended up with.

The vocabulary, stated precisely

  • Target population — the entire set of units you want to say something about, defined so that any given unit is unambiguously in or out. "All U.S. voters" is not yet a definition; "all people who will cast a ballot in the November election" is closer, and is unobservable at survey time, which is itself the source of an entire literature on likely-voter modelling.
  • Unit — the thing one row is. Person? Household? Session? Order line? Get this wrong and every count, mean and standard error is wrong by an unknown factor. It is the same question as "what is one row?" from part 1's checklist, asked before the data exist.
  • Sampling frame — the concrete, enumerable list from which the sample is actually drawn. A phone number file, a customer table, a school register, a URL crawl, a panel's membership. The frame, not the population, is what you can sample.
  • Sample — the subset of the frame you selected. Respondents — the subset of the sample that actually produced usable data. These are not the same set, and the difference is where non-response lives.
  • Parameter — a number describing the population. Fixed, unknown, not random. Written with Greek letters: μ, σ, p, ρ.
  • Statistic (or estimate) — the same number computed from the sample. Known, and random, because it would have come out differently had a different sample been drawn. Written with Latin letters or hats: x̄, s, p̂.

The three gaps — and only one of them is chance

Target population — everyone the claim is about
↓ coverage gap — units the frame cannot reach (undercoverage), plus units in the frame that should not be (overcoverage, duplicates, dead records). Systematic.
Sampling frame — the list you can actually draw from
↓ sampling gap — the luck of which frame members you happened to draw. Random, and the only gap with a standard error.
Selected sample — the units you tried to measure
↓ response gap — refusals, non-contacts, drop-outs, missing fields, records that failed a pipeline. Systematic whenever willingness to respond is related to the answer.
Respondents — the rows you end up analysing
  • Only the middle gap is the one your formulas describe. A confidence interval computed from the respondents is an honest statement about the frame — more precisely, about the sub-population that would have responded — and it is silent about the two gaps on either side of it.
  • This is why a margin of error of "±3 points" printed next to a poll is a statement about arithmetic, not about accuracy. It bounds the sampling gap only. The coverage and response gaps are, in modern surveys, routinely larger.
  • Undercoverage examples that mattered. A frame of telephone subscribers in the 1930s excluded most of the electorate. A frame of landline numbers today excludes the majority of young adults. A frame of app users excludes everyone without a smartphone. A frame of "customers with purchase amount > 0" excludes everyone who abandoned a cart — which is precisely the group a churn model needs.
  • Overcoverage and duplication are less discussed and just as damaging: a person with two phone numbers has twice the selection probability of a person with one, which quietly reweights the whole sample toward the doubly-listed.

03 The sampling designs

A probability sample is one in which every unit in the frame has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That single property is what makes a standard error computable at all: the randomness in the estimate comes from a mechanism you built, so you can reason about it. Everything else on this page is a variation on how to spend that randomness.

Simple random sampling — the benchmark

  • How it is drawn. Number the frame 1…N; draw n distinct numbers so that every subset of size n is equally likely. In practice: generate a uniform random key per row and take the n smallest, or run a partial Fisher–Yates shuffle. Never "take the first n" of anything, and never sort by an ID and slice.
  • Without replacement (SRSWOR) is the usual meaning: each unit appears at most once, every unit has selection probability n/N, and the standard error carries a finite-population correction. With replacement (SRSWR) makes draws independent, which is mathematically tidier and what most textbook formulas silently assume.
  • What it costs. A complete, enumerated frame — which is exactly what you usually do not have — and, for anything with a physical component, travel to n scattered places. It is the design against which all others are measured, and rarely the one anybody fields.
  • What it does to variance. Nothing clever. Var(x̄) = (S²/n)·(N − n)/(N − 1). The correction only matters when the sample is a large fraction of the population: at n/N = 1% it shaves the standard error by 0.5%, which is why polling a country of 200 million and a town of 20,000 with n = 1000 gives nearly identical precision. What matters is n, not n/N.

Systematic sampling — one random number instead of n

  • How it is drawn. Compute the step k = floor(N/n), pick a random start s in 1…k, then take units s, s + k, s + 2k, …. You need a single random number and a way to walk the list in order, which is why it is the design of choice for a production line, a queue, a click stream, or an interviewer working down a street.
  • What it costs. Almost nothing operationally — and it does not even require you to know N in advance if you are willing to use reservoir-style variants. This is genuinely the cheapest probability design there is.
  • What it does to variance. If the list order is unrelated to the variable, it behaves like a simple random sample. If the list is sorted by something related to the variable — payroll ordered by salary, houses ordered along a street — it acts like a stratified sample and does better than SRS, because the systematic step forces one unit from each region of the ordering.
  • The one way it fails, and it fails badly. Periodicity. If the list has a cycle whose length is a multiple of k, the sample locks onto one phase of the cycle and every selected unit is alike. The classic case: a housing list where every 10th dwelling is a corner unit, sampled with k = 10. Then your sample is 100% corner units and your estimate of floor area is wrong by a constant that no n corrects. The defence is to know your list order, or to randomise it first.

Stratified sampling — divide first, then randomise inside

  • How it is drawn. Partition the frame into H non-overlapping strata that are internally homogeneous — region, firm size, age band, device type — and take an independent simple random sample inside each. Then combine the stratum estimates weighted by the stratum's share of the population, not by its share of the sample.
  • What it costs. You must know each unit's stratum before sampling, i.e. the stratifying variable has to be on the frame. That is the binding constraint in practice: you can stratify a customer file by country and plan tier because the file has those columns; you cannot stratify it by satisfaction.
  • What it does to variance. It removes the between-stratum variability from the estimator entirely. That is not a heuristic, it is an identity, derived in §04. If the strata differ in their means, stratification beats a simple random sample of the same n, sometimes by a factor of several. If the strata have identical means it buys exactly nothing — and it never hurts, beyond the cost of finding out.
  • It has a second, non-statistical purpose: it guarantees you will have usable numbers for every stratum. A simple random sample of 1,000 might yield 11 people from a 1% subgroup, which supports no analysis at all. Stratification lets you deliberately oversample such a group and then weight it back down.

Cluster sampling — take whole groups

  • How it is drawn. Partition the frame into clusters that occur naturally — city blocks, schools, hospitals, households, sessions — sample a few clusters at random, and measure every unit inside the chosen ones.
  • What it costs. Almost nothing, which is the entire point. You need a frame of clusters, not of units — there is no list of all households in a country, but there is a list of all census blocks. And the fieldwork collapses: 40 interviews in 2 blocks instead of 40 interviews in 40 towns.
  • What it does to variance. It inflates it, often severely, because units inside a cluster resemble each other. Neighbours have similar incomes; pupils in a school share a teacher; events in one session share a user. Each additional unit from an already-sampled cluster carries much less new information than a fresh unit would.
  • The damage is captured by the intracluster correlation ρ — the share of total variance that lies between clusters rather than within them. With clusters of size m, the design effect is approximately 1 + (m − 1)ρ. Even a mild ρ = 0.05 with m = 20 gives deff ≈ 1.95: your 40 observations are worth about 20.
  • The correct response is not to abandon clustering — it is affordable and SRS is not — but to account for it: more clusters with fewer units each, and standard errors that use the clustered formula. Analysing a cluster sample as if it were a simple random sample is one of the most common ways to publish an interval that is half as wide as it should be.

Multistage, probability-proportional-to-size, and the designs that actually get fielded

  • Multistage sampling is the working compromise and the design behind virtually every national survey: sample clusters (primary units) first, then sample within the chosen clusters rather than taking them whole. Three or four stages are routine — region, then district, then block, then household, then a person within the household.
  • Multistage designs are usually also stratified at the top: strata of regions, clusters within strata, units within clusters. The resulting selection probabilities are known but unequal, which is fine as long as you keep track of them.
  • Probability proportional to size (PPS). When clusters differ hugely in size, selecting them with equal probability is wasteful. Instead give a cluster a selection probability proportional to its size, then take a fixed number of units from whichever cluster is chosen. The two unequal probabilities cancel, every unit ends up with the same overall chance of selection, and the workload per cluster is constant.
  • Unequal probabilities are not bias — as long as they are known. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator reweights each unit by the reciprocal of its selection probability and is unbiased for any probability design whatsoever. Bias comes from unknown or zero selection probabilities, not from unequal ones.

Convenience and quota samples — the ones with no standard error

  • A convenience sample takes whoever is easiest: the people in your building, the users who happened to be logged in, the first 1,000 rows. There is no chance mechanism, so π is undefined, so no formula in this series applies — not the standard error, not the confidence interval, not the p-value. What you have is one number with an unknown error.
  • A volunteer or self-selected sample is worse than convenience, because the selection is made by the respondent on the basis of how they feel about the topic: the exact variable under study drives inclusion.
  • Quota sampling looks scientific and is not. Interviewers are told to fill demographic quotas — so many men, so many over 65 — but choose which individuals freely. The margins match the population by construction; the within-cell selection is at the interviewer's discretion, and interviewers systematically pick more approachable, more available, more central people. This is not a hypothetical failure mode: quota sampling is what produced the most famous wrong headline in polling history, in 1948.
  • The honest thing to do with a non-probability sample is to report it as a case study, or to model the selection explicitly and state the assumption you are making. What you must not do is print a margin of error next to it.

04 Why stratification beats simple random sampling at the same n

This is the one place in the part where a design choice buys measurable precision, and it is worth deriving rather than asserting. The claim is exact: stratifying with proportional allocation removes the between-strata variance from the estimator, and nothing else changes.

Split the population variance in two

  • Let the population be partitioned into H strata. Stratum h holds N_h units, a share W_h = N_h/N of the population, with mean μ_h and variance S_h². The overall mean is the weighted average μ = Σ W_h μ_h.
  • The total population variance splits into a within and a between piece. This is exactly the identity that ANOVA is built on (part 9) and exactly the identity behind the law of total variance; here it does design work.
the variance decomposition (finite population, ignoring the N/(N−1) bookkeeping)S² = Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² + Σₕ Wₕ (μₕ − μ)² └─ within ─┘ └── between ──┘ derivation, by conditioning on the stratumS² = E[(y − μ)²] = E[ E[(y − μ)² | stratum] ] = E[ Var(y|h) + (μₕ − μ)² ] = Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² + Σₕ Wₕ(μₕ − μ)²

Now compare the two estimators

  • A simple random sample of size n has Var(x̄) = S²/n, and by the decomposition that includes the between-strata term. A simple random sample can, by bad luck, land mostly in the high stratum — and that possibility is exactly what the between term prices in.
  • The stratified estimator x̄_str = Σ W_h x̄_h takes n_h units from stratum h. The strata are sampled independently, so their variances add, weighted by W_h².
  • Set n_h = n W_h — proportional allocation, the sample mirrors the population — and the algebra collapses: the between term is gone. Not reduced: gone. It cannot appear, because the design has forbidden the sample composition from varying.

Read that last line as a design instruction: stratify on whatever separates the strata means most. A stratifier that does not predict the outcome is free but worthless; one that does is the cheapest variance reduction in statistics. And because the difference is a sum of squares, proportional stratification can never do worse than a simple random sample — the worst case is that it does exactly the same.

Optimal allocation: put your sample where the variance is

  • Proportional allocation is not optimal. Minimising Σ W_h² S_h²/n_h subject to Σ n_h = n gives Neyman allocation: sample a stratum in proportion to its share of the population times its internal standard deviation.
  • The intuition is exact: spend interviews where the answers disagree. A stratum in which everyone gives the same answer needs almost no sample; a stratum with huge internal spread needs a lot. Size alone is the wrong guide.
  • With unequal per-unit costs c_h, the same argument gives n_h ∝ W_h S_h/√c_h — sample more where the variance is high and the interviews are cheap.
  • The gain of Neyman over proportional is another sum of squares, so again it can never be negative. But it needs an estimate of every S_h in advance, which usually comes from a pilot or last year's survey, and a badly wrong guess can lose more than the optimisation gains.

The design effect: one number that summarises all of it

  • The design effect is the ratio of the variance your design actually delivers to the variance a simple random sample of the same size would have. It converts any design into a single, comparable currency.
  • Dividing n by the design effect gives the effective sample size: the number of independently, randomly chosen units your data are genuinely worth. Report this, not the raw row count, whenever the design is not SRS.
  • For a cluster design with clusters of average size m̄ and intracluster correlation ρ, the design effect has a closed form worth memorising. It explains why a survey of 2,000 people in 50 villages can carry less information than 400 people chosen at random.
  • The formula is not a hand-wave — you can watch it hold. The clustered population in §03's sandbox has ρ = 0.59 in blocks of m = 20, so 1 + (m − 1)ρ = 12.2; the simulation there reports a design effect of about 12.4 over a thousand repeats. The same population under stratification reports about 0.44, and the two numbers are the whole argument of §03 and §04 in one place.
  • Weighting also costs precision. Unequal weights w_i — from unequal selection probabilities, or from post-stratification — inflate the variance by roughly 1 + CV²(w). This is why survey statisticians trim extreme weights: an unbiased estimator with a variance three times larger can easily have a larger mean squared error than a slightly biased one.

Fixing the composition after the fact: weighting, post-stratification, raking

  • You cannot always stratify in advance — the stratifier may not be on the frame, or non-response may wreck the composition you designed. Post-stratification repairs it afterwards: compute the estimate within known groups, then reweight those groups to match census totals.
  • Raking (iterative proportional fitting) extends this to several variables at once when you know each variable's marginal totals but not the joint table: adjust weights to match age, then sex, then region, then age again, and iterate to convergence.
  • All of this rests on an assumption that is easy to state and impossible to check: within each weighting cell, respondents are like non-respondents. Weighting corrects for observed composition. It is powerless against a difference that is invisible in the variables you weighted on — and if the survey missed a group entirely, its weight is applied to zero people.
  • The 2016 and 2020 election polls are the standard cautionary tale. Many 2016 state polls did not weight by education, and the education composition of respondents had drifted sharply from the electorate's; adding that weight fixed most of the 2016 miss. It did not fix 2020 — polls in the last fortnight overstated the Democratic margin by 3.9 points nationally and 4.3 points in state polls, with the error largest in the states most favourable to the other side. That pattern is the signature of a differential non-response that is not captured by any demographic being weighted on.
The ML mirror. Class weights, importance weighting for covariate shift, and inverse-propensity weighting in off-policy evaluation are all the Horvitz–Thompson estimator wearing different clothes, and all pay the same price: an unbiased estimator with a fat-tailed weight distribution can have a worse mean squared error than a biased one, which is why weight clipping is universal. 05 The catalogue of bias

"Bias" in this part means something narrower and more dangerous than the everyday word: a systematic difference between what your process produces and the quantity you meant to measure. It is a property of the procedure, not of any one dataset, and its defining feature is that repeating the procedure reproduces it exactly.

The shooting-at-a-target picture

  • Imagine shots at a target. An unbiased process scatters around the bullseye — individual shots miss, but the cloud is centred, so averaging many shots converges on the truth. A biased process also scatters, but the whole cloud sits up and to the right. Averaging converges on a point that is not the bullseye.
  • Precision and accuracy are independent. A tight cluster in the wrong place is precise and inaccurate, and it is far more dangerous than a loose cluster in the right place, because tightness is exactly what makes a result look trustworthy. A narrow confidence interval around a biased estimate is a confident lie.
  • The diagnostic asymmetry is brutal: you can measure precision from the data alone, and you cannot measure bias from the data alone. Detecting bias always requires something external — a benchmark, a known total, a second measurement process, or a hard argument about the mechanism.

Selection bias — who ends up in the data

  • Undercoverage. The frame cannot reach part of the population. Structural, usually documented, and usually ignored anyway.
  • Non-response. Selected units do not answer. This is only harmless if responding is unrelated to the answer, and it usually is not: people at home at 6 p.m. differ from those who are not, and people who will talk to a pollster about politics differ from those who will not. Non-response is now the dominant survey problem: response rates to telephone opinion polls fell from around 36% in the late 1990s to roughly 6% by 2018, meaning that 94 of every 100 people selected contribute nothing, and any difference between those 6 and those 94 goes straight into the estimate.
  • Volunteer / self-selection bias. Respondents recruit themselves. The people who write online reviews, call in to a phone poll, or return a mail-in questionnaire are the people who cared enough — which is to say, they are selected on an extreme value of the very variable being measured. The 1976 newspaper survey in which 70% of 10,000 self-selected parents said having children was not worth it, against roughly 91% saying it was in a subsequent probability sample, is the textbook demonstration.
  • Survivorship bias. The units that failed are missing from the frame, so the frame is conditioned on success. The wartime armour analysis is the cleanest case: engineers plotted the bullet holes in aircraft that returned and proposed armouring where the holes were. The correct inference was the opposite — armour where the returning planes had no holes, because planes hit there did not return to be measured. Mutual-fund performance tables, "habits of successful founders", and any long-run backtest over a surviving universe have exactly the same structure.
  • Selecting on the outcome. Keeping only the cases where the event happened deletes the comparison group and makes any predictor look irrelevant. This is the failure worked through in part 1's opening section, and it is worth re-reading here, because it is the same error the whole of §07 is about.
  • Selecting on a collider. The subtlest of the family, and the one that catches trained analysts: conditioning on a variable that is caused by two others induces a correlation between them that does not exist in the population. Restrict a health study to hospital patients and two unrelated diseases become negatively associated among the patients, because either one alone can get you admitted. §07 draws this.
  • Data snooping and the vast search effect. If you look at enough subsets, thresholds, time windows and model specifications, something will be "significant" by construction. Ask 20,000 people to flip a coin ten times and someone will get ten heads; announcing that person as gifted is selection bias, not evidence. The defences are pre-specification and a genuinely held-out set — see leakage and honest evaluation.

Measurement and response bias — what the units say once you reach them

  • Question wording is not a detail; it can move an answer further than any sampling error. The classic split-ballot experiment asked half the respondents whether speeches against democracy should be allowed and the other half whether they should be forbidden. 62% said they should not be allowed; only 46% said they should be forbidden. Logically identical positions, a 16-point gap, and it replicates across countries and decades.
  • Loaded and leading questions. "Do you support the government's failed policy on X?" measures the adjective. So, more subtly, do "welfare" versus "assistance to the poor", "estate tax" versus "death tax", and "not allowing" versus "banning".
  • Question order. An earlier item primes the frame in which the later one is read. Ask about crime first and satisfaction with the police falls.
  • Response-option effects. Offering "don't know" can move a distribution by ten points; the number and labelling of scale points changes the mean of a rating; the mid-point invites satisficing.
  • Social desirability. Respondents over-report voting, exercise, charitable giving and hand-washing, and under-report drinking, smoking, income and socially disapproved opinions. The effect depends on the mode: people are more honest to a web form than to a human interviewer, which means mode changes are confounded with time in any tracking survey.
  • Recall bias. Retrospective questions get worse with distance and are systematically distorted by outcome: people who developed a disease remember their exposures more thoroughly than people who did not, which manufactures an association in a case-control study.
  • Interviewer effects. The same question asked by different people gets different answers. Interviewer identity is a cluster, and it inflates variance exactly like any other cluster.
  • Instrument bias. A scale reading 200 g heavy, a sensor drifting with temperature, a tokenizer silently dropping a script, a logging change deployed on a Tuesday. This is the one kind of measurement bias you can catch — with calibration against a known standard, and by plotting every variable against time.

06 The sample-size illusion: why a big biased sample is worse than a small clean one

Everyone accepts "quality beats quantity" as a slogan and then behaves as though more rows were always better. The slogan is a theorem, and it is quantitative: for any fixed bias there is a sample size beyond which every additional observation is worthless, and you can compute exactly where that point is.

Put the two errors on the same axis

  • Take a proportion near 50%, where the population standard deviation of the 0/1 variable is σ = 0.5 — 50 percentage points. The chance error of an unbiased sample mean is σ/√n, and it falls forever. The bias b is a constant. The total error is their Pythagorean sum.
  • The two are equal when σ/√n = b, that is at n* = (σ/b)². Below n*, collecting more data genuinely helps. Above it, the RMSE curve has already flattened onto the bias floor and further collection buys a rounding error.
  • The numbers are stark. A two-point bias — a house effect a pollster would consider small — puts n* = (50/2)² = 625. A sample of a million people with a two-point bias has an RMSE of 2.0006 points; a perfectly drawn random sample of 625 people has an RMSE of 2.0 points. The extra 999,375 interviews bought 0.0006 of a percentage point.
  • Run the same arithmetic on the 1936 mail poll. Bias ≈ 19 points, n = 2.4 million. Its RMSE was 19.0 points; the equivalent perfectly-drawn random sample has size 2500/19² ≈ 7. Two and a half million responses carried the accuracy of seven.

The exact version: error is quality × quantity × difficulty

  • There is an identity that makes all of this precise and requires no modelling assumptions at all. Take a finite population of N units with values x₁…x_N. Let Rᵢ = 1 if unit i's value is recorded and 0 otherwise, so the recorded sample has size n = ΣRᵢ. Then the error of the sample mean factors into exactly three pieces.
  • Data quality is the population correlation between "did this unit end up in the data" and "what is this unit's value". If the recording mechanism knows nothing about the values — a genuine random sample — this correlation is zero in expectation, and everything else in the identity is multiplied by zero.
  • Data quantity enters only through the fraction sampled, and it enters as √((N − n)/n), which is huge whenever n ≪ N. That factor is what amplifies a tiny defect into a large error.
  • The consequence is genuinely counter-intuitive and worth stating in full: for a fixed defect correlation, the error grows with the population size. A self-selected 1% sample of a big country is more wrong than a self-selected 1% sample of a small one. Collecting more of the same flawed data makes the confidence interval narrower and the answer no better — the more data, the surer you are of the wrong number.
  • Numbers to fix it: a defect correlation of −0.005 — one part in two hundred, undetectable by any diagnostic — on a survey of 2.3 million people covering 1% of the population gives an effective sample size of about 404. The nominal 2.3 million and the effective 404 differ by a factor of 5,700.

When is more data actually the answer?

  • When the target is a tail, not an average. If the quantity of interest is the behaviour of rare events — a search query issued once in a million, a rare failure mode, a long-tail item — then you need enormous volume simply to accumulate enough relevant records. No amount of clean random sampling from a small dataset produces observations that are not there. This is the honest case for big data, and note that it is an argument about coverage of the tail, not about precision of a mean.
  • When the model is high-capacity. Estimating a mean needs hundreds of observations; estimating millions of parameters needs vastly more, and the extra data reduces variance in a genuinely high-dimensional estimation problem. But this reduces the statistical error only — a systematically unrepresentative training set is a bias, and it survives scaling exactly as it does in a poll.
  • When you can spend some of the volume on quality. The most valuable thing a huge dataset offers is often the ability to draw a small, genuinely random, carefully audited subsample from it — one small enough to plot, inspect and label properly.
  • The reverse case is also worth naming: a small dataset lets you look at every outlier, chase every missing value, and read individual records. Those activities remove bias. At a million rows they are infeasible, and the bias stays.
How this lands in machine learning. The size of a training set bounds the variance of what you learn; the way it was collected bounds the bias, and nothing in the loss curve reveals it. A model trained on ten billion logged interactions from one country, one language and one interface has a very small standard error and an unknown offset. 07 Observational study vs experiment — and the lurking variable

The distinction is a single question: who decided which units got the treatment? If the analyst assigned it, the study is an experiment. If the units, their circumstances, their doctors or an algorithm assigned it, the study is observational — and no matter how large, clean, well-measured and beautifully modelled it is, an association in it may point in any direction relative to the causal effect.

What "causes" means, stated so that it can be reasoned about

  • Give every unit two potential outcomes: C₁, what would happen if it were treated, and C₀, what would happen if it were not. Both are properties of the unit and exist before any assignment — they are the unit's full response profile.
  • You observe exactly one of them. Whichever arm the unit ends up in, the other outcome is counterfactual and permanently unobservable. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference, and it is a missing-data problem in which half the table is missing by construction.
  • The causal effect is defined on the two columns: the average of C₁ minus the average of C₀, over the whole population. It answers "what if everyone were treated versus what if nobody were".
  • The association is defined on the observed data: the mean outcome among the treated minus the mean outcome among the untreated. It answers "how do these two groups of people differ".
  • These are different questions about different sets of people, and there is no reason for them to have the same answer. In general they do not — not even the same sign.

A population where the treatment does nothing and the association is perfect

  • Eight units. Four have C₀ = C₁ = 0 and four have C₀ = C₁ = 1: for every single unit the treatment changes nothing, so the causal effect is exactly 0. Now suppose the four units with the good profile all choose the treatment and the four with the bad profile all decline.
  • The observed data show every treated unit with outcome 1 and every untreated unit with outcome 0. The association is exactly 1 — as strong as an association can possibly be — from a treatment with no effect at all.
  • Nothing about this is a small-sample artefact. Multiply the population by a million and the association stays at 1 while the causal effect stays at 0. The confidence interval around the association shrinks toward a point, and the point is wrong.
  • Now push it. Suppose the analyst reads the association causally and persuades most people to take the treatment. The healthy units keep taking it; three of the four unhealthy units start. The association falls from 1 to 4/7 ≈ 0.57 — the intervention appears to have made things worse, though nothing changed for anybody. Association is not merely an imperfect estimate of the causal effect; it is a different quantity that moves for its own reasons.

Confounding, drawn

  • A confounder (or lurking variable) is a variable that influences both who gets treated and what the outcome is. It creates a path from treatment to outcome that is not a causal path, and any association travelling along that path gets attributed to the treatment.
  • The standard example: people who eat little red meat also, on average, exercise more, drink less and smoke less. Any of those could produce the observed difference in disease rates. The comparison is not "meat versus no meat" but "one kind of life versus another kind of life".
  • Directed graphs make this visible. Draw an arrow from each cause to each effect. A back-door path is a path from X to Y that starts with an arrow into X. As long as a back-door path is open, the observed association mixes the causal effect with the confounder's contribution.
  • Two operations close a back-door path: conditioning on the confounder — compare treated and untreated units within levels of Z — or deleting the arrow into X, which is precisely what random assignment does.
  • For the linear case the damage has a closed form, and it is the omitted-variable-bias formula every regression course teaches without saying it is a causal statement.

The collider: the mistake that adjustment makes worse

  • Not every variable should be adjusted for. A collider is a variable with two arrows pointing into it — a common effect rather than a common cause. Conditioning on a collider opens a path that was closed and manufactures an association between its two causes.
  • Concretely: suppose talent and luck are independent, and either one is enough to get a startup funded. Among funded startups, talent and luck are negatively correlated — a funded company that was not lucky must have been talented. Restricting the study to funded companies creates a relationship that does not exist in the world.
  • The same structure generates hospital-admission artefacts, the apparent trade-off between attractiveness and competence among people one is willing to date, and the negative correlation between a candidate's test score and their interview score among hired employees.
  • The practical rule: adjust for causes of the treatment; do not adjust for anything caused by the treatment, and do not adjust for common effects of the treatment and the outcome. "Throw every available variable into the regression" is not a conservative default — it is a way of turning a clean study into a confounded one.

When observational evidence does become believable

  • Adjustment rests on an assumption usually written as "no unmeasured confounding": within the levels of the variables you measured, treatment is as good as randomly assigned. It is unverifiable from the data, because the confounder you missed is by definition not in the data.
  • What makes an observational case convincing is therefore never one study. It is (i) replication across many studies with different designs and different populations, (ii) each controlling for the plausible confounders anyone has proposed, (iii) a mechanism that makes the causal link scientifically plausible, and often (iv) a dose–response gradient, and (v) randomised evidence in a related system where randomising is possible.
  • The smoking–cancer case cleared every one of those bars over decades: repeated cohort and case-control studies with different confounder sets, cellular damage demonstrated in the laboratory, a clear dose–response relationship, and randomised animal experiments. One observational study is not evidence of that kind. It is a reason to design a better study.
  • The quasi-experimental toolkit exists for exactly this situation: natural experiments where something outside the units assigned the treatment; instrumental variables, a variable that moves the treatment and affects the outcome only through it; difference-in-differences, comparing before-and-after changes across a treated and an untreated group; regression discontinuity, comparing units just either side of an arbitrary eligibility cut-off; and matching / propensity scores, which construct comparable groups after the fact. Each replaces randomisation with an assumption. The assumption is the price, and it should be stated as prominently as the estimate.

08 What randomisation actually buys

This is the centre of the part. Randomised assignment is not a ritual, a fairness gesture, or a way of being unbiased in the colloquial sense. It does one specific, extraordinary thing: it makes the treatment and control groups comparable in expectation on every variable simultaneously — including all the ones nobody measured, named, or thought of. Nothing else in statistics does that, and everything called causal inference is an attempt to buy some fraction of it on credit.

The argument, in three lines

  • Each unit carries its full response profile (C₀, C₁): everything about it that determines how it will respond, measured or not. If you assign treatment by a coin flip, the coin cannot possibly know the profile, so assignment is statistically independent of it.
  • Independence means the treated group's average C₁ is the same, in expectation, as the whole population's average C₁; likewise for the control group and C₀. Substituting turns the association straight into the causal effect.
  • The proof is three substitutions long and it is the most valuable three lines in applied statistics.

What the independence is doing, and why it is irreplaceable

  • Read the independence assumption again: X is independent of (C₀, C₁). The response profile is not a variable you measured; it is everything about the unit that matters for the outcome. Randomisation makes assignment independent of all of it at once.
  • Contrast with adjustment. Conditioning on Z buys you independence given the variables in Z. Every variable you failed to put in Z — every one you did not think of, could not measure, or did not know existed — remains a potential confounder for ever, and no diagnostic run on the data can tell you whether one is there.
  • This is the difference between a guarantee and a hope. Matching on age, sex and baseline severity gives you balance on age, sex and baseline severity. A coin flip gives you balance, in expectation, on age, sex, baseline severity, genotype, diet, optimism, the weather on the day of enrolment, and the thing that will be discovered in 2043 to matter more than any of them.
  • Randomisation also earns its second keep, which is easy to overlook: it manufactures the reference distribution. Because you know exactly how the assignment was made, you can compute what the difference between groups would have looked like had the treatment done nothing — by re-shuffling the labels. That is the basis of the permutation test in part 7, and it is why a randomised experiment lets you attach a p-value that means something.

Balance in a single experiment is not guaranteed — it is bounded

  • "Balanced in expectation" is not "balanced". Any one randomisation will show some imbalance in every covariate; the guarantee is that the imbalance is centred on zero and its size is calculable.
  • For a fixed set of n units split at random into two halves, the difference in group means of any variable has standard deviation 2S/√n, where S is that variable's standard deviation among the units. At n = 100 and S = 1 that is 0.20; at n = 400 it is 0.10. Quadruple the trial and halve the imbalance — the same 1/√n that governs everything else.
  • The crucial and easily missed point: that bound applies to every variable at once, whether or not you measured it. There is no variable, however exotic, whose expected imbalance under randomisation is anything but zero.
  • At small n, however, an unlucky draw is genuinely possible, and the standard responses are design-side, not analysis-side: blocked randomisation (randomise within strata of a strong prognostic variable, which forces balance on it and leaves randomisation to handle everything else), minimisation (assign each arriving unit to whichever arm currently reduces total imbalance, with a random component), and rerandomisation (draw an assignment, reject it if a pre-specified balance criterion fails, redraw — and then analyse with a randomisation test that respects the restricted set).
  • What is not a valid response is to test each baseline covariate for a significant imbalance and adjust for the ones that fail. Under a correct randomisation those tests have a null hypothesis that is true by construction, so 5% of them will be "significant" by definition, and choosing adjustments from that list is data snooping. Pre-specify the covariates to adjust for; adjust for them regardless of what the table shows.

What randomisation does not buy

  • External validity. Randomisation makes the comparison inside your sample valid. It says nothing about whether the sample resembles the population you care about. A perfectly randomised trial on 300 undergraduates is internally impeccable and may generalise to nobody. Randomising selection and randomising assignment are different purchases and you must pay for both.
  • Compliance. Assignment is what you randomised; treatment received is what the units did. When they diverge, the clean comparison is intention-to-treat — analyse everyone in the arm they were assigned to, whatever they actually did. Analysing "as treated" quietly restores self-selection and throws away the entire benefit.
  • Attrition. Units that drop out after assignment reintroduce selection, and differential drop-out between arms is fatal. Randomisation protects the assignment; it does not protect the measurement.
  • Interference. The whole framework assumes one unit's outcome depends only on its own assignment. Vaccinate half a village and the unvaccinated half benefits; show half the users a new feed and they talk to the other half; put half a marketplace on a new price and the supply moves. Interference breaks the definition of a potential outcome, and it is the single most common reason a well-run online experiment gives a wrong answer at scale.
  • Precision. Randomisation makes you unbiased; it does nothing about variance. A randomised experiment can be perfectly valid and completely uninformative because it was too small. That is what §09's blocking and factorial designs, and part 5's power calculations, are for.
The bridge to part 3. Everything above rests on a phrase used casually and defined precisely: a random sample. It means a specific chance mechanism, and once one exists, the statistic it produces has a probability distribution of its own — over all the samples that might have been drawn. Part 3 studies that distribution, shows that it is nearly normal whatever the population looks like, and derives the standard error. Every interval and every p-value in the rest of the series is a consequence of the design decision made on this page.

09 Designing the experiment

Given that you are going to randomise, everything else in the design is an attempt to get the largest possible signal out of the smallest possible number of units. There are exactly two levers: increase the signal by choosing the treatments well, and reduce the noise by choosing how treatments are assigned to units.

The vocabulary

  • Experimental unit — the object to which a treatment is applied and on which the response is measured. This is the unit of randomisation, and the sample size for every formula is the number of units, not the number of measurements. Randomise by clinic and measure 500 patients, and your n is the number of clinics.
  • Response — the variable measured. Chosen before the experiment runs; choosing it afterwards is the outcome-switching that makes results unreproducible.
  • Factor — an input variable, quantitative or qualitative, whose effect you want to study. Level — a value a factor takes. Treatment — a particular combination of levels, one per factor.
  • Replication — more than one unit per treatment. Without it there is no way to estimate the noise, and therefore no way to say whether a difference is larger than noise. Two measurements on the same unit are not replication; they are repeated measures, and treating them as replicates is pseudoreplication — a mistake that inflates the apparent n and shrinks every standard error accordingly.
  • Control group — units that get no treatment, or the current standard. Its purpose is to supply the counterfactual: without it, you are comparing the treated units to a hope. "Compared to baseline" is not a control group, because time passed, seasons changed, and the units got older.

The four principles

  1. Control. Hold constant everything you can, and provide a comparison group for everything you cannot. This is why the control arm is given a placebo rather than nothing: so that "being in a study, being handled, expecting an effect" is common to both arms and cancels in the comparison.
  2. Randomisation. Assign treatments by an explicit chance mechanism, so that the remaining differences are known to be chance differences of a calculable size, and so that a reference distribution exists (§08).
  3. Replication. Enough units per treatment to estimate the noise and to make the standard error small enough to answer the question. Sample size is a design parameter chosen in advance from the precision required, not a number you discover afterwards.
  4. Blocking. Group the units into homogeneous blocks and randomise within each block, so that the between-block variability never enters the treatment comparison. Blocking is to experiments precisely what stratification is to surveys — the same variance decomposition, the same gain, the same requirement that you can identify the grouping in advance.

Blocking, and why it works

  • Suppose you want to compare three preparations and your units vary enormously in some baseline way — runners of different fitness, plots of land of different quality, servers of different capacity, users of different activity level.
  • A completely randomised design throws all units into one pool and assigns treatments at random. The unit-to-unit variability sits inside every comparison and inflates the error.
  • A randomised block design forms blocks of similar units — or uses the same unit for every treatment, which is the best block there is — and randomises the treatments within each block. When you difference two treatment means, every block effect appears identically in both and cancels exactly.
  • The matched-pairs design is the two-treatment special case, and the most efficient design available when it is applicable: pair units on the prognostic variable and randomise within each pair, or apply both treatments to the same unit in random order (a crossover). The analysis then works on the differences within pairs, and every source of between-unit variation vanishes.
  • Blocking generalises. A Latin square blocks in two directions at once — rows and columns — with each treatment appearing once in every row and every column. Incomplete block designs handle blocks smaller than the number of treatments while still allowing every treatment pair to be compared free of block effects.
  • The cost of blocking is degrees of freedom: each block consumes one. With very few units, or with blocks that turn out not to be homogeneous after all, blocking can lose more than it gains. It also cannot be undone — a crossover assumes no carry-over from period to period, which is an assumption about the science, not the statistics.

Factorial designs: never vary one thing at a time

  • Suppose two factors, each at two levels. The intuitive plan is one factor at a time: hold B fixed and vary A, then hold A fixed and vary B. It feels careful. It is strictly worse.
  • A complete factorial design runs all combinations — here four. With the same total number of observations it gives you the same two main-effect comparisons and the interaction, which the one-at-a-time plan cannot estimate at all because it never observes the fourth corner.
  • Interaction is the case where the effect of A depends on the level of B. It is extremely common and it is usually the interesting finding: a drug that helps one group and harms another, a feature that works on mobile and not on desktop, a price change that works in one market only. A design that cannot detect interaction will report an average of two opposite effects and call it "no effect".
  • Factorials are also efficient in a way that surprises people. Each main effect is estimated using all the runs, not half of them — the four-corner design gives two independent estimates of each main effect where the one-at-a-time plan gives one. More information from fewer observations.
  • The cost is combinatorial: k factors at two levels each need 2ᵏ treatments — 10 factors is 1,024. Fractional factorial designs run a carefully chosen subset (a half, a quarter, a sixteenth) and deliberately confound high-order interactions, which are usually negligible, with the main effects you care about. Choosing which interactions to sacrifice is the whole craft of screening design.

Placebo, blinding, and the parts of the effect that are not the treatment

  • The placebo effect is real, substantial and still not fully understood: the belief that one is being treated produces measurable change, especially in subjective outcomes like pain, mood and fatigue, and it varies with the ritual — an injection outperforms a pill, a larger pill outperforms a smaller one.
  • A placebo control receives an inert intervention indistinguishable from the real one. Its purpose is not kindness; it is to make the placebo effect operate equally in both arms so that it cancels in the difference. Without it, the treatment arm's effect is treatment plus expectation, and you cannot separate the two.
  • Single-blind: the subject does not know their arm. Double-blind: neither the subject nor the person administering the treatment and recording the outcome knows. Both matter, and for different reasons — an unblinded subject reports differently, and an unblinded assessor measures differently, especially on any endpoint requiring judgement.
  • Triple-blind extends the concealment to the analysts, which prevents the data-dependent analytic choices that are otherwise made unconsciously. Pre-registering the analysis plan achieves much of the same thing.
  • Blinding is impossible for some interventions — surgery, exercise programmes, a redesigned interface. Then blind the assessment at least: whoever scores the outcome should not know the arm. And where a sham procedure is ethical, use one: several surgical procedures have failed against sham surgery.
  • Active controls. When an effective treatment already exists, comparing the new one to a placebo is often unethical and always uninformative. The right control is the current standard of care, and the question becomes superiority or non-inferiority against it.

When you are not allowed to randomise

  • Ethics constrains experimentation far more than statistics does. You cannot randomly assign people to smoke, to be poor, to be unemployed, to endure a hurricane, or to be discriminated against. The most important questions in medicine, economics and social policy are therefore permanently observational at the individual level.
  • Equipoise is the standard that licenses a randomised trial at all: there must be genuine uncertainty in the expert community about which arm is better. Randomising once you already know is unethical; so is continuing after an interim analysis has established the answer, which is why trials have pre-specified stopping rules and data-monitoring committees.
  • Other constraints are practical rather than ethical: you cannot randomise a country's monetary policy, a competitor's pricing, or the weather. And some treatments cannot be withheld once released — you cannot un-ship a feature to half your users if it changes the market they both operate in.
  • Even so, the space of what can be randomised is usually larger than people assume. Encouragement designs randomise an invitation to take the treatment rather than the treatment itself, which is ethical when the treatment itself is not, and is analysed with instrumental-variable methods. Stepped-wedge designs roll a programme out to all units eventually but randomise the order. Randomising the timing, the dose, the framing, or the reminder is often available when randomising the exposure is not.

Leakage is a frame problem

  • Leakage is usually taught as "information from the test set got into training". Stated as a design problem it is sharper: your training frame contains information that the deployment frame will not, so the sample you validated on is not a sample from the population you will serve.
  • That reframing tells you where to look. Any feature computed using data from after the prediction time; any aggregate computed over the full dataset before splitting (means for scaling, target encodings, vocabulary, TF-IDF statistics); any identifier that correlates with the label because of how the data were assembled; any duplicate row that lands on both sides of the split.
  • It also tells you the fix, and it is a design fix, not a modelling one: split first, then do everything else inside the training fold. Fit the scaler on the training fold. Build the encoding on the training fold. For temporal data, split by time and never across it. For grouped data, split by group. See leakage and preprocessing order.
  • The most expensive form is the one nobody detects: the target leaks through a feature that only exists because the outcome already happened — an "account_status" field updated at churn, a "num_support_tickets" that includes tickets filed after the incident. Test performance is superb, production performance is at chance.

Logged data is an observational study — with a confounder you deployed yourself

  • Every record in a behavioural log was produced under a policy: a ranking model, a business rule, a set of eligibility filters. That policy decided which items were shown and therefore which could be clicked. The log is not a sample of user preferences; it is a sample of user responses to what the previous system chose to show.
  • In the language of §07, the deployed policy is a confounder: it causes the exposure (what is shown) and is correlated with the outcome (what is clicked, because the policy was trying to maximise clicks). Naively fitting a new model to that log estimates an association, and it estimates it inside the previous policy's support.
  • The feedback loop makes it worse over time: the new model is trained on data produced by the old one, deploys, produces data, trains the next one. Items the policy never shows are never clicked, never appear promising, and are never shown — a self-confirming frame that shrinks.
  • The standard remedies are the ones §03 and §08 predict. Log the propensities: record the probability with which each item was shown, and reweight by their reciprocals — which is exactly the Horvitz–Thompson estimator from §03. Inject deliberate randomisation: serve a small fraction of traffic from a uniformly random or explicitly exploring policy, which creates a genuinely unconfounded slice — the same trade-off the exploration term makes in reinforcement learning. And run an online test before believing any offline number.

The A/B test, read as an experiment

  • An A/B test is a completely randomised design with two treatments, an enormous n, and a unit that has to be chosen carefully. Everything in §08 and §09 applies verbatim, including the failure modes.
  • Choose the unit of randomisation, and it is rarely the request. Randomising per request means the same user sees both arms, which contaminates the comparison and breaks any outcome measured per user. Randomise per user, and analyse per user.
  • Interference. Two-sided marketplaces, social features and shared inventory all violate the assumption that one unit's outcome depends only on its own arm. The fixes are design-side: cluster randomisation by geography or social component, switchback designs that randomise time blocks, or a budget-split design.
  • Peeking. Watching a p-value and stopping when it dips below 0.05 inflates the false-positive rate far past the nominal level. It is exactly the data-snooping problem of §05, and the design-side fixes are a fixed sample size chosen in advance, or a proper sequential procedure with spending functions.
  • Novelty and primacy effects. A change can move a metric because it is different, not because it is better. Both decay. A one-week test measures a transient; the effect you care about needs a longer horizon or a holdback group.
  • Sample-ratio mismatch is the single most useful diagnostic in the whole practice: if you randomised 50/50 and the arms hold 50.4% and 49.6% of users, the randomisation or the logging is broken, and every result is suspect regardless of how good it looks. It is a direct test of the design, not of the hypothesis. See A/B testing pitfalls.

Dataset construction as a survey you are designing

  • Define the target population before you write the query. Which users, over what period, in which markets, with which eligibility? Write it down; it is the only thing that makes the coverage gap checkable later.
  • Enumerate the filters. Every WHERE clause is an undercoverage decision. Bot removal, minimum-activity thresholds, language detection, deduplication, quality classifiers — each removes a group, and the removed group is never in any evaluation. Corpus filtering is a sampling design with enormous downstream consequences; see data filtering.
  • Stratify deliberately. If a segment matters and is rare, oversample it and record the weights, exactly as a survey would. Do not discover after launch that the model has 40 examples of it.
  • Treat labelling as measurement. Annotators are interviewers: they have biases, they drift, they differ from each other, and the wording of the annotation guideline is question wording. Measure inter-annotator agreement, rotate assignments so that annotator identity is not confounded with any condition, and keep an adjudication process.
  • Hold out a genuinely untouched set. One that is split before any exploration, never used for a decision, and if possible collected from a later time period — a temporal holdout answers a question a random one cannot: does this still work next month?
  • Report the effective sample size, not the row count. A dataset of 10 million rows drawn from 4,000 users has an n much closer to 4,000 for anything that varies at the user level.
The one-sentence version. A model can only ever learn the joint distribution of its training frame; deployment samples from the target population. Every gap between those two — coverage, non-response, survivorship, the logging policy, the filters — is a bias that no amount of capacity, tuning or data volume removes, and none of it appears in the loss curve.

11 The interrogation, end to end

A checklist for the two situations you will actually be in: designing a data collection, and being handed data somebody else produced. The second is far more common and far more dangerous, because the design decisions have already been made and are usually undocumented.

Questions to ask of data you did not produce

  1. What is one row? Unit, and the level at which anything varies. Everything downstream depends on it.
  2. What was the target population, and who wrote it down? If no one did, the analysis has no population and can only be descriptive.
  3. What was the frame? The actual list or query. Then: who is in the population and not in the frame, and who is in the frame twice?
  4. What is the selection mechanism, and is any selection probability zero? A unit with zero probability is invisible and unfixable.
  5. What fraction of the selected units produced data, and is there any evidence about how the missing ones differ? Non-response of 90% is not a footnote.
  6. Was anything conditioned on the outcome? Filters, survivorship, "successful cases only", "customers who churned". Draw the graph and check for colliders.
  7. Who chose the treatment? If it was the units, or a policy, the study is observational and the word "causes" is unavailable.
  8. What was measured, by what instrument, and did it change? Question wording, sensor calibration, code version, logging schema. Plot every variable against time and look for the step.
  9. How many independent units are there really? Clusters, repeated measures, duplicates. Compute the effective sample size, not the row count.
  10. What would a row that does not exist have looked like? The single best question on this list, and the only one that reliably finds survivorship and undercoverage.

Designing a collection: the order of operations

  1. Write the question as a comparison between two specific quantities in a specific population. If it cannot be phrased that way, the design cannot be chosen.
  2. Decide whether you need generalisation, causation, or both, and buy the corresponding randomisation. They are separate purchases (§08).
  3. Define the unit, the population, and the frame, in that order, and record the gap between the last two.
  4. Choose the design. Stratify on whatever predicts the outcome and is on the frame; cluster only where cost forces it, and then budget the design effect (§03–§04).
  5. Fix the sample size in advance from the precision you need, using the design effect — not the raw n (§04, and part 4's sample-size planning).
  6. Pre-specify the primary outcome, the analysis, and the stopping rule. Every decision you leave until after you have seen the data is a degree of freedom that inflates your false-positive rate.
  7. Build in the measurement controls: blinding where possible, split-ballot tests of wording, calibration of instruments, rotation of annotators.
  8. Plan for non-response and attrition before they happen: a follow-up protocol, a random subsample of refusers pursued harder, and a pre-specified sensitivity analysis.
  9. Run a pilot. It is the only way to get the variance estimates that both the allocation and the sample size depend on, and it always finds an instrument problem.
  10. Document the design in the dataset — selection probabilities, weights, strata, cluster identifiers, the exact filters. Analysis two years later is impossible without them.
