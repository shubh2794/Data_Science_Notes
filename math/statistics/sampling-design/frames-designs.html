02 Population, frame, sample — and the gap that eats surveys

Four words that beginners use interchangeably and that professionals keep rigidly apart. Most real-world bias does not enter at the moment of sampling; it enters earlier, in the list you sampled from, and it is invisible to every diagnostic you can run on the data you ended up with.

The vocabulary, stated precisely

  • Target population — the entire set of units you want to say something about, defined so that any given unit is unambiguously in or out. "All U.S. voters" is not yet a definition; "all people who will cast a ballot in the November election" is closer, and is unobservable at survey time, which is itself the source of an entire literature on likely-voter modelling.
  • Unit — the thing one row is. Person? Household? Session? Order line? Get this wrong and every count, mean and standard error is wrong by an unknown factor. It is the same question as "what is one row?" from part 1's checklist, asked before the data exist.
  • Sampling frame — the concrete, enumerable list from which the sample is actually drawn. A phone number file, a customer table, a school register, a URL crawl, a panel's membership. The frame, not the population, is what you can sample.
  • Sample — the subset of the frame you selected. Respondents — the subset of the sample that actually produced usable data. These are not the same set, and the difference is where non-response lives.
  • Parameter — a number describing the population. Fixed, unknown, not random. Written with Greek letters: μ, σ, p, ρ.
  • Statistic (or estimate) — the same number computed from the sample. Known, and random, because it would have come out differently had a different sample been drawn. Written with Latin letters or hats: x̄, s, p̂.

The three gaps — and only one of them is chance

Target population — everyone the claim is about
↓ coverage gap — units the frame cannot reach (undercoverage), plus units in the frame that should not be (overcoverage, duplicates, dead records). Systematic.
Sampling frame — the list you can actually draw from
↓ sampling gap — the luck of which frame members you happened to draw. Random, and the only gap with a standard error.
Selected sample — the units you tried to measure
↓ response gap — refusals, non-contacts, drop-outs, missing fields, records that failed a pipeline. Systematic whenever willingness to respond is related to the answer.
Respondents — the rows you end up analysing
  • Only the middle gap is the one your formulas describe. A confidence interval computed from the respondents is an honest statement about the frame — more precisely, about the sub-population that would have responded — and it is silent about the two gaps on either side of it.
  • This is why a margin of error of "±3 points" printed next to a poll is a statement about arithmetic, not about accuracy. It bounds the sampling gap only. The coverage and response gaps are, in modern surveys, routinely larger.
  • Undercoverage examples that mattered. A frame of telephone subscribers in the 1930s excluded most of the electorate. A frame of landline numbers today excludes the majority of young adults. A frame of app users excludes everyone without a smartphone. A frame of "customers with purchase amount > 0" excludes everyone who abandoned a cart — which is precisely the group a churn model needs.
  • Overcoverage and duplication are less discussed and just as damaging: a person with two phone numbers has twice the selection probability of a person with one, which quietly reweights the whole sample toward the doubly-listed.

The last line is the one to memorise. Bias is a product: missing 40% of the population costs nothing if the missing 40% are just like everyone else, and missing 2% is fatal if that 2% is where all the signal lives. It also tells you the two levers — shrink the fraction missed, or find evidence that the missed are not different.

The frame gap — sampling harder never closes it set the coverage, then draw repeatedly
The machine-learning translation. Your training set is a sample; its frame is whatever the logging pipeline happened to record. If production traffic contains users, languages, devices or item types the log never held, you have undercoverage, and validation accuracy is an honest estimate of performance on the frame and a fiction about performance in the world. That mismatch is the same object as distribution shift, seen from the other end of the pipeline.

03 The sampling designs

A probability sample is one in which every unit in the frame has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That single property is what makes a standard error computable at all: the randomness in the estimate comes from a mechanism you built, so you can reason about it. Everything else on this page is a variation on how to spend that randomness.

Simple random sampling — the benchmark

  • How it is drawn. Number the frame 1…N; draw n distinct numbers so that every subset of size n is equally likely. In practice: generate a uniform random key per row and take the n smallest, or run a partial Fisher–Yates shuffle. Never "take the first n" of anything, and never sort by an ID and slice.
  • Without replacement (SRSWOR) is the usual meaning: each unit appears at most once, every unit has selection probability n/N, and the standard error carries a finite-population correction. With replacement (SRSWR) makes draws independent, which is mathematically tidier and what most textbook formulas silently assume.
  • What it costs. A complete, enumerated frame — which is exactly what you usually do not have — and, for anything with a physical component, travel to n scattered places. It is the design against which all others are measured, and rarely the one anybody fields.
  • What it does to variance. Nothing clever. Var(x̄) = (S²/n)·(N − n)/(N − 1). The correction only matters when the sample is a large fraction of the population: at n/N = 1% it shaves the standard error by 0.5%, which is why polling a country of 200 million and a town of 20,000 with n = 1000 gives nearly identical precision. What matters is n, not n/N.

Systematic sampling — one random number instead of n

  • How it is drawn. Compute the step k = floor(N/n), pick a random start s in 1…k, then take units s, s + k, s + 2k, …. You need a single random number and a way to walk the list in order, which is why it is the design of choice for a production line, a queue, a click stream, or an interviewer working down a street.
  • What it costs. Almost nothing operationally — and it does not even require you to know N in advance if you are willing to use reservoir-style variants. This is genuinely the cheapest probability design there is.
  • What it does to variance. If the list order is unrelated to the variable, it behaves like a simple random sample. If the list is sorted by something related to the variable — payroll ordered by salary, houses ordered along a street — it acts like a stratified sample and does better than SRS, because the systematic step forces one unit from each region of the ordering.
  • The one way it fails, and it fails badly. Periodicity. If the list has a cycle whose length is a multiple of k, the sample locks onto one phase of the cycle and every selected unit is alike. The classic case: a housing list where every 10th dwelling is a corner unit, sampled with k = 10. Then your sample is 100% corner units and your estimate of floor area is wrong by a constant that no n corrects. The defence is to know your list order, or to randomise it first.

Stratified sampling — divide first, then randomise inside

  • How it is drawn. Partition the frame into H non-overlapping strata that are internally homogeneous — region, firm size, age band, device type — and take an independent simple random sample inside each. Then combine the stratum estimates weighted by the stratum's share of the population, not by its share of the sample.
  • What it costs. You must know each unit's stratum before sampling, i.e. the stratifying variable has to be on the frame. That is the binding constraint in practice: you can stratify a customer file by country and plan tier because the file has those columns; you cannot stratify it by satisfaction.
  • What it does to variance. It removes the between-stratum variability from the estimator entirely. That is not a heuristic, it is an identity, derived in §04. If the strata differ in their means, stratification beats a simple random sample of the same n, sometimes by a factor of several. If the strata have identical means it buys exactly nothing — and it never hurts, beyond the cost of finding out.
  • It has a second, non-statistical purpose: it guarantees you will have usable numbers for every stratum. A simple random sample of 1,000 might yield 11 people from a 1% subgroup, which supports no analysis at all. Stratification lets you deliberately oversample such a group and then weight it back down.

Cluster sampling — take whole groups

  • How it is drawn. Partition the frame into clusters that occur naturally — city blocks, schools, hospitals, households, sessions — sample a few clusters at random, and measure every unit inside the chosen ones.
  • What it costs. Almost nothing, which is the entire point. You need a frame of clusters, not of units — there is no list of all households in a country, but there is a list of all census blocks. And the fieldwork collapses: 40 interviews in 2 blocks instead of 40 interviews in 40 towns.
  • What it does to variance. It inflates it, often severely, because units inside a cluster resemble each other. Neighbours have similar incomes; pupils in a school share a teacher; events in one session share a user. Each additional unit from an already-sampled cluster carries much less new information than a fresh unit would.
  • The damage is captured by the intracluster correlation ρ — the share of total variance that lies between clusters rather than within them. With clusters of size m, the design effect is approximately 1 + (m − 1)ρ. Even a mild ρ = 0.05 with m = 20 gives deff ≈ 1.95: your 40 observations are worth about 20.
  • The correct response is not to abandon clustering — it is affordable and SRS is not — but to account for it: more clusters with fewer units each, and standard errors that use the clustered formula. Analysing a cluster sample as if it were a simple random sample is one of the most common ways to publish an interval that is half as wide as it should be.

Multistage, probability-proportional-to-size, and the designs that actually get fielded

  • Multistage sampling is the working compromise and the design behind virtually every national survey: sample clusters (primary units) first, then sample within the chosen clusters rather than taking them whole. Three or four stages are routine — region, then district, then block, then household, then a person within the household.
  • Multistage designs are usually also stratified at the top: strata of regions, clusters within strata, units within clusters. The resulting selection probabilities are known but unequal, which is fine as long as you keep track of them.
  • Probability proportional to size (PPS). When clusters differ hugely in size, selecting them with equal probability is wasteful. Instead give a cluster a selection probability proportional to its size, then take a fixed number of units from whichever cluster is chosen. The two unequal probabilities cancel, every unit ends up with the same overall chance of selection, and the workload per cluster is constant.
  • Unequal probabilities are not bias — as long as they are known. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator reweights each unit by the reciprocal of its selection probability and is unbiased for any probability design whatsoever. Bias comes from unknown or zero selection probabilities, not from unequal ones.

Convenience and quota samples — the ones with no standard error

  • A convenience sample takes whoever is easiest: the people in your building, the users who happened to be logged in, the first 1,000 rows. There is no chance mechanism, so π is undefined, so no formula in this series applies — not the standard error, not the confidence interval, not the p-value. What you have is one number with an unknown error.
  • A volunteer or self-selected sample is worse than convenience, because the selection is made by the respondent on the basis of how they feel about the topic: the exact variable under study drives inclusion.
  • Quota sampling looks scientific and is not. Interviewers are told to fill demographic quotas — so many men, so many over 65 — but choose which individuals freely. The margins match the population by construction; the within-cell selection is at the interviewer's discretion, and interviewers systematically pick more approachable, more available, more central people. This is not a hypothetical failure mode: quota sampling is what produced the most famous wrong headline in polling history, in 1948.
  • The honest thing to do with a non-probability sample is to report it as a case study, or to model the selection explicitly and state the assumption you are making. What you must not do is print a margin of error next to it.

One clustered population, 400 units in 20 blocks, and six ways to pick 40 of them. Watch which units get selected, then read the design effect: the same 40 observations are worth about 90 randomly chosen units under stratification and 3 under cluster sampling.

04 Why stratification beats simple random sampling at the same n

This is the one place in the part where a design choice buys measurable precision, and it is worth deriving rather than asserting. The claim is exact: stratifying with proportional allocation removes the between-strata variance from the estimator, and nothing else changes.

Split the population variance in two

  • Let the population be partitioned into H strata. Stratum h holds N_h units, a share W_h = N_h/N of the population, with mean μ_h and variance S_h². The overall mean is the weighted average μ = Σ W_h μ_h.
  • The total population variance splits into a within and a between piece. This is exactly the identity that ANOVA is built on (part 9) and exactly the identity behind the law of total variance; here it does design work.
the variance decomposition (finite population, ignoring the N/(N−1) bookkeeping)S² = Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² + Σₕ Wₕ (μₕ − μ)² └─ within ─┘ └── between ──┘ derivation, by conditioning on the stratumS² = E[(y − μ)²] = E[ E[(y − μ)² | stratum] ] = E[ Var(y|h) + (μₕ − μ)² ] = Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² + Σₕ Wₕ(μₕ − μ)²

Now compare the two estimators

  • A simple random sample of size n has Var(x̄) = S²/n, and by the decomposition that includes the between-strata term. A simple random sample can, by bad luck, land mostly in the high stratum — and that possibility is exactly what the between term prices in.
  • The stratified estimator x̄_str = Σ W_h x̄_h takes n_h units from stratum h. The strata are sampled independently, so their variances add, weighted by W_h².
  • Set n_h = n W_h — proportional allocation, the sample mirrors the population — and the algebra collapses: the between term is gone. Not reduced: gone. It cannot appear, because the design has forbidden the sample composition from varying.
simple random sampleVar(x̄) = S²/n = (1/n)[ Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² + Σₕ Wₕ(μₕ − μ)² ] stratified, any allocationx̄_str = Σₕ Wₕ x̄ₕ Var(x̄_str) = Σₕ Wₕ² Sₕ² / nₕ stratified, proportional allocation nₕ = n·WₕVar(x̄_prop) = Σₕ Wₕ² Sₕ² / (nWₕ) = (1/n) Σₕ Wₕ Sₕ² — the WITHIN term alone so the gain is exactly the between-strata varianceVar(SRS) − Var(prop) = (1/n) Σₕ Wₕ(μₕ − μ)² ≥ 0, with equality iff every μₕ = μ

Read that last line as a design instruction: stratify on whatever separates the strata means most. A stratifier that does not predict the outcome is free but worthless; one that does is the cheapest variance reduction in statistics. And because the difference is a sum of squares, proportional stratification can never do worse than a simple random sample — the worst case is that it does exactly the same.

Optimal allocation: put your sample where the variance is

  • Proportional allocation is not optimal. Minimising Σ W_h² S_h²/n_h subject to Σ n_h = n gives Neyman allocation: sample a stratum in proportion to its share of the population times its internal standard deviation.
  • The intuition is exact: spend interviews where the answers disagree. A stratum in which everyone gives the same answer needs almost no sample; a stratum with huge internal spread needs a lot. Size alone is the wrong guide.
  • With unequal per-unit costs c_h, the same argument gives n_h ∝ W_h S_h/√c_h — sample more where the variance is high and the interviews are cheap.
  • The gain of Neyman over proportional is another sum of squares, so again it can never be negative. But it needs an estimate of every S_h in advance, which usually comes from a pilot or last year's survey, and a badly wrong guess can lose more than the optimisation gains.

The design effect: one number that summarises all of it

  • The design effect is the ratio of the variance your design actually delivers to the variance a simple random sample of the same size would have. It converts any design into a single, comparable currency.
  • Dividing n by the design effect gives the effective sample size: the number of independently, randomly chosen units your data are genuinely worth. Report this, not the raw row count, whenever the design is not SRS.
  • For a cluster design with clusters of average size m̄ and intracluster correlation ρ, the design effect has a closed form worth memorising. It explains why a survey of 2,000 people in 50 villages can carry less information than 400 people chosen at random.
  • The formula is not a hand-wave — you can watch it hold. The clustered population in §03's sandbox has ρ = 0.59 in blocks of m = 20, so 1 + (m − 1)ρ = 12.2; the simulation there reports a design effect of about 12.4 over a thousand repeats. The same population under stratification reports about 0.44, and the two numbers are the whole argument of §03 and §04 in one place.
  • Weighting also costs precision. Unequal weights w_i — from unequal selection probabilities, or from post-stratification — inflate the variance by roughly 1 + CV²(w). This is why survey statisticians trim extreme weights: an unbiased estimator with a variance three times larger can easily have a larger mean squared error than a slightly biased one.

Three strata, different means and different internal spreads. Slide the separation to zero and watch the two sampling distributions become identical — stratification buys nothing when the strata means agree. Slide it up and the stratified curve collapses inward while n never changes.

Fixing the composition after the fact: weighting, post-stratification, raking

  • You cannot always stratify in advance — the stratifier may not be on the frame, or non-response may wreck the composition you designed. Post-stratification repairs it afterwards: compute the estimate within known groups, then reweight those groups to match census totals.
  • Raking (iterative proportional fitting) extends this to several variables at once when you know each variable's marginal totals but not the joint table: adjust weights to match age, then sex, then region, then age again, and iterate to convergence.
  • All of this rests on an assumption that is easy to state and impossible to check: within each weighting cell, respondents are like non-respondents. Weighting corrects for observed composition. It is powerless against a difference that is invisible in the variables you weighted on — and if the survey missed a group entirely, its weight is applied to zero people.
  • The 2016 and 2020 election polls are the standard cautionary tale. Many 2016 state polls did not weight by education, and the education composition of respondents had drifted sharply from the electorate's; adding that weight fixed most of the 2016 miss. It did not fix 2020 — polls in the last fortnight overstated the Democratic margin by 3.9 points nationally and 4.3 points in state polls, with the error largest in the states most favourable to the other side. That pattern is the signature of a differential non-response that is not captured by any demographic being weighted on.
The ML mirror. Class weights, importance weighting for covariate shift, and inverse-propensity weighting in off-policy evaluation are all the Horvitz–Thompson estimator wearing different clothes, and all pay the same price: an unbiased estimator with a fat-tailed weight distribution can have a worse mean squared error than a biased one, which is why weight clipping is universal.
