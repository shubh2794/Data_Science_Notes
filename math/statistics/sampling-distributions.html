Sampling Distributions & the CLT

The load-bearing part. Part 1 described a batch of numbers; part 2 said where a good batch comes from. This part asks the question that turns data into inference: what would this statistic have been, over all the samples that might have been drawn instead? The answer — the sampling distribution — has a centre, a spread and a shape, and the rest of the series is nothing but arithmetic on those three facts.

01 The hinge: a statistic is a random variable

Almost every confusion in inferential statistics traces back to one sentence that is usually said too fast. Here it is, said slowly. The parameter is a fixed number you do not know. The statistic is a known number that is random — not because the world is random, but because which units ended up in your sample was decided by a chance mechanism you built in part 2. Everything on this page is the study of that randomness.

Estimator, estimate, and why the distinction is not pedantry

  • An estimator is a rule — a function of the sample, written before any data arrive. "Add up the observations and divide by n" is an estimator. Because the sample is random, the estimator is a random variable: it has an expectation, a variance, a shape.
  • An estimate is the number that rule produced on the one sample you actually drew. It is not random; it is 68.42. It has no variance. Asking "what is the standard deviation of 68.42?" is a category error.
  • The standard error is a property of the estimator, reported alongside the estimate. It answers: if the whole study were run again from scratch, how far from this number would the next one land?
  • Same object, three names, depending on which foot you are standing on. Statisticians write X̄ (capital) for the estimator and x̄ (lower case) for the realised estimate. This page uses x̄ throughout and relies on context, but the distinction is the one to keep in your head.

The thought experiment that makes it concrete

You have one sample. You will only ever have one sample. Nonetheless, imagine this:

  1. Draw a sample of size n by the mechanism your design specifies. Compute the statistic. Write it down.
  2. Put the sample back. Draw again — a genuinely fresh sample, by the same mechanism. Compute the statistic. Write it down.
  3. Do this a thousand times, a million times, all the times.
  4. Now look at the histogram of all those written-down numbers. That histogram is the sampling distribution of the statistic.
This is a thought experiment, and it never has to be performed. The sampling distribution is a mathematical consequence of two things you already know: the population (or the model for it) and the sampling mechanism. The law of large numbers tells you where it is centred; the central limit theorem tells you what shape it takes; the standard error tells you how wide it is. The whole of parts 4 to 9 consists of reading probabilities off that curve.

Where the randomness comes from — and where it does not

  • In a survey or an observational study, the randomness is the selection: which units got in. If nothing random was done at selection time, there is no reference distribution and no standard error — only an error. This is the probability-sample requirement from part 2, and it is why a self-selected panel cannot honestly print "±3%".
  • In a randomised experiment, the randomness is the assignment: which units got the treatment. The reference distribution is over the assignments that might have been made, and it exists even if the units were not randomly selected — which is exactly why an experiment can license a causal claim on a convenience sample (part 2, §08).
  • In a simulation, the randomness is the generator. Same mathematics, no fieldwork.
  • In a model of measurement — "the true value plus noise" — the randomness lives in the noise term. The formulas are identical; only the story attached to them changes.
  • Not: "the population is random". Population parameters are fixed. Not: "the data are uncertain". The recorded numbers are what they are. The uncertainty is entirely about which numbers you got to see.

The two population models this page uses

  • The box model. A finite population of N tickets; you draw n of them. If you draw with replacement (or if n is a tiny fraction of N), the draws are independent and identically distributed and all the clean formulas hold exactly. If you draw without replacement — which is what a simple random sample actually does — the draws are slightly negatively dependent, and one correction factor appears (§09).
  • The i.i.d. model. X₁, …, Xₙ are independent draws from a distribution F with mean μ and variance σ². There is no finite population at all; F is the "data-generating process". This is the model machine learning uses, and the one every theorem below is stated in.
  • The two are interchangeable in practice whenever n/N is small. Where they differ, the difference is the finite-population correction, and it is always in your favour: sampling without replacement is more precise, never less.
The sentence to carry forward. "The sample mean is 68.42" is a fact about one sample. "The sample mean is 68.42, standard error 0.31" is a claim about a whole distribution of samples that never existed. Only the second one is statistics — and the rest of this page is the licence to say it.

02 The three distributions everyone confuses

There are exactly three histograms in play at any moment, they live on different axes, they have different widths, and they answer different questions. Mixing any two of them produces a specific, common, and expensive error. Learn to say which one you are looking at before you say anything else.

① population distribution
All the units that could be drawn
Fixed. Not random. Centre μ, spread σ, whatever shape reality gave it — skewed, bimodal, lumpy. You never see it. Increasing n does not change it.
② the distribution of one sample
The n numbers in your dataset
Random. This is what part 1 plots. It imitates ① — same centre, same spread, same shape — and gets better at imitating it as n grows. Its width is about σ, and it does not shrink with n.
③ sampling distribution of a statistic
One dot per hypothetical sample
Random. Centred on the parameter, width σ/√n, shape driven towards normal by the CLT. It is not a distribution of data at all — it is a distribution of summaries. It does shrink with n.

03 The sampling distribution of the mean: centre, spread, shape

Three questions, three answers, and two of them are exact for every sample size and every population shape. Only the third — the shape — needs the central limit theorem, and that is why the CLT gets a section of its own while these get derived here in full.

Setup

the modelX₁, X₂, …, Xₙ independent, each with mean μ and variance σ², drawn from the same population the two statisticsthe sum Sₙ = X₁ + X₂ + ⋯ + Xₙ the average x̄ₙ = Sₙ / n = (1/n)·∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ

Centre — the mean is unbiased, and the proof needs no independence at all

expectation is linear — that is the entire argumentE[x̄ₙ] = E[(1/n)·∑Xᵢ] = (1/n)·∑E[Xᵢ] = (1/n)·(nμ) = μ so the bias is exactly zerobias(x̄ₙ) = E[x̄ₙ] − μ = 0 for every n, for every population with a finite mean and for the sum, by the same two linesE[Sₙ] = nμ
  • Notice what was not used: independence, identical distributions, normality, large n. Linearity of expectation holds regardless. Unbiasedness of the sample mean is about as robust as a result gets.
  • Notice what was used: that each E[Xᵢ] = μ, i.e. every unit in the population had the same chance-weighted expectation. That is precisely what a probability sample of the right frame buys you. If the frame is wrong, E[x̄ₙ] = μ_F ≠ μ, and no amount of n repairs it — the whole argument of part 2, §02.
  • "Unbiased" means the estimator is right on average over repeated samples. It says nothing about your one sample being close. That is the spread's job.

Spread — the standard error, derived from the variance of a sum

Variances do not add in general; they add when the terms are uncorrelated. Write it out once and the 1/√n law falls out.

the variance of a sum, in generalVar(∑Xᵢ) = ∑Var(Xᵢ) + 2·∑ᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) independent draws kill every covariance termCov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) = 0 for i ≠ j ⟹ Var(Sₙ) = n·σ² and dividing a random variable by n divides its variance by n²Var(x̄ₙ) = Var(Sₙ/n) = Var(Sₙ)/n² = nσ²/n² = σ²/n take the square root — the SQUARE ROOT LAWSE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n SE(Sₙ) = σ·√n
  • The average and the sum move in opposite directions. The average gets more precise like 1/√n; the sum's absolute wobble grows like √n. Both are the same fact, viewed through a division by n. §04 makes this the centrepiece, because it is the single most misread consequence of the law of large numbers.
  • Population size is absent. N appears nowhere. A sample of 1 000 is exactly as precise about a nation of 60 million as about a town of 60 thousand — provided both were sampled properly. This astonishes everyone the first time and is the most practically useful fact in the subject (§09).
  • What "standard error" means. It is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. Different name, same object, applied to a statistic rather than to data. Reserve "standard deviation" for the spread of ① or ②, and "standard error" for the spread of ③, and you will never write the wrong one on an axis again.
  • Independence is doing real work here. If the draws are positively correlated with common correlation ρ, the covariance terms come back:
equally correlated draws — the design-effect formula from part 2Var(x̄ₙ) = (σ²/n)·[1 + (n − 1)·ρ] deff = 1 + (n − 1)·ρ and the sting in the tailas n → ∞ with ρ > 0 fixed: Var(x̄ₙ) → σ²·ρ ≠ 0 correlated data do not average away — more of them buys nothing beyond a point

With ρ = 0.05 and n = 1 000, the variance is 1 + 999(0.05) = 50.95 times the independent value: your 1 000 correlated rows carry the information of about 20 independent ones. That is the design effect of part 2, §04, and it is the same arithmetic that ruins standard errors computed on clustered users, repeated measurements, and autocorrelated time series.

The sample variance is unbiased too — and this is where n − 1 comes from

start from the algebraic identity∑(Xᵢ − x̄)² = ∑Xᵢ² − n·x̄² take expectations, using E[Y²] = Var(Y) + (E[Y])² on each pieceE[∑Xᵢ²] = n·(σ² + μ²) E[n·x̄²] = n·(σ²/n + μ²) = σ² + nμ² subtractE[∑(Xᵢ − x̄)²] = nσ² + nμ² − σ² − nμ² = (n − 1)·σ² so dividing by n − 1, and ONLY by n − 1, is unbiasedE[s²] = E[ ∑(Xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) ] = σ²
  • The −1 is not a fudge and not a small-sample patch: it is the exact price of having estimated the centre from the same data. Dividing by n understates the variance by a factor (n−1)/n — a 20% error at n = 5. Part 1, §05 introduced s; this is the reason its denominator looks odd.
  • But s itself is biased low for σ. Unbiasedness does not survive a square root: since the square root is concave, E[s] = E[√(s²)] < √(E[s²]) = σ by Jensen's inequality. At n = 5 drawing from an exponential population, E[s²] = 1.00 but E[s] ≈ 0.867. Nobody corrects for this in practice; you should merely know it is there.
  • Which matters because s/√n — the estimated standard error — is what you actually report, and its own noise is exactly why part 4 will replace z with t.

Shape — and the two cases where you already know it exactly

  • Normal population ⟹ normal mean, exactly, for every n. A sum of independent normals is normal; scaling preserves normality. So x̄ₙ ~ Normal(μ, σ²/n) exactly, even at n = 2. No approximation, no CLT.
  • 0/1 population ⟹ the mean is a rescaled binomial, exactly. The count Sₙ is Binomial(n, p), so the proportion p̂ = Sₙ/n has an exactly known distribution for every n (§10). The normal enters only as an approximation to that.
  • Anything else — and this is the general case — the exact shape of x̄ₙ is some convolution nobody wants to compute. That is what the CLT is for (§06).
  • Two exact facts you can use to predict the shape before invoking any theorem, because they hold for every n:
skewness and excess kurtosis of the mean, exactlyskew(x̄ₙ) = γ₁ / √n exkurt(x̄ₙ) = γ₂ / n where these are the POPULATION shape numbersγ₁ = E[(X − μ)³]/σ³ γ₂ = E[(X − μ)⁴]/σ⁴ − 3

Checked by simulation on an exponential population (γ₁ = 2, γ₂ = 6), two million samples each: at n = 4 the measured skew of x̄ is 1.001 against a theory of 1.000, and the excess kurtosis 1.498 against 1.500; at n = 64, 0.2507 against 0.2500 and 0.0943 against 0.0938. The asymmetry of the population is inherited by the mean, divided by √n. That single formula is, in advance, the entire answer to "how large must n be?" — and §07 turns it into a number.

All of it at once, on a box you can enumerate by hand

Four tickets in a box: 1, 2, 3, 4. Draw n = 2 with replacement. There are 16 equally likely samples, so the sampling distribution can be written out in full — no theorem, no simulation, just counting.

  • The population is flat. Its sampling distribution at n = 2 is already triangular — the march toward a bell has begun at the second observation. That is the central limit theorem visible in a hand tally.
  • Both variance formulas — the plain one and the finite-population one — are confirmed to the last digit by enumeration. Nothing here is asymptotic; §03's results are exact statements about finite boxes.
  • Sampling without replacement gives a variance of 0.4167 rather than 0.625: a third less, because half the population is being examined. That is what the correction factor is doing.
Summary card. E[x̄ₙ] = μ exactly, always. SE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n exactly, whenever the draws are independent. skew(x̄ₙ) = γ₁/√n exactly. Only the shape needs a limit theorem, and even that comes with a rate.

04 The law of large numbers — and the way everybody misreads it

The law of large numbers says the sample mean settles down on the population mean. It is proved in four lines, it comes in two strengths, and it is misread so consistently that the misreading has its own name. What it does not say is at least as important as what it does.

The tool: Markov, then Chebyshev

Markov's inequality — for a non-negative Y with a finite meanP(Y ≥ t) ≤ E[Y]/t for every t > 0 why: split the integral at t and throw away the left pieceE[Y] = ∫₀^∞ y·f(y) dy ≥ ∫ₜ^∞ y·f(y) dy ≥ t·∫ₜ^∞ f(y) dy = t·P(Y ≥ t) Chebyshev's inequality — apply Markov to Y = (X − μ)²P(|X − μ| ≥ t) = P((X − μ)² ≥ t²) ≤ E[(X − μ)²]/t² = σ²/t² in standardised form, with k standard deviationsP(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² k = 2 → ≤ 25%, k = 3 → ≤ 11.1%, k = 4 → ≤ 6.25%

Chebyshev is distribution-free and therefore very loose — for an exponential the true P(|X − μ| ≥ 2σ) is 5.0%, not 25%. Its virtue is that it needs nothing but a finite variance, which is exactly what the next proof requires.

The weak law, proved

apply Chebyshev to x̄ₙ, whose variance you derived in §03P(|x̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(x̄ₙ)/ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0 as n → ∞, for every fixed ε > 0 that is convergence IN PROBABILITY, writtenx̄ₙ →ᵖ μ
  • Read the quantifiers. Fix a tolerance ε — say 0.01 — first. Then the probability that x̄ₙ misses μ by more than that tolerance goes to zero. You do not get to shrink ε as n grows; the statement is one ε at a time.
  • The proof assumed a finite variance for convenience. The weak law actually holds under the weaker condition that the mean exists at all.
  • It also gives a sample size, not just a limit. For a proportion, σ² = p(1−p) ≤ ¼, so P(|p̂ − p| > 0.1) ≤ 1/(4n(0.1)²) = 25/n. To make that at most 10% you need n ≥ 250. Compare: the sharper Hoeffding bound below asks for 150, and the CLT — which needs an approximation, not a bound — asks for 68.

  • Almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability, never the reverse. The standard counterexample: let U be uniform on (0,1) and Xₙ = √n when U < 1/n, else 0. Then P(|Xₙ| > ε) = 1/n → 0, so Xₙ →ᵖ 0 — yet E[Xₙ²] = 1 for every n, so it does not converge in mean square at all.

The misreading: absolute error grows even as relative error shrinks

"The law of averages" is usually invoked to mean that a run of bad luck will be compensated by a run of good luck. It will not be. Nothing pulls the sum back. The proportion converges only because the denominator outruns the wandering numerator.

for a fair coin, σ = ½ — the two quantities move in opposite directionsSD(Sₙ − n/2) = σ√n = 0.5·√n GROWS SD(x̄ₙ − ½) = σ/√n = 0.5/√n SHRINKS typical absolute gaps (exact binomial, and the √(2/π) normal approximation)n = 100 E|Sₙ − n/2| = 3.98 heads as a proportion 0.0399 n = 10 000 E|Sₙ − n/2| = 39.89 heads as a proportion 0.00399 n = 1 000 000 E|Sₙ − n/2| ≈ 398.94 heads as a proportion 0.000399
  • Between 100 and a million tosses the typical surplus of heads over tails grows a hundredfold, from about 4 to about 399, while the typical error in the rate falls a hundredfold. Both statements are true at once and they are the same statement.
  • So the gambler's fallacy is not a misunderstanding of probability; it is a confusion of Sₙ with x̄ₙ. After ten heads you are ten heads "up", and you will stay about ten heads up forever — it just stops mattering, proportionally.
  • Two more things the law of large numbers does not say. It does not apply to sums — their spread diverges. It does not apply when the mean does not exist — §08 shows a population where the running average never settles at all. And it says nothing about how fast anything happens: that is §06 and §07.
  • One thing it does say, that is easy to miss: applied to indicator variables, it says the empirical proportion of any event converges to its probability, so the whole empirical distribution converges to the population's. That is why a histogram of a large sample looks like the population's histogram, and it is the formal content of the plug-in principle from part 1.

05 The normal curve, exactly as much as this page needs

The full theory of distributions belongs on a Probability page, and there is not one yet — so this section carries only what the central limit theorem and everything downstream actually consume: the curve, standardising, the 68–95–99.7 rule, quantiles, and the one diagnostic plot that tells you whether normality is a reasonable description.

The curve

the standard normal density and its cumulativeφ(z) = (1/√(2π))·e^(−z²/2) Φ(z) = ∫₋∞^z φ(u) du the general normal, mean μ and SD σf(x) = (1/(σ√(2π)))·e^(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)) written X ~ Normal(μ, σ²) standardising — the move that makes ONE table enough for all of themz = (x − μ)/σ ⟹ Z ~ Normal(0, 1) and back the other way, for percentilesx = μ + z·σ
  • A normal distribution is completely pinned down by two numbers. Know μ and σ and you know every probability there is to know about it. Very few distributions are that obliging, and it is a large part of why this one is everywhere.
  • z is unit-free — it is a count of standard deviations. z = 2 means "two SDs above average", whether the units are inches, dollars or milliseconds. This is the same z-score as part 1, §06, now used for probability rather than position.
  • Standardising does not make data normal. It re-centres and re-scales; it cannot change shape. A value 3 SDs above the mean is unremarkable in a heavy-tailed sample; under a normal law it happens once in 741 times. Same number, entirely different meaning.

Working with the curve: the four-step drill

  1. Draw it and shade the area you want. Half of all normal-approximation errors are sign errors that a sketch prevents.
  2. Standardise every boundary: z = (x − μ)/σ.
  3. Rewrite the shaded area as a difference of left-tail areas: P(a < X < b) = Φ(z_b) − Φ(z_a).
  4. Look up or compute the two numbers and subtract.

The diagnostic: a normal quantile plot

  • Sort the values. Plot the i-th smallest against the quantile of a standard normal that the i-th smallest of n draws "should" sit at. The usual plotting position is Φ⁻¹((i − 3/8)/(n + 1/4)); the exact convention barely matters.
  • Straight line ⟹ normal. The slope estimates σ and the intercept μ, which is a pleasant bonus.
  • Curved upward at both ends (an S lying on its back) ⟹ heavy tails — extremes further out than normal. This is what real financial returns, latencies, claim sizes and file sizes look like.
  • Curved one way only ⟹ skew. Points above the line at the top and above it at the bottom means a long right tail.
  • Flat steps ⟹ discreteness or rounding. A cliff at one end ⟹ a floor, a cap, or censoring.

06 The central limit theorem

The most consequential theorem in applied statistics, and the one most often quoted in a form it does not have. Here is the precise statement, the hypotheses it genuinely needs, the six things it does not say, and the equivalent phrasings you will meet in the wild.

The statement

hypotheses — and there are only three of themX₁, X₂, … are INDEPENDENT X₁, X₂, … are IDENTICALLY DISTRIBUTED, with mean μ = E[X] the variance is FINITE and POSITIVE: 0 < σ² = Var(X) < ∞ conclusion — standardise the mean and the shape convergesZₙ = (x̄ₙ − μ)/(σ/√n) = √n·(x̄ₙ − μ)/σ lim(n→∞) P(Zₙ ≤ z) = Φ(z) for every real z that mode of convergence is called convergence IN DISTRIBUTIONZₙ ⇝ Normal(0, 1) — the CDFs converge, pointwise, at every continuity point
  • Nothing whatsoever is assumed about the shape of the population. Discrete, continuous, skewed, bimodal, bounded, spiky — irrelevant, provided the variance is finite. That is what makes the theorem remarkable rather than merely useful.
  • The standardisation is not decoration. x̄ₙ on its own converges to the constant μ — it collapses to a spike, and a spike has no shape. Multiplying by √n is precisely the magnification that holds the picture still: Var(√n·(x̄ₙ − μ)) = σ² for every n. Multiply by n^(1/4) and you still get a spike; by n and the picture blows up. √n is the unique right zoom.
  • The theorem is about probabilities, not about the random variable. Zₙ does not "become" a normal random variable; the probability of every interval converges to the normal one.

Five ways of writing the same thing

all identical in content; pick whichever the problem hands youZₙ ≈ Normal(0, 1) x̄ₙ ≈ Normal(μ, σ²/n) ← the working form, for averages x̄ₙ − μ ≈ Normal(0, σ²/n) √n·(x̄ₙ − μ) ≈ Normal(0, σ²) ← the form the theorem is stated in Sₙ ≈ Normal(nμ, nσ²) ← the working form, for sums and counts and, crucially for practice, s may replace σ√n·(x̄ₙ − μ)/s ⇝ Normal(0, 1) because s →ᵖ σ, by Slutsky's theorem

The last line is what makes the theorem usable at all: you never know σ. It is a theorem in its own right, and the reason it is only asymptotically true is why part 4 replaces the normal with the t distribution at small n.

What the CLT does not say

Frequently claimedActually
"With enough data, the data become normal."Never. The population is what it is. Collecting a million incomes gives you a million incomes with the same skew — a beautifully resolved picture of a non-normal distribution. Only averages are driven towards normality.
"n ≥ 30 is enough."Not a theorem, and not even a good rule of thumb. It is roughly right for mildly skewed populations and disastrously wrong for skewed or lumpy ones. §07 replaces it with an actual formula.
"x̄ converges to a normal distribution."x̄ₙ converges to the constant μ. It is the standardised version that converges to a normal. Getting this backwards makes the √n look arbitrary.
"So I can compute any probability I like."Only near the centre. The theorem controls the CDF uniformly, but a relative error in a tail probability of 10⁻⁶ can be enormous even when the absolute error is 10⁻⁴. Extreme tails are the province of large-deviation theory, not the CLT (§08).
"It applies to any statistic."To sums and averages — and, via the delta method, to smooth functions of them. It does not apply to the maximum, the minimum, or the range: their limits are extreme-value distributions, an entirely different family.
"It needs the observations to be continuous / symmetric / bounded."None of those. It needs independence, identical distribution, and a finite positive variance. Nothing else.

Beyond i.i.d.: the hypotheses can be relaxed, but not for free

  • Non-identical distributions. The Lindeberg–Feller theorem replaces "identically distributed" with a condition saying that no single term dominates the total variance: for every ε > 0, the fraction of the total variance contributed by terms exceeding ε·(total SD) must vanish. A sufficient and easier-to-check version is Lyapunov's condition on third moments. The intuition is the whole content: the CLT works because the sum is a democracy of many small contributions. Give one term a controlling stake and it fails.
  • Dependence. There are CLTs for stationary sequences with fast-decaying dependence, for martingales, and for many other structures — but the variance in them is not σ²/n. It is a long-run variance that sums the autocovariances, and using σ²/n in the presence of dependence understates the standard error, often by a lot (§03, and §08).
  • Multivariate. The vector version holds with a covariance matrix in place of σ², and it is what licenses the joint normality of regression coefficients in part 6.
  • The delta method. If √n(Yₙ − μ)/σ ⇝ Normal(0,1) and g is differentiable with g′(μ) ≠ 0, then g(Yₙ) ≈ Normal(g(μ), (g′(μ))²σ²/n). This is how the CLT reaches log-odds, ratios, correlations and rates — you get a standard error for almost any smooth transformation for the price of one derivative.
  • A little history. The binomial case was proved first, for a fair coin; then extended to arbitrary success probabilities; the general i.i.d. version was first proved rigorously around 1901, and the near-minimal Lindeberg condition arrived in the 1920s. Two centuries of gradual weakening of the hypotheses — which is why "the CLT" is a family, not a single theorem.

07 How fast? The rate of convergence, and the death of “n ≥ 30”

A limit theorem with no rate is not actionable. Fortunately the CLT has two rate results — one a hard bound, one a sharp approximation — and together they turn "is n big enough?" into arithmetic you can do on the back of an envelope. The answer depends almost entirely on one number: the population's skewness.

The hard bound: Berry–Esseen

for i.i.d. X with a finite third absolute central moment ρ = E|X − μ|³sup₍z₎ |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C·ρ / (σ³·√n) the constant is known to lie in a narrow rangeC ≤ 0.4690 for i.i.d. summands C ≥ (√10 + 3)/(6√(2π)) ≈ 0.4097
  • The rate is 1/√n, and it is uniform in z — one bound covering the whole CDF at once. It is a genuine guarantee, not an approximation.
  • It is also loose, because it must cover the worst distribution with that third moment. For an exponential population it overstates the true error by about a factor of nine. Use it to know that convergence cannot be slower than 1/√n; use the next result to know what the error actually is.
  • Note the shape of the bound: ρ/σ³ is a standardised third moment — a close cousin of skewness. The distributions that converge slowly are precisely the lopsided ones.

08 Where the CLT fails outright

Slow convergence is a nuisance you can pay off with data. The failures in this section are different: no n fixes them, because a hypothesis of the theorem is simply false. Real data violate these hypotheses more often than the textbook framing suggests, so it is worth knowing exactly which one is broken and what to do instead.

Failure 1 — infinite variance

The Cauchy distribution has density 1/(π(1 + x²)). It looks innocuous: symmetric, unimodal, a perfectly respectable bell-ish curve. Its tails decay like 1/x², which is just slow enough that E|X| diverges — so it has no mean and no variance at all. And it has a startling property:

for X₁ … Xₙ i.i.d. Cauchy(0, 1)x̄ₙ ~ Cauchy(0, 1) exactly, for EVERY n so averaging accomplishes precisely nothingP(|x̄ₙ| > 3) = 1 − (2/π)·arctan(3) = 20.48% at n = 1, at n = 10, at n = 10⁶ IQR(x̄ₙ) = 2 for every n — the spread does not shrink at all
  • Averaging a thousand Cauchy observations gives you a number distributed exactly like a single one. The law of large numbers fails, the CLT fails, and the running average never settles — it drifts, then a single draw resets it, forever.
  • The general picture. For heavy-tailed populations with tail index α (the tail decaying like x^(−α)): if α > 2 the variance is finite and the ordinary CLT applies; if 1 < α < 2 the mean exists but the variance does not, and normalised sums converge to an α-stable law with the scaling n^(1−1/α) instead of √n; if α ≤ 1 even the mean fails to exist and the running average diverges. The Cauchy is the case α = 1.
  • The constructive half. The failure is a property of the statistic, not only of the population. The median of n Cauchy draws converges perfectly well, with standard error π/(2√n) — the same 1/√n law, from an entirely different theorem (the asymptotic normality of sample quantiles, which needs only a positive density at the quantile). Change the statistic and the machinery comes back.

Failure 2 — dependence

  • The CLT's σ²/n is derived by killing every covariance term. With equally correlated observations, Var(x̄ₙ) = (σ²/n)[1 + (n−1)ρ] → σ²ρ — a floor that more data never breaks through. Nothing converges to a point, so nothing converges to a normal around that point either.
  • Where you meet it. Repeated measurements on the same subject; multiple sessions from one user; rows sharing a cluster, a school, a shop, a shard; anything ordered in time with autocorrelation; augmented copies of the same image; overlapping windows in a time series.
  • The tell. Your intervals are far too narrow and everything is "significant". The diagnostic is to compute the effective sample size rather than the row count: n_eff ≈ n / deff, with deff = 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of size m — the design effect from part 2 — or, for a time series, n_eff ≈ n·(1 − r₁)/(1 + r₁) for a first-order autocorrelation r₁.
  • The fix. Aggregate to the independent unit (one row per user, per cluster, per day) and analyse that; or use a variance estimator that accounts for the structure — clustered standard errors, block bootstrap, mixed models. Never simply use the row count.

Failure 3 — one term dominates

  • The CLT works because a sum is a democracy of many comparable contributions. If a single term carries a fixed fraction of the total variance, the Lindeberg condition fails and the limit is whatever that term's distribution is, blurred a little.
  • Practical instances: total revenue where one enterprise customer is 40% of the book; a sum of file sizes containing one 200 GB file; a "portfolio" of ten positions of which one is nine tenths of the exposure; a mean over classes where one class holds 95% of the mass.
  • The diagnostic is direct: compute each term's share of the total variance, sort descending, and look at the top one. If it exceeds a few percent, the sum is not going to be normal, and the sample-size rule of §07 does not apply because the population is not really a single population.

Failure 4 — the deep tail, even when the CLT is working perfectly

  • Berry–Esseen bounds the error in the CDF additively. At n = 10 000 with a mild population, that absolute error might be 10⁻⁴ — excellent. But if you are computing a p-value of 10⁻⁶, an absolute error of 10⁻⁴ is a relative error of 100×.
  • So: the normal approximation is reliable for 95% intervals, decent for 99% ones, and should be treated with suspicion for the extreme tail probabilities that large-scale testing produces — genome scans, ad-serving experiments, anomaly thresholds at six sigma. Those need exact tests, saddlepoint approximations, or large-deviation results, not Φ.
  • The same caution reads forward: a "5-sigma" claim rests on the normal approximation being accurate at 10⁻⁷, which is exactly where it is least trustworthy.

Failure 5 — the sample is not a sample

The most common failure of all, and the only one with no mathematical remedy. Every theorem above conditions on a chance mechanism having generated the sample. If the data were self-selected, convenience-collected, filtered on the outcome, or logged with a coverage gap, then E[x̄ₙ] = μ_F ≠ μ and the sampling distribution is beautifully, precisely centred on the wrong number. The standard error shrinks like 1/√n; the bias does not shrink at all. This is part 2, §06, and it outranks everything on this page.

Consequence 1 — the population's size is irrelevant

  • N does not appear in σ/√n. The precision of an estimate is governed by how many units you looked at, not by what fraction of the whole they were. A properly drawn sample of 1 000 gives the same margin of error in a country of 300 million as in a village of 3 000.
  • The correction factor √((N−n)/(N−1)) is essentially 1 whenever n is a small fraction of N: it is 0.995 at n/N = 1%, 0.975 at 5%, 0.949 at 10%. Below a 5% sampling fraction, ignoring it costs you under 3%.
  • At the other extreme it does exactly the right thing: at n = N it is zero. A census has no sampling error, which is correct and reassuring.
  • Everyone's intuition says otherwise — that a bigger population needs a bigger sample. It is worth sitting with the algebra until the intuition changes, because this single fact is what makes polling, auditing and quality control possible at all.

10 Proportions, the binomial, and the normal approximation

Half of all applied statistics is about a proportion — a click rate, a conversion, a defect rate, an accuracy. The good news is that a proportion is a mean in disguise, so everything above applies unchanged. The extra structure is that its exact sampling distribution is known, which lets you check the normal approximation instead of trusting it.

A proportion is a mean of zeros and ones

  • A useful consequence: rare events are relatively harder to estimate. At n = 1 000, the 95% margin is ±3.10 points at p = 0.5 but only ±0.62 points at p = 0.01 — yet as a fraction of the thing being measured, 0.62 points on a 1% rate is a ±62% relative error, against ±6% at p = 0.5.

The half is not a fudge factor. The exact distribution puts its mass on integers; the normal spreads it continuously; the bar for k occupies the interval from k − ½ to k + ½, so the matching boundary is k + ½. It is worth quantifying how much it buys, and how much the conditions actually promise:

The margin of error you have read a thousand times. Because p(1−p) ≤ ¼, a 95% margin for any proportion is at most 1.96 × 0.5/√n = 0.98/√n. At n = 1 067 that is exactly 3.0 points — which is why national polls are so often about a thousand people. It is a bound on the sampling gap alone, and in a modern survey it is very likely the smallest of the three gaps in play.

The number nobody prints

accuracy on a held-out set of n examples is a proportionSE(accuracy) = √(p(1 − p)/n) 95% margin = 1.96·SE at a true accuracy of 90% — the honest error bar on a leaderboard entryn = 100 → SE 3.00 pts → ±5.9 points n = 500 → SE 1.34 pts → ±2.6 points n = 1 000 → SE 0.95 pts → ±1.9 points n = 5 000 → SE 0.42 pts → ±0.8 points n = 10 000 → SE 0.30 pts → ±0.6 points

An accuracy of "90.4%" on a 500-example validation set is a claim with a 95% interval running from roughly 87.8% to 93.0%. Reporting it to one decimal place is a fiction with two significant figures too many.

Comparing two models: the arithmetic that ends most arguments

two models on SEPARATE test sets — the pessimistic, unpaired caseSE(Â − B̂) = √(p₁(1 − p₁)/n + p₂(1 − p₂)/n) ≈ √(2p(1 − p)/n) at p ≈ 0.90 and n = 500, a truly 1-point-better modelSE of the difference = 0.0190 the real gap 0.010 is only 0.53 SE wide P(the WORSE model scores higher) = Φ(−0.53) = 29.9% the n at which a 1-point gap is a 2-SE separationn = 4·(p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂))/δ² = 4(0.18)/0.0001 ≈ 7 200 examples
  • Nearly every 1-point difference reported on a small validation set is noise. Three times in ten, the worse model wins the bake-off outright. This is not a subtle statistical point; it is the reason model rankings fail to replicate.
  • Pairing is free and it is the biggest available win. Evaluate both models on the same examples and the shared difficulty cancels. Only the examples where the two models disagree carry information: with b cases where A is right and B wrong, and c the reverse, the difference in accuracy is (b − c)/n with standard error √(b + c − (b − c)²/n)/n. At n = 500 with a 3% disagreement rate, the SE of the difference falls from 0.0190 to 0.0077 — a factor of 2.5 — and the required n from 7 200 to about 1 200. Never compare models on different splits when you can compare them on the same one.
  • The winner's curse. Take the best of k models by validation score. Its score is the maximum of k noisy numbers, which is biased upward — for independent scores the expected maximum sits about 1.16 SE above the truth at k = 5, 1.54 at k = 10, 1.87 at k = 20 and 2.25 at k = 50, creeping up like √(2 ln k). Picking the best of 20 models whose scores carry SE 1.3 points inflates the winner's apparent score by about 2.4 points on average. This is regression to the mean wearing a lanyard, and it is why a final untouched test set exists.
  • Cross-validation folds are not independent — they share training data, so their scores are positively correlated. The familiar "SD across folds divided by √k" therefore understates the true standard error, sometimes badly, and there is no unbiased estimator of the variance of k-fold cross-validation from a single run. Treat the fold spread as a lower bound on the uncertainty; use repeated CV or a proper held-out set when the decision matters.
  • The test set is a sample too. Every reuse of it — every threshold tuned, every architecture chosen, every early stop — turns it a little more into training data, and its optimism compounds exactly as the multiplicity problem of part 9 predicts. See model evaluation, §leakage.
Two models, one validation set — how often does the worse one win? the sampling distribution of a leaderboard number
The habit to build. Never report a held-out score without its standard error, and never rank two models whose gap is under two standard errors of the difference. It costs one line of arithmetic and it removes most of the churn from model selection. The same logic, applied to users instead of examples, is online A/B testing; applied to the bias–variance split, it is why measured learning curves are noisy.

12 What this machinery buys: the next two parts, in advance

Everything established here exists to be spent. Two specific purchases follow immediately, and it is worth seeing them now — the derivations in parts 4 and 5 are two lines each once the sampling distribution is in hand.

Purchase one — the confidence interval (part 4, Estimation & Confidence Intervals)

start from the CLT statement, which you now haveP( −1.96 < (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) < 1.96 ) ≈ 0.95 rearrange the inequality to put μ in the middle — pure algebra, no new ideaP( x̄ − 1.96·σ/√n < μ < x̄ + 1.96·σ/√n ) ≈ 0.95 the general shape of every interval in the seriesestimate ± (critical value) × (standard error)

Three loose ends part 4 must tie, all of them visible from here. σ is unknown, so s goes in its place, and the extra noise in s means the critical value comes from a t distribution rather than a normal — a correction that matters below about n = 30 and is negligible above it. The 0.95 is approximate, and §07 tells you exactly how approximate. And the probability statement is about the interval, not about μ: 95% of the intervals this recipe produces contain the fixed μ, which is not the same as "there is a 95% chance μ is in this one".

Purchase two — the significance test (part 5, Hypothesis Testing)

assume a null value μ₀ and ask where your statistic sits in its sampling distributiontest statistic = (estimate − null value) / SE = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) the p-value is a tail area of the SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION — this page's objectp = P( a statistic at least this extreme | the null is true )

Which makes the duality unsurprising: the interval contains μ₀ exactly when the test at the matching level fails to reject it. They are the same computation, read in two directions.

And the rest of the series, in one line each

  • Part 6, Regression & Correlation — the fitted slope b is a statistic, so it has a sampling distribution, a standard error, and hence an interval and a test. The multivariate CLT is what makes the coefficients jointly normal.
  • Part 7, Resampling — when no formula for the standard error exists, the bootstrap builds the sampling distribution computationally: resample your data with replacement, recompute the statistic, and look at the spread.   • Part 8, Categorical Data & Chi-Square — sums of squared standardised counts. Each standardised count is approximately normal by §10; the sum of squares of normals is a chi-square, and the conditions on expected counts are the §10 conditions in disguise.
  • Part 9, ANOVA & Multiple Comparisons — the F ratio compares two variance estimates, each built from sums of squares of approximately normal quantities. The whole apparatus rests on §06 holding for the group means.

13 Practice

The checks to run, the traps to avoid, and a set of worked problems whose answers are all computable from what is above.

The checklist, before you trust any standard error

  1. Which distribution am I looking at? ①, ②, or ③. Say it out loud. Most disagreements about a chart end here.
  2. Is this SD or SE? They differ by √n. Check the axis label, then check the code that produced it.
  3. Are the observations independent? Name the unit that was randomised. Count those, not rows. If there are clusters, repeats, or a time index, compute the effective sample size.
  4. Does the population have a finite variance? Look at the largest few values. If the top 1% carries most of the total, the mean is a fragile statistic and §07's n is going to be large.
  5. What is the skewness, and does n ≥ 44γ₁² hold? One line of code, and it replaces the whole "n ≥ 30" conversation.
  6. Is the population discrete? If so, apply the continuity correction before comparing anything to a normal.
  7. Was the sample a probability sample of the right frame? If not, the standard error is a precise measurement of the wrong quantity.
  8. How far into the tail am I working? Beyond about three standard errors, treat normal-approximation probabilities as indicative only.
  9. Is n/N large enough for the finite-population correction to matter? Above 5%, yes.
  10. Have I reused this held-out set before? Every reuse inflates the optimism and shrinks the effective independence.

Worked problems

  1. Coin tosses. Toss a fair coin 100 times. How many tails, give or take how many?
    Answer. E[S] = np = 50; SE(S) = √(np(1−p)) = √25 = 5. So 50, give or take 5. By the empirical rule, about 95% of the time between 40 and 60. The exact binomial probability of landing in [40, 60] is 96.5%.
  2. Incomes. A population has μ = 67 000 and σ = 38 000. Sample 100 people. What is the chance the sample mean exceeds 70 800?
    Answer. SE = 38 000/√100 = 3 800, so 70 800 is exactly one SE above μ, and P = 1 − Φ(1) = 15.87%. Worth noticing: the population is heavily skewed, so this uses the CLT, and §07 warns that at n = 100 with a lognormal-like γ₁ ≈ 6 the CDF error could be around 3–4 points. The right answer is "about 16%, ±4".
  3. Rounding errors. A hundred amounts are each rounded to the nearest integer, so each error is Uniform(−0.5, 0.5). What is the chance the cumulative rounding error exceeds 10 in absolute value?
    Answer. μ = 0, σ² = 1/12, so SE(S₁₀₀) = √(100/12) = 2.887 and 10/2.887 = 3.46. P ≈ 2(1 − Φ(3.464)) = 0.00053. Chebyshev would have said only "at most 1/12 = 0.083" — the CLT is about 160 times sharper, and here it is trustworthy because the population is symmetric (§07).
  4. Sample size for a poll. You want a 95% margin of error of ±2 points on a proportion, with no pilot data.
    Answer. Worst case p = ½: n = (1.96/(2 × 0.02))² = 49² = 2 401. At ±3 points, 1 067; at ±1 point, 9 604.
  5. The claims trap. With the 99%/1% claims population of §07, someone reports a 95% interval for the mean claim from n = 30, as x̄ ± 1.96·s/√30. What actually happens?
    Answer. With probability 0.74 the sample contains no large claim at all, so x̄ = 100 and s = 0 — the interval is the single point 100, and it misses μ = 199 with certainty. Nominal coverage 95%, actual coverage near 0% in three quarters of all studies. The failure is not marginal; it is total.
  6. Correlated rows. You have 10 000 rows from 500 users, 20 per user, with an intra-user correlation of 0.4. What is the effective sample size?
    Answer. deff = 1 + (20 − 1)(0.4) = 8.6, so n_eff ≈ 10 000/8.6 ≈ 1 163. Your standard errors are too small by √8.6 = 2.9×, and every p-value is correspondingly wrong.
  7. A model comparison. Model A scores 91.2% and model B 90.4% on the same 800-example validation set; they disagree on 4% of examples. Is A better?
    Answer. Paired: δ = 0.008, SE = √((0.04 − 0.008²)/800) = 0.00705, so the gap is 1.13 SE. Not established. Unpaired it would be 0.55 SE — worse still. For a 2-SE separation you would need about 4(0.04 − 0.000064)/0.008² ≈ 2 500 paired examples.
  8. Reading the rate. A population has γ₁ = 3. How large must n be for a 95% z-interval to have roughly its stated coverage?
    Answer. For a 1% Kolmogorov error, n ≈ 44 × 9 = 396; for a rougher 5%, n ≈ 1.8 × 9 = 17. Somewhere between the two, depending on how much coverage error you will tolerate — which is a decision, not a fact, and should be stated as one.

Things to be able to derive from scratch

  • E[x̄] = μ — two lines, linearity of expectation.
  • Var(x̄) = σ²/n — variance of a sum plus the 1/n² from the division.
  • E[s²] = σ² — the identity ∑(xᵢ − x̄)² = ∑xᵢ² − n x̄², then take expectations.
  • Chebyshev from Markov, and the weak law from Chebyshev.
  • SE(p̂) = √(p(1−p)/n) — the 0/1 variance, then §03.
