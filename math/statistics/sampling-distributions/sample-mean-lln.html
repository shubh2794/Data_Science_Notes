The Sampling Distribution of the Mean

The sample mean as a random variable: unbiasedness proved without independence, the standard error derived from the variance of a sum, why the sample variance divides by n − 1, the exact shapes, and the law of large numbers proved through Markov and Chebyshev with Hoeffding's sharper bound.

03 The sampling distribution of the mean: centre, spread, shape

Three questions, three answers, and two of them are exact for every sample size and every population shape. Only the third — the shape — needs the central limit theorem, and that is why the CLT gets a section of its own while these get derived here in full.

Setup

the modelX₁, X₂, …, Xₙ independent, each with mean μ and variance σ², drawn from the same population the two statisticsthe sum Sₙ = X₁ + X₂ + ⋯ + Xₙ the average x̄ₙ = Sₙ / n = (1/n)·∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ

Centre — the mean is unbiased, and the proof needs no independence at all

expectation is linear — that is the entire argumentE[x̄ₙ] = E[(1/n)·∑Xᵢ] = (1/n)·∑E[Xᵢ] = (1/n)·(nμ) = μ so the bias is exactly zerobias(x̄ₙ) = E[x̄ₙ] − μ = 0 for every n, for every population with a finite mean and for the sum, by the same two linesE[Sₙ] = nμ
  • Notice what was not used: independence, identical distributions, normality, large n. Linearity of expectation holds regardless. Unbiasedness of the sample mean is about as robust as a result gets.
  • Notice what was used: that each E[Xᵢ] = μ, i.e. every unit in the population had the same chance-weighted expectation. That is precisely what a probability sample of the right frame buys you. If the frame is wrong, E[x̄ₙ] = μ_F ≠ μ, and no amount of n repairs it — the whole argument of part 2, §02.
  • "Unbiased" means the estimator is right on average over repeated samples. It says nothing about your one sample being close. That is the spread's job.

Spread — the standard error, derived from the variance of a sum

Variances do not add in general; they add when the terms are uncorrelated. Write it out once and the 1/√n law falls out.

the variance of a sum, in generalVar(∑Xᵢ) = ∑Var(Xᵢ) + 2·∑ᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) independent draws kill every covariance termCov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) = 0 for i ≠ j ⟹ Var(Sₙ) = n·σ² and dividing a random variable by n divides its variance by n²Var(x̄ₙ) = Var(Sₙ/n) = Var(Sₙ)/n² = nσ²/n² = σ²/n take the square root — the SQUARE ROOT LAWSE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n SE(Sₙ) = σ·√n
  • The average and the sum move in opposite directions. The average gets more precise like 1/√n; the sum's absolute wobble grows like √n. Both are the same fact, viewed through a division by n. §04 makes this the centrepiece, because it is the single most misread consequence of the law of large numbers.
  • Population size is absent. N appears nowhere. A sample of 1 000 is exactly as precise about a nation of 60 million as about a town of 60 thousand — provided both were sampled properly. This astonishes everyone the first time and is the most practically useful fact in the subject (§09).
  • What "standard error" means. It is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. Different name, same object, applied to a statistic rather than to data. Reserve "standard deviation" for the spread of ① or ②, and "standard error" for the spread of ③, and you will never write the wrong one on an axis again.
  • Independence is doing real work here. If the draws are positively correlated with common correlation ρ, the covariance terms come back:
equally correlated draws — the design-effect formula from part 2Var(x̄ₙ) = (σ²/n)·[1 + (n − 1)·ρ] deff = 1 + (n − 1)·ρ and the sting in the tailas n → ∞ with ρ > 0 fixed: Var(x̄ₙ) → σ²·ρ ≠ 0 correlated data do not average away — more of them buys nothing beyond a point

With ρ = 0.05 and n = 1 000, the variance is 1 + 999(0.05) = 50.95 times the independent value: your 1 000 correlated rows carry the information of about 20 independent ones. That is the design effect of part 2, §04, and it is the same arithmetic that ruins standard errors computed on clustered users, repeated measurements, and autocorrelated time series.

The sample variance is unbiased too — and this is where n − 1 comes from

start from the algebraic identity∑(Xᵢ − x̄)² = ∑Xᵢ² − n·x̄² take expectations, using E[Y²] = Var(Y) + (E[Y])² on each pieceE[∑Xᵢ²] = n·(σ² + μ²) E[n·x̄²] = n·(σ²/n + μ²) = σ² + nμ² subtractE[∑(Xᵢ − x̄)²] = nσ² + nμ² − σ² − nμ² = (n − 1)·σ² so dividing by n − 1, and ONLY by n − 1, is unbiasedE[s²] = E[ ∑(Xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) ] = σ²
  • The −1 is not a fudge and not a small-sample patch: it is the exact price of having estimated the centre from the same data. Dividing by n understates the variance by a factor (n−1)/n — a 20% error at n = 5. Part 1, §05 introduced s; this is the reason its denominator looks odd.
  • But s itself is biased low for σ. Unbiasedness does not survive a square root: since the square root is concave, E[s] = E[√(s²)] < √(E[s²]) = σ by Jensen's inequality. At n = 5 drawing from an exponential population, E[s²] = 1.00 but E[s] ≈ 0.867. Nobody corrects for this in practice; you should merely know it is there.
  • Which matters because s/√n — the estimated standard error — is what you actually report, and its own noise is exactly why part 4 will replace z with t.

Shape — and the two cases where you already know it exactly

  • Normal population ⟹ normal mean, exactly, for every n. A sum of independent normals is normal; scaling preserves normality. So x̄ₙ ~ Normal(μ, σ²/n) exactly, even at n = 2. No approximation, no CLT.
  • 0/1 population ⟹ the mean is a rescaled binomial, exactly. The count Sₙ is Binomial(n, p), so the proportion p̂ = Sₙ/n has an exactly known distribution for every n (§10). The normal enters only as an approximation to that.
  • Anything else — and this is the general case — the exact shape of x̄ₙ is some convolution nobody wants to compute. That is what the CLT is for (§06).
  • Two exact facts you can use to predict the shape before invoking any theorem, because they hold for every n:
skewness and excess kurtosis of the mean, exactlyskew(x̄ₙ) = γ₁ / √n exkurt(x̄ₙ) = γ₂ / n where these are the POPULATION shape numbersγ₁ = E[(X − μ)³]/σ³ γ₂ = E[(X − μ)⁴]/σ⁴ − 3

Checked by simulation on an exponential population (γ₁ = 2, γ₂ = 6), two million samples each: at n = 4 the measured skew of x̄ is 1.001 against a theory of 1.000, and the excess kurtosis 1.498 against 1.500; at n = 64, 0.2507 against 0.2500 and 0.0943 against 0.0938. The asymmetry of the population is inherited by the mean, divided by √n. That single formula is, in advance, the entire answer to "how large must n be?" — and §07 turns it into a number.

All of it at once, on a box you can enumerate by hand

Four tickets in a box: 1, 2, 3, 4. Draw n = 2 with replacement. There are 16 equally likely samples, so the sampling distribution can be written out in full — no theorem, no simulation, just counting.

the population — flat, symmetric, four valuesμ = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4)/4 = 2.5 σ² = [(−1.5)² + (−0.5)² + 0.5² + 1.5²]/4 = 5/4 = 1.25 σ = 1.1180 all 16 samples, tallied by their meanx̄ = 1.0 1.5 2.0 2.5 3.0 3.5 4.0 how many: 1 2 3 4 3 2 1 (16 in total) read the three answers straight off the tallyE[x̄] = 40/16 = 2.5 = μ ✓ unbiased Var(x̄) = 110/16 − 2.5² = 0.625 = σ²/n ✓ the square root law SE(x̄) = √0.625 = 0.7906 = σ/√2 ✓ and now draw the same n = 2 WITHOUT replacement — only 6 samplesx̄ = 1.5 2.0 2.5 2.5 3.0 3.5 E[x̄] = 15/6 = 2.5 ✓ still unbiased Var(x̄) = 40/6 − 2.5² = 0.41667 = (σ²/n)·(N − n)/(N − 1) = 0.625 × 2/3 ✓ the FPC
  • The population is flat. Its sampling distribution at n = 2 is already triangular — the march toward a bell has begun at the second observation. That is the central limit theorem visible in a hand tally.
  • Both variance formulas — the plain one and the finite-population one — are confirmed to the last digit by enumeration. Nothing here is asymptotic; §03's results are exact statements about finite boxes.
  • Sampling without replacement gives a variance of 0.4167 rather than 0.625: a third less, because half the population is being examined. That is what the correction factor is doing.
Summary card. E[x̄ₙ] = μ exactly, always. SE(x̄ₙ) = σ/√n exactly, whenever the draws are independent. skew(x̄ₙ) = γ₁/√n exactly. Only the shape needs a limit theorem, and even that comes with a rate.

04 The law of large numbers — and the way everybody misreads it

The law of large numbers says the sample mean settles down on the population mean. It is proved in four lines, it comes in two strengths, and it is misread so consistently that the misreading has its own name. What it does not say is at least as important as what it does.

The tool: Markov, then Chebyshev

Markov's inequality — for a non-negative Y with a finite meanP(Y ≥ t) ≤ E[Y]/t for every t > 0 why: split the integral at t and throw away the left pieceE[Y] = ∫₀^∞ y·f(y) dy ≥ ∫ₜ^∞ y·f(y) dy ≥ t·∫ₜ^∞ f(y) dy = t·P(Y ≥ t) Chebyshev's inequality — apply Markov to Y = (X − μ)²P(|X − μ| ≥ t) = P((X − μ)² ≥ t²) ≤ E[(X − μ)²]/t² = σ²/t² in standardised form, with k standard deviationsP(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² k = 2 → ≤ 25%, k = 3 → ≤ 11.1%, k = 4 → ≤ 6.25%

Chebyshev is distribution-free and therefore very loose — for an exponential the true P(|X − μ| ≥ 2σ) is 5.0%, not 25%. Its virtue is that it needs nothing but a finite variance, which is exactly what the next proof requires.

The weak law, proved

apply Chebyshev to x̄ₙ, whose variance you derived in §03P(|x̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(x̄ₙ)/ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0 as n → ∞, for every fixed ε > 0 that is convergence IN PROBABILITY, writtenx̄ₙ →ᵖ μ
  • Read the quantifiers. Fix a tolerance ε — say 0.01 — first. Then the probability that x̄ₙ misses μ by more than that tolerance goes to zero. You do not get to shrink ε as n grows; the statement is one ε at a time.
  • The proof assumed a finite variance for convenience. The weak law actually holds under the weaker condition that the mean exists at all.
  • It also gives a sample size, not just a limit. For a proportion, σ² = p(1−p) ≤ ¼, so P(|p̂ − p| > 0.1) ≤ 1/(4n(0.1)²) = 25/n. To make that at most 10% you need n ≥ 250. Compare: the sharper Hoeffding bound below asks for 150, and the CLT — which needs an approximation, not a bound — asks for 68.

  • Almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability, never the reverse. The standard counterexample: let U be uniform on (0,1) and Xₙ = √n when U < 1/n, else 0. Then P(|Xₙ| > ε) = 1/n → 0, so Xₙ →ᵖ 0 — yet E[Xₙ²] = 1 for every n, so it does not converge in mean square at all.
  • A warning worth internalising: Xₙ →ᵖ b does not imply E[Xₙ] → b. Let P(Xₙ = n²) = 1/n and P(Xₙ = 0) = 1 − 1/n. Then Xₙ →ᵖ 0 while E[Xₙ] = n → ∞. Convergence of a distribution and convergence of its moments are different things — a fact that comes back to bite in §08.

A sharper bound when the data are bounded: Hoeffding

for X₁…Xₙ independent with values in [0, 1] and mean pP(|x̄ₙ − p| > ε) ≤ 2·exp(−2nε²) inverted, it hands you a distribution-free intervalεₙ = √( ln(2/α) / (2n) ) ⟹ P( |x̄ₙ − p| ≤ εₙ ) ≥ 1 − α

Exponential in n, not merely polynomial — a genuine improvement. But not always: at n = 100, ε = 0.1, p = ½, Chebyshev gives 0.250, Hoeffding gives 0.271 (worse!), and the exact binomial answer is 0.0352.

Hoeffding overtakes Chebyshev once nε² is comfortably large, and both remain enormously conservative — which is the price of a guarantee that holds for every distribution rather than an approximation that holds for most. Bounds like this are the backbone of generalisation guarantees in learning theory; the CLT is what applied statistics actually uses.

The misreading: absolute error grows even as relative error shrinks

"The law of averages" is usually invoked to mean that a run of bad luck will be compensated by a run of good luck. It will not be. Nothing pulls the sum back. The proportion converges only because the denominator outruns the wandering numerator.

for a fair coin, σ = ½ — the two quantities move in opposite directionsSD(Sₙ − n/2) = σ√n = 0.5·√n GROWS SD(x̄ₙ − ½) = σ/√n = 0.5/√n SHRINKS typical absolute gaps (exact binomial, and the √(2/π) normal approximation)n = 100 E|Sₙ − n/2| = 3.98 heads as a proportion 0.0399 n = 10 000 E|Sₙ − n/2| = 39.89 heads as a proportion 0.00399 n = 1 000 000 E|Sₙ − n/2| ≈ 398.94 heads as a proportion 0.000399
  • Between 100 and a million tosses the typical surplus of heads over tails grows a hundredfold, from about 4 to about 399, while the typical error in the rate falls a hundredfold. Both statements are true at once and they are the same statement.
  • So the gambler's fallacy is not a misunderstanding of probability; it is a confusion of Sₙ with x̄ₙ. After ten heads you are ten heads "up", and you will stay about ten heads up forever — it just stops mattering, proportionally.
  • Two more things the law of large numbers does not say. It does not apply to sums — their spread diverges. It does not apply when the mean does not exist — §08 shows a population where the running average never settles at all. And it says nothing about how fast anything happens: that is §06 and §07.
  • One thing it does say, that is easy to miss: applied to indicator variables, it says the empirical proportion of any event converges to its probability, so the whole empirical distribution converges to the population's. That is why a histogram of a large sample looks like the population's histogram, and it is the formal content of the plug-in principle from part 1.
