09 The standard error and the price of precision

One formula, three practical consequences, and a correction for when the population is small enough to run out of. This is the section you will actually use when someone asks "how much data do we need?"

The formulas, collected

the mean — known and estimatedSE(x̄) = σ/√n estimated: ŜE(x̄) = s/√n the sum, the proportion, and the difference of two independent meansSE(Sₙ) = σ√n SE(p̂) = √(p(1 − p)/n) est. √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) SE(x̄₁ − x̄₂) = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂) sampling WITHOUT replacement from a finite population of NSE(x̄) = (σ/√n)·√((N − n)/(N − 1)) the finite-population correction where the correction comes from: SRSWOR makes the draws slightly NEGATIVELY dependentCov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) = −σ²/(N − 1) ⟹ Var(x̄) = (σ²/n)·[1 − (n−1)/(N−1)] = (σ²/n)·(N−n)/(N−1)

Verified by direct simulation on a population of N = 500 with σ = 9.37: at n = 250 the measured SD of the sample mean over 200 000 draws is 0.4195, against 0.4194 from the corrected formula and 0.5926 from the uncorrected one. Ignore the correction when you are sampling half the population and your error bars are 41% too wide.

Consequence 1 — the population's size is irrelevant

  • N does not appear in σ/√n. The precision of an estimate is governed by how many units you looked at, not by what fraction of the whole they were. A properly drawn sample of 1 000 gives the same margin of error in a country of 300 million as in a village of 3 000.
  • The correction factor √((N−n)/(N−1)) is essentially 1 whenever n is a small fraction of N: it is 0.995 at n/N = 1%, 0.975 at 5%, 0.949 at 10%. Below a 5% sampling fraction, ignoring it costs you under 3%.
  • At the other extreme it does exactly the right thing: at n = N it is zero. A census has no sampling error, which is correct and reassuring.
  • Everyone's intuition says otherwise — that a bigger population needs a bigger sample. It is worth sitting with the algebra until the intuition changes, because this single fact is what makes polling, auditing and quality control possible at all.

Consequence 2 — precision is bought at a quadratic price

the square root law, read as an exchange rateto divide the margin of error by 2 → multiply n by 4 to divide it by 3 → multiply n by 9 to divide it by 10 → multiply n by 100 shaving just 10% off the current marginn → n/0.81 = 1.2346·n an extra 23.5% of the data, for a 10% gain
  • The marginal return on data collection falls without limit. The first hundred observations are transformative; the hundred-thousandth-and-first is not. This alone explains why sampling beats a census on cost, and why the sensible response to "our estimate is too noisy" is often "the design is wrong", not "collect more".
  • It also sets the ceiling on data collection as a strategy. If the bias is b, then by n* = (σ/b)² the chance error has already fallen below the bias and further collection is buying resolution on the wrong number (part 2, §06).

Consequence 3 — you can plan the sample size in advance

solve the margin-of-error equation for nm = z*·σ/√n ⟹ n = (z*·σ/m)² for a proportion, using the worst case p = ½ so you need no pilotn = (z*/(2m))² 95%: n = (0.98/m)² the numbers behind every "±3 points" you have ever readm = 3.0% → n ≈ 1 067 m = 2.0% → n = 2 401 m = 1.0% → n = 9 604

Two honest caveats. You need σ to plan for a mean, and you do not have it — so use a pilot, a previous study, or the crude range/4 rule. And every number above bounds the sampling gap only: it is silent about coverage and non-response, which in real surveys are usually larger (part 2, §02).

The 1/√n law, priced a straight line of slope −1/2 on log–log
The unit of measurement. From here on, every quantity in this series is measured in standard errors. A confidence interval is "estimate ± a few SE". A test statistic is "distance, in SE". An F ratio is one variance divided by another. If you can compute a standard error for a statistic, you can do inference on it; if you cannot, you cannot — which is precisely the gap the bootstrap of part 7 exists to fill.

10 Proportions, the binomial, and the normal approximation

Half of all applied statistics is about a proportion — a click rate, a conversion, a defect rate, an accuracy. The good news is that a proportion is a mean in disguise, so everything above applies unchanged. The extra structure is that its exact sampling distribution is known, which lets you check the normal approximation instead of trusting it.

A proportion is a mean of zeros and ones

label each unit 1 if it has the property, 0 if notp̂ = (number of 1s)/n = x̄ of the 0/1 variable and the count is Sₙ = n·p̂ the population's mean and SD, for a 0/1 variableμ = 0·(1 − p) + 1·p = p σ² = (0 − p)²(1 − p) + (1 − p)²·p = p(1 − p) σ = √(p(1 − p)) so §03 hands you everything at onceE[p̂] = p SE(p̂) = √(p(1 − p)/n) E[Sₙ] = np SE(Sₙ) = √(np(1 − p))
  • σ is determined by p. Unlike a general mean, a proportion's spread is not a free parameter — which is why you can plan a sample size without a pilot study. It is largest at p = ½ (σ = 0.5) and vanishes at 0 and 1.
  • A useful consequence: rare events are relatively harder to estimate. At n = 1 000, the 95% margin is ±3.10 points at p = 0.5 but only ±0.62 points at p = 0.01 — yet as a fraction of the thing being measured, 0.62 points on a 1% rate is a ±62% relative error, against ±6% at p = 0.5.

The exact distribution: binomial

the binomial setting — four conditions, all of which must hold1. a FIXED number n of trials 2. each trial has two outcomes 3. the same success probability p every time 4. the trials are INDEPENDENT the probability of exactly k successesP(X = k) = C(n, k)·pᵏ·(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ C(n, k) = n! / (k!·(n − k)!) mean and variance, which agree with the 0/1 mean aboveE[X] = np Var(X) = np(1 − p)
  • A simple random sample without replacement is not the binomial setting — p changes as units are removed. The exact answer there is the hypergeometric distribution. The binomial is an excellent approximation whenever n is a small fraction of N, by the same argument as the finite-population correction: the two variances differ by exactly the factor (N−n)/(N−1).
  • Condition 3 is the one that quietly fails in practice: a click rate that drifts across the day, a defect rate that depends on the shift, a conversion rate that differs by traffic source. Mixing heterogeneous p's gives a distribution that is less variable than the binomial if the trials are independent, and much more variable if they are clustered — which is the overdispersion every practitioner eventually meets.
what a simple random sample of n from N units, K of which are "successes", really givesP(X = k) = C(K, k)·C(N − K, n − k) / C(N, n) the HYPERGEOMETRIC distribution its mean is the same as the binomial's; its variance carries the FPCE[X] = np Var(X) = np(1 − p)·(N − n)/(N − 1), with p = K/N a concrete check: N = 1 000, K = 300, n = 100binomial variance = 100(0.3)(0.7) = 21.000 hypergeometric = 21.000 × 900/999 = 18.919 the factor is 0.901

So the binomial overstates the variance by exactly the factor you already know. At a 10% sampling fraction that is a 5% error in the standard error — usually ignorable, never mysterious.

The normal approximation, and the half you must not forget

standardise the count and use the CLTZ = (X − np)/√(np(1 − p)) ⇝ Normal(0, 1) the CONTINUITY CORRECTION — a staircase approximated by a smooth curveP(X ≤ k) ≈ Φ((k + ½ − np)/√(np(1 − p))) P(X ≥ k) ≈ 1 − Φ((k − ½ − np)/√(np(1 − p))) the usual conditions for using it at allnp ≥ 10 and n(1 − p) ≥ 10 (some texts say 5; 10 is safer)

The half is not a fudge factor. The exact distribution puts its mass on integers; the normal spreads it continuously; the bar for k occupies the interval from k − ½ to k + ½, so the matching boundary is k + ½. It is worth quantifying how much it buys, and how much the conditions actually promise:

  • The correction is worth roughly a factor of five to fifty. Skipping it leaves an 8% error in the CDF no matter how large n is, because the error it removes is the lattice step, which shrinks only like 1/√n on its own.
  • "np ≥ 10" buys about 2%, not 0.1%. Read the rows with np = 10: at p = ½ the corrected error is 0.14%, but at p = 0.01 it is 2.04%. The condition is really a proxy for the skewness rule of §07 — since γ₁ = (1 − 2p)/√(p(1−p)), the requirement n ≳ 44γ₁² becomes roughly np ≳ 44 for small p. If you want 1% rather than 2%, that is the number to hit.
  • For the small-p, large-n regime the right approximation is usually the Poisson with mean np, not the normal — but that belongs on a Probability page.
Binomial → normal, with the conditions lighting up bars are exact; the curve is the approximation
The margin of error you have read a thousand times. Because p(1−p) ≤ ¼, a 95% margin for any proportion is at most 1.96 × 0.5/√n = 0.98/√n. At n = 1 067 that is exactly 3.0 points — which is why national polls are so often about a thousand people. It is a bound on the sampling gap alone, and in a modern survey it is very likely the smallest of the three gaps in play.
