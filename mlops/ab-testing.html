A/B Testing

The only routine way to know whether a change caused an improvement: randomly split traffic into control and treatment, measure a pre-declared metric, and use statistics to separate a real effect from noise. Everything hard about it lives in the design — the unit of randomization, the metric's variance, and the discipline not to peek.

01 Framing the experiment

An A/B test is a randomized controlled experiment. Its power comes from one thing: randomization breaks the link between the treatment and everything else, so a difference in outcomes has only one plausible explanation.

  • Think in potential outcomes: each user has an outcome Y(1) if treated and Y(0) if not. Only one is ever observed, so the individual effect is unknowable — but the average is estimable, because random assignment makes the two groups exchangeable in expectation.
  • State the hypothesis before you look: H₀ : Δ = 0 against H₁ : Δ ≠ 0, with the primary metric, the direction you care about, the analysis unit, and the stopping rule all written down first.
  • Offline metrics answer "is the model better on held-out data?"; the online test answers "does the product improve when real users meet it?" They disagree often enough that shipping on offline numbers alone is a known failure mode.

02 Randomization unit & interference

Choosing what gets randomized is the single most consequential design decision, and the one most often made by accident.

Picking the unit

  • User (a stable id or cookie) is the default: a person sees one consistent experience, so the test can measure retention, repeat visits, and anything that accumulates over days.
  • Session or request gives many more units and tighter intervals, but only for changes a user cannot notice across visits — a latency or infrastructure change, never a visible UI change.
  • Cluster (city, market, account) is forced on you when units interact; the effective sample size becomes the number of clusters, not people.

SUTVA and interference

  • The standard analysis assumes SUTVA: one unit's assignment does not affect another's outcome. Real systems break this constantly.
  • Marketplaces: treatment buyers consume the same limited supply as control, so a "lift" is partly stolen demand and vanishes at full rollout. Social graphs: treated users change what their control friends see. Shared state: one cache, one budget, one model serving both arms.
  • Mitigations: cluster/market randomization, switchback designs that flip the whole system on a schedule, and budget-split designs.

Trusting the splitter

  • Run A/A tests: identical arms should return significant results at exactly the nominal rate. If they do not, the pipeline — not the feature — is broken.
  • Check sample ratio mismatch: if a 50/50 split delivers 50.4/49.6 on millions of users, the readout must be discarded, not explained away.
Takeaway: the randomization unit sets both the ceiling on sensitivity and the set of assumptions you are quietly making. Write it down; validate it with an A/A test and an SRM check on every readout.

03 Metrics, variance & CUPED

Sensitivity is governed by σ²/n. You can buy n with time and traffic; reducing σ² is free and usually far more effective.

A metric hierarchy

  • One primary metric — the overall evaluation criterion the decision hangs on; short-term and measurable, but predictive of long-term value.
  • Guardrails that must not regress: latency, crash rate, revenue, unsubscribes, fairness slices. These are tested for non-inferiority, not for lift.
  • Diagnostics explain why the primary moved. Promoting a diagnostic to a decision metric after the fact is how teams ship noise.

Variance is the budget

  • Bounded metrics (binary conversion) have variance p(1−p) ≤ 0.25 and behave well. Revenue per user is heavy-tailed: a few whales dominate the difference, so the estimate is noisy at realistic n. Remedies, declared in advance: cap or winsorize the top percentile, or use a bounded proxy.

Ratio metrics and the delta method

Click-through rate is clicks over impressions, but the randomization unit is the user. Numerator and denominator are then both random and correlated, so the textbook binomial standard error is wrong — usually far too small, which manufactures significance. The delta method fixes it with a first-order Taylor expansion of the ratio around the two means. Compute per-user totals and apply the formula; a user-level bootstrap gives the same answer more slowly.

delta method for R = X̄ / ȲVar(R) ≈ (1/Ȳ²)·[ Var(X̄) − 2R·Cov(X̄, Ȳ) + R²·Var(Ȳ) ]

CUPED — variance reduction with pre-experiment data

How a user behaved before the experiment is a covariate X predicting their in-experiment outcome Y, and can be subtracted off. The adjusted metric keeps the same expectation but scales variance by 1 − ρ²: a correlation of 0.7 removes about half the variance — equivalent to doubling traffic, for free.

04 Signal vs noise

B looks better — but is it real? Confidence intervals shrink as 1/√n, so a true 2-point lift on a 10% baseline only becomes visible once each arm holds thousands of users. Drag the sample size.

Conversion rate: A vs B with 95% confidence intervals True rates 10% vs 12%; significance emerges only with enough samples
  • The plot holds the observed rates fixed and only shrinks the intervals — the honest picture of what n buys: the estimate does not get better, it gets less uncertain.
  • The "do the intervals overlap?" eyeball test is conservative — non-overlapping intervals imply significance, but slightly overlapping intervals can still be significant. The correct check is a single interval on the difference, which is what the z-statistic in the readout computes.
  • The two errors are not symmetric: a false positive ships a change that quietly costs money forever; a false negative merely delays a good idea that can be retested.
Takeaway: always analyse the interval on the difference, not two separate intervals — and report the effect size, not just the verdict.

05 Power & sample size

Decide before launch how long the test must run. A test that cannot detect the effect you care about is not a test — it is a delay with a p-value attached.

The sample-size formula

Fix α (false-positive rate, usually 0.05 two-sided), the power 1 − β (usually 0.80), and the minimum detectable effect Δ — the smallest lift that would change your decision. The per-arm sample size for comparing two means is then:

per-arm sample size · two-sided testn ≈ 2σ²·(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² / Δ² z₀.₉₇₅ = 1.96, z₀.₈₀ = 0.84, (1.96 + 0.84)² ≈ 7.85 rearranged: what you can detect with the traffic you haveMDE ≈ (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)·√(2σ²/n)

A worked example

  • Baseline p = 5%, so σ² = p(1−p) = 0.0475; you want a 5% relative lift, i.e. Δ = 0.0025 absolute.
  • n ≈ 2 × 0.0475 × 7.85 / 0.0025² ≈ 119,000 users per arm — about 240,000 in total.
  • At 30,000 eligible users a day that is about eight days — round up to whole weeks, since weekday and weekend users are different populations.
  • The term is quadratic: halving the effect you want to detect quadruples the traffic. That is why 0.5% lifts are so expensive and why variance reduction pays.

p-values versus confidence intervals

A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null were true — not the probability the null is true, and not an effect size; a very large test returns p < 0.001 for lifts too small to care about. A confidence interval carries strictly more: effect, uncertainty, and significance in one object. Report it on the relative lift and compare against your practical threshold, not just zero.

Takeaway: pre-compute n from a business-meaningful MDE, run for whole weeks, and report a confidence interval. "Statistically significant" and "worth shipping" are different questions.

06 Peeking & multiple comparisons

The fixed-horizon p-value is only valid if you look once, at the pre-declared end. Every extra look is an extra chance to cross the line by luck.

Why peeking inflates false positives

Under the null the test statistic wanders like a random walk. A fixed-horizon test asks whether it sits outside ±1.96 at one specific moment; continuous monitoring asks whether it ever strays outside — and a random walk eventually strays anywhere. Stopping the first time you see p < 0.05 pushes the real false-positive rate from 5% toward 20% and beyond.

The cost of peeking: false-positive rate vs number of interim looks Nominal α = 0.05; naive stopping vs an always-valid sequential test

Sequential methods that are actually valid

  • Group sequential designs pre-schedule a few interim analyses and spend α across them, with a boundary strict early and relaxed at the end — early stopping for large effects, error rate preserved.
  • Always-valid inference (confidence sequences, mixture SPRT) gives an interval correct at every moment, so a dashboard can be watched continuously. The price is a wider interval — roughly a 20–50% longer run for the same power.
  • Either way the rule must be fixed in advance; switching to a sequential method after seeing the data restores the original problem.

Multiple comparisons

  • Testing m independent metrics at α = 0.05 gives a family-wise error rate 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ — with 20 metrics a false winner is more likely than not. Bonferroni (α/m) controls that rate but is brutally conservative; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate and is the sensible default for a long dashboard.
  • The cheapest correction is structural: one pre-registered primary at full α, guardrails as non-inferiority checks, everything else labelled exploratory.
Takeaway: either look once at a pre-computed sample size, or use a method designed for repeated looks. There is no third option that keeps the error rate you think you have.

07 Bandits, interleaving & other designs

The fixed 50/50 split is one point in a design space. Other designs trade unbiased estimation for speed, sensitivity, or lower cost of being wrong.

Multi-armed bandits

  • A bandit reallocates traffic toward whichever arm looks best (Thompson sampling, UCB), minimising regret — the cumulative cost of showing losing variants. Ideal for many short-lived, low-stakes variants chosen per request.
  • The catch: adaptive allocation biases the naive per-arm estimate, so effect sizes need adaptively-valid inference. Bandits optimise; A/B tests measure. Pick the one you actually need.

Interleaving for rankers

  • Blend two rankings into one result list per query and credit each click to the ranker that contributed the item. Every user is their own control, which removes almost all between-user variance.
  • Sensitivity is typically one to two orders of magnitude higher than a user-split test — ranking changes resolve in hours, not weeks.
  • Limits: it measures only relative ordering preference on a click signal, never revenue or retention, so it is a fast filter before a conventional A/B confirmation.

Other designs worth knowing

  • Switchback: flip the whole system between variants on a time schedule — the standard answer for marketplaces where interference invalidates user splits. Long-term holdback: keep a slice of users on the old experience for months to catch cumulative effects.
  • Ramped rollout: 1% → 5% → 20% → 50%, watching guardrails at each step. That is risk management rather than statistical power, and it pairs with production monitoring.
Takeaway: match the design to the question — bandits to reduce regret, interleaving to iterate fast on rankers, switchbacks when units interfere, fixed splits when you need a defensible number.

08 Pitfalls

Most bad experiment results are not statistical accidents — they are design or plumbing bugs that produce a confident, wrong number.

Effects that change over time

  • Novelty: existing users click the new thing because it is new, so the lift decays. Primacy: the mirror image — users trained on the old layout are slower at first, so the effect improves as they adapt.
  • Diagnose by plotting the effect by day and by user tenure. If the curve is still moving at the end, you measured a transient, not a steady state; new-user segments and long-term holdbacks are the cross-checks.

Segments and selection

  • Simpson's paradox: the effect can be positive in every segment yet negative overall when arm composition differs — usually a broken split or a ramp changed mid-flight.
  • Triggering: analysing only users who hit the changed code path sharpens the estimate, but the trigger must fire identically in both arms — filtering on post-treatment behaviour destroys randomization. Report intention-to-treat alongside it.

09 Checklist

A short pre-flight list that catches most of the damage before it reaches a decision meeting.

  • Before launch — write down the hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, randomization unit, MDE, sample size, run length, and interference risk.
  • At launch — verify the split with an SRM check, confirm both arms log identically, ramp gradually while watching guardrails.
  • During — do not stop early unless a pre-declared sequential design says so, or a guardrail breaches (a safety decision, not a statistical one).
  • At analysis — interval on the difference, delta-method errors for ratio metrics, CUPED where a covariate exists, FDR correction across the secondary dashboard.
  • At the decision — compare the interval to practical significance, check the effect by day and by segment, and record the result whether it shipped or not. The archive of flat and negative tests calibrates the next roadmap.
  • After launch — hand off to monitoring and drift detection, and keep offline evaluation paired with online results in LLMOps, alongside automated judges for generative systems.
Takeaway: a trustworthy experiment platform is a discipline, not a dashboard — pre-registration, an A/A-validated splitter, honest stopping rules, and a written record of everything you shipped and everything you did not.
