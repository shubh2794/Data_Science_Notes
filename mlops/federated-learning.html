Federated Learning

Train one shared model across many phones or organizations without moving their data anywhere. Each participant trains locally and returns only a model update; the server averages those updates. The idea is simple — the hard parts are non-identically-distributed data, a network that is orders of magnitude slower than a datacentre fabric, and the fact that "we only sent gradients" is not by itself a privacy guarantee.

01 Why keep data put

Federated learning moves the computation to the data instead of the data to a server. That inversion is driven by law, by physics, and by liability.

  • Regulation and consent: health records, financial transactions, and messages often cannot legally cross an organizational or national boundary, so a central training corpus is simply not constructible.
  • Volume and locality: the raw signal on a billion phones — every keystroke, every photo — dwarfs anything worth uploading. The useful summary (a model update) is far smaller than the data that produced it.
  • Liability: a central store of sensitive data is a permanent breach target. Data you never collected cannot leak.
  • The objective is unchanged from ordinary training — minimise average loss over the union of everyone's data — but it must be optimised while each shard Dk stays put and only w travels.
the federated objectiveminw F(w) = Σk=1..K (nk / n) · Fk(w) Fk(w) = (1/nk) · Σi ∈ Dk ℓ(w; xi, yi) n = Σk nk
Takeaway: federation does not change what you are optimising — only the constraint that no example may leave its owner. Every difficulty below follows from that one constraint.

02 Cross-device vs cross-silo

Two regimes wear the same name and share almost no engineering. Naming which one you are in resolves most design arguments immediately.

Cross-device

Millions to hundreds of millions of phones or browsers, each holding a tiny, personal, and wildly non-representative slice of data. Clients are anonymous, unreliable, and available only when idle, charging, and on unmetered network — so a given device may participate once and never again. You sample a cohort of a few hundred to a few thousand per round, expect a chunk of them to drop out mid-round, and never address a specific client by name. The model must be small enough to train inside a phone's memory and battery budget.

Cross-silo

Two to a hundred institutions — hospitals, banks, factories — each with a substantial labelled dataset and real compute. Participants are identified, contractually bound, and reliably online, so every round can include everyone and stateful algorithms are practical. Here the blocker is legal rather than physical, and the interesting questions shift toward incentives, auditability, and who is allowed to see the final weights.

Takeaway: cross-device is a systems problem dominated by unreliability and bandwidth; cross-silo is a governance problem dominated by trust. Do not import solutions across the boundary without checking which assumptions came along.

03 The FedAvg round

Server broadcasts the model, a sampled cohort trains locally for several epochs, the server takes a weighted average of what comes back. Repeat for hundreds of rounds.

Federated averaging Local training on each client → aggregate → improved global model
one fedavg round t1. server samples a cohort St of m clients and broadcasts wt 2. each client k runs E local epochs: wk ← wk − η · ∇Fk(wk; batch) 3. client returns the delta: Δk = wk − wt 4. server aggregates: wt+1 = wt + Σk ∈ St (nk / Σ nj) · Δk
  • With E = 1 local step and full participation, FedAvg reduces to large-batch synchronous SGD. Everything interesting comes from taking E > 1 — many local steps per communication — which is what makes the method affordable and also what makes it drift.
  • Averaging deltas rather than raw weights matters: deltas are small, compress well, and are the natural object for clipping and noising when differential privacy is added later.
  • Weighting by nk targets the population average. Uniform weighting targets the average client instead — a different objective, and often the fairer one when client sizes vary by orders of magnitude.

04 Communication vs computation

In the datacentre, compute is the scarce resource. In federation the network is, by a wide margin — so the currency of the whole field is rounds, not FLOPs.

A round costs a full model download and a full update upload for every selected client, and consumer uplinks are asymmetric and slow. More local epochs E means fewer rounds for the same progress — until local models drift so far apart that averaging them destroys the gains. The trade-off has an interior optimum, and it moves left (toward smaller E) as data becomes more non-IID.

total bytes over a training runbytes ≈ R · m · |w| · b · 2 (R rounds × m clients per round × params × bytes/param × down+up) example: R = 800, m = 500, |w| = 5 × 10⁶, b = 4 → ≈ 16 TB aggregate
Local epochs: communication saved vs accuracy lost More local work → fewer rounds, but client drift caps the final model
  • Compression attacks the same budget from the other side: quantize updates to 8 or fewer bits, send only the top-k largest coordinates with error feedback, or project the update through a shared random sketch. Order-of-magnitude uplink savings are routine.
  • Model size is the other lever — a smaller architecture, or training only an adapter or the final layers while a frozen backbone stays on device, cuts every round proportionally.
  • Compression interacts badly with privacy: aggressive sparsification changes the sensitivity of the update and can weaken or invalidate the noise calibration used for differential privacy, so the two must be designed together.
Takeaway: optimise rounds-to-target-accuracy, not epochs or wall-clock FLOPs. Local epochs, compression, and model size are three ways of spending the same communication budget.

05 Non-IID data & client drift

Textbook SGD assumes shuffled, identically distributed batches. Federation guarantees the opposite, and that single violation causes most of the field's open problems.

How the data differs

  • Label skew: one user photographs only their own dog; a rural clinic sees a different disease mix than an urban one. Feature skew: accents, handwriting, camera sensors, instrument calibration. Quantity skew: client dataset sizes span several orders of magnitude. Temporal skew: the clients available at 3 a.m. are not a random sample of users.

Client drift, and the fixes

With many local steps, each client converges toward its own optimum rather than the global one. Averaging those diverged points lands somewhere that is optimal for nobody, so the global model oscillates and plateaus below what centralized training would reach. FedProx adds a proximal term that penalises straying from the broadcast weights, trading some local progress for stability. SCAFFOLD instead estimates the difference between each client's gradient direction and the global one, and subtracts that control variate during local steps — it corrects drift more precisely but needs per-client state, which fits cross-silo far better than stateless cross-device. Server-side momentum and adaptive server optimisers (treating the averaged delta as a pseudo-gradient) are cheap, popular complements.

Systems heterogeneity

  • Stragglers: a synchronous round waits for the slowest device, so servers over-sample the cohort and simply discard late arrivals — which silently biases training toward fast, new, well-connected devices.
  • Partial participation adds variance to every aggregate; a larger cohort reduces it roughly as 1/√m at proportional bandwidth cost.
  • Batch normalization is a classic landmine: its running statistics are client-specific and averaging them across skewed clients is meaningless. Group or layer normalization is the standard substitute.
Takeaway: measure skew before blaming the algorithm. If a centralized run on pooled data and a federated run diverge, the gap is almost always non-IID drift, participation bias, or normalization — in that order.

06 Privacy isn't automatic

"The raw data never left the device" is a statement about data movement, not about privacy. A model update is a function of the training examples, and functions leak.

What updates leak

  • Gradient inversion: from a single client's update on a small batch, an adversary can often reconstruct recognisable inputs — images, and sometimes verbatim text — by optimising a synthetic batch until its gradient matches.
  • Membership inference: even without reconstruction, an update can reveal whether a particular record was in a client's dataset, which is itself the sensitive fact in a medical setting.
  • Large local batches and many local steps blur the signal, but blurring is a hope, not a bound.

Secure aggregation

A cryptographic protocol lets the server learn only the sum of the cohort's updates and nothing about any individual contribution. Clients pair up and exchange masks that cancel exactly when summed, with secret-sharing so the sum still recovers correctly if some clients drop out mid-round. The server sees a single aggregate, so gradient inversion has nothing individual to invert. It costs extra communication rounds and does not help if the aggregate itself is revealing — a cohort of one, or a rare feature present on a single device.

Differential privacy on the updates

For a formal guarantee, bound each client's influence and add calibrated noise. Clip every update to a fixed L2 norm S, sum, then add Gaussian noise scaled to S before dividing. The result satisfies user-level differential privacy: the presence or absence of any single participant barely changes the distribution of outputs. Privacy loss accumulates across rounds and is tracked with a composition accountant; more rounds means more noise for the same ε, which collides directly with the communication trade-off above. See differential privacy for the accounting machinery.

Takeaway: the three layers compose — federation limits collection, secure aggregation hides the individual, differential privacy bounds what the output can reveal. Only the third is a guarantee.

07 Threat model

Federation hands write access to your training loop to thousands of machines you do not control. That is a fundamentally different security posture from a datacentre job.

Attacks from clients

  • Data poisoning: a malicious participant mislabels its own examples to degrade the model. Its influence is bounded by its aggregation weight, so this needs many colluding clients to matter.
  • Model poisoning is stronger: the attacker crafts the returned update directly, scaling it to survive averaging. A single compromised client can meaningfully move an unprotected aggregate.
  • Backdoors: train the local model to behave normally except on a chosen trigger, then submit an update that implants that behaviour. Backdoors are hard to detect because held-out accuracy is unaffected.

Attacks from the server, and defences

  • An honest-but-curious server can inspect individual updates (see gradient inversion) or, worse, send a targeted model to one client to probe its data. Secure aggregation blocks the first; verifiable or attested aggregation is needed for the second.
  • Robust aggregation — coordinate-wise median, trimmed mean, or geometric-median rules — replaces the plain average so a few extreme updates cannot dominate. It costs some accuracy under benign non-IID skew, because a legitimately unusual client looks exactly like an attacker.
  • Norm clipping is the cheapest broad defence and is already present if you are doing DP; combined with noise it provably limits any single client's influence.
Takeaway: robustness and non-IID tolerance pull in opposite directions — every rule that suppresses outlying updates also suppresses genuinely underrepresented clients. Tune that dial deliberately.

08 Personalization, evaluation & use

A single global model is often the wrong product. And because you cannot look at the data, evaluation has to be federated too.

Personalization

  • The simplest and strongest baseline: train globally, then fine-tune locally on the device and keep the result private. Every user gets a model shaped by their own data at no extra communication cost.
  • Partial personalization shares a common representation while keeping the head, adapters, or normalization statistics on-device — the shared trunk still benefits from everyone.
  • Clustered federation groups clients with similar distributions and trains one model per cluster; meta-learning objectives explicitly optimise for a global model that adapts well in a few local steps.

Evaluation without a test set

  • Hold out data on device and have clients report only aggregate metrics — federated evaluation is a round with no weight update.
  • Report the distribution across clients, not just the mean: a model that is excellent on average and terrible for the bottom decile is a fairness failure the global number hides.
  • Deployment decisions still want a live comparison, which means an on-device A/B test of the candidate against the incumbent, plus drift monitoring on the reported aggregates.

Where it is used

  • Mobile keyboards (next-word prediction, on-device suggestion ranking), wake-word and speech personalization, and on-device content ranking.
  • Cross-hospital diagnostic models and cross-bank fraud detection, where the training corpus can never be pooled.
  • Local training is itself an inference-budget problem, so it pairs directly with model acceleration — quantized or adapter-only training is often what makes on-device rounds feasible at all.
Takeaway: global model plus local adaptation is usually better and cheaper than fighting non-IID skew with a single set of weights. Evaluate per-client, not just on average.

09 Pitfalls & checklist

Most federated projects fail for unglamorous reasons: the wrong regime, an unmeasured baseline, or a privacy claim nobody can defend.

  • Establish the ceiling first. Train centrally on whatever pooled or synthetic proxy you can legally assemble. If federation cannot come within a few points of that, you have an algorithm bug, not a fundamental limit.
  • Declare the regime. Cross-device or cross-silo, stateless or stateful, sampled cohort or full participation — half the literature does not apply to your case.
  • Measure the skew. Label distribution per client, dataset-size distribution, and participation frequency. These predict drift better than any hyperparameter.
  • Count rounds, not epochs. Rounds-to-target is the cost function; tune E, cohort size, and compression against it, and re-tune when skew changes.
  • Never claim "private" without a mechanism. Name the layer: secure aggregation (hides individuals from the server) or differential privacy (bounds output leakage, with an ε you can state). "Data never left the device" is not one of them.
  • Watch participation bias. Dropped stragglers, charging-only eligibility, and device-tier gating all skew who the model is for. Report metrics per client decile.
  • Assume some clients are hostile. Norm-clip every update, consider robust aggregation, and monitor for trigger-style backdoors that leave held-out accuracy untouched.
  • Plan the rollback. A bad global model reaches every device at once; keep the previous checkpoint servable and stage releases the same way you would any other deployment.
Takeaway: federated learning buys a real, legally meaningful reduction in data collection — and charges for it in rounds, in accuracy under skew, and in operational complexity. Take the deal only when the constraint is genuine.
