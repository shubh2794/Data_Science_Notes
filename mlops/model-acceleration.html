Model Acceleration

Trained models are often too big or slow to serve cheaply. Acceleration shrinks them and speeds them up — quantization, pruning, distillation, and runtime tricks — usually for a small, controllable accuracy cost.

MLOps & Deployment Efficiency Serving

01 Why accelerate

  • Cost: inference, run millions of times, dominates the lifetime compute bill.
  • Latency: interactive apps need fast responses.
  • Edge: phones and devices have tight memory/power budgets.

03 Quantization

  • Store/compute weights (and sometimes activations) in lower precision — fp16 → int8 → 4-bit — shrinking memory and speeding math.
  • PTQ (post-training) is quick; QAT (quantization-aware training) recovers more accuracy.
  • The biggest single lever for serving LLMs (e.g. 4-bit weights → 4× smaller).

04 Pruning

  • Remove weights/neurons/heads that contribute little — unstructured (sparse weights) or structured (whole channels/heads, which actually speeds up dense hardware).
  • Often followed by fine-tuning to recover accuracy.

05 Distillation & runtime

  • Knowledge distillation: train a small student to mimic a big teacher.
  • Runtime: batching, the KV cache, speculative decoding (a small draft model proposes tokens a big model verifies in one parallel pass — see its own page), graph compilation, and fused kernels (FlashAttention).
  • Architectural: MoE activates only part of the model per token.

06 Takeaways

  • Acceleration trades a little accuracy for big size/latency/cost wins — and the methods compound.
  • Quantization (esp. 4-bit) is the biggest single lever; pruning and distillation add more.
  • Runtime: batching, KV cache, speculative decoding, compilation.
  • Complements Distillation and MoE.
