Monitoring & Drift

A model is trained on a frozen snapshot of the world; production keeps moving. Monitoring is the standing instrumentation that watches inputs, predictions, and outcomes to catch the moment the deployed model stops being valid — ideally before it becomes a business loss.

01 The core problem: a frozen map of a moving world

  • Training data is a finite map; the real world is effectively infinite and keeps changing. The map goes stale even when nothing in your code breaks — this staleness is drift.
  • A model learns a joint distribution P(X, Y). The clean way to name drift is by which factor of the decomposition moves: the input distribution P(X), the outcome mix P(Y), or the rule connecting them P(Y|X).
  • Real vs virtual drift is the distinction that makes monitoring hard. Real drift moves the decision boundary and degrades accuracy; virtual drift moves only the inputs without changing the correct answer — the model may still be right even though its inputs look unfamiliar. Input statistics alone cannot tell the two apart.
P(X, Y) = P(Y|X) · P(X) = P(X|Y) · P(Y)
covariate / data drift: P(X) ≠ P′(X), P(Y|X) = P′(Y|X)
label / prior drift: P(Y) ≠ P′(Y), P(X|Y) = P′(X|Y)
concept drift: P(Y|X) ≠ P′(Y|X), P(X) = P′(X)
dataset shift: both P(X) and P(Y|X) move

02 Drift taxonomy

  • Covariate / data drift — P(X) changes, P(Y|X) stable. Inputs look different but the input→output rule still holds. A new product launch shifts the query mix; a sensor is recalibrated. Detectable now from inputs alone, but often benign.
  • Label / prior drift — P(Y) changes. The outcome mix shifts: the fraud rate jumps, the spam ratio climbs. Equivalent to P(X|Y) staying fixed while the class balance moves. It silently breaks any model whose calibration assumed the old base rate.
  • Concept drift — P(Y|X) changes. The spooky one: same input, different correct answer. House prices during a shock, "good investment" after a rate move. Nothing about the inputs looks off, so you usually catch it only via outcomes or business metrics — never input statistics.
  • Dataset shift = both P(X) and P(Y|X) move at once.
  • Temporal shapes dictate the detection window: sudden (a pipeline change or product launch — abrupt step), gradual (a slow taste/behaviour shift where old and new concepts overlap for a while), incremental (a steady ramp through intermediate states), recurring / seasonal (weekday vs weekend, holidays — comes back on a cycle).
  • A detector tuned for a sudden step will lag on gradual drift; a window shorter than the seasonal cycle will mistake every Monday for drift. Match the window to the shape.

03 Why deployed models degrade — and why it's usually not the model

  • Non-ML causes dominate real incidents. A study of 96 ML-pipeline failures over 15 years found 60 were non-ML: dependency breaks, wrong-version deploys, hardware/server failures, train–serve skew, and schema/units changes. The most common "drift" alert is a broken upstream pipeline, not a decaying model.
  • Train–serve skew is the classic trap: a feature computed one way offline and another way online (different default fills, time zones, unit conversions) makes the model see inputs it was never trained on — indistinguishable from drift at the metric level until you trace it.
  • ML-specific causes: covariate / label / concept drift, edge cases, bad data collection, and degenerate feedback loops where the model's own outputs reshape its future inputs.
  • A practitioner observation, repeated across monitoring teams, holds that a large fraction — often quoted around ~80% — of detected drifts trace back to human or pipeline error rather than genuine model decay. Treat this as a directional, anecdotal figure, not a controlled result. Its operational implication is solid regardless: route alerts to root-cause triage, not to auto-retrain.

04 Detection math — distribution distances

Bin a feature into shared bins; pᵢ is the current share and qᵢ the reference share in bin i. These metrics quantify how far the live distribution has moved from training.

PSI = ∑ᵢ (pᵢ − qᵢ) · ln(pᵢ / qᵢ) (0.1 = watch · 0.25 = act)
  • Population Stability Index is effectively a symmetrized KL — the round-trip relative entropy. Thresholds of thumb: PSI < 0.1 → no meaningful shift; 0.1 ≤ PSI < 0.25 → moderate, watch; PSI ≥ 0.25 → major shift, investigate. (Some teams act at 0.2.) Because it is a distance and not a p-value, it is sample-size stable.
DKL(P ‖ Q) = ∑ᵢ pᵢ · ln(pᵢ / qᵢ) (asymmetric · 0 = identical · → ∞ if any qᵢ = 0)
  • Kullback–Leibler divergence is asymmetric — DKL(P‖Q) ≠ DKL(Q‖P) — and unbounded; it blows up to infinity wherever qᵢ = 0 while pᵢ > 0. In practice you floor empty bins with a tiny ε to keep it finite.
JSD(P ‖ Q) = ½ · DKL(P ‖ M) + ½ · DKL(Q ‖ M), M = ½(P + Q)
  • Jensen–Shannon divergence is the symmetric, smoothed, always-finite cousin of KL. The JS distance is √JSD; a common drift line is ≈ 0.1. Its range depends on the log base: [0, ln 2] in nats (natural log) or [0, 1] in bits (log₂) — the bound is stated both ways here precisely because the base matters and is easy to get wrong.
D = supx |F₁(x) − F₂(x)| (Kolmogorov–Smirnov: max gap between empirical CDFs)
  • KS two-sample statistic is nonparametric and one-dimensional: the largest vertical gap between two empirical CDFs. It returns a p-value, which is exactly why it is dangerous at scale (see §06).
W₁(P, Q) = ∫₀¹ |F⁻¹P(u) − F⁻¹Q(u)| du = ∫ |FP(x) − FQ(x)| dx
  • Wasserstein-1 (earth-mover's) distance reports the magnitude of the shift in the feature's own units (often normalized by σ; drift line ≈ 0.1 σ). It is the pragmatic default for high-dimensional and embedding spaces.
χ² = ∑ᵢ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢ (categorical homogeneity: observed vs expected counts)
MMD²(P, Q) = E[k(x, x′)] + E[k(y, y′)] − 2·E[k(x, y)] (kernel two-sample, multivariate)
  • Chi-square is the standard test for categorical features. Maximum Mean Discrepancy is a kernel two-sample test that handles multivariate data directly — common in research, rarely the production default.

06 Streaming detectors — watching the error stream directly

  • Page–Hinkley is a CUSUM-style sequential test for a mean shift. With xₜ the observed value, x̄ₜ the running mean, and δ a tolerance:
mₜ = ∑i≤t (xᵢ − x̄ᵢ − δ), Mₜ = mini≤t mᵢ, alarm when (mₜ − Mₜ) > λ
  • DDM (Drift Detection Method) watches the online error rate pₜ with standard deviation sₜ = √(pₜ(1−pₜ)/t), tracking the running minimum (p_min + s_min):
warning when pₜ + sₜ ≥ p_min + 2·s_min
drift when pₜ + sₜ ≥ p_min + 3·s_min
  • The 2σ / 3σ warning and drift constants are the canonical formulation; exact factors vary by implementation. Treat them as the standard version, not the only one.
  • ADWIN (Adaptive Windowing) keeps a window W, splits it into an older sub-window W₀ and a recent W₁, and declares drift — dropping W₀ — when the means diverge past a cut derived from a Hoeffding bound at confidence δ:
|mean(W₀) − mean(W₁)| > εcut, εcut from a Hoeffding bound at confidence δ
Sequential alarm — the warning → drift two-stage sequence An error stream crosses the 2σ warning band, then the 3σ drift band

07 Three monitoring layers — and the two-clocks problem

  • Layer 1 — inputs / features. Cheapest and available instantly: schema / range / null checks plus per-feature distribution distance (PSI / KS / Wasserstein). Catches pipeline breakage and covariate drift. Problem: feature distributions wobble constantly and most wobble is benign → alert fatigue. This overlaps directly with the schema and range checks in data quality and governance — see Data Quality & Governance.
  • Layer 2 — predictions / output distribution. Low-dimensional and easy to two-sample-test. Because the model weights are fixed, a shift in the output distribution is a strong proxy for a shift in inputs — often the best single signal you can get without labels.
  • Layer 3 — accuracy / business outcome. The only layer that directly answers "is the model still good?", but gated by ground-truth availability. The model-evaluation metrics measured offline are the baseline this layer degrades away from.
  • Latency increases and trustworthiness increases as you go down the stack: inputs are instant but ambiguous; outcomes are definitive but late.
inputs (instant, noisy) → predictions (fast proxy) → accuracy / business (late, definitive)
  • The two-clocks / ground-truth-lag problem. Input drift is observable now; accuracy drift is observable only when labels arrive. Feedback-loop length sets how fast you can measure quality — a recommender gets a click in seconds, while a fraud or credit model gets labels weeks to months later via a dispute window. If such a model breaks and labels lag, damage compounds before detection.
  • Natural labels (an ETA that self-corrects when the trip ends) are a luxury; most tasks need proxy metrics — click-through, completion rate, dwell time, downstream conversion — that approximate quality in real time.
  • Degenerate feedback loops: the model's outputs shape the next inputs (popularity bias compounds). Detect via output diversity and long-tail coverage; correct with randomized exploration and positional features.

08 Choosing a test — the sample-size trap

  • KS is sample-size-cursed. Its statistical power explodes with n, so the p-value flags trivial changes — it fires on a ~0.5% shift once n exceeds roughly 100,000. Good for small data (< ~1,000) or when tiny deviations genuinely matter; a poor default in a big production stream. The detector viz above demonstrates exactly this: flip n to 100k and KS turns red while PSI stays calm.
  • PSI / KL / JS are sample-size-stable distance metrics (not p-values), so they behave predictably in production. PSI flags only big changes; JS is slightly more sensitive than KL.
  • Wasserstein is the pragmatic middle ground: more sensitive than PSI, less trigger-happy than KS, and it reports the drift magnitude in interpretable units. Preferred for high-dimensional and embedding spaces, where KS degrades.
  • Chi-square for categoricals; MMD for multivariate research settings. And always reduce dimensionality before multivariate two-sample testing — testing 500 raw features independently guarantees false alarms.

09 Windowing & alerting

  • Reference vs current window. A fixed reference (the training baseline) is compared against a sliding or current window. The window must be ≥ the seasonal cycle — a sub-week window cannot see a weekly pattern and will alarm every weekend.
  • Sliding vs cumulative. A sliding window tracks recency and forgets the past quickly (good for spotting recent shifts); a cumulative window retains history (good for a stable long-run baseline). Choose by whether recency or stability matters more.
  • The alerting dilemma. Tighter thresholds catch drift early but drown the team in false alarms → engineers mute the alerts → real drift is missed. The whole game is tuning the threshold and requiring a sustained breach, not a single spike.
  • Route to triage, not to auto-retrain. Since most alerts are pipeline or version errors, an alert should open a root-cause investigation rather than blindly kicking off a retraining job. Surfacing these signals well — on a single shared view — is the role of monitoring dashboards.

10 LLM-specific drift

  • Embedding / data drift. The input-prompt embedding distribution shifts — new topics, a product launch changes the query mix. Measure the distance between embedding distributions or centroids across time windows. Because embeddings are high-dimensional, Wasserstein is preferred over KS.
  • Concept drift in LLMs. Prompts look statistically similar (low data drift) but the desired answer changed — "good investment" after a market shift. Only catchable via downstream business metrics and user feedback, not input statistics.
  • Prompt / response drift. Changes to prompt templates, retrieved context, or a silent provider model-version update can shift outputs even when user inputs are perfectly stable. This is why pinning and re-evaluating the provider model is a monitoring concern — see LLMOps.
  • The two-layer pattern. Layer 1 is a cheap statistical embedding-distance test that fires an alert. Layer 2 samples the drifted prompts and uses an LLM-as-judge to classify the cause ("new topic", "intent shift", "rising complexity", "language-style change"), turning a raw alarm into an actionable diagnosis. Output-quality drift is tracked via judge scores, toxicity / refusal rates, latency, and user thumbs — all detailed under LLMOps.

11 Remediation ladder — alert to action

  • Triage first. Confirm it is real drift, not a broken pipeline or a wrong-version deploy — statistically the most likely cause. Most of the ladder below is skipped once you find a schema mismatch.
  • Rollback. If quality dropped right after a deploy, revert to the last-good model or prompt before doing anything else.
  • Retraining triggers. Scheduled (cron), threshold-based (a PSI or accuracy breach), or continuous. Refresh the data window and revalidate against the held-out and online metrics — the retraining runs and their metrics are logged in experiment tracking so the loop is auditable.
  • Shadow deployment. Run the candidate model on live traffic without serving its outputs, and compare to the incumbent before promotion. Canary = serve to a small percentage first, then ramp. The statistical machinery for comparing shadow / canary against the incumbent lives in A/B Testing; the rollout and rollback mechanics belong to model deployment.
  • For LLMs: update prompts and system instructions, refresh the RAG corpus and index, re-pin or re-evaluate the provider model version, and adjust guardrails — again in concert with LLMOps.

12 Takeaways

  • Name drift by which factor moves: P(X) (covariate), P(Y) (label), P(Y|X) (concept) — and remember input stats cannot distinguish real from virtual drift.
  • Most alerts are pipeline / version errors, not model decay — triage before you retrain.
  • Pick the test for the data: PSI / JS / Wasserstein for stability at scale, Wasserstein for embeddings, chi-square for categoricals; KS only on small samples.
  • Monitor three layers (inputs → predictions → outcomes) and bridge the ground-truth lag with proxy metrics.
  • Require sustained breaches, route to triage, and remediate up the ladder: rollback → retrain → shadow → canary.
