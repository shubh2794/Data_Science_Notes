Audio Models

Audio models are neural networks that take a sampled sound wave — usually speech at 16 kHz — and turn it into a sequence of vectors that a head can map to text, a speaker, an emotion, or a language label. This hub is the note on the category itself: the two front ends (raw waveform versus log-mel spectrogram), the three training signals (supervised, self-supervised, weakly supervised), the three ways of emitting text (CTC, sequence-to-sequence, transducer), the lineage from Deep Speech 2 to Whisper large-v3-turbo, a table of verified configurations, and a guide to choosing. The model pages in this hub are Whisper and wav2vec 2.0; HuBERT, WavLM, XLS-R, MMS, Conformer and Deep Speech 2 appear throughout as reference points, not as pages of their own.

01 What audio models are

An audio model is a network whose input is a one-dimensional pressure signal sampled thousands of times per second and whose output is a much shorter sequence of learned vectors, one per 20 to 40 milliseconds of sound. Everything that distinguishes one audio model from another is a choice about how that compression happens, what signal trains it, and what is decoded from the vectors afterwards.

the shape most models in this hub share (Deep Speech 2 uses recurrent layers, Conformer a convolution-augmented block, in place of the plain Transformer encoder)waveform x ∈ ℝᴺ → front end → frames F ∈ ℝ^(T×c) → Transformer encoder → H ∈ ℝ^(T′×d) → head sequence lengths for 30 s of 16 kHz audioN = 480,000 samples T′ ≈ 1,500 encoder positions (≈ 320 samples = 20 ms per position)

Why audio needs its own architectures

  • The sequence is long and redundant. One second of 16 kHz speech is 16,000 numbers but carries only a handful of phonemes. Text Transformers are not normally run on 480,000-token inputs; an audio model's first job is to shrink the time axis by roughly two to three orders of magnitude before any attention runs.
  • The output is shorter than the input and not aligned to it. A 3-second utterance may have 40 characters of transcript and 150 encoder frames, and nobody tells the model which frames produce which characters. CTC, attention-based decoders and transducers are three answers to that alignment problem.
  • Labels are expensive. Transcribing an hour of speech is commonly quoted as taking a human several hours, while untranscribed audio is nearly free. That imbalance is why the two landmark models in this hub are defined by their training signal: wav2vec 2.0 by self-supervision on unlabelled audio, Whisper by weak supervision on 680,000 hours of imperfect internet transcripts.

What sits in this category and what does not

  • In: speech encoders and recognisers — models that read audio. Their representations also power speaker verification, emotion recognition, keyword spotting and language identification.
  • Out: speech synthesis (text-to-speech, vocoders), music generation and neural audio codecs. They share front ends with the models here but invert the direction of the mapping, and they are not covered on this hub.
Takeaway: an audio model is a learned compressor from samples to roughly 50 vectors per second, plus a head that resolves the alignment between those vectors and a much shorter label sequence. The interesting differences are in the front end, the training signal and the decoder — not in the Transformer in the middle, which is largely standard.

02 From samples to frames: the two front ends

Before any Transformer layer runs, the waveform must become a sequence of frames. Two front ends dominate: a hand-designed log-mel spectrogram followed by a small convolutional stem (Whisper, Conformer), or a learned stack of strided 1-D convolutions applied directly to raw samples (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, XLS-R, MMS). Whisper's stem and the wav2vec 2.0 stack both land at about 50 encoder positions per second; Conformer subsamples further, to one position per 40 ms (25 per second).

Switching the sample rate shows why the conv stack is tied to 16 kHz: its hop is a fixed number of samples, so at 8 kHz each position covers 40 ms of sound instead of 20.

The log-mel spectrogram

  • A short-time Fourier transform on 25 ms windows every 10 ms, magnitude squared, projected onto a bank of triangular filters spaced on the mel scale (roughly linear below 1 kHz and logarithmic above), then a logarithm. Whisper uses 80 mel channels; large-v3 and large-v3-turbo use 128.
  • Whisper then applies two convolutions of filter width 3 with GELU, the second with stride 2, so 3,000 mel frames become 1,500 encoder positions. Every input is padded or trimmed to exactly 30 seconds, so the encoder always sees the same length.
  • The spectrogram discards phase and fixes the time–frequency trade-off by hand. That is a mild loss of information, but a strong prior: the model starts from features that already look like what the ear resolves.

The learned convolutional feature encoder

  • wav2vec 2.0 feeds normalised raw samples into seven 512-channel temporal convolutions (with GELU and, in the base configuration, a group normalisation after the first layer). The first layer's kernel of 10 and stride of 5 plays the role of a learned filterbank.
  • The stack's receptive field is 400 samples and its hop 320 samples — the same 25 ms window and a 20 ms hop, about 49 frames per second, which matches the window and hop of classical speech features.
  • HuBERT, WavLM, XLS-R and MMS reuse this exact stack. Their released configurations carry identical conv_kernel and conv_stride lists, which is why they all share a 20 ms frame rate.
Takeaway: the two front ends differ in who designs the filterbank — a signal-processing recipe or gradient descent — but converge on the same budget of about 50 positions per second. In practice both are used at the sample rate they were trained on (Whisper also resamples all input to 16 kHz); the conv stack's hop is a fixed number of samples, so it cannot be re-parameterised in milliseconds, whereas a spectrogram's window and hop could in principle be recomputed for another rate.

03 The design axes that separate audio models

Five choices describe most models in this category. Reading a new speech paper is mostly a matter of locating it on these five axes; the Transformer in the middle is usually unremarkable.

How the axes interact

  • Self-supervised encoders are encoder-only by construction: the pretraining objective lives on encoder frames, and after pretraining a linear CTC head is the cheapest way to produce text. Their pretraining teaches acoustics; the fine-tuning labels teach spelling.
  • Whisper's weak supervision is what makes an encoder–decoder natural: the targets are already text, so a decoder can learn spelling, punctuation, casing and a language model jointly — and special tokens can select the task.
  • Transducers sit between the two: frame-synchronous like CTC, so they stream, but with a prediction network that conditions on previous labels, removing CTC's independence assumption. They are a common choice for on-device streaming recognition; the Conformer paper itself trains its encoder with a single-LSTM transducer decoder.
Takeaway: classify any audio model by front end, training signal, output layer, wiring and hours of audio. Most of the practical behaviour — whether it streams, whether it needs labels, whether it hallucinates on silence — follows from those five choices.

04 Lineage: from CTC recurrent nets to weakly supervised Transformers

Modern audio models descend from three lines that met around 2020: end-to-end recognisers trained with CTC, self-supervised representation learning on raw waveforms, and sequence-to-sequence Transformers borrowed from translation. Whisper and wav2vec 2.0 are each the clearest example of one line.

Reading the lineage

  • The self-supervised line answered "how do we use audio without transcripts?" and peaked in low-resource results — 10 minutes of labels giving usable recognition.
  • The weakly supervised line answered "what if we simply collect far more imperfect transcripts?" and peaked in robustness — a single model that works tolerably across accents, noise and domains without fine-tuning.
  • These are not rivals in a strict sense: MMS is a wav2vec 2.0-style encoder scaled across languages, and later systems pseudo-label audio with an earlier Whisper to train the next one, which blurs the boundary between weak and self-generated supervision.
Takeaway: the category's history is a sequence of answers to the label-cost problem — CTC removed the need for alignments, self-supervision removed most of the need for transcripts, and weak supervision replaced careful transcripts with sheer quantity.

Conflicts worth knowing

  • Attention heads in the BASE encoders. The wav2vec 2.0 paper states 8 heads for BASE and the HuBERT paper's table repeats 8, while the released BASE checkpoints' configuration files specify 12. The head count does not change the parameter count (the projections are d × d either way), so the reported 95M is consistent with both. This hub quotes the paper and flags the configuration. WavLM has the same kind of mismatch in the other direction: its paper's text gives 8 heads for Base and 12 for Large, while the released configurations use 12 and 16.
  • XLS-R 1B width. The XLS-R paper's architecture table lists the 1B model with width 1024 and FFN 4096, the same as 0.3B, but the released 1B configuration uses 1280 and 5120. Only the configuration reproduces the reported 965M (48 blocks at width 1024 would give roughly 0.6B), so the table entry looks like a typo; this hub uses the configuration.
  • Masking probability. The wav2vec 2.0 paper uses p = 0.065 for pretraining; the released configuration's mask_time_prob of 0.05 is the value the library applies when the checkpoint is fine-tuned (as a SpecAugment-style mask), and should not be read as the pretraining setting.
  • "Parameters" means different things. Whisper's figures count the whole encoder–decoder; the self-supervised figures count the pretraining model including its pretraining-only heads (the quantiser for wav2vec 2.0, cluster embeddings for HuBERT); a fine-tuned CTC checkpoint adds a small output layer (d × vocabulary) and drops the quantiser.
Takeaway: the two families use the same widths (768, 1024, 1280) and similar depths; they differ in whether half the weights sit in a text decoder. That single fact explains most of the difference in what they can do out of the box.

06 CTC: training without alignments, worked by hand

Connectionist Temporal Classification lets an encoder-only model emit one symbol per frame — a character, a subword, or a special blank — and defines the transcript as whatever remains after merging repeated symbols and deleting blanks. The loss sums the probability of every frame-level path that collapses to the right transcript.

CTC alignments on the extended label lattice choose a target and a frame count · sample another valid path · every count comes from the DP

Figure. Rows are the extended label sequence (a blank before, between and after every symbol); columns are frames. A valid path starts in one of the first two rows, ends in one of the last two, and at each step stays, moves down one row, or skips a blank — the skip is forbidden when it would jump between two identical symbols, which is why "see" and "hello" need extra frames. The count of valid paths is the forward algorithm run with every probability set to 1; the sampled path is drawn uniformly from them using the backward counts. Frame counts below the minimum are raised automatically.

Hand-working the count for "cat" in 4 frames

  • Three symbols need at least 3 frames. With 4 frames there is one spare frame, which can be a blank in any of the 4 gaps around the letters, or a repetition of any of the 3 letters. Blanks at the start, between c and a, between a and t, or at the end give 4 paths; repeating c, a or t gives 3 more — 7 alignments in all.
  • For "see", the two e's must be separated by a blank, so the minimum is 4 frames and with exactly 4 frames there is a single path: s e ∅ e. That forced blank is the whole reason CTC has a blank symbol beyond silence.
  • The number of alignments grows combinatorially with T, which is why the loss must be computed by dynamic programming rather than by enumeration.

What CTC assumes, and what it costs

  • Conditional independence. Given the audio, each frame's output ignores the others. The model cannot learn that "their" is likelier than "thair" except through acoustic context, so CTC recognisers often gain noticeably from an external language model or a subword vocabulary.
  • Monotonic alignment and T ≥ U. Output order follows time, which suits speech. At a 20 ms frame rate, one frame per character is rarely a binding limit for speech, though it can matter for fast speech with a character vocabulary.
  • Peaky outputs. Trained CTC models emit the blank on most frames and each symbol in a short spike, so frame-level timestamps from CTC are approximate. This behaviour is widely observed; its cause is discussed in the literature rather than settled.
  • Why it pairs with self-supervised encoders: a single linear layer on top of a pretrained encoder, trained with CTC on as little as 10 minutes of labels, is the recipe behind the wav2vec 2.0 low-resource results.
Takeaway: CTC turns "which frames say which letter?" into a sum the forward algorithm computes exactly. It is fast, streamable and alignment-free, at the price of treating frames as independent — a price a language model or a decoder pays back.

07 Sequence-to-sequence, transducers, and Whisper's task tokens

An attention decoder removes CTC's independence assumption by generating the transcript autoregressively while cross-attending to the encoder. A transducer keeps CTC's frame-by-frame structure but adds a network over previous labels. Whisper takes the decoder route and uses the decoder's prompt to specify the task.

attention encoder–decoder: exact chain rule over output tokensP(y | x) = ∏ᵤ P(yᵤ | y₁, …, yᵤ₋₁, Enc(x)) transducer: joint network over encoder frame t and prediction state uP(k | t, u) = softmax( J( Enc(x)ₜ , Pred(y₍<ᵤ₎) ) ) summed over monotonic lattice paths

Whisper's multitask format

  • The decoder's input begins with a short sequence of special tokens: <|startoftranscript|>, a language tag (99 languages in the original release; large-v3 adds Cantonese), <|transcribe|> or <|translate|>, and optionally <|notimestamps|>. If the audio has no speech the model is trained to emit <|nospeech|>.
  • Timestamps are tokens too: times are quantised to 20 ms, so a 30-second window needs 1,501 timestamp tokens, predicted interleaved with the text. Segment-level timestamps therefore come from the same decoder, not from a separate aligner.
  • Text uses the GPT-2 byte-level BPE vocabulary for the English-only models and a refitted vocabulary of the same size for the multilingual ones (51,865 entries in the released multilingual configuration, 51,866 in large-v3). The decoder's context is 448 tokens.
  • Previous-window text can be fed as a prompt, which is how long audio is transcribed window by window with some continuity.

What the decoder buys and what it costs

  • Buys: an internal language model (punctuation, casing, spelling), task selection through tokens, translation into English, and outputs of any length within the context.
  • Costs: sequential decoding (one decoder pass per token), no native streaming (the encoder sees a fixed 30-second window), and failure modes that CTC does not have — repetition loops and text generated for non-speech audio. These "hallucinations" are widely reported in practice; their frequency depends heavily on decoding settings and on voice-activity filtering, and is not reported as a single number in the paper.
  • Transducer middle ground: frame-synchronous like CTC, so it can stream with bounded latency, while the prediction network conditions on history. The cost is a three-dimensional (T × U × vocabulary) joint computation during training.
Takeaway: choose the output layer by the deployment constraint: CTC for cheap, parallel, alignment-friendly decoding; a transducer for streaming with a language model built in; an attention decoder when the model should also punctuate, translate, identify the language and follow task tokens.

08 Self-supervised objectives: contrastive, masked prediction, denoising

The self-supervised encoders all mask spans of the frame sequence and ask the Transformer to say something about the hidden frames from the visible ones. They differ in what the target is: a learned quantised code chosen among distractors (wav2vec 2.0), an offline cluster id (HuBERT), or a cluster id for speech that has been overlapped with noise or another speaker (WavLM).

Three targets, three failure modes they avoid

  • Contrastive with a learned quantiser (wav2vec 2.0). The target for a masked step is the quantised version of that step's own local feature, and the model must pick it out from 100 distractors drawn from other masked steps of the same utterance. Without the diversity loss the quantiser can collapse onto a few codes and make the task trivial; the penalty pushes towards using all 2 × 320 entries equally.
  • Masked prediction of offline clusters (HuBERT). Targets come from k-means: 100 clusters on 39-dimensional MFCCs for the first iteration, then 500 clusters on intermediate-layer features of the previous model. The loss is ordinary cross-entropy, applied to masked frames only (α = 1 in the paper's weighting). Targets are fixed during a run, so there is no collapse to guard against, at the cost of extra clustering passes.
  • Denoising masked prediction (WavLM). Some utterances in a batch are mixed with noise or a segment of another utterance, and the model must still predict the clusters of the primary speaker. This teaches speaker and separation information that ASR-oriented objectives under-emphasise, and WavLM Large reported gains over HuBERT Large on 14 of the SUPERB subtasks.
Takeaway: all three objectives are BERT-style masking on a sequence without a vocabulary; the design problem is inventing a vocabulary — a learned codebook or a clustering — that is informative enough to be worth predicting and stable enough not to collapse.

09 Weak supervision at scale

Whisper's bet was that transcripts found alongside audio on the internet — imperfect, unaligned, sometimes machine-generated — are good enough if there are enough of them. The training set was 680,000 hours, roughly 700 times the 960 hours of LibriSpeech that most self-supervised papers fine-tune on.

composition of the 680,000 hours (the paper's data figure)English transcription 438,218 h (65%) · X → English translation 125,739 h (18%) · non-English transcription 117,113 h (17%, 96 languages) training schedule reported for the original modelsbatch 256 segments × 30 s · 2²⁰ ≈ 1 M updates · 2–3 passes over the data

Filtering is most of the method

  • Transcripts that look machine-generated (for instance all upper-case or all lower-case, or lacking punctuation) were removed, because training on another recogniser's output teaches its mistakes.
  • A language detector checked that the spoken language matched the transcript language; mismatched English transcripts became translation data instead of being discarded.
  • Audio was cut into 30-second segments paired with the transcript text that falls within each window, including segments with no speech, which is where the <|nospeech|> training signal comes from.

What the scale bought

  • Robustness, measured zero-shot. The paper compares Whisper, never fine-tuned on LibriSpeech, with a supervised model of similar LibriSpeech accuracy: across other English datasets Whisper makes on average 55.2% fewer relative errors. Its large-v2 model scores 2.7% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean without seeing that training set.
  • Coverage. One checkpoint transcribes and translates many languages, though accuracy per language tracks the hours of that language in training closely; low-resource languages remain weak.
  • Diminishing returns in English. The paper's own scaling study shows English WER flattening with more data and model size, which is part of the motivation for later work mixing in pseudo-labelled audio (large-v3: 1 M h weak plus 4 M h pseudo-labelled by large-v2, reported as a 10–20% error reduction over large-v2).
Takeaway: weak supervision trades label quality for label quantity and wins on robustness; self-supervision trades labels for unlabelled audio and wins on low-resource adaptation. MMS shows the trade can flip: on the 54 FLEURS languages both cover, the MMS paper reports a 58% relative WER reduction over Whisper (its CTC model decoded with n-gram language models) with far less labelled data.

10 How to choose between them

The practical choice is rarely "Whisper or wav2vec 2.0" in the abstract. It is a question about labels, languages, latency and what the output must contain. The rules of thumb below are common practice rather than theorems; benchmark on your own audio before committing.

Costs that are easy to miss

  • Decoder cost scales with output length. An encoder-only CTC model does one pass over the audio; an encoder–decoder does one encoder pass plus one decoder pass per generated token. This is the main motivation for large-v3-turbo's 4-layer decoder and for distilled Whisper variants.
  • Fine-tuning versus prompting. A self-supervised encoder is not usable for transcription until fine-tuned; Whisper is usable at once but harder to change, and fine-tuning its decoder on a narrow domain can erode its multilingual and multitask behaviour.
  • Sample rate. The wav2vec 2.0 family expects 16 kHz; 8 kHz telephone audio must be upsampled, and the model has never seen telephone band-limiting unless it was in pretraining.
  • Silence and music. Decoder models can produce plausible text for non-speech input; a voice-activity detector in front is a common mitigation.
Takeaway: Whisper when you want a working transcript today with no labels; a self-supervised encoder plus CTC when you have some labels, an under-served language, or need representations for tasks other than transcription; a transducer when latency is the constraint.

13 Takeaways

The category in a dozen lines, each of which the sections above justify with a figure, a table or a formula.

Mechanics

  • Compress first. Whisper's and wav2vec 2.0's front ends both reduce 16,000 samples per second to about 50 positions per second — 320 samples per position — before the Transformer runs.
  • Log-mel versus waveform is a choice of who designs the filterbank. The learned conv stack (kernels 10, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2; strides 5, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2) has a 25 ms receptive field and a 20 ms hop, mirroring the classical window and hop.
  • Three training signals: supervised (Deep Speech 2, Conformer), self-supervised (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, WavLM, XLS-R, MMS), weakly supervised (Whisper). They differ mainly in what labels they need.
  • Three output layers: CTC (independent frames, fast, alignable), attention decoder (conditional, flexible, sequential), transducer (conditional and streamable).
  • CTC's blank is required to separate repeated symbols; the alignment count explodes combinatorially and is summed by dynamic programming.
  • Span masking hides far more than p: a 6.5% start probability with 10-frame spans masks about half the sequence.
  • Self-supervised targets must be invented: a learned codebook (with a diversity penalty) or offline clusters (with iterative refinement).

Models and choices

  • Weak supervision at 680,000 hours buys zero-shot robustness; its accuracy per language tracks the hours of that language.
  • Half of Whisper is a text decoder. Cutting it from 32 to 4 layers nearly halves the model (1,550M → 809M), which is what turbo does.
  • Configuration files and papers disagree in small places (BASE head counts, masking probability); quote the source you used.
  • Choose by constraint: no labels and formatted text → Whisper; few labels or a rare language → self-supervised encoder + CTC; latency → transducer.
Takeaway: audio models are Transformers with a front end that compresses time, a training signal that works around the cost of transcripts, and an output layer that solves alignment. Learn those three choices and the rest of the category reads as variations.
