BERT

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers — the model that made "pretrain once, fine-tune everywhere" the default in NLP. An encoder-only Transformer that reads the whole sequence at once and emits one contextual vector per token. It never generates text; it produces representations that a small task head can turn into a label, a span, or a similarity score.

NLP Encoder-only Pretraining Representation learning

01 What BERT is

Strip away the branding and BERT is a plain Transformer encoder stack with a clever pretraining objective bolted on top and then thrown away.

The shape of the computation

  • Input: a sequence of n sub-word ids. Output: a tensor of shape n × H — one H-dimensional vector per token, each of which has seen every other token in the sequence.
  • Every block is the standard pair: multi-head self-attention, then a position-wise feed-forward network, each wrapped in a residual connection and a LayerNorm. Original BERT uses post-norm (LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))) and a GELU activation in the FFN.
  • There is no causal mask and no cross-attention: it is the encoder half of the original encoder–decoder design, used on its own.
one encoder blockh′ = LayerNorm( h + MultiHead(h, h, h) ) h″ = LayerNorm( h′ + W₂ · GELU(W₁ h′ + b₁) + b₂ ) attention, no causal maskAttn(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √dₖ ) V

Two phases, one backbone

  • Pretraining is expensive, unsupervised and done once: fill in corrupted tokens over billions of words of raw text. It learns syntax, coreference, selectional preferences and a lot of world knowledge as a side effect.
  • Fine-tuning is cheap and done per task: add a linear head, train every parameter for 2–4 epochs on a few thousand labelled examples. The representation transfers; only the head is new.
Takeaway: BERT is a deeply bidirectional Transformer encoder, pretrained by filling in masked words, then fine-tuned for understanding tasks. The pretraining head is scaffolding — the useful artifact is the stack of hidden states.

02 Why bidirectional, and why masking is required

Bidirectionality sounds obviously good. The interesting part is why you cannot get it from an ordinary language model, and what BERT gives up to obtain it.

The self-seeing problem

  • A left-to-right LM predicts xᵢ from x₁…xᵢ₋₁. That is a valid probability model, but the representation of a token never incorporates anything to its right — and disambiguating "he sat by the bank" needs the words that follow.
  • The naive fix — run one forward and one backward model and concatenate — gives each direction shallow access to the other. Predecessors did exactly this, and the two halves never interact inside a layer.
  • Letting every token attend to every other token while still predicting each token breaks immediately: in a multi-layer stack the target token leaks into its own prediction through the layer below. The objective becomes copying, and the model learns nothing.

Denoising instead of prediction

  • BERT's answer is to change the task: corrupt a subset of positions, then reconstruct those positions from the uncorrupted rest. Nothing leaks because the answer is genuinely absent from the input.
  • This makes BERT a denoising autoencoder, not a language model. It cannot score the probability of a sentence and cannot sample text left to right.
  • The cost is signal density: an autoregressive model gets a training signal from every position, BERT only from the ~15% it corrupted. That is a large part of why later encoder work (ELECTRA) went hunting for denser objectives.
Takeaway: masking is not a trick for convenience — it is the only way to condition on both sides at every layer without the label leaking into the input. Trading generation for bidirectional context is the whole design bet.

03 Masked Language Modeling

The objective

  • Sample a set M of ~15% of the token positions, corrupt them to produce x̃, and minimise the cross-entropy of the originals at exactly those positions. Unmasked positions contribute nothing to the loss.
  • The MLM head is small and discarded after pretraining: a dense projection, GELU, a LayerNorm, and an output matrix tied to the input embedding table, so the vocabulary projection costs no extra parameters.
masked language modelling lossℒ_MLM = − ∑_{i ∈ M} log P( xᵢ | x̃ ) per-position head, over vocabulary VP( xᵢ = v | x̃ ) = softmax( Eᵀ · LN(GELU(W hᵢ + b)) )ᵥ E is the shared token-embedding matrixE : |V| × H hᵢ : final hidden state at position i

The 80 / 10 / 10 rule

  • Of the 15% chosen positions: 80% become [MASK], 10% are replaced by a random vocabulary token, and 10% are left untouched — but all three still contribute to the loss.
  • The random and unchanged slices matter. Because the model cannot tell which positions were corrupted, it is forced to build a useful representation of every token rather than only of [MASK] slots, and it learns to be sceptical of the surface token it sees.
  • This also narrows the pretrain/fine-tune gap: [MASK] never appears downstream, so relying on it entirely would leave the encoder tuned for inputs it will never meet again.
Takeaway: MLM is cross-entropy on a 15% subset. The corruption recipe — not the loss — is what keeps the learned representation honest at every position.

04 Next Sentence Prediction, and why it faded

The original recipe had a second head: a binary classifier over the [CLS] vector asking whether segment B truly followed segment A. It was dropped within a year.

  • The setup. Half the training pairs are consecutive spans from the same document; half pair a span with a random span from elsewhere in the corpus. The [CLS] state feeds a two-way softmax, and the loss is added to the MLM term.
  • The motivation was reasonable: question answering and entailment are pair tasks, and MLM alone gives no explicit pressure to model discourse-level relations between two segments.
  • The problem is that the negative is far too easy. A random span usually comes from a different topic entirely, so lexical overlap alone solves the task — the model learns topic matching, not coherence, and the gradient stops being informative early in training.
  • What replaced it. RoBERTa removed NSP, trained on full-length contiguous spans, and improved on every benchmark. ALBERT kept a pair objective but fixed the negatives: sentence-order prediction uses the same two segments with their order swapped, so topic overlap gives no signal and only coherence does.
original combined pretraining lossℒ = ℒ_MLM + ℒ_NSP ℒ_NSP = − log P( y | h_[CLS] ), y ∈ { IsNext, NotNext }
Takeaway: NSP is a lesson about negative sampling, not about sentence pairs. An auxiliary objective only helps when its negatives are hard enough to require the capability you actually want.

05 Input representation

Before any attention happens, every position is the sum of three learned vectors. Hover the columns below to see which three are being added.

position i, token t, segment se ᵢ = E_tok[t] + E_seg[s] + E_pos[i] h⁰ᵢ = Dropout( LayerNorm( e ᵢ ) )
Three embeddings, summed hover a column · segment ids mark sentence A vs B · positions are learned, not sinusoidal

Tokens and special symbols

  • WordPiece sub-words, roughly 30k entries, with continuation pieces marked ##. Any string is representable, and rare words decompose rather than becoming [UNK] — see Tokenization.
  • [CLS] is prepended to every sequence. It has no meaning of its own; it is a free slot whose final state becomes the sequence-level summary once a classification head trains it to be one.
  • [SEP] terminates each segment, [PAD] fills a batch to equal length and is excluded by the attention mask, and [MASK] is the pretraining corruption token.

Segments and positions

  • The segment embedding is a two-row table: every token in sentence A adds row 0, every token in sentence B adds row 1. That is how one encoder handles pair inputs without a second stack.
  • The position embedding is a learned 512 × H table, not a sinusoid. It is fully absolute, and because row 512 was never trained the model has a hard length ceiling — extending it requires interpolation or retraining.
Takeaway: summing rather than concatenating keeps the width at H and lets attention treat "which word", "which sentence" and "where" as one blended signal from layer one onward.

06 Fine-tuning recipes

Same backbone, four output shapes. Fine-tuning is deliberately boring: attach a linear layer, unfreeze everything, train briefly.

Hyperparameters that actually matter

  • Learning rates in the 2e-5 to 5e-5 range with a linear warmup then decay; anything near 1e-3 destroys the pretrained weights in the first few hundred steps.
  • Two to four epochs. Small datasets overfit quickly and fine-tuning runs are famously seed-sensitive — a handful of restarts is normal practice, not a smell.
  • For pair tasks, a cross-encoder (both texts in one sequence, attention across them) is far more accurate than encoding each side separately — and far slower, since every candidate pair needs its own forward pass.
  • Parameter-efficient alternatives freeze the backbone and train adapters or low-rank updates instead; see Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning and PEFT.
Takeaway: the head is trivial; the discipline is in the optimiser. Small learning rate, short schedule, and the pretrained representation survives intact.

07 BERT as an embedding model

The obvious idea — take h_[CLS] out of an off-the-shelf BERT and use it as a sentence vector — works badly. Understanding why explains most of modern retrieval training.

Why raw [CLS] disappoints

  • Nothing in MLM ever asks [CLS] to summarise meaning. Without NSP or a fine-tuned classifier, it is an untrained slot, and cosine similarity between two such vectors is close to noise.
  • Contextual embeddings are strongly anisotropic: they occupy a narrow cone in ℝ^H, so random unrelated sentences already score high cosine similarity and the useful signal is a thin margin on top of a large common component. A few dominant, high-frequency directions do most of the damage.
  • Mean-pooling all token states beats [CLS] by a wide margin, and both are beaten by simply removing the offending structure.

What fixes it

  • Post-hoc whitening. Centre the embeddings and decorrelate them so the covariance is isotropic. Cheap, training-free, and often worth several points on similarity benchmarks.
  • Contrastive fine-tuning. Train a bi-encoder (one BERT, two texts, shared weights) so paired texts are close and in-batch negatives are far. This is the Sentence-BERT idea, later sharpened by dropout-based contrastive objectives.
  • Bi-encoders let you embed a corpus once and search with a dot product; cross-encoders stay for reranking the top few dozen. See Embeddings.
mean pooling with attention maskv = ( ∑ᵢ mᵢ · hᵢ ) / ( ∑ᵢ mᵢ ) contrastive objective, temperature τℒ = − log [ exp(sim(vᵢ, vᵢ⁺)/τ) / ∑ⱼ exp(sim(vᵢ, vⱼ)/τ) ]
Takeaway: a pretrained encoder gives you good token representations, not good sentence vectors. Sentence-level geometry has to be trained for — pooling choice and contrastive objective, not the backbone, decide retrieval quality.

08 The BERT family

Almost every successor changes one of three things: the corruption objective, the parameter budget, or the positional scheme. Read the table as a list of ablations that stuck.

ModelYearWhat it changedWhy it matters

ALBERT2019Cross-layer parameter sharing, factorised embedding V×E×H, sentence-order predictionOrder-of-magnitude fewer parameters at similar quality — but not faster at inference
DistilBERT2019Six layers distilled from a twelve-layer teacher~40% smaller and ~60% faster while retaining most of the accuracy
ELECTRA2020Replaced-token detection: a small generator corrupts, the discriminator labels every token real/fakeLoss on all positions, not 15% — dramatically more compute-efficient
DeBERTa2020Disentangled attention (separate content and relative-position vectors), enhanced mask decoderRelative positions generalise better; v3 swaps MLM for an ELECTRA-style objective
ModernBERT2024RoPE, GeGLU, alternating local/global attention, unpadding, 8k context, code in the corpusBrings a decade of decoder engineering back to encoders — long inputs at high throughput
  • Multilingual and domain variants are recipe changes, not architecture changes: mBERT shares one WordPiece vocabulary across ~100 languages, while domain models re-pretrain on scientific, biomedical or legal corpora and win mainly through vocabulary coverage.
  • The through-line: data and objective density beat architectural novelty. The two biggest jumps in the table (RoBERTa, ELECTRA) came from training better and from getting a gradient at every position.
Takeaway: if you are picking an encoder today, start from a modern one — the original checkpoint is a historical reference, not a strong baseline.

09 Compute, inference, and where encoders still win

The practical case for BERT in a world of large decoders is arithmetic, not nostalgia.

Cost model

  • Per layer, the FFN dominates parameters (8H² with a 4× expansion) and attention projections add 4H², giving roughly 12·L·H² non-embedding parameters — about 85M for base, plus ~23M of embeddings.
  • Cost is quadratic in sequence length through the attention matrix and linear through the FFN. Below a few hundred tokens the FFN dominates; past that the n² term takes over, which is exactly why 512 was a comfortable ceiling.
  • Crucially, classification is one forward pass over the whole input — no autoregressive loop, no KV cache, and the entire batch is parallel. A base-sized encoder classifies short texts in single-digit milliseconds on commodity hardware.
non-embedding parametersN ≈ 12 · L · H² base: 12·12·768² ≈ 85M large: 12·24·1024² ≈ 302M forward FLOPs per sequence (rough)C ≈ 2 · N · n + 4 · L · n² · H

Where a decoder LLM is the wrong tool

  • High-volume classification and routing. Spam, intent, safety filters, moderation — millions of calls a day where a 110M-parameter encoder is orders of magnitude cheaper than prompting a generative model for a one-word answer, and easier to calibrate.
  • Token-level structure. NER, span extraction and sequence labelling map naturally onto per-position outputs; asking a generative model for character offsets is a workaround, not a fit.
  • Retrieval and reranking. Bi-encoders and cross-encoders built on encoders remain the backbone of search stacks, where latency budgets are measured in milliseconds.
  • Deterministic, on-premise, small. A fine-tuned encoder is a fixed function you can version, quantise, and run on a CPU — with no sampling variance to defend.
Takeaway: when the output is a label or a span rather than prose, encoder-only is usually 100× cheaper and at least as accurate. See Encoder–Decoder Models for the full trade space.

10 Limitations

Four constraints are structural, not fixable by better training data.

  • Not generative. The denoising objective gives no coherent way to sample a sequence. You can fill single blanks, but free-form generation belongs to the decoder-only world.
  • Hard 512-token ceiling. Learned absolute positions simply do not exist past row 512, so long documents must be chunked with overlap and their predictions merged — losing exactly the cross-chunk context that motivated bidirectionality in the first place.
  • Pretrain/fine-tune mismatch. [MASK] never appears downstream, so ~1.5% of pretraining tokens are inputs the model will never see again. The 80/10/10 rule softens this; ELECTRA removes it entirely.
  • Fixed heads. Every new label set needs new supervised data and a new fine-tuning run. There is no zero-shot path, no instruction following, and no way to change the task at inference time.
  • Static knowledge and inherited bias. The corpus is frozen at pretraining time, and social biases present in it are measurable in the embeddings and transfer into every downstream head.
Takeaway: the 512 ceiling and the missing zero-shot path are the two limits that actually decide architecture choices in practice.

11 Pitfalls & what to remember

The failure modes below account for most of the "my BERT fine-tune is worse than logistic regression" reports.

Pitfalls

  • Silent truncation. Tokenizers drop everything past max_length without complaining. Long inputs quietly lose their tail — check the token-length distribution before blaming the model.
  • Learning rate too high. Above roughly 1e-4 the pretrained weights are overwritten in the first epoch — catastrophic forgetting dressed up as a bad dataset. Warm up, then decay.
  • Wrong tokenizer for the checkpoint. Cased versus uncased, and vocabulary mismatches between a checkpoint and its tokenizer, produce garbage that still runs.
  • Sub-word/label misalignment in token tasks. Words split into pieces; decide explicitly whether to label the first sub-word and ignore the rest, and apply the same rule at evaluation.
  • Raw [CLS] as a sentence embedding without fine-tuning or whitening — see section 07.
  • Unstable small-data runs. Variance across seeds on a few thousand examples is real; report a mean over restarts rather than a lucky maximum.

What to remember

  • BERT = bidirectional Transformer encoder, pretrained with MLM (+ originally NSP), fine-tuned with a small head.
  • Masking buys both-side context without the target leaking into its own input; 80/10/10 keeps every position honest.
  • Input = token + segment + position embeddings, summed; [CLS] is a slot that fine-tuning teaches to summarise.
  • Descendants won mainly by training longer (RoBERTa) or densifying the loss (ELECTRA), not by redesigning the block.
  • It is the understanding half of the family tree — GPT is the generating half, and the two are still complementary.
Takeaway: reach for an encoder when the answer is a label, a tag or a vector; reach for a decoder when the answer is text. Most production NLP stacks contain both.
