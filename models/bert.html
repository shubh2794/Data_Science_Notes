BERT

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers — the encoder-only model that made "pretrain once, fine-tune everywhere" the default in NLP in 2018–2019. It reads the whole sequence at once and returns one contextual vector per token; a small task head turns those vectors into a label, a tag per token, a span, or a score. This note covers it in depth: what came before it (ELMo, GPT-1), why bidirectional pretraining needs masking, the input format and WordPiece vocabulary, the Base and Large configurations with a parameter count recomputed from the released configuration, masked language modelling worked by hand, next-sentence prediction and why it was dropped, the pretraining data and 128 → 512 schedule, every fine-tuning head, what probing studies found inside it, the published results, its successors, how it compares with GPT and encoder–decoder models, sentence embeddings, practical use through the Transformers library, and the places where the paper and its released code disagree.

01 What BERT is

BERT is a stack of Transformer encoder blocks with no attention mask, pretrained on unlabelled text by reconstructing tokens that were hidden from it, then fine-tuned — every weight, plus one new output layer — on each downstream task. The paper describes its architecture as "almost identical" to the original Transformer encoder; the novelty is the objective and the transfer recipe.

the shape of the computationtoken ids (n) → embeddings (n × H) → L encoder blocks → T₁ … Tₙ (n × H) masked language modelling, over the corrupted positions Mℒ_MLM = − (1/|M|) ∑ᵢ∈M log P( xᵢ | x̃ ) original pretraining loss (Appendix A.2: sum of the two means)ℒ = ℒ_MLM + ℒ_NSP

Two phases, one backbone

  • Pretraining, done once: 1M steps over BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia (about 3.3 billion words), predicting masked tokens and whether two text spans are consecutive. The two pretraining heads are scaffolding.
  • Fine-tuning, done per task: start from the same pretrained weights, add a task layer (for classification a single K × H matrix), and train everything for 2–4 epochs. The paper states that every result in it can be replicated in at most an hour on a single Cloud TPU starting from the pretrained model.
  • BERT does not generate text and does not assign a probability to a sentence. It produces representations.

The headline results (as reported)

  • New state of the art on eleven tasks: GLUE score 80.5 (7.7 points over GPT-1's 72.8 on the official leaderboard), MultiNLI accuracy 86.7, SQuAD v1.1 test F1 93.2, SQuAD v2.0 test F1 83.1. These are examined in §23.
Takeaway: BERT is a deeply bidirectional Transformer encoder pretrained by filling in masked tokens, then fine-tuned end to end. The pretraining heads are thrown away; the useful artefact is the stack of contextual hidden states.

30 Limitations

Constraints that come from the design rather than from any particular dataset.

  • Not generative. The denoising objective gives no principled way to sample a sequence or score its probability. Single blanks can be filled; free-form generation belongs to decoder LLMs.
  • 512-token ceiling. The learned position table has 512 rows. Longer documents must be chunked with overlap and predictions merged, losing cross-chunk context; extending the table needs further training (or a newer encoder with relative or rotary positions — see Context-Length Extension).
  • Pretrain/fine-tune mismatch. 12% of pretraining input positions are [MASK], a token fine-tuning never shows. 80/10/10 reduces the effect; ELECTRA removes it.
  • Sparse training signal. Only ~15% of positions give a loss; the paper's Appendix C.1 shows MLM converging slightly more slowly than a left-to-right model per step, although it overtakes it in accuracy almost immediately.
  • Independence of masked predictions. Several masked tokens are predicted independently given the corrupted input, so dependencies among them (two halves of a name) are not modelled jointly — a point XLNet was designed around.
  • Fixed heads. Each new label set needs labelled data and a fine-tuning run; there is no instruction following. The main zero-shot route is indirect: fine-tune on NLI once, then pose each candidate label as a hypothesis (Textual Entailment).
  • Frozen knowledge and inherited bias. The corpus is fixed at pretraining time, and social biases in it are measurable in the representations and can transfer to downstream heads.
Takeaway: the 512 ceiling and the lack of a generative or zero-shot interface are the two limits that decide architecture choices in practice; the sparse loss and [MASK] mismatch are what successors fixed.

31 Pitfalls

Mistakes that recur when fine-tuning, evaluating or quoting BERT.

When fine-tuning and serving

  • Silent truncation. With truncation=True the tokenizer drops everything past max_length without warning. Check the token-length distribution — WordPiece produces more tokens than words — before blaming the model.
  • Learning rate too high. Rates well above the paper's 2e-5–5e-5 range can wipe out what pretraining learned (catastrophic forgetting) or make runs diverge. Use warm-up and linear decay.
  • Wrong tokenizer for the checkpoint. Cased and uncased vocabularies differ (28,996 vs 30,522 entries); a mismatched pair runs without error and produces nonsense ids.
  • Sub-word/label misalignment. For token tasks, label the first sub-word of each word and set the rest to -100 (the paper's choice), and apply the same rule at evaluation.
  • Left padding. BERT's positions are absolute; pad on the right.
  • Missing token_type_ids for pairs. Building [CLS] a [SEP] b [SEP] by hand without segment ids puts every token in segment A.
  • NSP label direction. In the library, 0 means "B follows A" and 1 means "random".

When evaluating and quoting

  • Single-seed results on small data. Variance across seeds on a few thousand examples is large (the paper itself used restarts); report a mean and spread.
  • Raw [CLS] or mean-pooled vectors as sentence embeddings without contrastive fine-tuning — they score below averaged GloVe on STS (§28).
  • Quoting "110M / 340M" as exact. The configurations give 109.5M and 335.1M (§08).
  • Mixing the two GLUE numbers. 80.5 is the official GLUE score; 82.1 is the paper's Table 1 average without WNLI.
  • Assuming attention FLOPs forced the 512 limit. At 512 tokens attention scores are about a tenth of the forward FLOPs (§15); the limit is the position table.
Takeaway: most BERT failures are bookkeeping — truncation, tokenizer mismatch, label alignment, padding side, a single lucky seed — rather than modelling problems.

34 What to remember

Ten statements, each backed by a section above.

  • BERT = bidirectional Transformer encoder, pretrained with MLM (+ NSP originally), fine-tuned end to end with a small head.
  • Masking makes bidirectional pretraining possible: without it the target leaks into its own prediction through the layers.
  • 15% chosen, 80/10/10: 12% of positions show [MASK]; only the chosen 15% carry the loss.
  • Input = token + segment + position, summed; [CLS] first, [SEP] after each segment; 512 learned positions.
  • Base 109.5M, Large 335.1M from the configuration — slightly under the paper's 110M / 340M.
  • NSP was a weak objective: easy random negatives; RoBERTa dropped it without loss, ALBERT replaced it with order prediction.
  • Bidirectionality was the big effect in the paper's own ablation; model size helped every task.
  • Heads are tiny: a K × H matrix for classification, two vectors for span QA; the pooler adds a tanh layer the paper does not mention.
  • Successors won by training more and getting denser losses (RoBERTa, ELECTRA), then by modernising attention and context (DeBERTa, ModernBERT).
  • Raw BERT vectors are poor sentence embeddings; bi-encoders trained contrastively (Sentence-BERT onward) are what retrieval uses.
Takeaway: reach for an encoder when the answer is a label, a tag, a span or a vector; reach for a decoder when the answer is text. BERT is the model that established the first half of that rule.
