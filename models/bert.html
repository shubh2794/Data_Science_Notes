BERT

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers — the encoder-only model that made "pretrain once, fine-tune everywhere" the default in NLP in 2018–2019. It reads the whole sequence at once and returns one contextual vector per token; a small task head turns those vectors into a label, a tag per token, a span, or a score. This note covers it in depth: what came before it (ELMo, GPT-1), why bidirectional pretraining needs masking, the input format and WordPiece vocabulary, the Base and Large configurations with a parameter count recomputed from the released configuration, masked language modelling worked by hand, next-sentence prediction and why it was dropped, the pretraining data and 128 → 512 schedule, every fine-tuning head, what probing studies found inside it, the published results, its successors, how it compares with GPT and encoder–decoder models, sentence embeddings, practical use through the Transformers library, and the places where the paper and its released code disagree.

Model Architectures Encoder-only Masked LM Pretraining Fine-tuning

01 What BERT is

BERT is a stack of Transformer encoder blocks with no attention mask, pretrained on unlabelled text by reconstructing tokens that were hidden from it, then fine-tuned — every weight, plus one new output layer — on each downstream task. The paper describes its architecture as "almost identical" to the original Transformer encoder; the novelty is the objective and the transfer recipe.

the shape of the computationtoken ids (n) → embeddings (n × H) → L encoder blocks → T₁ … Tₙ (n × H) masked language modelling, over the corrupted positions Mℒ_MLM = − (1/|M|) ∑ᵢ∈M log P( xᵢ | x̃ ) original pretraining loss (Appendix A.2: sum of the two means)ℒ = ℒ_MLM + ℒ_NSP

Two phases, one backbone

  • Pretraining, done once: 1M steps over BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia (about 3.3 billion words), predicting masked tokens and whether two text spans are consecutive. The two pretraining heads are scaffolding.
  • Fine-tuning, done per task: start from the same pretrained weights, add a task layer (for classification a single K × H matrix), and train everything for 2–4 epochs. The paper states that every result in it can be replicated in at most an hour on a single Cloud TPU starting from the pretrained model.
  • BERT does not generate text and does not assign a probability to a sentence. It produces representations.

The headline results (as reported)

  • New state of the art on eleven tasks: GLUE score 80.5 (7.7 points over GPT-1's 72.8 on the official leaderboard), MultiNLI accuracy 86.7, SQuAD v1.1 test F1 93.2, SQuAD v2.0 test F1 83.1. These are examined in §23.
Takeaway: BERT is a deeply bidirectional Transformer encoder pretrained by filling in masked tokens, then fine-tuned end to end. The pretraining heads are thrown away; the useful artefact is the stack of contextual hidden states.

02 Before BERT: ELMo and GPT-1

The BERT paper positions itself against two 2018 models, and its Appendix A.4 lists the differences explicitly. Both predecessors pretrain a unidirectional language model; they differ in how the result is used.

Which input tokens can a position's representation depend on? click a token · switch model · counts are computed from each model's connectivity

ELMo's forward LSTM sees positions 1…i and its backward LSTM sees i…n; the two streams meet only when their top-layer states are concatenated for the downstream model. GPT-1 sees positions 1…i in every layer. BERT sees all n positions in every layer, which the paper calls "jointly conditioned on both left and right context in all layers".

ELMo: feature-based, shallowly bidirectional

  • Two-layer biLSTM language model (4096 units, 512-dimensional projections) over a character-CNN input; a forward and a backward LM are trained, sharing only the input and softmax layers.
  • Used as features: a task-specific, softmax-weighted sum of the layers (scaled by a learned γ) is fed into an existing task architecture, which is still designed and trained per task.
  • BERT's criticism: each token's representation is a concatenation of a left-only and a right-only state — "shallow" bidirectionality.

GPT-1: fine-tuning, unidirectional

  • A 12-layer, 768-wide Transformer decoder trained left to right on BooksCorpus, then fine-tuned end to end with a linear head — the same transfer recipe BERT uses. BERT-Base was deliberately sized to match it.
  • Appendix A.4 lists the other differences the authors could not remove: GPT trained on BooksCorpus (800M words) only, BERT on BooksCorpus plus Wikipedia (2,500M words); GPT introduced [SEP] and [CLS] only at fine-tuning, while BERT learns them (and the A/B segment embeddings) during pretraining; GPT used batches of 32,000 words, BERT 128,000 words (both for 1M steps); BERT's Appendix A.4 says GPT used one fine-tuning learning rate, 5e-5, for all tasks (the GPT-1 paper itself reports 6.25e-5 "for most tasks"), while BERT picks one per task on the dev set.
  • Because of those confounds, the paper's claim that bidirectionality causes the gain rests on its own ablation (§13), which trains a left-to-right model on BERT's data and recipe.
Takeaway: ELMo gave bidirectional features; GPT-1 gave a fine-tunable Transformer but only left context. BERT's pitch was to have both at once — and that required a new objective, because a bidirectional model cannot be trained as an ordinary language model.

03 Why bidirectional, and why masking is required

A standard language model predicts each token from the tokens before it. If every position may attend to every other position, the token being predicted is already in the input, and in a multi-layer stack it can reach its own prediction indirectly — the paper's phrase is that each word could "see itself".

Counting the paths by which a target token reaches its own prediction change sequence length and depth · toggle the attention pattern and whether the target is masked

Nodes are positions at each layer; an edge means "may attend to". The count of paths from the target's input to the output that must predict it is computed by dynamic programming over the edges. Under full attention with the target visible there are exactly n^(L−1) routes for L layers, multiplying with depth; masking the input removes the information, not the edges, so the count of paths carrying the answer drops to zero. Under causal attention the prediction for position i is made from position i−1, whose inputs never include position i.

Three ways out

  • Two unidirectional models (ELMo): valid, but each direction never sees the other inside a layer. The paper calls this "strictly less powerful than a deep bidirectional model" and notes it is twice as expensive and awkward for QA, where a right-to-left model could not condition the answer on the question.
  • Causal masking (GPT): valid and dense — every position is a target — but only left context.
  • Hide the answer (BERT): corrupt a subset of input positions and predict only those. The answer is absent from the input, so full attention is safe. The price is that only the corrupted ~15% of positions give a loss.
Takeaway: masking is what makes bidirectional pretraining well-posed. It turns BERT into a denoising model rather than a language model: it cannot score a sentence's probability or sample text left to right, and it gets a training signal from a fraction of positions.

The special tokens

  • [CLS] is always first. Its final hidden state, written C ∈ ℝᴴ, is the "aggregate sequence representation" used for classification. The paper warns in a footnote that C "is not a meaningful sentence representation without fine-tuning, since it was trained with NSP".
  • [SEP] ends each segment. A pair is [CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]; a single text is [CLS] A [SEP]. [PAD] fills a batch and is excluded by the attention mask; [MASK] exists only for pretraining; [UNK] replaces any word that cannot be built from vocabulary pieces.
  • A "sentence" in the paper is "an arbitrary span of contiguous text, rather than an actual linguistic sentence"; in pretraining the two segments are typically much longer than single sentences.

Segments and positions

  • The segment table has two rows (type_vocab_size = 2): row 0 for A, row 1 for B. That is how one encoder handles pairs without a second stack; in the Transformers library it is the token_type_ids input.
  • The position table is learned, 512 × H. There is no row 512, so 512 tokens is a hard ceiling for the released checkpoints (Transformers — learned positions). The paper trains rows beyond 128 only in the last 10% of steps (§14).
Takeaway: summing keeps the width at H; "which token", "which segment" and "where" are blended into one vector from layer one. The segment embedding and learned [CLS]/[SEP] are part of what BERT pretrains that GPT-1 did not.

05 WordPiece and the 30,522-entry vocabulary

BERT's tokeniser runs in two stages: a "basic" pre-tokeniser (optional lower-casing and accent stripping, splitting on whitespace and punctuation, and isolating CJK characters), then WordPiece, which splits each word greedily into the longest prefixes found in the vocabulary. Non-initial pieces carry a ## prefix.

The toy vocabulary is listed in the figure; the algorithm is the one in the released tokenizer: start at the word's first character, take the longest vocabulary entry that matches (with ## prepended when not at the start), advance, repeat. If some remainder has no match at all, the whole word becomes [UNK] — not just the failing piece. "unaffable → un ##aff ##able" is the example in the released code's docstring.

Why post-LN mattered little here

  • At 12 and 24 layers, post-LN trains stably with the 10,000-step learning-rate warm-up BERT used. The general argument about post-LN needing warm-up at depth is in Transformers — pre-norm against post-norm.
  • Later encoders changed this block (ModernBERT: pre-norm, GeGLU, rotary positions, no biases in most layers — §26), which is one reason weights cannot be moved between the two families.
Takeaway: nothing in the BERT block is new. Post-LN, GELU, learned absolute positions, a 4× FFN and a tied output layer were all conventional by late 2018; the "B" in BERT is entirely the absence of the causal mask.

09 Masked language modelling: 15% and 80 / 10 / 10

The data generator picks 15% of the WordPiece positions for prediction. Each chosen position is replaced by [MASK] 80% of the time, by a random token 10% of the time, and left unchanged 10% of the time. The model must recover the original token at every chosen position, whatever it now shows.

Why the 10% random and 10% unchanged

  • Pretrain/fine-tune mismatch. [MASK] never appears downstream. If every chosen position showed [MASK], the model could learn "only the [MASK] slots need a good representation". The paper: the encoder "does not know which words it will be asked to predict or which have been replaced by random words, so it is forced to keep a distributional contextual representation of every input token".
  • Unchanged tokens "bias the representation towards the actual observed word". Random tokens teach the model not to trust the surface token completely; at 1.5% of all tokens the paper judges the noise harmless.
  • Unlike a denoising autoencoder, only the chosen positions are predicted, not the whole input.
Takeaway: 15% of positions carry the loss; of those, 80% show [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% the truth. That makes 12% of all input positions [MASK] — the part of the input distribution fine-tuning never sees.

12 Next Sentence Prediction, and why it was dropped

The second pretraining task is binary: given segments A and B, is B the text that actually followed A (IsNext, 50%) or a random span from the corpus (NotNext, 50%)? The final hidden state C of [CLS], through the pooler, feeds a two-way softmax. The paper reports that the final model reaches 97–98% NSP accuracy.

NSP loss (C = final [CLS] state)ℒ_NSP = − log softmax( W_nsp · tanh( W_p·C + b_p ) )[y] y ∈ {IsNext, NotNext} sentence-order prediction (ALBERT): same two segments, order swapped for negativespositive: (A, B) negative: (B, A)
How far does word overlap alone get on NSP versus SOP? a no-learning baseline: predict "positive" when the two segments share enough words · slide the threshold

A toy corpus of four short documents on different topics. Every consecutive sentence pair is a positive; NSP negatives pair a sentence with every sentence of the other documents; SOP negatives are the positives reversed. The score is the Jaccard overlap of content words (stop-words removed), computed for every pair. Because overlap is symmetric, a swapped pair scores exactly what the original did, so no threshold can beat 50% on SOP — the feature carries no order information at all.

What went wrong with NSP

  • The negatives are too easy. A random segment usually comes from another document, so topic mismatch — which MLM already learns — nearly solves the task. ALBERT's authors put it as NSP conflating "topic prediction" with "coherence prediction", the easier of which dominates.
  • It shortens the context. Packing two segments whose combined length is at most 512, half of them unrelated, means the model often reads two half-length unrelated spans instead of one long coherent one.
  • ALBERT reports that a model trained with NSP solves SOP only at chance level, while a model trained with SOP solves NSP "to a reasonable degree".
Takeaway: NSP is a lesson about negative sampling. An auxiliary objective helps only if its negatives cannot be separated by a feature the model already has — sentence-order prediction keeps the topic fixed so that only coherence remains.

Reading the two tables together

  • Bidirectionality is the large effect. In BERT's Table 5, "LTR & No NSP" falls 9.0 points on MRPC and 10.1 F1 on SQuAD relative to "No NSP". Adding a BiLSTM recovers SQuAD partly (77.8 → 84.9) but not GLUE.
  • NSP's effect is small and depends on the input format. BERT's "No NSP" kept the two-segment input and removed only the loss; RoBERTa suggests this is why it hurt: "It is possible that the original BERT implementation may only have removed the loss term while still retaining the SEGMENT-PAIR input format." Filling the window with contiguous text and dropping NSP "matches or slightly improves downstream task performance".
  • Sentence-pair inputs (much shorter than 512) did worst in RoBERTa's table even with NSP, and even with a larger batch to equalise tokens — evidence that long contiguous context, not the pair objective, is what mattered.
Takeaway: the left-to-right versus bidirectional gap is several points; the NSP versus no-NSP gap is a fraction of a point and flips sign once the input is long contiguous text. Later encoders (RoBERTa, ELECTRA, DeBERTa, ModernBERT) all train without NSP.

14 Pretraining data, schedule and the 128 → 512 switch

BooksCorpus (800M words) plus English Wikipedia (2,500M words, text passages only — lists, tables and headers removed). The paper stresses using a document-level corpus rather than a sentence-shuffled one such as the Billion Word Benchmark, "in order to extract long contiguous sequences".

The pretraining schedule, and how many tokens it really processes slide the fraction of steps run at length 128 · the learning-rate curve uses the paper's warm-up and linear decay

Tokens are counted as batch × sequence length × steps, treating every sequence as full length, so they are an upper bound: short_seq_prob and segment packing leave some padding. "Epochs" divides tokens by the 3.3B words of the corpus; WordPiece produces somewhat more tokens than words, so the true number of passes is lower still.

The published hyperparameters (Appendix A.2)

  • Batch 256 sequences; 1,000,000 steps; Adam with learning rate 1e-4, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, L2 weight decay 0.01; warm-up over the first 10,000 steps then linear decay; dropout 0.1 everywhere; GELU.
  • Sequence length 128 for 90% of the steps, then 512 for the remaining 10% "to learn the positional embeddings" — because "attention is quadratic to the sequence length".
  • Hardware: BERT-Base on 4 Cloud TPUs in pod configuration (16 TPU chips); BERT-Large on 16 Cloud TPUs (64 chips); "each pretraining took 4 days to complete".

An internal inconsistency in the paper

  • The paper describes the batch as "256 sequences * 512 tokens = 128,000 tokens/batch" (256 × 512 is 131,072) and the whole run as "approximately 40 epochs over the 3.3 billion word corpus" — which is what 1M full-length steps give (131B tokens / 3.3B ≈ 39.7).
  • But the same appendix says 90% of steps used length 128. With that schedule, the run processes about 29.5B + 13.1B = 42.6B tokens — roughly 13 passes, not 40. The "128,000 words per batch" in Appendix A.4 (the GPT comparison) inherits the same full-length assumption. The paper does not reconcile the two; the figure computes both.
Takeaway: 3.3B words, 1M steps of 256 sequences, lr 1e-4 with 10k warm-up, and mostly 128-token sequences. RoBERTa later showed this was far from saturated: ten times the data and larger batches for longer kept improving results (§26).

15 Compute: FLOPs per sequence, and where encoders still win

An encoder pass is one parallel forward over the whole input: no autoregressive loop and no KV cache. Its cost has a part linear in length (the weight matrices) and a part quadratic in length (the attention scores).

Counts matrix multiplies only (projections, FFN, QKᵀ and attention-times-V); softmax, LayerNorm and the embedding lookup are ignored. FLOPs are not latency: at long lengths the n × n score matrix also costs memory traffic, which is what FlashAttention-style kernels address (Attention — cost). Lengths beyond 512 are impossible for the released checkpoints and are drawn only to locate the crossover.

Where a fine-tuned encoder is still the right tool

  • High-volume classification and routing — intent, moderation, spam, safety filters — where a ~110M-parameter model with one forward pass costs orders of magnitude fewer FLOPs per call than generating even a short answer with a multi-billion-parameter decoder.
  • Token-level outputs — NER, span extraction, tagging — which map directly to per-position heads (§17).
  • Retrieval and reranking — bi-encoders for first-stage search and cross-encoders for reranking (§28).
  • Whether an encoder also matches a large prompted decoder in accuracy depends on the task and the amount of labelled data; measure both on the target data.
Takeaway: at BERT's 512-token limit, attention scores are only about 10% (Base) of the forward FLOPs — the weight matrices dominate. The crossover is at n = 6H, thousands of tokens. The 512 ceiling comes from the learned position table and from attention memory, not from attention FLOPs.

16 [CLS], the pooler, and what "C" actually is

The paper defines C as "the final hidden vector corresponding to the first input token ([CLS])" and computes classification as log(softmax(C·Wᵀ)). The released code adds a layer the paper does not mention.

paper (§4.1)logits = W · C W ∈ ℝ^(K × H) released code and the Transformers librarypooled = tanh( W_p · T_[CLS] + b_p ) logits = W · Dropout(pooled) + b
  • The pooler is an H × H dense layer with tanh, trained during pretraining by the NSP objective. BertModel returns it as pooler_output; the library documents it as "the classification token after processing through a linear layer and a tanh activation function … trained from the next sentence prediction (classification) objective during pretraining".
  • BertForSequenceClassification and BertForMultipleChoice classify from pooler_output; token classification and question answering ignore it and use the per-token states.
  • Models pretrained without NSP have no trained pooler: RoBERTa's classification head applies its own dense + tanh layer on the first token, initialised from scratch during fine-tuning.
  • Practical consequence: last_hidden_state[:, 0] and pooler_output are different vectors. Neither is a good sentence embedding without further training (§28).
Takeaway: in the released model, classification reads tanh(W_p·T_[CLS]), not the raw C of the paper. The difference is one pretrained H × H layer — 590,592 parameters in Base.

18 Span question answering: start, end, and the no-answer threshold

For SQuAD the paper adds only a start vector S and an end vector E. Each passage token gets a start score S·Tᵢ and an end score E·Tⱼ; the best span maximises their sum subject to j ≥ i. For SQuAD 2.0, "no answer" is a span that starts and ends at [CLS].

The start and end scores are invented for illustration (a trained model produces them as S·Tᵢ and E·Tⱼ); every probability, the best span, and the decision are computed from them. The maximum-length limit is a decoding heuristic used by the released SQuAD script (a flag, default 30 tokens), not part of the paper's formula.

  • Start and end are predicted independently; the constraint j ≥ i is applied only at decoding. The most probable start and the most probable end can form an invalid span, which is why decoding searches pairs.
  • Passages longer than the window are split into overlapping chunks (the released script uses a stride, doc_stride, default 128) and the best-scoring span across chunks wins.
  • Recipes: SQuAD v1.1 — 3 epochs, learning rate 5e-5, batch 32 (the best system first fine-tuned on TriviaQA); SQuAD v2.0 — 2 epochs, 5e-5, batch 48, no TriviaQA.
Takeaway: span QA is two token-classification heads plus a search over valid pairs. Unanswerable questions reuse [CLS] as a null span, and a dev-tuned margin τ decides between the best span and "no answer".

Instability on small datasets

  • The paper: "for BERT-Large we found that fine-tuning was sometimes unstable on small datasets, so we ran several random restarts and selected the best model on the Dev set". Restarts changed only data shuffling and classifier initialisation.
  • Later analyses located causes. "On the Stability of Fine-tuning BERT" (arXiv 2006.04884) attributes failed runs mainly to optimisation difficulty early in training (vanishing gradients) and recommends small learning rates with bias correction and more iterations. "Revisiting Few-sample BERT Fine-tuning" (arXiv 2006.05987) points to the released optimizer omitting Adam's bias correction (§32), and finds that re-initialising the top layers and training longer both help.
  • Large datasets (100k+ examples) were "far less sensitive to hyperparameter choice", per the paper.

Alternatives to full fine-tuning

  • Feature-based use keeps BERT frozen (§20). Adapters were first demonstrated on BERT ("Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning for NLP", arXiv 1902.00751), and low-rank updates generalise the idea — see PEFT and discriminative learning rates.
Takeaway: small learning rate (2e-5 to 5e-5), warm-up, 2–4 epochs, and several seeds on small data. Report a mean over seeds, not the best run.

21 What BERT learns: attention patterns and probing

A 2019 wave of analysis papers ("BERTology") asked what the pretrained model encodes without any task supervision. Two results are the most cited: individual attention heads align with specific syntactic relations, and different kinds of linguistic information peak at different depths.

Single attention heads as dependency parsers (BERT-Base) sort or filter · each bar is the best single head's accuracy against a fixed-offset baseline · gains are computed

From "What Does BERT Look At?" (arXiv 1906.04341), Table 1, evaluated on the Wall Street Journal dependency annotations. For each relation, the accuracy is how often the dependent's most-attended word is its head, for the single best head (layer-head in the label); the baseline always predicts a fixed relative position (offset in parentheses). "All" asks one head to handle every relation, which no head does well.

Attention patterns

  • Heads fall into recurring patterns: attend broadly, attend to the next or previous token, attend to [SEP], attend to punctuation. Over half of BERT-Base's attention in layers 6–10 goes to [SEP] — against about 1/64 expected for a token appearing twice in 128 — which the authors interpret as a "no-op" when a head's pattern does not apply.
  • Specific heads track syntax: direct objects attend to their verbs (86.8%), determiners to their nouns (94.3%), and one head (5-4) links coreferent mentions with 65% antecedent accuracy, close to a rule-based system (69%).
  • Caution: attention weights are not explanations by themselves; a head can attend to a token whose value vector contributes little. Probing results show what is decodable, not what the model uses.

Layer-wise probing

  • "BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline" (arXiv 1905.05950) trains small probes on mixtures of BERT-Large's layers and finds the order in which information peaks follows the traditional pipeline: "POS tagging, parsing, NER, semantic roles, then coreference" — syntax lower, semantics higher — with the model able to revise lower-level decisions using higher-level context.
  • This matches the feature-based NER result (§20): useful features concentrate in upper-middle layers, and the top layer is shaped by the pretraining objective. General interpretability tools are covered on Transformers — reading the residual stream.
Takeaway: MLM pretraining alone produces heads that approximate specific syntactic relations and layers that recapitulate the NLP pipeline. These are measurements of what can be read out of BERT, not proof of how it computes its answers.

25 ELECTRA: a loss on every token

MLM gets a gradient from ~15% of positions. ELECTRA keeps a small MLM model as a generator and trains the main model — the discriminator — to say, for every position, whether the token is original or was replaced by a generator sample.

generator (MLM) and discriminator (per-token binary)x̂ᵢ ~ p_G( xᵢ | x_masked ) for i ∈ m D(x, t) = sigmoid( wᵀ h_D(x)ₜ ) combined objective (λ = 50 in the paper)min ∑ₓ ℒ_MLM(x, θ_G) + λ · ℒ_Disc(x, θ_D)
  • If the generator happens to sample the original token, that position is labelled "original" — so the discriminator is never asked to call a correct token fake.
  • The generator works best at 1/4 to 1/2 the discriminator's size; a generator that is too strong makes the task too hard. The generator is discarded after pretraining; only the discriminator is fine-tuned.
  • ELECTRA's own ablation separates the two benefits: "ELECTRA 15%" (discriminator loss only on the masked 15%) does much worse than full ELECTRA, so most of the gain comes from learning from all positions; "Replace MLM" (no [MASK] in the input) slightly beats BERT, so removing the mismatch helps a little.
  • Efficiency: ELECTRA-Small (14M parameters) trained 4 days on one GPU scores 79.9 GLUE dev, above GPT-1 (78.8), which used about 30× more training compute.
Takeaway: ELECTRA fixes MLM's two weaknesses at once — sparse loss and the [MASK] mismatch — and most of the gain comes from the dense loss. It is the strongest argument that BERT's objective, not its architecture, was the bottleneck.

26 RoBERTa, ALBERT, DistilBERT, DeBERTa and ModernBERT in more detail

What each successor measured, with the numbers that justify its design.

RoBERTa — the same model, trained properly

  • Data: BooksCorpus + Wikipedia (16GB), plus CC-News, OpenWebText and Stories, 160GB in total. Its Table 4 accumulates changes on the Large architecture: 16GB at 100K steps with 8K batches → +data → 300K steps → 500K steps, with SQuAD v1.1/2.0, MNLI and SST-2 rising at each step.
  • Byte-level BPE with a 50K vocabulary (as in GPT-2) instead of WordPiece, so there is no [UNK]; peak learning rate and warm-up retuned for the large batch; Adam β₂ = 0.98 for stability.

ALBERT — fewer parameters, not fewer FLOPs

  • Factorised embedding: the vocabulary is embedded into E = 128 dimensions and projected to H. For V = 30,000 and H = 4,096 (xxlarge) that is 30,000·128 + 128·4,096 ≈ 4.4M instead of 30,000·4,096 ≈ 123M parameters.
  • Cross-layer sharing: all layers use the same weights, so parameters stop growing with depth — but each layer still costs the same compute. ALBERT-xxlarge (235M parameters, H = 4,096) is listed as 0.3× BERT-large's training speed.

DistilBERT — compression by distillation

  • Student initialised from every other layer of BERT-Base; trained with a soft-target distillation loss, the MLM loss and a cosine loss aligning hidden states. The resulting 66M-parameter model is 71% faster than BERT-Base on a phone for QA (excluding tokenisation).

DeBERTa — separate content and position

  • Each token has a content vector and uses relative-position embeddings; attention scores sum content-to-content, content-to-position and position-to-content terms. Absolute positions enter only in the "enhanced mask decoder" just before the MLM output.
  • A DeBERTa-Large trained on half of RoBERTa-Large's data beat it on MNLI, SQuAD v2.0 and RACE (per its abstract).

ModernBERT — a 2024 encoder with decoder-era engineering

  • 22 layers (Base, 149M) and 28 layers (Large, 395M); every third layer global attention (RoPE θ = 160,000), the rest a 128-token sliding window (θ = 10,000); vocabulary 50,368 (a multiple of 64); trained on 1.7T tokens at length 1,024, then extended to 8,192 over another 300B tokens.
  • Unpadding removes padding tokens and processes a batch as one concatenated sequence with variable-length attention kernels; together with local attention this is what makes 8k inputs affordable.
Takeaway: RoBERTa is "BERT without NSP, trained ten times harder"; ALBERT trades parameters for compute; DistilBERT compresses; ELECTRA and DeBERTa change the objective and the attention; ModernBERT imports the long-context machinery of modern decoders.

Why raw BERT vectors disappoint

  • Nothing in MLM asks any single vector to summarise a sentence, and [CLS] was trained only by NSP; the paper itself says C is not a meaningful sentence representation without fine-tuning.
  • Contextual embeddings are anisotropic — they occupy a narrow cone, so unrelated sentences already have high cosine similarity ("How Contextual are Contextualized Word Representations?", arXiv 1909.00512). Post-hoc whitening (centre and decorrelate) partly corrects this without training.

What Sentence-BERT does

  • One BERT encodes each sentence separately (shared weights); vectors are mean-pooled (its default, which beat [CLS] pooling). For NLI training a classifier sees (u, v, |u − v|); at inference only cosine similarity of u and v is used.
  • Cost: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences takes about 50 million cross-encoder passes (~65 hours with BERT, per the paper) versus about 5 seconds to embed each sentence once with SBERT and compare vectors.
  • Modern retrievers train the same bi-encoder with in-batch contrastive losses (Contrastive Learning — the InfoNCE loss) and store the vectors in an approximate-nearest-neighbour index (Vector Databases). A cross-encoder BERT then reranks the top candidates. BERT embeddings also underlie the BERTScore metric (NLP Evaluation Metrics).

30 Limitations

Constraints that come from the design rather than from any particular dataset.

  • Not generative. The denoising objective gives no principled way to sample a sequence or score its probability. Single blanks can be filled; free-form generation belongs to decoder LLMs.
  • 512-token ceiling. The learned position table has 512 rows. Longer documents must be chunked with overlap and predictions merged, losing cross-chunk context; extending the table needs further training (or a newer encoder with relative or rotary positions — see Context-Length Extension).
  • Pretrain/fine-tune mismatch. 12% of pretraining input positions are [MASK], a token fine-tuning never shows. 80/10/10 reduces the effect; ELECTRA removes it.
  • Sparse training signal. Only ~15% of positions give a loss; the paper's Appendix C.1 shows MLM converging slightly more slowly than a left-to-right model per step, although it overtakes it in accuracy almost immediately.
  • Independence of masked predictions. Several masked tokens are predicted independently given the corrupted input, so dependencies among them (two halves of a name) are not modelled jointly — a point XLNet was designed around.
  • Fixed heads. Each new label set needs labelled data and a fine-tuning run; there is no instruction following. The main zero-shot route is indirect: fine-tune on NLI once, then pose each candidate label as a hypothesis (Textual Entailment).
  • Frozen knowledge and inherited bias. The corpus is fixed at pretraining time, and social biases in it are measurable in the representations and can transfer to downstream heads.
Takeaway: the 512 ceiling and the lack of a generative or zero-shot interface are the two limits that decide architecture choices in practice; the sparse loss and [MASK] mismatch are what successors fixed.

31 Pitfalls

Mistakes that recur when fine-tuning, evaluating or quoting BERT.

When fine-tuning and serving

  • Silent truncation. With truncation=True the tokenizer drops everything past max_length without warning. Check the token-length distribution — WordPiece produces more tokens than words — before blaming the model.
  • Learning rate too high. Rates well above the paper's 2e-5–5e-5 range can wipe out what pretraining learned (catastrophic forgetting) or make runs diverge. Use warm-up and linear decay.
  • Wrong tokenizer for the checkpoint. Cased and uncased vocabularies differ (28,996 vs 30,522 entries); a mismatched pair runs without error and produces nonsense ids.
  • Sub-word/label misalignment. For token tasks, label the first sub-word of each word and set the rest to -100 (the paper's choice), and apply the same rule at evaluation.
  • Left padding. BERT's positions are absolute; pad on the right.
  • Missing token_type_ids for pairs. Building [CLS] a [SEP] b [SEP] by hand without segment ids puts every token in segment A.
  • NSP label direction. In the library, 0 means "B follows A" and 1 means "random".

When evaluating and quoting

  • Single-seed results on small data. Variance across seeds on a few thousand examples is large (the paper itself used restarts); report a mean and spread.
  • Raw [CLS] or mean-pooled vectors as sentence embeddings without contrastive fine-tuning — they score below averaged GloVe on STS (§28).
  • Quoting "110M / 340M" as exact. The configurations give 109.5M and 335.1M (§08).
  • Mixing the two GLUE numbers. 80.5 is the official GLUE score; 82.1 is the paper's Table 1 average without WNLI.
  • Assuming attention FLOPs forced the 512 limit. At 512 tokens attention scores are about a tenth of the forward FLOPs (§15); the limit is the position table.
Takeaway: most BERT failures are bookkeeping — truncation, tokenizer mismatch, label alignment, padding side, a single lucky seed — rather than modelling problems.

34 What to remember

Ten statements, each backed by a section above.

  • BERT = bidirectional Transformer encoder, pretrained with MLM (+ NSP originally), fine-tuned end to end with a small head.
  • Masking makes bidirectional pretraining possible: without it the target leaks into its own prediction through the layers.
  • 15% chosen, 80/10/10: 12% of positions show [MASK]; only the chosen 15% carry the loss.
  • Input = token + segment + position, summed; [CLS] first, [SEP] after each segment; 512 learned positions.
  • Base 109.5M, Large 335.1M from the configuration — slightly under the paper's 110M / 340M.
  • NSP was a weak objective: easy random negatives; RoBERTa dropped it without loss, ALBERT replaced it with order prediction.
  • Bidirectionality was the big effect in the paper's own ablation; model size helped every task.
  • Heads are tiny: a K × H matrix for classification, two vectors for span QA; the pooler adds a tanh layer the paper does not mention.
  • Successors won by training more and getting denser losses (RoBERTa, ELECTRA), then by modernising attention and context (DeBERTa, ModernBERT).
  • Raw BERT vectors are poor sentence embeddings; bi-encoders trained contrastively (Sentence-BERT onward) are what retrieval uses.
Takeaway: reach for an encoder when the answer is a label, a tag, a span or a vector; reach for a decoder when the answer is text. BERT is the model that established the first half of that rule.
