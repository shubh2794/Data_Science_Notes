09 Masked language modelling: 15% and 80 / 10 / 10

The data generator picks 15% of the WordPiece positions for prediction. Each chosen position is replaced by [MASK] 80% of the time, by a random token 10% of the time, and left unchanged 10% of the time. The model must recover the original token at every chosen position, whatever it now shows.

Why the 10% random and 10% unchanged

  • Pretrain/fine-tune mismatch. [MASK] never appears downstream. If every chosen position showed [MASK], the model could learn "only the [MASK] slots need a good representation". The paper: the encoder "does not know which words it will be asked to predict or which have been replaced by random words, so it is forced to keep a distributional contextual representation of every input token".
  • Unchanged tokens "bias the representation towards the actual observed word". Random tokens teach the model not to trust the surface token completely; at 1.5% of all tokens the paper judges the noise harmless.
  • Unlike a denoising autoencoder, only the chosen positions are predicted, not the whole input.
Takeaway: 15% of positions carry the loss; of those, 80% show [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% the truth. That makes 12% of all input positions [MASK] — the part of the input distribution fine-tuning never sees.

10 MLM worked by hand, and the masking-strategy ablation

The loss is ordinary cross-entropy averaged over the chosen positions only. Four numbers are enough to see what it rewards.

A hand-worked batch

Take the paper's sentence "my dog is hairy" in a sequence where three positions were chosen: hairy was replaced by [MASK], dog by the random token apple, and is was left unchanged. Suppose the model assigns the true tokens these probabilities:

  • The unchanged position is cheap to get right (the answer is in the input), which is why it is only 10% of the chosen set: if it were most of it, copying would dominate.
  • The paper's Table 6 reports MLM perplexity on held-out training data: 3.99 for BERT-Base and 3.23 for BERT-Large, i.e. mean losses of ln 3.99 = 1.38 and ln 3.23 = 1.17 nats per predicted token. A uniform guess over 30,522 entries would be ln 30,522 ≈ 10.3.

Takeaway: MLM is cross-entropy on the chosen subset. The corruption mix barely moves fine-tuned results but visibly matters when the representations are used frozen — which is exactly where the pretrain/fine-tune mismatch cannot be repaired.

11 Masking as the released code actually does it

The paper's two sentences on masking hide several implementation choices in the released data generator. They matter when reproducing BERT, and several were changed by later work.

ChoiceReleased code (create_pretraining_data.py)Consequence

  • Dynamic masking (RoBERTa) regenerates the mask every time a sequence is fed. RoBERTa found it "comparable or slightly better" than static masking at BERT's scale and adopted it because it "becomes crucial when pretraining for more steps or with larger datasets".
  • Span masking (SpanBERT, arXiv 1907.10529) masks contiguous spans and predicts them from the span boundaries; higher masking rates were later argued to work better for larger encoders (ModernBERT uses 30%, citing "Should You Mask 15% in Masked Language Modeling?", arXiv 2202.08005).
Takeaway: "15% at random" is really "round(0.15·n), capped at 20, chosen once per duplicate, per word piece". Each of those details was later revised — dynamic masks, whole words or spans, and higher rates.

12 Next Sentence Prediction, and why it was dropped

The second pretraining task is binary: given segments A and B, is B the text that actually followed A (IsNext, 50%) or a random span from the corpus (NotNext, 50%)? The final hidden state C of [CLS], through the pooler, feeds a two-way softmax. The paper reports that the final model reaches 97–98% NSP accuracy.

NSP loss (C = final [CLS] state)ℒ_NSP = − log softmax( W_nsp · tanh( W_p·C + b_p ) )[y] y ∈ {IsNext, NotNext} sentence-order prediction (ALBERT): same two segments, order swapped for negativespositive: (A, B) negative: (B, A)
How far does word overlap alone get on NSP versus SOP? a no-learning baseline: predict "positive" when the two segments share enough words · slide the threshold

A toy corpus of four short documents on different topics. Every consecutive sentence pair is a positive; NSP negatives pair a sentence with every sentence of the other documents; SOP negatives are the positives reversed. The score is the Jaccard overlap of content words (stop-words removed), computed for every pair. Because overlap is symmetric, a swapped pair scores exactly what the original did, so no threshold can beat 50% on SOP — the feature carries no order information at all.

What went wrong with NSP

  • The negatives are too easy. A random segment usually comes from another document, so topic mismatch — which MLM already learns — nearly solves the task. ALBERT's authors put it as NSP conflating "topic prediction" with "coherence prediction", the easier of which dominates.
  • It shortens the context. Packing two segments whose combined length is at most 512, half of them unrelated, means the model often reads two half-length unrelated spans instead of one long coherent one.
  • ALBERT reports that a model trained with NSP solves SOP only at chance level, while a model trained with SOP solves NSP "to a reasonable degree".
Takeaway: NSP is a lesson about negative sampling. An auxiliary objective helps only if its negatives cannot be separated by a feature the model already has — sentence-order prediction keeps the topic fixed so that only coherence remains.

BERT's ablation found that removing NSP hurt; RoBERTa's found that removing it did not. Both are real results from different comparisons, and the difference between them is instructive.

Reading the two tables together

  • Bidirectionality is the large effect. In BERT's Table 5, "LTR & No NSP" falls 9.0 points on MRPC and 10.1 F1 on SQuAD relative to "No NSP". Adding a BiLSTM recovers SQuAD partly (77.8 → 84.9) but not GLUE.
  • NSP's effect is small and depends on the input format. BERT's "No NSP" kept the two-segment input and removed only the loss; RoBERTa suggests this is why it hurt: "It is possible that the original BERT implementation may only have removed the loss term while still retaining the SEGMENT-PAIR input format." Filling the window with contiguous text and dropping NSP "matches or slightly improves downstream task performance".
  • Sentence-pair inputs (much shorter than 512) did worst in RoBERTa's table even with NSP, and even with a larger batch to equalise tokens — evidence that long contiguous context, not the pair objective, is what mattered.
Takeaway: the left-to-right versus bidirectional gap is several points; the NSP versus no-NSP gap is a fraction of a point and flips sign once the input is long contiguous text. Later encoders (RoBERTa, ELECTRA, DeBERTa, ModernBERT) all train without NSP.

14 Pretraining data, schedule and the 128 → 512 switch

BooksCorpus (800M words) plus English Wikipedia (2,500M words, text passages only — lists, tables and headers removed). The paper stresses using a document-level corpus rather than a sentence-shuffled one such as the Billion Word Benchmark, "in order to extract long contiguous sequences".

The pretraining schedule, and how many tokens it really processes slide the fraction of steps run at length 128 · the learning-rate curve uses the paper's warm-up and linear decay

Tokens are counted as batch × sequence length × steps, treating every sequence as full length, so they are an upper bound: short_seq_prob and segment packing leave some padding. "Epochs" divides tokens by the 3.3B words of the corpus; WordPiece produces somewhat more tokens than words, so the true number of passes is lower still.

The published hyperparameters (Appendix A.2)

  • Batch 256 sequences; 1,000,000 steps; Adam with learning rate 1e-4, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, L2 weight decay 0.01; warm-up over the first 10,000 steps then linear decay; dropout 0.1 everywhere; GELU.
  • Sequence length 128 for 90% of the steps, then 512 for the remaining 10% "to learn the positional embeddings" — because "attention is quadratic to the sequence length".
  • Hardware: BERT-Base on 4 Cloud TPUs in pod configuration (16 TPU chips); BERT-Large on 16 Cloud TPUs (64 chips); "each pretraining took 4 days to complete".

An internal inconsistency in the paper

  • The paper describes the batch as "256 sequences * 512 tokens = 128,000 tokens/batch" (256 × 512 is 131,072) and the whole run as "approximately 40 epochs over the 3.3 billion word corpus" — which is what 1M full-length steps give (131B tokens / 3.3B ≈ 39.7).
  • But the same appendix says 90% of steps used length 128. With that schedule, the run processes about 29.5B + 13.1B = 42.6B tokens — roughly 13 passes, not 40. The "128,000 words per batch" in Appendix A.4 (the GPT comparison) inherits the same full-length assumption. The paper does not reconcile the two; the figure computes both.
Takeaway: 3.3B words, 1M steps of 256 sequences, lr 1e-4 with 10k warm-up, and mostly 128-token sequences. RoBERTa later showed this was far from saturated: ten times the data and larger batches for longer kept improving results (§26).

15 Compute: FLOPs per sequence, and where encoders still win

An encoder pass is one parallel forward over the whole input: no autoregressive loop and no KV cache. Its cost has a part linear in length (the weight matrices) and a part quadratic in length (the attention scores).

forward FLOPs for one sequence of n tokens (multiply + add = 2)C_fwd ≈ 2 · (12·L·H²) · n + 4 · L · n² · H the two terms are equal when24·L·H²·n = 4·L·n²·H ⇔ n = 6H (4,608 for Base, 6,144 for Large) training estimateC_train ≈ 3 · C_fwd per sequence ≈ 6 · N · D when the n² term is small

Counts matrix multiplies only (projections, FFN, QKᵀ and attention-times-V); softmax, LayerNorm and the embedding lookup are ignored. FLOPs are not latency: at long lengths the n × n score matrix also costs memory traffic, which is what FlashAttention-style kernels address (Attention — cost). Lengths beyond 512 are impossible for the released checkpoints and are drawn only to locate the crossover.

Where a fine-tuned encoder is still the right tool

  • High-volume classification and routing — intent, moderation, spam, safety filters — where a ~110M-parameter model with one forward pass costs orders of magnitude fewer FLOPs per call than generating even a short answer with a multi-billion-parameter decoder.
  • Token-level outputs — NER, span extraction, tagging — which map directly to per-position heads (§17).
  • Retrieval and reranking — bi-encoders for first-stage search and cross-encoders for reranking (§28).
  • Whether an encoder also matches a large prompted decoder in accuracy depends on the task and the amount of labelled data; measure both on the target data.
Takeaway: at BERT's 512-token limit, attention scores are only about 10% (Base) of the forward FLOPs — the weight matrices dominate. The crossover is at n = 6H, thousands of tokens. The 512 ceiling comes from the learned position table and from attention memory, not from attention FLOPs.
