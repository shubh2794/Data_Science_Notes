CLIP

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training — an image encoder and a text encoder trained together, on 400 million web image-caption pairs, so that an image and a description of it land close together in one shared embedding space. This note follows CLIP itself: the symmetric contrastive loss and its temperature, why the batch is the negative set, how the WIT dataset was built, the exact encoders and training recipe, zero-shot classification and prompt ensembling, what the paper measured (27-dataset transfer, few-shot parity, distribution-shift robustness, contamination, bias), where CLIP ended up inside other systems, and its direct successors — ALIGN, LiT, OpenCLIP's scaling laws on LAION and SigLIP's sigmoid loss.

Model Architectures Multimodal Contrastive Zero-shot Dual encoder

01 What CLIP is

CLIP is a pair of encoders — one for images, one for text — each followed by a linear projection and L2 normalisation into a shared space, trained so that in every batch of N (image, text) pairs the N true pairings score a higher cosine similarity than the N² − N false ones. The only point where the two modalities meet is a single dot product.

the two embeddings, each unit lengthuᵢ = normalize( W_I · f_img(imageᵢ) ) vⱼ = normalize( W_T · f_txt(textⱼ) ) scaled cosine similarities — an N × N logit matrixLᵢⱼ = s · (uᵢ · vⱼ) s = exp(t), t learned, s ≤ 100 symmetric cross-entropy with the diagonal as the targetsℒ = ½ · [ (1/N) ∑ᵢ −log softmaxⱼ(Lᵢ·)[i] + (1/N) ∑ⱼ −log softmaxᵢ(L·ⱼ)[j] ]

What goes in and what comes out

  • Inputs: an RGB image (224 × 224 for most released models; 288, 384, 448 or 336 for the larger ones) and a short piece of text: the paper caps it at 76 byte-pair tokens, and the released tokenizer allows 77 positions including the start and end markers.
  • Outputs: one vector per image and one per text, both of the same dimension (512, 640, 768 or 1024 depending on the model), both of norm 1. Their dot product is a cosine similarity in [−1, 1].
  • No labels. The supervision is the pairing itself — which caption was scraped next to which image. There is no class vocabulary anywhere in training.

What the pairing buys

  • Zero-shot classification: embed a sentence per class ("a photo of a dog."), embed the image, pick the most similar sentence. The best model, ViT-L/14 at 336 px, reached 76.2% ImageNet top-1 this way — which the paper describes as matching the original fully supervised ResNet-50 (it quotes no figure; single-crop top-1 for that model is usually cited at roughly 75–76%) — without training on any of ImageNet's 1.28 million labelled images.
  • Retrieval in both directions, a similarity score between any image and any text, and a language-aligned image encoder that later systems reuse as a component.
Takeaway: CLIP is "predict which caption goes with which image" at web scale. Everything useful about it — zero-shot classifiers written in English, retrieval, robustness, reuse inside generators and assistants — follows from having two encoders whose outputs are directly comparable.

02 Why learn vision from language

In 2020 the strongest vision models were trained to predict a fixed list of categories chosen by a dataset's designers. Adding a concept meant collecting and labelling more images. Natural-language captions describe a practically open set of concepts and already exist, attached to images, in enormous numbers.

The limits of fixed label sets

  • A classifier head has a fixed output list. An ImageNet model has 1,000 outputs. The large weakly supervised systems of the time were still label-bound: by the CLIP paper's count, Instagram-hashtag pretraining and JFT-300M pretraining limited their targets to 1,000 and 18,291 classes respectively. The CLIP paper's point was that both "carefully design, and in the process limit, their supervision".
  • No dynamic outputs. A static softmax classifier cannot be pointed at a new category without retraining the head, which in turn needs labelled examples of it.
  • Supervision cost. Crowd-labelled data demands a "gold label" per image in a format a learner can consume; captions need no annotation at all.

Why earlier language-supervised attempts had not taken off

  • The idea was old — predicting caption words from images goes back to at least 1999 — and 2016–2020 work (bag-of-words prediction on YFCC100M, visual n-grams, VirTex, ICMLM, ConVIRT) showed it produced usable features.
  • But the headline numbers were weak: Visual N-Grams reached only 11.5% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy, against the then state of the art of 88.4% and the roughly 50% of classic pre-deep-learning methods.
  • The CLIP paper's diagnosis was scale: VirTex, ICMLM and ConVIRT trained "for accelerator days on one to two hundred thousand images", while the label-bound weakly supervised systems trained for accelerator years on millions to billions. CLIP closed that gap with a 400M-pair dataset and a training objective chosen for efficiency.

What language supervision offers beyond self-supervision

Self-supervised image methods (contrastive or generative) also avoid labels, but they only learn a representation; mapping it to a task still needs labelled data for a classifier. Learning from text "doesn't 'just' learn a representation but also connects that representation to language", which is what makes a classifier specifiable in words at test time.

Takeaway: the motivation was the same bet that GPT made for text — that the aggregate supervision in web-scale raw data beats curated labels — transplanted to vision, with language as the interface that removes the fixed output layer.

Consequences of "no cross-attention"

  • Embeddings can be cached. A text embedding does not depend on which image it will be compared with, so a zero-shot classifier for 1,000 classes is computed once and reused for every image; a gallery of images can be embedded once and searched by any future query.
  • Comparison is cheap. Scoring an image against K texts is one K × d_e matrix–vector product. A model that fuses the two inputs with cross-attention (the vision-and-language BERT-style models the paper contrasts itself with) must run a full forward pass per pair.
  • Expressiveness is limited. Everything about an image that matters for matching must fit in one d_e-dimensional vector computed without knowing the query. This is one root of the compositional weaknesses discussed in Limitations.
Takeaway: the paper states it directly: "the only interaction in a CLIP model between the image and text domain is a single dot product in a learned joint embedding space." That design is what makes CLIP a reusable, cacheable component rather than a pairwise scorer.

04 The symmetric contrastive loss

Treat the N × N matrix of scaled cosine similarities as logits. Read along a row and it is an N-way classification problem — "which of these N texts belongs to image i?"; read down a column and it is the reverse problem for text j. CLIP averages the cross-entropy of both.

Where it comes from

  • The paper names its lineage: the batch construction and objective were "first introduced in the area of deep metric learning as the multi-class N-pair loss", "popularized for contrastive representation learning … as the InfoNCE loss", and adapted to (medical) image–text pairs by ConVIRT. CLIP is described as "a simplified version of ConVIRT trained from scratch".
  • Each row term is exactly an InfoNCE loss with the matching caption as the positive and the other N − 1 captions as negatives. Its interpretation as a lower bound on mutual information, I ≥ ln N − ℒ, and the general behaviour of such losses are covered in Contrastive Learning — the InfoNCE / NT-Xent objective.

Why both directions

  • The two terms normalise over different sets. A row softmax asks an image to prefer its caption over other captions; a column softmax asks a caption to prefer its image over other images. With only one term, loosely speaking, one tower would be trained only as the query side of a retrieval problem and the other only as the key side.
  • Downstream CLIP is used in both directions — image → text for zero-shot classification, text → image for retrieval and search — so training both is the natural match.
  • The terms are not redundant: in the worked example in the next section they differ (0.375 versus 0.369 nats), because a row and a column containing the same diagonal entry have different competitors.

What is not in the loss

There is no margin, no explicit hard-negative mining, no momentum encoder, no memory queue and no label smoothing (ALIGN later added label smoothing; see ALIGN). All negatives are simply the other items in the same batch, weighted automatically by the softmax.

Takeaway: CLIP's loss is two batch-wide classification problems sharing one logit matrix. Its floor of difficulty is set by N (a random model scores ln N), and its sharpness by the scale s — the next three sections unpack the arithmetic, the gradient and the scale.

06 What the gradient does to the embeddings

Differentiate the loss with respect to one logit and the answer is "predicted probability minus target". Every negative is pushed away in proportion to how much probability it steals, so the softmax performs hard-negative weighting automatically — and the logit scale decides how aggressively.

gradient of the row term with respect to one logit∂ℒ_I / ∂Lᵢⱼ = (1/N) · ( Pᵢⱼ − 𝟙[i = j] ) Pᵢⱼ = softmaxⱼ(Lᵢ·) full gradient on a logit, both terms (Qᵢⱼ is the column softmax)∂ℒ / ∂Lᵢⱼ = (1 / 2N) · ( Pᵢⱼ + Qᵢⱼ − 2·𝟙[i = j] ) chain rule to a normalised image embedding∂ℒ / ∂uᵢ = s · ∑ⱼ (∂ℒ / ∂Lᵢⱼ) · vⱼ

Reading it on the worked example

  • Row I₂ had probabilities 0.145, 0.532, 0.323. The row-term gradient on its logits is (1/3)·(0.145, −0.468, 0.323): pull T₂ in with weight 0.468, push T₃ away with weight 0.323 and T₁ with only 0.145. T₃, the close competitor, gets more than twice the push of T₁.
  • Because the weights on a row sum to zero, the image embedding moves toward its caption and away from a probability-weighted average of the other captions: Δuᵢ ∝ s · ( vᵢ − ∑ⱼ Pᵢⱼ vⱼ ) for the row term.
  • The embedding is then renormalised to the sphere, so only the tangential component of that update survives; this is standard for cosine-similarity losses and is why the similarity, not the norm, carries all information.

Hard-negative weighting is built in

  • A negative whose similarity is already far below the positive receives almost no probability and therefore almost no gradient. The update concentrates on confusable pairs — two photographs of dogs with different captions, say — without any explicit mining step.
  • The concentration grows with s. At a low scale probabilities are spread and every negative is pushed a little; at a high scale almost all of the push goes to the one or two nearest negatives. The temperature explorer shows this share directly.
  • The gradient on a row vanishes when Pᵢᵢ → 1. Once a pair is separated by several units of logit, it stops contributing — which is why the loss, and learning, is dominated by the hardest items in each batch.
Takeaway: CLIP's loss sends each embedding toward its partner and away from a softmax-weighted mix of the batch's other items. Large batches supply more candidates to be hard; a large logit scale focuses the gradient on them.

07 Temperature and the logit scale

Cosines live in [−1, 1], so a softmax over raw cosines can never be confident: with N = 32,768 captions, even a perfect +1 against −1 for everything else leaves the correct caption at about 0.02%. CLIP multiplies every cosine by a learned scale s = exp(t) = 1/τ before the softmax, starts it at 1/0.07 and caps it at 100.

What the paper says, precisely

  • The temperature "is directly optimized during training as a log-parameterized multiplicative scalar to avoid turning as a hyper-parameter" — i.e. the learned variable is t and the multiplier is exp(t). Optimising the log keeps the scale positive and makes a step in t a proportional change in s.
  • It "was initialized to the equivalent of 0.07" from the instance-discrimination paper it cites, and "clipped to prevent scaling the logits by more than 100 which we found necessary to prevent training instability". Table 18 lists the "maximum temperature" as 100.0 — the paper uses "temperature" loosely there for the multiplier.
  • The reference zero-shot notebook multiplies similarities by a hard-coded 100 — the ceiling value, and the value the released checkpoints' learned scale is commonly reported to have reached.

What the scale controls

  • Achievable confidence. The softmax can only be as peaked as s × (cos_pos − cos_neg) allows. Real CLIP cosines are small — the CLIPScore authors report image–caption cosines ranging "from roughly zero to roughly .4" — so without a large s the loss could not approach zero.
  • Where the gradient goes. As s grows, the softmax probability — and hence the push (see the gradient) — concentrates on the nearest negatives. Too small and every negative gets a little push; too large and the loss is driven by a handful of pairs, some of which are false negatives (a second photo of the same thing with a different caption).
  • Nothing, for ranking. Multiplying all logits by the same positive number does not change which caption wins. Zero-shot accuracy and retrieval rankings are independent of s; only the probabilities (and so calibration and any thresholding) depend on it.

Why cap it?

Nothing in the loss stops t from growing: once the batch is mostly separated, a larger scale always lowers the loss on the separated pairs. The paper says only that clipping was "necessary to prevent training instability"; a plausible reading is that very large logits saturate the softmax, concentrate the gradient on a few (possibly false) negatives and leave little numerical headroom in mixed precision. The cap trades a little achievable sharpness for stability. The general role of temperature in contrastive objectives is discussed in Contrastive Learning — temperature; here it is enough that CLIP learns it and bounds it.

Takeaway: exp(t) is a learned inverse temperature, initialised at 14.29 and clipped at 100. It sets how confident CLIP's softmax can be and where the gradient concentrates, but it never changes an argmax — which is why zero-shot top-1 accuracy is unaffected by it and calibrated probabilities are not.

08 The batch is the negative set

CLIP has no negative sampler. For each image, the negatives are simply the other N − 1 captions in the batch, and vice versa. That makes the batch size a modelling choice as much as a systems one — and CLIP chose 32,768.

Why a bigger batch helps

  • A harder task. Picking the right caption out of 32,768 is harder than out of 256, so the model must encode finer distinctions to keep the loss down. The paper frames each training step as a "randomly created proxy to a computer vision dataset which contains 1 example per class and has 32,768 total classes defined via natural language descriptions".
  • More hard negatives. The softmax focuses the gradient on confusable negatives (see the gradient); a larger batch makes it more likely that confusable ones are present at all.
  • A looser ceiling. The InfoNCE bound I ≥ ln N − ℒ caps what the loss can certify at ln N: 5.5 nats at N = 256, 10.4 nats at 32,768. The bound's tightness is debated; see Contrastive Learning — negatives.

Why it is not free

  • The similarity matrix grows as N² while the encoders' cost grows as N. At N = 32,768 the encoders still dominate the FLOPs — the OpenCLIP paper lists 87.7 GMACs (about 175 GFLOPs) per sample for ViT-L/14's two towers together, while the similarity matmul adds about 2·N·d_e ≈ 50 MFLOPs per image — but the memory of a naive full matrix (4 GiB in fp32 before the softmax's own buffers) is prohibitive on one device, and every device needs every other device's embeddings.
  • False negatives become more common: at 32,768 web pairs, a batch will often contain several captions that also describe a given image. The loss still labels them wrong.
  • Later work found the benefit saturates. SigLIP reports that for both its sigmoid loss and a softmax baseline, gains "quickly diminish", with "a more reasonable batch size of 32 k being sufficient" (see SigLIP).
Takeaway: in CLIP, "how many negatives" and "how big is the batch" are the same question. 32,768 buys a hard 32,768-way task every step at the price of a 2³⁰-entry similarity matrix that must be sharded — the next section shows how.

09 Sharding the N × N similarity across devices

The paper's one-sentence description: "the calculation of embedding similarities was also sharded with individual GPUs computing only the subset of the pairwise similarities necessary for their local batch of embeddings." Each device owns a horizontal strip of the matrix, not the whole of it.

with D devices, local batch n = N / D1. each device encodes its n images and n texts → u_local [n, d_e], v_local [n, d_e] 2. all-gather embeddings from every device → U [N, d_e], V [N, d_e] 3. compute its strips: u_local · Vᵀ and v_local · Uᵀ → two [n, N] blocks 4. local loss over those rows; sum the losses across devices memory per device instead of N²2 · n · N logits n = 256, N = 32,768 → 2 × 32 MiB = 64 MiB (fp32)

Details that matter

  • The all-gather must pass gradients. The loss on device 1's rows depends on device 2's text embeddings; those gradients must flow back to device 2's encoder. The OpenCLIP reproduction notes that PyTorch's standard torch.distributed.all_gather "can not be differentiated through", while torch.distributed.nn.functional.all_gather can — "imperative for correct gradient calculation". Using the wrong one silently trains with gradients only from local negatives.
  • Where the 64 MiB figure comes from. The OpenCLIP paper's appendix gives exactly this arithmetic: before sharding, "4 GB of VRAM in FP32" per worker; after, two n × N matrices, "64 MB" at local batch 256. With 32,768 / 256 = 128 devices, each holds 1/64 of the full matrix (two strips of 1/128).
  • Communication is an all-gather of N × d_e numbers per modality — at d_e = 768 and fp16, 32,768 × 768 × 2 bytes ≈ 48 MiB per modality per step — small next to the encoders' gradient all-reduce.
  • Other memory savers in CLIP's run. Mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics, and half-precision stochastically rounded text-encoder weights, all listed in the paper's training section.

An alternative that avoids the gather

SigLIP's pairwise sigmoid loss needs no row normalisation, so each device can compute its block against one neighbour's texts at a time, pass the texts around a ring with collective permutes, and accumulate. Its paper states that "at any point in time only" a b × b block "is materialized in memory", reducing the peak from |B|² to b² and enabling batches above one million. See SigLIP.

Takeaway: sharding turns the 4 GiB matrix into per-device strips of a few tens of MiB, at the cost of an all-gather that must be differentiable. Getting that detail wrong is a quiet failure: training still runs and the loss still looks plausible.

10 WIT: building 400 million image-text pairs

CLIP's dataset, WebImageText (WIT), was built by searching for image-text pairs whose text contains one of 500,000 queries, then keeping at most 20,000 pairs per query. The query list sets which concepts are covered; the cap sets how evenly.

What a per-query cap does to a long-tailed pool a toy Zipf pool calibrated so that 16,000 of 386,000 matched queries exceed 20,000 pairs (the point MetaCLIP reports) · slide the cap and the tail exponent

How the query list was built (paper, footnote 1)

  • Every word occurring at least 100 times in English Wikipedia;
  • augmented with bi-grams with high pointwise mutual information;
  • plus the names of Wikipedia articles above a certain search volume;
  • plus every WordNet synset not already in the list.

The search matched queries against the text only (the paper stresses "sub-string matches"), unlike webly supervised work that relied on image search engines with their own vision-based ranking. The resulting dataset "has a similar total word count as the WebText dataset used to train GPT-2". It was never released.

What the balancing step does

  • Web text is extremely long-tailed: a few terms ("photo", common stopwords) match hundreds of millions of captions, most terms match few or none. Without a cap, a random sample of the pool would be dominated by generic head terms.
  • MetaCLIP, which reconstructed the procedure on CommonCrawl, reports that of 500k entries, 114k had no matches, and only 16k entries (3.2%) had more than 20k matches — yet those accounted for 94.5% of all 5.6B matches. After capping at 20k, "out of 400M pairs, only 20k texts containing 'photo' are kept", against 54M "photo" instances in the pool.
  • So the cap mostly removes head-term pairs and leaves the tail untouched, flattening the concept distribution. MetaCLIP's authors write that "the main ingredient to the success of CLIP is its data and not the model architecture or pre-training objective", and their experiments single out the metadata-plus-balancing curation as the part that matters; that is their interpretation, supported by controlled data experiments (see the data-centric successors).

11 Why contrastive, not captioning

CLIP's first design predicted captions. It was too slow to scale. The paper's Figure 2 compares three objectives by how quickly zero-shot ImageNet accuracy rises with images processed: a Transformer language model, a bag-of-words predictor, and a contrastive bag-of-words model. Each step to the next was a multiplicative gain in efficiency.

The argument

  • Predictive objectives "try to predict the exact words of the text accompanying each image. This is a difficult task due to the wide variety of descriptions, comments, and related text that co-occur with images." Much of that difficulty is irrelevant to vision.
  • The contrastive task is "potentially easier": it only needs enough information to tell captions apart, not to reproduce them. The paper cites earlier findings that contrastive objectives learn better representations than equivalent predictive ones, and that generative image models need over an order of magnitude more compute for the same representation quality.
  • An appendix model makes the comparison concrete at full scale: "LM RN50", trained on the same data for the same epochs with an autoregressive caption loss, reaches a 27-dataset linear-probe average of 71.8 against contrastive CLIP-RN50's 75.8, and 65.2 against 73.3 on ImageNet (Table 10; averages computed here from its 27 columns).

What was given up

A contrastive model can only choose among texts it is handed; a captioner can produce new ones. The paper lists this as a limitation and suggests "joint training of a contrastive and generative objective" — the route CoCa later took, reaching 86.3% zero-shot ImageNet (see successors). Generative image-to-text models are the domain of Vision-Language Models.

Takeaway: choosing the objective was the decisive efficiency decision. Predicting words is roughly 12× slower at buying zero-shot ImageNet accuracy than matching whole captions, at the cost of being unable to generate text.

12 Image encoders: the modified ResNets

Five of CLIP's eight image encoders are ResNets — a ResNet-50, a ResNet-101, and three larger models scaled EfficientNet-style to roughly 4×, 16× and 64× a ResNet-50's compute. All share three changes to the classic design, the most distinctive being an attention layer in place of global average pooling.

The three modifications

  • ResNet-D-style stem and downsampling. The paper cites the ResNet-D improvements from the "bag of tricks" paper. Concretely, the reference implementation uses three 3 × 3 stem convolutions instead of one 7 × 7, an average pool instead of the stem's max pool, and an average pool ahead of the 1 × 1 convolution on each downsampling shortcut.
  • Anti-aliased "rect-2 blur pooling". In each downsampling bottleneck the 3 × 3 convolution runs at stride 1 and a 2 × 2 average pool then does the downsampling (ahead of the block's final 1 × 1 convolution); on the shortcut, the average pool comes before the 1 × 1 projection. Downsampling is therefore averaged rather than strided. (The stem's first 3 × 3 convolution keeps stride 2.) This reduces aliasing and makes features more stable under small shifts; the frequency-domain reasoning is in Frequency Domain.
  • Attention pooling. The final global average pool is replaced by "a single layer of 'transformer-style' multi-head QKV attention where the query is conditioned on the global average-pooled representation of the image." The mean of the final feature map is prepended as an extra token, learned position embeddings are added, and only that token acts as the query over all spatial positions; its output, projected, is the image feature.

Two apparent conflicts, and how far they can be reconciled

  • 49,152 or 49,408? The reference tokenizer builds its vocabulary as 256 byte symbols, 256 byte-plus-end-of-word symbols, 48,894 merges and 2 special tokens. For practical purposes, CLIP reads short captions.

Why the [EOS] token, and why causal attention

  • Under a causal mask, only the last position has seen the whole sentence, so it is the natural summary position — the same reasoning as reading a GPT model's final hidden state.
  • The reference code finds it with argmax over token ids: the end-of-text token has the largest id (49,407), so its position is where the id is maximal. Padding with zeros after [EOS] is therefore harmless.
  • The causal mask was kept so that pretrained language-model initialisation or an auxiliary LM loss could be added; the paper leaves both as future work, and the released models were trained from scratch with the contrastive loss alone.

15 Projection and L2 normalisation

Each tower ends in a single learned linear map to the shared dimension d_e, followed by division by the vector's length. No non-linear projection head. In the reference code the ViT and text projections are bias-free matrices; for the ResNets the projection is the attention pool's output layer, which does carry a bias. After normalisation, a dot product is a cosine and every embedding lies on the unit sphere.

projection then normalisationz = W · f u = z / ‖z‖₂ ‖u‖₂ = 1 dot product of unit vectors is the cosine, and relates to distanceu · v = cos θ ‖u − v‖₂² = 2 − 2·(u · v)

A small worked example

  • Suppose an image's projected vector is z = (3, 4, 0). Its length is √(9 + 16) = 5, so u = (0.6, 0.8, 0).
  • A caption projects to (0, 2, 2), length √8 = 2.828, so v = (0, 0.707, 0.707).
  • u · v = 0.8 × 0.707 = 0.566. The same answer comes from the raw vectors: (3·0 + 4·2 + 0·2) / (5 × 2.828) = 8 / 14.14 = 0.566. Squared Euclidean distance: 2 − 2 × 0.566 = 0.869.
  • Doubling z to (6, 8, 0) changes nothing after normalisation — the length of a CLIP projection carries no information about the match.

Why linear, and why normalise

  • The paper did "not use the non-linear projection" popularised by image-only contrastive methods, because it "did not notice a difference in training efficiency", and speculated that such heads "may be co-adapted with details of current image only in self-supervised representation learning methods". The general argument for projection heads is in Contrastive Learning — projection heads.
  • Normalising makes the logit bounded (|s·cos| ≤ s), which is what gives the learned scale its meaning, and it stops the loss being lowered by simply inflating vector norms.
  • Because distance and cosine are monotonically related on the sphere, CLIP embeddings drop straight into nearest-neighbour search with either metric (see Embeddings — similarity).

Embedding dimensions and which features to use

  • d_e is 1024 (RN50), 512 (RN101, ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16), 640 (RN50x4), 768 (RN50x16, ViT-L/14, ViT-L/14@336px) and 1024 (RN50x64). Embeddings from different models are not comparable, even at equal dimension.
  • For linear probes the paper uses the feature before the projection (I_f) for the ViTs, and the penultimate layer for the other models it evaluates. Many VLMs likewise tap an internal layer of the vision tower rather than the projected vector (see CLIP as a VLM vision tower).
Takeaway: the projection is one linear layer per tower and the normalisation is non-negotiable: without it, similarities are not cosines and the logit scale loses its meaning. Note that the reference encode_image / encode_text return unnormalised vectors; only the forward pass normalises.

17 Zero-shot classification: a classifier written in text

To classify into K classes, write a sentence for each class, embed the K sentences once, and use the resulting K unit vectors as the weight matrix of a linear classifier over image embeddings. The paper calls the text encoder a hypernetwork that "generates the weights of a linear classifier based on the text".

A hand-worked example with realistic cosines

  • Three classes; an image of a cat. Suppose the cosines to "a photo of a cat.", "a photo of a dog." and "a photo of a car." are 0.31, 0.27 and 0.12 — values in the range CLIP actually produces.
  • Logits at s = 100: 31, 27, 12. Softmax: e⁰ : e⁻⁴ : e⁻¹⁹ → 0.982, 0.018, 0.000. Prediction: cat.
  • At s = 14.29: logits 4.43, 3.86, 1.71 → 0.613, 0.346, 0.041. Same argmax, far less confident. At s = 1 the three probabilities would be 0.36, 0.34, 0.30.

Properties that follow

  • The label set is part of the model. Removing the correct class does not produce "none of the above"; the softmax redistributes over what remains. Adding a class can change predictions for images of other classes (the bias section shows this changing harmful outcomes).
  • Amortised cost. The K text embeddings are computed once and cached; per image, zero-shot inference is one image forward pass and a K × d_e product.
  • Zero-shot in the paper's sense means zero examples from the evaluation dataset, not necessarily unseen concepts — the pretraining data may well contain the categories. The paper is explicit that it uses the term for generalisation to unseen datasets.
Takeaway: CLIP's zero-shot classifier is an ordinary linear classifier whose weights were written in English instead of learned from labelled images. Its accuracy depends on the image encoder, the class names, the prompt wording and the chosen label set.

18 Prompt engineering and ensembling

A bare class name is a poor caption: CLIP was trained on sentences, and many labels are ambiguous. Wrapping the name in a template, tailoring the template to the dataset, and averaging the embeddings of many templates each add accuracy — on ImageNet, 1.3 points for the default template and a further 3.5 for an 80-template ensemble.

Averaging template embeddings on a toy problem each template embeds a class slightly off its "true" direction · averaging K unit vectors and renormalising cancels the noise · seeded toy data, not CLIP outputs

Three problems with bare labels, and the fixes the paper reports

  • Distribution gap. "It's relatively rare in our pre-training dataset for the text paired with the image to be just a single word." The template "A photo of a {label}." "improves accuracy on ImageNet by 1.3%".
  • Polysemy. ImageNet contains both "construction cranes and cranes that fly"; in Oxford-IIIT Pets "boxer" is a dog breed but could be an athlete. Task-specific context helps: "A photo of a {label}, a type of pet." for Pets, "a type of food" for Food101, "a type of aircraft" for FGVC Aircraft, quotes around the text for OCR datasets, "a satellite photo of a {label}." for satellite imagery.
  • Bad class names. The released ImageNet notebook also rewrites names by trial and error: "nail" became "metal nail" (CLIP read it as fingernail), "kite" became "kite (bird of prey)", "red wolf" became "red wolf or maned wolf". The notebook reports these edits add 1.5% top-1 on ViT-B/32.

Why averaging works (and its limits)

Each template embeds the class plus template-specific noise (style words, the word "photo", article choice). Averaging many unit vectors keeps the shared class direction and partially cancels the rest — the toy figure shows accuracy rising and then flattening as K grows. The limit is correlated error: if every template shares a misreading (say, the fingernail sense of "nail"), averaging cannot remove it, which is why the class-name edits exist.

Takeaway: write labels as captions, disambiguate with task context, and average many template embeddings into one prototype per class. These are free at inference time and worth about as much as 4× more model compute, but they were tuned against labelled validation data — a caveat the paper itself raises.

20 Zero-shot versus few-shot versus linear probe

The obvious expectation — one labelled example beats zero — fails for CLIP: its zero-shot classifier matches a 4-shot logistic regression trained on its own features, averaged over 20 datasets. With enough labels the probe wins by a wide margin, and CLIP's features then beat the best ImageNet model of the time.

Few-shot parity

  • On the 20 datasets with at least 16 examples per class, zero-shot CLIP "matches the average performance of a 4-shot linear classifier trained on the same feature space" and "nearly matches the best results of a 16-shot linear classifier across publicly available models" (a BiT-M ResNet-152x2 trained on ImageNet-21K).
  • The paper's explanation: a zero-shot classifier is told the concept in words, while a one-shot learner must infer it from an image that contains many concepts, so "many different hypotheses can be consistent with the data".
  • Per dataset, the labelled examples a probe needs to match zero-shot range from under 1 (Flowers102 and EuroSAT, where zero-shot loses even to one-shot) to an estimated 184 (FER2013); median 5.4, mean 20.8. On ImageNet, zero-shot matches a 16-shot probe.

The counter-intuitive drop

Moving from zero-shot to a 1- or 2-shot probe lowers accuracy, because the probe discards the text prior. The authors tried an L2 penalty pulling the probe toward the zero-shot weights, but hyper-parameter search "would often select for such a large value of this regularizer that the resulting few-shot classifier was 'just' the zero-shot classifier". Humans behave oppositely: on Oxford-IIIT Pets, human accuracy rose from 53.7% zero-shot to 75.7% with one example per class (zero-shot CLIP: 93.5%), with the gain concentrated on images the people had been unsure about.

21 Distribution-shift robustness and "effective robustness"

ImageNet models lose much of their accuracy on natural variations of ImageNet — new photos, sketches, renditions, adversarially filtered images, unusual viewpoints. Zero-shot CLIP, never trained on ImageNet's distribution, loses far less. The paper measures this with the "effective robustness" framework of Taori et al.

Three models with ImageNet accuracy near CLIP's, on six test sets ResNet-101 and zero-shot CLIP ViT-L/14@336px share 76.2% on ImageNet (both from Fig. 13) · add CLIP's ImageNet-adapted linear probe (Table 16)

The framework

  • Taori et al. found that across many ImageNet models, accuracy under natural shift is "well modeled as a linear function of logit-transformed accuracy" on ImageNet. A model that simply sits on that line is not "more robust"; it is just more accurate.
  • Effective robustness is accuracy under shift above that line's prediction; relative robustness is any improvement in shifted accuracy. The paper uses 7 shifts: ImageNetV2, ImageNet-Sketch, Youtube-BB, ImageNet-Vid, ObjectNet, ImageNet-A and ImageNet-R.
  • A ResNet-101 "makes 5 times as many mistakes" on these shifts as on the ImageNet validation set.

What zero-shot CLIP does

  • "All zero-shot CLIP models improve effective robustness by a large amount and reduce the size of the gap between ImageNet accuracy and accuracy under distribution shift by up to 75%."
  • Against a ResNet-101 with the same 76.2% ImageNet accuracy (Figure 13): ImageNetV2 64.3 → 70.1, ImageNet-R 37.7 → 88.9, ObjectNet 32.6 → 72.3, ImageNet-Sketch 25.2 → 60.2, ImageNet-A 2.7 → 77.1.
  • Averaged over those five shifted sets (computed here), ResNet-101 scores 32.5 and CLIP 73.7: a drop from ImageNet of 43.7 points versus 2.5. This five-set average leaves out Youtube-BB and ImageNet-Vid and is not the paper's fitted "up to 75%" statistic.
  • Zero-shot CLIP set a new state of the art on 5 of the 7 shifts (ImageNet-R, ObjectNet, ImageNet-Sketch, ImageNet-Vid, Youtube-BB), with the paper attributing the ImageNet-R gain partly to "significant amounts of creative content" in WIT.

What it does not show

The paper is careful: these results "do not necessarily mean that supervised learning on ImageNet causes a robustness gap". CLIP differs in data size, data diversity and supervision type all at once. A footnote adds that a zero-shot model "can still exploit spurious correlations that are shared between the pre-training and evaluation distributions". A later controlled study, "Data Determines Distributional Robustness in Contrastive Language Image Pre-training", compared five candidate causes and found "the more diverse training distribution is the main cause for the robustness gains", with dataset size, language supervision and the contrastive loss contributing little. That is one study's conclusion on its experimental scale, but it points away from the loss and toward the data.

Takeaway: at equal ImageNet accuracy, zero-shot CLIP is dramatically better on sketches, renditions, ObjectNet and ImageNet-A. That is effective robustness — being above the line that ImageNet-trained models sit on — and it is a property of the zero-shot model, as the next section shows.

What later work did about it

This finding motivated robust fine-tuning methods. WiSE-FT interpolates the weights of the zero-shot and fine-tuned models and reports 4 to 6 points better accuracy under distribution shift than prior work on ImageNet's shifts while also raising ImageNet accuracy by 1.6 points. LP-FT (linear probe first, then fine-tune) argues that full fine-tuning with a random head "distorts" pretrained features, reporting on average 2% higher in-distribution but 7% lower out-of-distribution accuracy for plain fine-tuning versus linear probing. General fine-tuning strategies are in Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning.

Takeaway: CLIP's robustness is fragile under adaptation. If you fine-tune or probe on a target distribution, expect in-distribution gains and some out-of-distribution losses; weight interpolation or linear-probe-then-fine-tune are the standard mitigations.

23 Data-overlap (contamination) analysis

With 400M web images, some evaluation images are bound to be in the training set. Rather than de-duplicate against every possible benchmark in advance, the paper measured the overlap for 35 datasets and estimated how much it inflated accuracy. The answer: rarely more than 0.1 points.

The procedure

  • A purpose-built near-duplicate detector (below) finds nearest neighbours of each test image in the training set; the authors inspect them and set a per-dataset threshold "to keep high precision while maximizing recall".
  • Each test set is split into Overlap (above threshold) and Clean. Zero-shot RN50x64 is evaluated on All, Overlap and Clean; All − Clean is the estimated inflation. A one-tailed binomial test and 99.5% Clopper–Pearson intervals check significance.

The findings

  • Of 35 datasets, 9 had no detected overlap — synthetic or specialised sets (MNIST, CLEVR, GTSRB) and sets created after WIT (ObjectNet, Hateful Memes). Median overlap 2.2%, mean 3.2%.
  • Overall accuracy shifted by more than 0.1 points on only 7 datasets, of which only 2 were significant after Bonferroni correction. The largest estimated gain was 0.6 on Birdsnap (12.1% overlap).
  • The largest overlap was Country211 at 21.5% (it was built from YFCC100M, part of WIT), yet accuracy rose only 0.2 — the duplicates' captions usually did not mention the location the task asks for.
  • Caveats the paper raises: detector recall over 400M images cannot be checked, and Overlap and Clean can differ in difficulty (on Kinetics-700 many "overlaps" were black transition frames, producing an apparent 20% drop).

The duplicate detector

CLIP's own embeddings were unsuitable — too semantic (different soccer balls look "identical") and sometimes blind to real duplicates resized with different interpolation. The authors trained a separate ResNet-50 (anti-aliased, weight norm instead of batch norm to avoid leaking information through batch statistics, GELU) with the same InfoNCE loss at a fixed temperature of 0.07, on augmented copies of about 30M WIT images at batch size 1,712.

Takeaway: inflation is overlap fraction times the accuracy advantage on overlapping items, and both factors were small for CLIP. The method — split, re-evaluate, test — is reusable for any web-scale model.

24 Limitations

The paper devotes a section to its own limitations, and later work added more. They fall into four groups: tasks CLIP cannot do well, things the dual-encoder cannot represent, weaknesses of the evaluation, and costs of the approach.

Task-level weaknesses the paper reports

  • Fine-grained categories: "differentiating models of cars, species of flowers, and variants of aircraft" (though Stanford Cars was a strength relative to the ResNet-50 probe — the weakness is relative to task-specific models).
  • Abstract and systematic tasks: "counting the number of objects in an image" (CLEVR counts: 24.8% zero-shot for ViT-L/14@336px on an 8-way task).
  • Novel tasks unlikely to be in the pretraining data, such as distance to the nearest car (KITTI: 21.9%), where performance "can be near random". The authors are "confident that there are still many, many, tasks where CLIP's zero-shot performance is near chance level".
  • True out-of-distribution data. CLIP reads rendered text well but reaches only about 88% on MNIST handwritten digits (Table 11: 88.3; Table 14: 88.4) — below logistic regression on raw pixels (92.5%) — and 51% on street-view house numbers. Near-duplicate search found almost no MNIST-like images in WIT. The paper: CLIP "does little to address the underlying problem of brittle generalization"; it hopes training data covers everything, "a naive assumption that, as MNIST demonstrates, is easy to violate".

What the architecture cannot express

  • No generation. CLIP can only choose among the candidate texts it is given.
  • Polysemy and underspecified labels (crane, boxer, nail) — see prompts.
  • Compositionality. A single pooled vector per side, trained on a retrieval objective that can often be solved without word order, is weak at relations and attribute binding. The ARO benchmark ("When and why vision-language models behave like bags-of-words") reports "a severe lack of order sensitivity" in such models and argues contrastive pretraining does not require compositional information, proposing composition-aware hard negatives as a fix.
  • Typographic attacks. Because CLIP learned to read, text in an image can override its content. The companion analysis of CLIP's "multimodal neurons" (2021) demonstrated that attaching a handwritten label to an object can flip the prediction to the written word. How reliably this transfers to any given model and prompt set varies, but it is a direct consequence of the OCR ability measured in the benchmarks.

Evaluation caveats the authors raise

  • "We repeatedly queried performance on full validation sets to guide the development of CLIP", which is "unrealistic for true zero-shot scenarios".
  • The 27-dataset suite is "somewhat haphazardly assembled" and "undeniably co-adapted with the development and capabilities of CLIP".

Cost and efficiency

  • The authors estimate "around a 1000x increase in compute is required for zero-shot CLIP to reach overall state-of-the-art performance", infeasible on the hardware of the time.
  • CLIP "does not address the poor data efficiency of deep learning" — the 405-years-at-one-image-per-second calculation in training.
  • Adding a few labelled examples lowers accuracy with a plain linear probe (see few-shot).
Takeaway: CLIP is strong where the web has dense, well-captioned coverage and weak on counting, spatial and relational reasoning, specialised imagery and anything it has never seen. The dual-encoder design adds its own blind spots: word order, attribute binding and susceptibility to text in the image.

25 Bias and social impact: what the paper measured

Because anyone can define CLIP's classes in plain language, the paper argues "class design" becomes a primary source of harm, alongside the uncurated training data. Its bias probes are explicitly "preliminary" and "not comprehensive", but the numbers are specific.

FairFace accuracy

  • A logistic-regression probe on CLIP features beat both an Instagram-pretrained ResNeXt probe and FairFace's own model on most race, gender and age classification tests; zero-shot CLIP was mixed. Gender classification accuracy exceeded 95% for every race category.

26 Embedding geometry and the modality gap

CLIP's image and text embeddings do not mix on the sphere: they occupy two separate narrow cones, with matched pairs far from cosine 1. This "modality gap" was characterised after the CLIP paper; its existence is well established, its causes partly explained, and its practical consequences still debated.

A toy of the gap: a shared semantic plane plus an offset along one extra axis u = cos β · e + sin β · g, v = cos β · e − sin β · g · watch the cosines shrink while the ranking stays put · toy construction, not measured CLIP embeddings

What was found ("Mind the Gap", 2022)

  • The cone effect. The representations of a deep network occupy "a narrow cone", even at random initialisation; the average cosine between unrelated embeddings is well above 0.
  • Two towers, two cones. Independently initialised encoders produce different cones, so the modalities start apart. During training, "contrastive learning keeps the different modalities separate by a certain distance, which is influenced by the temperature parameter".
  • Size. Defined as the distance between the centres of the normalised image and text embeddings, the gap in CLIP is 0.82 (measured on 5,000 MSCOCO pairs). The authors' loss-landscape probe found this distance to be a minimum of the contrastive loss at CLIP's temperature: shifting embeddings to close it raised the loss.
  • Consequences. Deliberately changing the gap affected zero-shot accuracy on some tasks and fairness: increasing it from 0.82 to 0.97 reduced the denigration harms of the bias probe for all races, at a 0.0008 top-1 accuracy cost.

Why it matters in practice

  • Small absolute cosines. The gap is one reason matched image–text cosines are around 0.3 rather than near 1, and why the logit scale must be large (see temperature). In the toy, the same offset compresses every cosine by cos² β and shifts it by −sin² β while leaving the ranking unchanged.
  • Cross-modal versus within-modal scores are not comparable. An image–image cosine of 0.8 and an image–text cosine of 0.3 can indicate equally strong relations. Thresholds must be set per modality pair.
  • Swapping embeddings across modalities (using a text embedding where an image embedding is expected) is not free: the two occupy different regions. DALL·E 2, for instance, does not feed a CLIP text embedding straight into its image decoder; it trains a separate prior to produce a CLIP image embedding from the caption (see text-to-image).

Evidential status

The gap itself is a robust measurement reproduced across contrastive models. The cone-effect explanation is supported by experiments and a simplified theory; the claim that the gap is harmful or helpful is task-dependent and remains contested. Treat "close the gap to improve CLIP" as a hypothesis, not a rule.

Takeaway: CLIP's shared space is shared for ranking, not for absolute distances. Image and text embeddings sit in separate cones about 0.82 apart, which keeps cross-modal cosines small and makes thresholds modality-specific.

28 CLIP inside text-to-image systems

Within a year of release, CLIP was steering image generators, then conditioning them. Three roles recur: a guide that scores candidate images against a prompt, a latent space that a generator inverts, and a frozen text encoder whose token states the generator attends to.

1. CLIP as a guide or scorer

  • Early open-source pipelines optimised a generator's latent (or pixels) by gradient ascent on the CLIP cosine between the image and the prompt. This works, but the image can drift toward whatever maximises CLIP's score rather than what a person would call a good picture — adversarial textures and written words among them.
  • GLIDE (2021) compared CLIP guidance with classifier-free guidance for a diffusion model and reported that human evaluators preferred classifier-free guidance for both photorealism and caption similarity. Guidance itself is covered in Diffusion Models — guidance.
  • As a metric, CLIP score remains the standard automatic measure of prompt alignment (see CLIPScore).

2. CLIP's image-embedding space as the generator's target (unCLIP / DALL·E 2)

  • "Hierarchical Text-Conditional Image Generation with CLIP Latents" (2022) uses two stages: a prior that "generates a CLIP image embedding given a text caption", and a diffusion decoder "that generates an image conditioned on the image embedding". The authors call it unCLIP "since it generates images by inverting the CLIP image encoder".
  • They report that explicitly generating the image embedding "improves image diversity with minimal loss in photorealism and caption similarity", that decoding interpolations between image embeddings yields image variations that keep semantics and style, and that diffusion priors were more compute-efficient and better than autoregressive ones.

3. CLIP's text tower as a frozen conditioning encoder (Stable Diffusion)

  • Stable Diffusion v1 is a latent diffusion model "that uses a fixed, pretrained text encoder (CLIP ViT-L/14)"; its model card specifies that "the non-pooled output of the text encoder is fed into the UNet backbone … via cross-attention". So the generator sees all 77 per-token hidden states (77 × 768), not the single projected CLIP embedding.
  • Stable Diffusion 2.x switched to OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 as the text encoder. Later systems often use several text encoders, including large text-only models, to improve prompt fidelity (see Diffusion Models — latent diffusion).
  • Consequences inherited from CLIP: the 77-token limit truncates long prompts, and CLIP's weakness on counting, spatial relations and attribute binding shows up as generators that misplace or miscount objects.
Takeaway: in generation CLIP has served as a critic, as a latent space to invert, and — most durably — as a frozen text encoder. Each role imports CLIP's strengths (broad concept coverage) and its blind spots (composition, counting, short context).

29 CLIP as the vision tower of assistants that see

The most common modern use of a CLIP image encoder is not zero-shot classification but as a frozen or lightly tuned feature extractor feeding a language model. Its features are already aligned with language, which makes the connector between the two cheap to train.

A representative recipe (LLaVA)

  • LLaVA (2023) "connects a vision encoder and LLM"; the vision encoder is a pretrained CLIP ViT-L/14, whose grid of patch features is mapped by a trainable projection into the LLM's token-embedding space.
  • LLaVA-1.5 used "CLIP-ViT-L-336px with an MLP projection" and reported state-of-the-art results across 11 benchmarks with 1.2M public training examples, training "in ~1 day on a single 8-A100 node". At 336 px with 14-px patches that is a 24 × 24 grid — 576 visual tokens per image (see the token arithmetic).
  • These systems use the patch-level features from inside the encoder, not CLIP's single projected embedding: the language model needs spatially resolved information, which a pooled vector has discarded.

Why CLIP towers, and what they cost

  • For: broad concept coverage, some OCR, strong robustness, and features that a linear or MLP connector can map into word-like tokens with little data.
  • Against: the inherited blind spots (counting, spatial relations, fine text at low resolution), fixed input resolution, and hundreds of tokens per image. Later assistants increasingly adopted SigLIP-family towers or higher-resolution tiling schemes.
  • Connector designs, instruction tuning and evaluation of these assistants are the subject of Vision-Language Models — fusion; this page only notes that the vision side is often a CLIP descendant.
Takeaway: a CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 px, read out as 576 patch tokens and projected into an LLM, became a default visual front end. What CLIP cannot see, the assistant built on it usually cannot either.

31 LiT: lock the image tower, train only the text

"LiT: Zero-Shot Transfer with Locked-image text Tuning" (2021) asks whether both towers need to learn. Its answer: take a strong pretrained image model, freeze it, and train only a text tower to read out its representations. This "Locked-image Tuning" beat CLIP and ALIGN on zero-shot ImageNet by about 9 points.

The design space

  • Each tower can be L (locked, pretrained), U (unlocked, pretrained) or u (unlocked, random). CLIP and ALIGN are uu; LiT is Lu.
  • In a thorough small-scale study on the 15M-image YFCC100M subset, "locking the image tower almost always works best", a pretrained image tower "significantly helps", a pretrained text tower helps only marginally, and locking the text tower "does not work well".
  • The explanation offered: locking gives worse contrastive loss on the tuning data but better loss on out-of-distribution data such as COCO captions — the pretrained image representation, trained on cleaner labels, generalises better than one re-fitted to noisy captions.

Results and efficiency

  • With a ViT-g/14 pretrained on JFT-3B, 32k batch and 18B image–text pairs seen, LiT reached 85.2% zero-shot ImageNet and 82.5% on ObjectNet (an earlier version reported 84.5%, corrected after a model-loading bug).
  • With only 300M pairs seen, LiT reached 81.7%; the paper notes from-scratch CLIP needed 12.8B pairs, "40 times more", to reach 76.2%.
  • A locked tower needs no image gradients, and without augmentation its embeddings "can be precomputed once", cutting the cost of training further. The paper also reports incremental gains from larger text towers (base → large → huge) — a point CLIP's width-only text scaling did not explore.
Takeaway: LiT separates representation learning from language alignment: learn the image representation however you like (here supervised on JFT-3B), then teach a text tower to address it. The cost is that the image representation's own biases and gaps are frozen in.

32 OpenCLIP, LAION and reproducible scaling laws

OpenAI released CLIP's weights but not WIT. The open reproduction — the OpenCLIP code base trained on the public LAION datasets — made CLIP-style training reproducible, produced larger public models, and measured scaling laws for contrastive image–text learning ("Reproducible scaling laws for contrastive language-image learning", 2022).

The data

  • LAION-5B: 5.85B image–text pairs from Common Crawl, 2.32B of them English (the "LAION-2B" subset); LAION-400M is an earlier English set.
  • LAION was filtered with OpenAI's CLIP ViT-B/32: English pairs with cosine below 0.28 and other pairs below 0.26 were removed, which "removed around 90% of the original 50 billion images". An open CLIP is therefore trained on data selected by the closed one — a circularity worth remembering when comparing them.

The study

  • ViT-B/32 through ViT-g/14, data scales of 80M, 400M and 2B, and 3B, 13B or 34B samples seen; up to 1,520 A100 GPUs. The recipe follows CLIP (InfoNCE, AdamW with β₂ = 0.98 and weight decay 0.2), except that loss spikes at ViT-L/14 and above were fixed "by switching from mixed precision with float16 to mixed precision with bfloat16".
  • Best result: ViT-H/14 on LAION-2B with 34B samples seen, 78.0% zero-shot ImageNet. At equal architecture, their ViT-L/14 on LAION-2B scored 75.2 against OpenAI's 75.5 under the paper's evaluation.
  • Fitted exponents on zero-shot ImageNet error: α = −0.11 for OpenCLIP/LAION versus −0.16 for OpenAI/WIT, and −0.13 versus −0.24 on the robustness sets — a steeper curve for WIT. On retrieval the order reverses (COCO: −0.08 versus −0.05). The authors conclude "the training distribution plays a key role in scaling laws".
  • Bottlenecks: ViT-B models gained nothing from 400M → 2B data at 3B or 13B samples seen; the larger dataset only paid off at 34B samples. Extrapolating, they predicted 79.1% for a ViT-g/14 at 2B/34B and 81.9% for a ViT-G/14 with 68B samples.

33 SigLIP: replacing the softmax with a pairwise sigmoid

"Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training" (2023) treats every (image, text) cell of the N × N matrix as an independent binary question — match or not — scored with a sigmoid. No row or column normalisation, so no cell needs to know about any other.

Worked on the same 3 × 3 cosines

  • At initialisation, t = 10, b = −10: the diagonal logits are 3 − 10 = −7, −7.5 and −7.2, so the positives contribute 7.00, 7.50 and 7.20; each negative contributes at most 0.0003. ℒ = 21.70 / 3 = 7.23. The model starts out believing almost nothing matches — which is correct for a random pair when one in N pairs is positive.
  • With b = −2: positives contribute 0.31, 0.47, 0.37; negatives between 0.15 and 0.69. ℒ = 1.12.
  • With b = 0, the negatives dominate (up to 2.13 each) and ℒ = 2.85. At a real batch of 32,768, each row would carry 32,767 negative terms of about ln 2 = 0.69 at the start of training — about 22,700 nats per row — which is the "heavy imbalance" the bias initialisation exists to avoid.

What the paper reports

  • The sigmoid loss "does not require a global view of the pairwise similarities for normalization", enabling the chunked ring implementation and batch sizes "over 1 million on relatively few devices".
  • Batch size: sigmoid "outperforms the softmax loss significantly with small batch sizes, and performs similarly at larger batch sizes"; both saturate — "a more reasonable batch size of 32 k being sufficient". In one SigLIP run (9B examples seen) the sigmoid peaked at 32k while softmax peaked at 98k; very large batches hurt both.
  • Cheap training: SigLiT (locked g/14 image tower) reached 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet using four TPUv4 chips for two days; a SigLiT B/8 reached 79.7% (Table 1 prints 79.8) in one day on four chips. SigLIP with an unlocked B/16 reached 71.0% on 16 chips in three days.
  • The bias term with −10 initialisation "consistently improves performance"; the paper's corruption experiments (random images, texts or batch alignment) report sigmoid training to be more robust to data noise than softmax.
Takeaway: SigLIP keeps CLIP's dual encoders and batch-as-negatives, but scores each pair independently with a learned scale and a strongly negative bias. That removes the global softmax, cuts the memory peak from N² to n², and works better at small batches. SigLIP-family towers have since been widely adopted as vision encoders in later vision–language systems.

35 Using CLIP in practice

Most practical mistakes with CLIP are mismatches with how the released models were trained and evaluated: preprocessing, tokenisation, normalisation, prompt format and the logit scale. The reference implementation pins each of them down.

The image side

  • Preprocessing (reference clip.load): resize the shorter side to the model's input size with bicubic interpolation, center-crop to a square, convert to RGB, scale to [0, 1], then normalise per channel with mean (0.48145466, 0.4578275, 0.40821073) and standard deviation (0.26862954, 0.26130258, 0.27577711). These are not the ImageNet statistics; using ImageNet's mean and std or bilinear resizing shifts inputs away from the training distribution.
  • Resolution is fixed per model (224, 288, 336, 384 or 448). The center crop discards the edges of non-square images, and small objects or small text may fall below the patch size. For wide images or small details, embed several crops and aggregate, or pick a higher-resolution model.

The text side

  • Use the model's own tokenizer: lower-cased BPE, 77 positions including start and end tokens. Long text raises an error unless truncation is enabled — and truncation silently drops the end of the prompt.
  • Write prompts as captions ("a photo of a {label}.") and, for classification, average a template ensemble per class (normalise each, average, renormalise) — see prompts.

Scoring

zero-shot classification, as in the reference notebooku = normalize( encode_image(x) ) W = [ normalize(prototypeₖ) ]ₖ logits = 100 · u · Wᵀ P = softmax(logits) retrieval and similarityscore(image, text) = u · v (cosine; no scale needed for ranking)
  • Always normalise both embeddings before the dot product; the encoders' raw outputs are not unit length.
  • The factor 100 (the model's learned logit_scale.exp()) matters only if you need probabilities. Omit it and the softmax becomes nearly uniform; the argmax is unchanged.
  • Thresholds on raw cosines are model-specific and modality-pair-specific (the modality gap): calibrate them on held-out data for the checkpoint you use.

Adapting to a task

  • Few labels: start from zero-shot; if you add a linear probe, expect the first few shots to underperform the zero-shot classifier, as the paper found.
  • Many labels: a logistic-regression probe on pre-projection features is a strong, cheap baseline (the paper's protocol). Full fine-tuning gains in-distribution accuracy but can lose robustness — interpolate weights with the zero-shot model (WiSE-FT) or probe before fine-tuning (LP-FT).
  • Choosing a checkpoint: ViT-B/32 is the cheapest released model (50 tokens per image); ViT-L/14@336px the most accurate of the original nine. Open successors trained on curated data (see the family) are usually stronger at equal size; embeddings from different checkpoints are not interchangeable.
Takeaway: bicubic resize, center crop, CLIP's own mean and std, CLIP's own tokenizer, normalised embeddings, caption-style prompts, and ×100 only when you need probabilities. Get those right before concluding that CLIP "does not work" on a task.

36 Pitfalls

Mistakes that recur when training, evaluating or deploying CLIP-style models, each with the section that explains it.

When training

  • A non-differentiable all-gather. Gathering embeddings with an operation that does not propagate gradients trains each device only on its local negatives while the loss still looks normal (sharding).
  • An unbounded or fixed temperature. Without the cap the scale can grow until training destabilises; a badly chosen fixed temperature can under- or over-focus the gradient (temperature).
  • Assuming bigger batches always help. SigLIP found gains saturate — its sigmoid loss peaked at 32k and its softmax baseline at 98k — and a 307k batch hurt both (SigLIP).
  • fp16 loss spikes at large scale — the OpenCLIP study fixed them by switching to bfloat16 (OpenCLIP).
  • Ignoring false negatives. In web data, several captions in a batch may fit an image; the loss punishes all but one. Deduplication and balancing reduce, but do not remove, the problem.

When evaluating

  • Calling it zero-shot when the prompts were tuned on the test labels. Template and class-name engineering used labelled validation data; the paper flags this itself (limitations).
  • Ignoring contamination. Web-scale training sets overlap benchmarks; measure it as in the overlap analysis.
  • Comparing zero-shot numbers across papers with different prompts, class names, resolutions or model sizes (family).
  • Confusing robustness with accuracy. A higher score on a shifted set may be explained by higher ImageNet accuracy alone; effective robustness is the excess over the trend (robustness).

When deploying

  • Open-set assumptions. The softmax always picks one of the supplied labels; an out-of-set image gets a confident wrong label unless you add explicit alternatives or thresholds (zero-shot).
  • Label-set harms. Adding or omitting a class can change who gets misclassified into a harmful category (bias).
  • Text in the image can override its content (typographic attacks), and counting or spatial questions are unreliable (limitations).
  • Mixing embeddings from different checkpoints, or comparing image–image and image–text cosines on one scale (geometry).
  • The name "WIT". CLIP's WebImageText is unreleased; the public "WIT" is a different, Wikipedia-based dataset.
Takeaway: the silent failures are in the plumbing (gathers, preprocessing, normalisation) and in the evaluation protocol (tuned prompts, contamination, mismatched comparisons), not in the loss formula.

38 What to remember

Ten statements that summarise CLIP and its family, each backed by a section above.

Ten statements

  • Two towers, one dot product. Image and text encoders, linear projections, L2 normalisation; the modalities interact only through a cosine.
  • The loss is a batch-wide matching game. Symmetric cross-entropy over the N × N logit matrix, with the other N − 1 items as negatives and softmax-weighted hard-negative emphasis.
  • The scale is learned and bounded. exp(t) starts at 14.29 and is capped at 100; it controls confidence and gradient focus but never the ranking.
  • Efficiency chose the objective. Matching whole captions buys zero-shot accuracy about 12× faster than predicting their words.
  • Balanced web data is the fuel. 400M pairs from 500k queries, capped at 20k per query; later work suggests the curation is a large part of the success.
  • Zero-shot is a linear classifier written in text — sensitive to class names, templates and the label set, and helped by ensembling in embedding space.
  • Text is worth about four labelled examples per class on average, but CLIP cannot easily combine that prior with a few labels.
  • Robustness belongs to the zero-shot model. Adapting to ImageNet raises ImageNet accuracy by 9.2 points and gives the robustness back.
  • The blind spots are structural: counting, relations, word order, unfamiliar domains, text-in-image attacks, and label-set-dependent harms.
  • The family kept the shape and changed the inputs: noisier data (ALIGN), locked towers (LiT), open data and scaling laws (OpenCLIP), curated data (MetaCLIP, DataComp, DFN) and a sigmoid loss (SigLIP).
Takeaway: CLIP showed that a simple contrastive objective on hundreds of millions of image–caption pairs yields an open-vocabulary, robust and reusable visual representation. Its successors have mostly confirmed that the data, not the loss, is where the leverage is.
