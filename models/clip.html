CLIP

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training — train an image encoder and a text encoder together so that an image and its caption land at the same spot in a shared embedding space. The result: open-vocabulary, zero-shot vision, and the backbone of modern text-to-image models.

Model Architectures Multimodal Contrastive

01 What CLIP is

  • A dual-encoder: an image encoder and a text encoder, each mapping its input to a vector in a shared space.
  • Trained so that a matching image and caption have high cosine similarity, and mismatched pairs low — no class labels, just image-text pairs from the web.
  • It brought GPT-style large-scale weak supervision to vision: learn from natural language instead of fixed label sets.
One sentence: CLIP learns a joint image-text embedding by contrastive learning, so you can compare any image to any text by cosine similarity.

02 The contrastive objective

For a batch of N pairs, score every image against every caption (an N×N matrix) and push the diagonal up, everything else down. Drag "training" to watch it sharpen.

Image × text similarity matrix Goal: the diagonal (matching pairs) lights up; off-diagonal goes dark
embeddings L2-normalized → logits = (Iₑ · Tₑᵀ) · exp(τ) loss = ½·[ CE(rows, diag) + CE(cols, diag) ] (symmetric InfoNCE)

The similarity matrix is treated as logits; a symmetric cross-entropy (image→text and text→image) makes each image pick its own caption and vice-versa. A learned temperature τ scales the logits.

03 Architecture

  • Image encoder: a ResNet-50/101 or a Vision Transformer (ViT).
  • Text encoder: a Transformer (~63M params, 12 layers).
  • Each output is linearly projected to the shared space and L2-normalized, so dot product = cosine similarity.
  • Trained from scratch (no ImageNet pretraining) on the pairs.

04 Data: scale over labels

  • ~400 million image-text pairs scraped from the web (WIT) — orders of magnitude larger than ImageNet's 1.2M.
  • The trade: weak supervision (noisy captions) for volume. Natural-language captions are a far richer, more abundant signal than fixed labels.

05 Zero-shot classification

No fine-tuning: turn the label set into sentences and pick the closest.

  1. Write each class as a prompt — "a photo of a {label}".
  2. Embed all prompts with the text encoder (cache them).
  3. Embed the image; compute cosine similarity to each prompt.
  4. Predict the highest-similarity prompt.
  • Prompt engineering & ensembling (averaging many prompt templates) measurably boosts accuracy.
  • Zero-shot CLIP matched a fully-supervised ResNet-50 on ImageNet — without seeing a single ImageNet label.

06 Why it matters

  • Open vocabulary: classify new categories by just writing their names — no retraining.
  • Robustness: strong on distribution shift compared to supervised models.
  • Foundation model: CLIP's text/image embeddings guide text-to-image systems (DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion) and are the visual backbone of many VLMs.
  • Also used for retrieval, captioning guidance, and as a similarity metric (CLIPScore).

07 Limitations

  • Fine-grained & counting: weak on subtle distinctions, counting, and abstract/systematic tasks.
  • Polysemy: a bare word ("boxer") can't be disambiguated without context.
  • Bias: inherits the biases of uncurated web data.
  • Performance still depends on prompt wording.

08 Takeaways

  • CLIP = dual encoders + contrastive alignment in a shared image-text space.
  • Symmetric InfoNCE over the N×N similarity matrix pushes matched pairs (the diagonal) together.
  • Zero-shot by phrasing labels as prompts and taking the closest embedding.
  • Open-vocabulary & foundational — the visual backbone behind VLMs and text-to-image.
  • Builds on ViTs, Transformers, and embeddings.
