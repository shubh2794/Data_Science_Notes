10 WIT: building 400 million image-text pairs

CLIP's dataset, WebImageText (WIT), was built by searching for image-text pairs whose text contains one of 500,000 queries, then keeping at most 20,000 pairs per query. The query list sets which concepts are covered; the cap sets how evenly.

What a per-query cap does to a long-tailed pool a toy Zipf pool calibrated so that 16,000 of 386,000 matched queries exceed 20,000 pairs (the point MetaCLIP reports) · slide the cap and the tail exponent

How the query list was built (paper, footnote 1)

  • Every word occurring at least 100 times in English Wikipedia;
  • augmented with bi-grams with high pointwise mutual information;
  • plus the names of Wikipedia articles above a certain search volume;
  • plus every WordNet synset not already in the list.

The search matched queries against the text only (the paper stresses "sub-string matches"), unlike webly supervised work that relied on image search engines with their own vision-based ranking. The resulting dataset "has a similar total word count as the WebText dataset used to train GPT-2". It was never released.

What the balancing step does

  • Web text is extremely long-tailed: a few terms ("photo", common stopwords) match hundreds of millions of captions, most terms match few or none. Without a cap, a random sample of the pool would be dominated by generic head terms.
  • MetaCLIP, which reconstructed the procedure on CommonCrawl, reports that of 500k entries, 114k had no matches, and only 16k entries (3.2%) had more than 20k matches — yet those accounted for 94.5% of all 5.6B matches. After capping at 20k, "out of 400M pairs, only 20k texts containing 'photo' are kept", against 54M "photo" instances in the pool.
  • So the cap mostly removes head-term pairs and leaves the tail untouched, flattening the concept distribution. MetaCLIP's authors write that "the main ingredient to the success of CLIP is its data and not the model architecture or pre-training objective", and their experiments single out the metadata-plus-balancing curation as the part that matters; that is their interpretation, supported by controlled data experiments (see the data-centric successors).

Takeaway: WIT is 400M pairs selected by a 500,000-query text search and balanced to at most 20,000 pairs per query. The cap is the under-appreciated step: it converts a head-heavy web pool into a broad concept distribution. Note the name clash — a separate Wikipedia-based dataset from 2021 is also called WIT.

11 Why contrastive, not captioning

CLIP's first design predicted captions. It was too slow to scale. The paper's Figure 2 compares three objectives by how quickly zero-shot ImageNet accuracy rises with images processed: a Transformer language model, a bag-of-words predictor, and a contrastive bag-of-words model. Each step to the next was a multiplicative gain in efficiency.

The efficiency multipliers of Figure 2 curve shape is illustrative (log-linear); the horizontal offsets — 3× and a further 4× — are the paper's · slide the target accuracy

The three objectives compared

ObjectiveWhat the text side must doRelative efficiency (paper)
Transformer language model (VirTex-like)generate the exact caption, token by tokenbaseline; the 63M-parameter LM "already uses twice the compute of its ResNet-50 image encoder"
Bag-of-words predictionpredict which words occur, ignoring orderlearns "three times" faster than the LM
Bag-of-words contrastive (CLIP)only say which whole caption in the batch belongs to the image"a further 4x efficiency improvement" — about 12× the LM overall

The argument

  • Predictive objectives "try to predict the exact words of the text accompanying each image. This is a difficult task due to the wide variety of descriptions, comments, and related text that co-occur with images." Much of that difficulty is irrelevant to vision.
  • The contrastive task is "potentially easier": it only needs enough information to tell captions apart, not to reproduce them. The paper cites earlier findings that contrastive objectives learn better representations than equivalent predictive ones, and that generative image models need over an order of magnitude more compute for the same representation quality.
  • An appendix model makes the comparison concrete at full scale: "LM RN50", trained on the same data for the same epochs with an autoregressive caption loss, reaches a 27-dataset linear-probe average of 71.8 against contrastive CLIP-RN50's 75.8, and 65.2 against 73.3 on ImageNet (Table 10; averages computed here from its 27 columns).

What was given up

A contrastive model can only choose among texts it is handed; a captioner can produce new ones. The paper lists this as a limitation and suggests "joint training of a contrastive and generative objective" — the route CoCa later took, reaching 86.3% zero-shot ImageNet (see successors). Generative image-to-text models are the domain of Vision-Language Models.

Takeaway: choosing the objective was the decisive efficiency decision. Predicting words is roughly 12× slower at buying zero-shot ImageNet accuracy than matching whole captions, at the cost of being unable to generate text.

12 Image encoders: the modified ResNets

Five of CLIP's eight image encoders are ResNets — a ResNet-50, a ResNet-101, and three larger models scaled EfficientNet-style to roughly 4×, 16× and 64× a ResNet-50's compute. All share three changes to the classic design, the most distinctive being an attention layer in place of global average pooling.

The three modifications

  • ResNet-D-style stem and downsampling. The paper cites the ResNet-D improvements from the "bag of tricks" paper. Concretely, the reference implementation uses three 3 × 3 stem convolutions instead of one 7 × 7, an average pool instead of the stem's max pool, and an average pool ahead of the 1 × 1 convolution on each downsampling shortcut.
  • Anti-aliased "rect-2 blur pooling". In each downsampling bottleneck the 3 × 3 convolution runs at stride 1 and a 2 × 2 average pool then does the downsampling (ahead of the block's final 1 × 1 convolution); on the shortcut, the average pool comes before the 1 × 1 projection. Downsampling is therefore averaged rather than strided. (The stem's first 3 × 3 convolution keeps stride 2.) This reduces aliasing and makes features more stable under small shifts; the frequency-domain reasoning is in Frequency Domain.
  • Attention pooling. The final global average pool is replaced by "a single layer of 'transformer-style' multi-head QKV attention where the query is conditioned on the global average-pooled representation of the image." The mean of the final feature map is prepended as an extra token, learned position embeddings are added, and only that token acts as the query over all spatial positions; its output, projected, is the image feature.
attention pooling on a ResNet-50 at 224 pxfeature map 7 × 7 × 2048 → 49 tokens + 1 mean token = 50 tokens of width 2048 q = W_q · mean(x), K = W_k · X, V = W_v · X (X includes the mean token) image feature = W_c · MultiHeadAttn(q, K, V) → width 1024 (RN50's embedding dimension)

EfficientNet-style scaling, checked by hand

The paper allocates "additional compute equally to increasing the width, depth, and resolution". Convolution FLOPs scale with depth, with width squared and with resolution squared, so each factor should contribute about the cube root of the target multiplier. For RN50x64 the arithmetic is exact:

RN50 → RN50x64, each dimension ≈ 64^(1/3) = 4× in computedepth: 16 → 64 blocks ×4 width²: (4096 / 2048)² = 2² ×4 resolution²: (448 / 224)² = 2² ×4 product = 64 RN50x4 and RN50x16, same bookkeepingx4: (26/16) · (2560/2048)² · (288/224)² = 1.625 · 1.563 · 1.653 = 4.2 x16: (40/16) · (3072/2048)² · (384/224)² = 2.5 · 2.25 · 2.939 = 16.5

The measured GFLOPs ratios on the paper's axis (21.5/6.1 = 3.5, 75.3/6.1 = 12.3, 265.9/6.1 = 43.6) are lower than these idealised multipliers, as expected when stems, pooling and projection layers do not scale with the backbone.

Takeaway: CLIP's ResNets are modernised ResNet-50s (ResNet-D, blur-pooled downsampling, attention pooling) scaled along width, depth and resolution together. The ResNet family topped out at RN50x64's 73.6% zero-shot ImageNet; the ViTs went further for less compute.

13 Image encoders: the Vision Transformers, and the model zoo

The other three encoders are Vision Transformers — ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 — plus ViT-L/14 fine-tuned for one extra epoch at 336 px. The paper "closely follow[s]" the original ViT with only "an additional layer normalization to the combined patch and position embeddings before the transformer" and "a slightly different initialization scheme".

Token arithmetic

  • Tokens = (resolution / patch)² + 1: 224/32 = 7 gives 49 + 1 = 50; 224/16 = 14 gives 197; 224/14 = 16 gives 257; 336/14 = 24 gives 577.
  • Self-attention cost grows with tokens squared: 50² = 2,500, 197² = 38,809, 257² = 66,049, 577² = 332,929 query–key pairs per head per layer. Going from 224 to 336 px on ViT-L/14 multiplies the attention matrix by 5.0 and the token count — hence the work of every linear layer — by 2.2.
  • The image feature is the class-token output after a final LayerNorm, then the linear projection. How patches, the class token and position embeddings work is covered in Vision Transformers — patchify and the class token.

What the zoo shows

  • The paper finds CLIP ViTs "about 3x more compute efficient than CLIP ResNets" on linear-probe transfer, replicating the original ViT finding that transformers are more compute-efficient than convnets on large enough data.
  • The 336-px fine-tune adds 0.9 points of zero-shot ImageNet (75.3 → 76.2) for one extra epoch — the FixRes observation that evaluating at higher resolution than training helps, done here with a short adaptation.
  • Every "CLIP" number in the paper, unless stated otherwise, refers to ViT-L/14@336px.
Takeaway: nine encoders, one recipe. ViT-B/32 is the cheap workhorse (151M parameters, 50 tokens), ViT-L/14@336px the paper's headline model (577 tokens, 76.2% zero-shot ImageNet). The ViTs dominate the ResNets per unit of compute.

14 The text encoder

A 63M-parameter, 12-layer, 512-wide Transformer with 8 heads and GPT-2's architecture modifications, reading lower-cased byte-pair tokens. The text's representation is the top layer's activation at the end-of-text token, layer-normalised and linearly projected.

Two apparent conflicts, and how far they can be reconciled

  • 49,152 or 49,408? The reference tokenizer builds its vocabulary as 256 byte symbols, 256 byte-plus-end-of-word symbols, 48,894 merges and 2 special tokens. The code sizes the merge list as 49152 − 256 − 2, so 49,152 counts bytes + merges + specials and leaves out the 256 end-of-word byte variants; including them gives 49,408 (the paper's Table 18 and the embedding table's actual size). That the §2.4 figure means exactly this is an inference from the code, not something the paper states.
  • 76 or 77? The paper says the maximum length was capped at 76; the released tokenize uses a 77-position context that includes both boundary tokens, leaving 75 for content. The paper does not say what the 76 counts, so this discrepancy is recorded rather than explained. Text longer than that raises an error unless truncation is requested. For practical purposes, CLIP reads short captions.

Why the [EOS] token, and why causal attention

  • Under a causal mask, only the last position has seen the whole sentence, so it is the natural summary position — the same reasoning as reading a GPT model's final hidden state.
  • The reference code finds it with argmax over token ids: the end-of-text token has the largest id (49,407), so its position is where the id is maximal. Padding with zeros after [EOS] is therefore harmless.
  • The causal mask was kept so that pretrained language-model initialisation or an auxiliary LM loss could be added; the paper leaves both as future work, and the released models were trained from scratch with the contrastive loss alone.

63M, from the configuration

text tower, w = 512, L = 12, vocabulary 49,408, 77 positions, projection to 512token embedding 49,408 · 512 = 25,296,896 positions 77 · 512 = 39,424 12 blocks 12 · (12w² + 13w) = 37,828,608 final LayerNorm 2 · 512 = 1,024 projection 512 · 512 = 262,144 total 63,428,096 ≈ 63.4M

Two-fifths of the "63M" is the token-embedding table. The paper found CLIP "less sensitive to the capacity of the text encoder", which is why it scaled only the text width with the image model; LiT later reported that larger text towers do help (see LiT).

Takeaway: CLIP's text tower is a small GPT-2-style encoder whose sentence vector is the [EOS] state. Remember its short context (75 content tokens) and its lower-casing — both constrain what prompts can express.

15 Projection and L2 normalisation

Each tower ends in a single learned linear map to the shared dimension d_e, followed by division by the vector's length. No non-linear projection head. In the reference code the ViT and text projections are bias-free matrices; for the ResNets the projection is the attention pool's output layer, which does carry a bias. After normalisation, a dot product is a cosine and every embedding lies on the unit sphere.

projection then normalisationz = W · f u = z / ‖z‖₂ ‖u‖₂ = 1 dot product of unit vectors is the cosine, and relates to distanceu · v = cos θ ‖u − v‖₂² = 2 − 2·(u · v)

A small worked example

  • Suppose an image's projected vector is z = (3, 4, 0). Its length is √(9 + 16) = 5, so u = (0.6, 0.8, 0).
  • A caption projects to (0, 2, 2), length √8 = 2.828, so v = (0, 0.707, 0.707).
  • u · v = 0.8 × 0.707 = 0.566. The same answer comes from the raw vectors: (3·0 + 4·2 + 0·2) / (5 × 2.828) = 8 / 14.14 = 0.566. Squared Euclidean distance: 2 − 2 × 0.566 = 0.869.
  • Doubling z to (6, 8, 0) changes nothing after normalisation — the length of a CLIP projection carries no information about the match.

Why linear, and why normalise

  • The paper did "not use the non-linear projection" popularised by image-only contrastive methods, because it "did not notice a difference in training efficiency", and speculated that such heads "may be co-adapted with details of current image only in self-supervised representation learning methods". The general argument for projection heads is in Contrastive Learning — projection heads.
  • Normalising makes the logit bounded (|s·cos| ≤ s), which is what gives the learned scale its meaning, and it stops the loss being lowered by simply inflating vector norms.
  • Because distance and cosine are monotonically related on the sphere, CLIP embeddings drop straight into nearest-neighbour search with either metric (see Embeddings — similarity).

Embedding dimensions and which features to use

  • d_e is 1024 (RN50), 512 (RN101, ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16), 640 (RN50x4), 768 (RN50x16, ViT-L/14, ViT-L/14@336px) and 1024 (RN50x64). Embeddings from different models are not comparable, even at equal dimension.
  • For linear probes the paper uses the feature before the projection (I_f) for the ViTs, and the penultimate layer for the other models it evaluates. Many VLMs likewise tap an internal layer of the vision tower rather than the projected vector (see CLIP as a VLM vision tower).
Takeaway: the projection is one linear layer per tower and the normalisation is non-negotiable: without it, similarities are not cosines and the logit scale loses its meaning. Note that the reference encode_image / encode_text return unnormalised vectors; only the forward pass normalises.

16 Training recipe and compute

All eight models were trained from scratch for 32 epochs over the 400M pairs with Adam and decoupled weight decay, a cosine learning-rate schedule and a 32,768 batch. Hyper-parameters were tuned on a ResNet-50 trained for one epoch and "adapted heuristically" for larger models.

The arithmetic of a run

steps400,000,000 / 32,768 = 12,207 steps per epoch × 32 = 390,625 steps images seen (the paper's figure)32 × 400M = 12.8 × 10⁹ images at one per second: 12.8 × 10⁹ s ≈ 405 years accelerator time of the two largest runsRN50x64: 592 × 18 = 10,656 V100-days ViT-L/14: 256 × 12 = 3,072 V100-days
  • The ViT-L/14 run used under a third of the RN50x64 run's GPU-days and reached a higher zero-shot ImageNet score (75.3 versus 73.6) — the compute-efficiency advantage from the model zoo in wall-clock terms.
  • The "405 years" line is the paper's own, used to make a point about data efficiency: CLIP compensates for deep learning's poor sample efficiency with scale rather than solving it.
  • The paper's appendix notes 32 epochs is where transfer performance "begins to plateau due to overfitting" in its smaller YFCC ablation; with 400M pairs, overfitting "is not a major concern", which is why the recipe is otherwise simple.

What the recipe leaves out

No colour jitter, flips or multi-crop; no dropout or stochastic depth listed; no pretrained initialisation; no auxiliary losses. Later open reproductions kept almost the same recipe (AdamW, β₂ = 0.98, weight decay 0.2) but switched from fp16 to bfloat16 mixed precision after loss spikes at ViT-L/14 scale and above (see OpenCLIP).

Takeaway: 390,625 steps of 32,768 pairs, 12.8 billion images seen, and between 3,000 and 11,000 V100-days for the big models. The recipe is deliberately plain; the novelty is the data, the objective and the scale.
