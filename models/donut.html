Donut — the OCR-free Document Understanding Transformer

Donut is an encoder–decoder Transformer from NAVER CLOVA that reads a document image directly and writes its answer as a token sequence: a Swin Transformer encoder turns the page pixels into a grid of feature vectors, and a BART-style decoder, prompted by a task token, generates a class label, a nested JSON record or a question's answer — with no OCR engine in the system at inference (its largest pretraining set was, however, labelled by one). This note covers it in detail: the problems of OCR pipelines it was built to remove, the preprocessing that puts a page on a 2560 × 1920 canvas, the Swin encoder's stages and windowed attention with their token and cost arithmetic, the multilingual decoder and its tokenizer, a parameter count recomputed from the released configuration, task prompts and the JSON ↔ token conversion, the teacher-forced reading objective, the SynthDoG synthetic-page generator, fine-tuning recipes, the field-F1 and tree-edit-distance metrics, the published results and ablations, limitations, the compressed DONUT-hole with the CKA similarity measure, its relatives Dessurt, Pix2Struct and Nougat, and practical use.

01 What Donut is

Donut (Document understanding transformer; "OCR-free Document Understanding Transformer", arXiv 2111.15664, ECCV 2022) is a Transformer encoder–decoder that maps a document image x to a token sequence y. The encoder is a Swin Transformer that turns the pixels into a set of feature vectors z; the decoder is a four-layer multilingual BART decoder that generates y one token at a time, attending to z through cross-attention. Every task — reading, classification, field extraction, question answering — is trained with the same loss: the cross-entropy of the next token.

encoder: the page image becomes a set of n feature vectors of width dz = Swin(x), x ∈ ℝ^(3 × H × W), z = {z₁ … zₙ}, zᵢ ∈ ℝ^d (donut-base: H × W = 2560 × 1920 → n = 80 · 60 = 4,800, d = 1,024) training objective: next-token cross-entropy with teacher forcing, over the target tokens after the promptL(θ) = − Σₜ₌₁ᵐ log p_θ(yₜ | y₁ … yₜ₋₁, z) inference: start from a task prompt and decode greedily until the end tokenŷₜ = argmax_v p_θ(v | prompt, ŷ₁ … ŷₜ₋₁, z), stop at </s>; then JSON = token2json(ŷ)

What goes in and what comes out

  • Input: one page image, resized and padded onto a fixed canvas (2560 × 1920 pixels, height × width, for the pretrained model), plus a short prompt of special tokens that names the task — <s_cord-v2>, <s_rvlcdip>, or <s_docvqa><s_question>…</s_question><s_answer>.
  • Output: a token sequence such as <s_menu><s_nm>Latte</s_nm>…</s_menu>, in which every JSON key is a pair of added special tokens. A deterministic parser turns it back into a JSON object; a key whose closing token is missing is lost.
  • Training: pretraining to read the whole page aloud ("pseudo-OCR") on 11M scanned pages and 2M synthetic ones, then fine-tuning on each downstream task with the target written as a token sequence.

02 Why remove the OCR stage

Before Donut, visual document understanding (VDU) systems were built in two stages: an OCR engine detects text boxes and recognises their characters, and a model such as BERT, LayoutLM or LayoutLMv2 interprets the recognised words. The Donut paper names three problems with making OCR a hard dependency: its computational cost, its inflexibility across languages and document types, and the propagation of its errors into every later stage.

The three problems, as the paper states them

  • Cost. High-quality OCR is expensive at inference time, and a strong engine is itself a large model — the paper notes that a recent advanced OCR system requires more than 80M parameters (its references are CRAFT text detection plus a four-stage recognition framework). In its classification table every OCR-based baseline is written "+ α" parameters, because the OCR engine's parameters and time come on top.
  • Inflexibility. An off-the-shelf engine rarely adapts to a new language or domain; training one requires large datasets and substantial compute.
  • Error propagation. A misread character becomes a wrong input token, and the downstream model has no access to the pixels to correct it. The paper says this is more severe in languages with complex character sets, such as Korean and Chinese, where OCR quality is relatively low. The usual remedy — a post-OCR correction module — adds system size and maintenance cost.

What the measurements looked like

The paper's own timings put numbers on the cost argument. On RVL-CDIP classification, LayoutLMv2 takes 1,489 ms per page including Microsoft's OCR API, and Donut 752 ms on an NVIDIA P40 — an older and slower GPU than the A100 used for training. On CORD receipt parsing the BERT, BROS, LayoutLM and LayoutLMv2 baselines take 1.6–1.7 s per receipt and Donut 1.2 s. The appendix also shows that the OCR-based baselines' accuracy on CORD varies strongly with which of four engines (EasyOCR, PaddleOCR, Microsoft Azure, CLOVA OCR) supplies the text, with CLOVA OCR the most accurate there. The tables are in results; the category-level version of the argument, with a probability model of compounding errors, is on the hub's pipeline versus end-to-end section.

Takeaway: the case against OCR is not that OCR is bad but that it is a separate system: its cost is paid on every page, its language coverage caps the whole pipeline's, and its mistakes are invisible to the model that comes after it. Donut's bet is that one network trained end to end can learn to read well enough to make that separation unnecessary.

Why information extraction is the hard case

The appendix breaks document IE into three sub-skills: (a) reading the text, (b) understanding what it means, and (c) predicting relations and structure among the extracted pieces. Earlier work that extracts a few fixed keys needs only (a) and (b). CORD and the in-service receipt set require (c) as well: which name, count and price belong to the same line item. The paper's Ticket and Business Card sets have depth-1 structure (each key once), CORD depth 2. That is why Donut reports a second, structure-aware metric (tree-edit-distance accuracy) next to field F1.

How the OCR-based baselines solve the same tasks

  • Classification: a linear layer and softmax on the [CLS] vector of the encoder.
  • IE: BIO tagging of the serialised OCR tokens. For depth-2 structures the paper uses group BIO tags — a pair such as Bg.Ik marks both the start or continuation of a group (a menu item) and of a key inside it.
  • DocVQA: extractive span prediction — start and end positions over the OCR tokens — so the answer must be a contiguous string of recognised words.
Takeaway: the benchmark set spans a single label (RVL-CDIP), flat records (tickets, business cards), nested records (CORD, Korean receipts) and free-text answers (DocVQA). A generative decoder handles all four with one output interface, where the encoder baselines need a different head for each.

04 Architecture at a glance

Donut is a standard encoder–decoder: a vision Transformer encoder (Swin) produces a sequence of image feature vectors, and a text Transformer decoder produces output tokens, reading the image vectors through cross-attention in every layer. Nothing in the network corresponds to text detection, character recognition or reading order; all of that must be learned inside these two modules.

Encoder

The paper says any CNN or Transformer image backbone could serve as the encoder; it chose Swin Transformer because it performed best in a preliminary document-parsing study. Swin first cuts the image into non-overlapping 4 × 4 patches, then applies blocks of window-based multi-head self-attention and a two-layer MLP, with patch-merging layers between stages that halve the grid and double the channel width. The output of the final block — the whole final-stage grid, not a pooled vector — is what the decoder sees.

Decoder

The decoder is BART's decoder, initialised from a publicly available multilingual BART checkpoint (asian-bart-ecjk, an mBART whose vocabulary was pruned to English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean). Only its first four layers are kept, "in further consideration of the speed–accuracy trade-off". It has no encoder of its own on the text side: the image features take the place of BART's text encoder output.

Interface

Training uses teacher forcing: the ground-truth token sequence is fed as decoder input, shifted by one, and every position is a classification. At test time the decoder starts from a task prompt — "inspired by GPT-3", in the paper's words — and generates. New special tokens are added for each task's prompt and for each JSON key; task prompts and the JSON conversion cover them.

Grid sizes are computed from the canvas: stage k has (H / 2^(k+1)) × (W / 2^(k+1)) tokens of width 128 · 2^(k−1). Depths {2, 2, 14, 2}, heads {4, 8, 16, 32} and the decoder's 4 layers × 16 heads × d = 1,024 are from the donut-base configuration on the Hugging Face Hub.

Takeaway: architecturally Donut is unremarkable — a Swin encoder, a truncated BART decoder, cross-attention between them. What makes it work is the resolution it runs at, the reading pretraining, and the token interface that turns generation into structured output.

05 From page to canvas: preprocessing

Donut's encoder is built for one fixed input size, so every page is fitted onto a fixed canvas without distortion: optionally rotated so its long side matches the canvas's, resized so its shorter side equals the canvas's shorter side, shrunk further if it still overflows, and padded to the exact canvas size. Aspect ratio is preserved; any mismatch becomes padding.

Worked example: an A4 page scanned at 300 dpi

An A4 page at 300 dpi is 2480 × 3508 pixels (w × h). On the 2560 × 1920 canvas: step 1 scales the shorter side 2480 to 1920, s₁ = 0.7742, giving 1920 × 2715 (w × h). Step 2 finds the height 2715 exceeds 2560 and shrinks by 2560 / 2715 = 0.9429, giving about 1810 × 2560. Step 3 pads (1920 − 1810) = 110 columns, 55 on each side. Overall the page is scaled by 0.73, so 10-point text, whose em is 300 · 10 / 72 = 41.7 pixels in the scan, is about 30 canvas pixels tall — about 7.6 of Swin's 4-pixel patches. The same page on the 1280 × 960 canvas is scaled by 0.365 and its 10-point em shrinks to about 15 pixels, under 4 patches.

Implementation details that matter

  • Axis alignment. The original code and the Hugging Face DonutImageProcessor rotate a page by 90° when its orientation disagrees with the canvas's (align_long_axis). donut-base's released processor enables it; the CORD fine-tuned processor and the fine-tuning configurations disable it, since receipts are already upright.
  • Random padding places the page at a random offset inside the canvas during training (the original prepare_input(random_padding=True)), a cheap augmentation that stops the model from tying content to absolute canvas position.
  • Legacy size order. The released preprocessor_config.json files store size as a list [width, height] — [1920, 2560] for donut-base — while the encoder config's image_size is [2560, 1920] as height, width. The Transformers processor reverses list-valued sizes when it loads them, which maps [1920, 2560] back to height 2560, width 1920.
  • Channels and normalisation. The model takes three channels; grayscale scans are converted to RGB first. The processors published with the Hub checkpoints normalise with mean 0.5 and standard deviation 0.5 per channel, whereas the original repository's prepare_input uses the ImageNet statistics (mean 0.485, 0.456, 0.406; std 0.229, 0.224, 0.225). Use the processor that ships with the checkpoint you load, and do not mix the two.

Page sizes are physical sizes times scan resolution (A4 210 × 297 mm; US Letter 8.5 × 11 in; an 80 × 200 mm receipt; a 13.33 × 7.5 in slide; an 87 × 55 mm ticket, drawn landscape). The steps follow the Transformers DonutImageProcessor: rotate if enabled and the orientations disagree, resize the shorter side to min(H, W) (integer truncation), thumbnail into H × W, pad centred. Takeaway: preprocessing decides how many pixels each character gets before the model sees anything. A tall receipt or a landscape page on a portrait canvas loses much of the canvas to padding; choose the canvas orientation, or enable long-axis alignment, to match the documents.

06 The Swin encoder: patches, stages and tokens

Donut's encoder is Swin-B "with slight modification": the stage depths are {2, 2, 14, 2} instead of Swin-B's {2, 2, 18, 2}, and the attention window is 10 × 10 instead of 7 × 7. Everything else follows Swin-B: 4 × 4 patches, a first-stage width C = 128 that doubles at each stage, heads {4, 8, 16, 32} (32 dimensions per head), relative position bias, and no absolute position embedding.

Why a hierarchical encoder suits pages

  • Fine early, coarse late. Stage 1's 4 × 4-pixel tokens can resolve strokes of small print; stage 4's 32 × 32-pixel tokens each summarise a region about one line of 10-point text tall (about 30 canvas pixels for an A4 page, see preprocessing) and a few characters wide, a sensible unit for the decoder to attend over.
  • Depth where tokens are cheap. A standard Swin-B puts 18 of its 24 blocks in stage 3; Donut keeps 14 of its 20 there, where there are 16× fewer tokens than at stage 1.
  • A plain ViT would not scale. A ViT-B with 16 × 16 patches on the same canvas would produce 160 × 120 = 19,200 tokens and compute global attention over all of them in every layer; Swin's windowed attention keeps the cost linear in image area (next section). The Donut paper's backbone ablation compares ResNet-152, EfficientNetV2, ViT-B and two Swin-B variants, and finds EfficientNetV2 and Swin best, choosing Swin "due to the high scalability of the Transformer-based architecture and higher performance over the EfficientNetV2's".

07 Window attention and shifted windows

Each Swin block computes self-attention only inside non-overlapping M × M windows of tokens, so a token attends to M² others instead of all hw. Consecutive blocks alternate between the regular window grid (W-MSA) and a grid displaced by (floor(M/2), floor(M/2)) tokens (SW-MSA), which lets information cross window borders. A learned relative position bias B is added to the attention logits inside each window.

Shifted windows and the cyclic shift

With only regular windows, no information would ever cross a window border. Shifting the grid by half a window in the next block puts tokens from four neighbouring windows into one window. Naively the shifted grid has (ceil(h/M) + 1) × (ceil(w/M) + 1) windows, some smaller than M × M; Swin instead cyclically rolls the feature map toward the top-left so the number of windows stays the same, and masks attention between tokens that were not neighbours before the roll. The relative-bias table has (2M − 1)² entries per head — 361 at M = 10 — because relative offsets along each axis range over −(M − 1) … M − 1; Swin notes the table can be bicubically interpolated when fine-tuning with a different window size, and the Donut fine-tuning configurations warn that some weights are re-initialised when the input resolution differs from pretraining.

08 The decoder and its multilingual tokenizer

The decoder is an mBART-style Transformer decoder with 4 layers, width d = 1,024, 16 heads and a 4,096-wide feed-forward layer, initialised from the first four decoder layers of asian-bart-ecjk — a 12 + 12-layer mBART whose embedding table was pruned to the sub-words used by English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean. Its tokenizer is a SentencePiece model loaded as an XLM-RoBERTa tokenizer.

09 Cross-attention: how the decoder reads the page

In each decoder layer, every output position forms a query from its hidden state and attends over all 4,800 encoder vectors, which supply the keys and values. This is the only path from pixels to text: to write the next character of an item name, the decoder has to draw its evidence from the region of the page where that name is printed.

What the paper observed

The paper visualises the decoder's cross-attention maps on an unseen receipt while it generates "3002-Kyoto Choco Mochi" and reports that each generated piece attends to the corresponding text region, even though Donut is trained with no localisation supervision. It suggests these maps "can be used as an auxiliary indicator" — a weak, unsupervised form of grounding. They are not boxes: the attention can be diffuse, split across repeated strings, or pulled toward the wrong one of two similar numbers, and nothing in training forces them to be sharp.

Cost of one decoding step

  • Cross-attention: per layer, per step, 16 heads × 4,800 scores of 64-dimensional dot products — about 4.9M multiply–accumulates, plus the same again to mix the values; across 4 layers roughly 39M per token, independent of how long the output is so far.
  • Self-attention: grows with the prefix length t, but t is at most 768 for CORD and the self-attention KV cache is small compared with the cross-attention cache above.
  • Consequence: decoding time grows at least linearly with output length; a long transcription at 1,536 tokens needs roughly 100 times the decoder work of a 15-token record, which is why short targets (a class, a short answer) decode quickly while pretraining-style full-page reading is slow.
What one decoding step looks at pick the token being generated · adjust how peaked the attention is

Illustrative, not model output: the attention map is a softmax over the 4,800-cell (80 × 60) stage-4 grid of a 2560 × 1920 canvas, with logits that fall off with distance from the box of the text being generated (the focus slider scales them), mimicking what the paper's Figure 8 shows. The receipt layout is invented for this note. The readout computes the share of attention mass inside the true box, the entropy, and the effective number of cells attended (e to the entropy).

Takeaway: cross-attention is Donut's reading head. It gives a free, unsupervised hint of where each output came from, but it is not a guarantee — which is the root of Donut's grounding and verification problems (limitations).

11 Task prompts and start tokens

Donut tells the decoder which task to perform with a prompt of special tokens placed before the output. The released code names them differently (as in the table), and the fine-tuned checkpoints expect the code's names exactly — a prompt that does not match the checkpoint is a likely reason for a fine-tuned Donut to generate nothing useful.

How the loss mask is built

The training code tokenizes prompt + target + </s> as one sequence and copies it into the labels, then sets to −100 (ignored by the cross-entropy) every padding position and every position up to and including the prompt end token. For CORD and RVL-CDIP the prompt end token is the task token itself, so only it is masked; for DocVQA it is <s_answer>, so the whole question is masked and the model is trained only to produce the answer, conditioned on the question it reads as input.

Categorical tokens

Where a field has a small closed set of values, a value can be a single added token instead of free text: the 16 RVL-CDIP classes become <advertisement/> … <specification/>, and DocVQA's yes/no answers become <yes/> and <no/>. Classification then takes three generated tokens plus the end token, which fits within the RVL-CDIP fine-tuning configuration's max_length of 8.

Takeaway: the prompt is Donut's task switch and the loss mask decides what is learned. A new task needs only a new start token, new key tokens, and targets written in the same scheme — no new head.

12 The JSON ↔ token-sequence conversion

A target record is serialised into tokens by a recursive rule: a dictionary becomes, for each key k, <s_k> + its serialised value + </s_k>; a list becomes its serialised items joined by <sep/>; a leaf becomes its string, or its categorical token if one exists. The generated sequence is parsed back by matching each opening key token with its closing token; the paper calls the mapping "one-to-one invertible" for well-formed sequences.

Worked example

The record {"menu": {"nm": "Latte", "cnt": "2"}, "total": {"total_price": "9,000"}}, with the key order kept, serialises to

<s_cord-v2><s_menu><s_nm>▁Latte</s_nm><s_cnt>▁2</s_cnt></s_menu><s_total><s_total_price>▁9,000</s_total_price></s_total></s>

— 18 tokens: the prompt, 10 key tokens (5 keys × open and close), 6 text pieces, and the end token. Two-thirds of the 17 predicted tokens are structure. Parsing reverses it: the first opening token is <s_menu>, its content contains further key tokens so it is parsed recursively into {"nm": "Latte", "cnt": "2"}, and so on. With the default reverse-alphabetical sorting the same record would be written total first, then menu.

What the conversion does not preserve

  • Types. Every leaf becomes a string; 2 and "2" serialise identically. Numbers keep whatever formatting the ground truth used ("9,000", "Rp. 39.000").
  • One-element lists. The parser returns a list only when it sees <sep/>, so ["Latte"] comes back as "Latte" — harmless for evaluation, which normalises both sides, but a schema consumer must handle both shapes.
  • Malformed output. If a closing token is missing, that key is lost without an error. At the top level its opening token is simply deleted; inside a group, the enclosing key's value can come back as raw text with the orphaned tag in it — dropping </s_total_price> yields {"total": "<s_total_price>11,500"}. If no key tokens are found at all the parser returns {"text_sequence": …} with the raw text. The paper states the policy as "if the output token sequence is wrongly structured, we simply treat the field is lost".
  • Vocabulary growth. Each new key adds two tokens and two new rows of the 1,024-wide embedding, initialised fresh; keys that are rare in training get little signal.

14 Pretraining: learning to read

      
Donut is pretrained to read: given a page image, generate all of its text in reading order, "from top-left to bottom-right, basically". The paper calls this a pseudo-OCR task and describes the model during pretraining as "a visual language model over the visual corpora". The corpora are 11M real scanned pages from IIT-CDIP and 2M synthetic pages from SynthDoG in four languages.

      
The data

      
    
        
  • IIT-CDIP — 11M scanned English document images (the same collection LayoutLM pretrains on). It has no reliable transcription, so the authors ran the commercial CLOVA OCR API over it to produce pseudo text labels. Donut is therefore OCR-free at inference, but its largest pretraining set was labelled by an OCR engine — its reading ability on real scans is learned partly from OCR outputs, errors included.
    • 
        
  • SynthDoG — 0.5M synthetic pages per language for English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean, rendered from Wikipedia text, where the transcription is exact by construction. The paper's argument for synthetic data is that real scanned corpora with transcriptions are not always available, "especially for languages other than English".

15 SynthDoG: the synthetic document generator

      
SynthDoG (Synthetic Document Generator) renders a fake document photo in four layers — a background image, a paper texture, a rule-based text layout, and text content in a chosen language and font — then degrades the result with photographic effects. Because the text is placed by the generator, its transcription is known exactly, so in principle any language with a text corpus, fonts and tokenizer coverage can be pretrained on without a single real labelled page.

  • Background: an ImageNet image, randomly Gaussian-blurred to mimic out-of-focus capture.
    •

    • 
        
  • Layout: a heuristic generator places several rectangular text regions (paragraphs) by randomly stacking grids; each region is split into lines; text size and margins are random.
    • 
        
  • Text: words and phrases sampled from Wikipedia in the target language, rendered in Noto fonts (chosen because they cover many scripts) with random colours.
    •

17 Metrics: field-level F1 and tree-edit-distance accuracy

      
Donut reports two metrics for information extraction. Field-level F1 counts a predicted key–value pair as correct only if exactly the same pair is in the ground truth. Tree-edit-distance (TED) accuracy converts both records to trees and measures how many node insertions, deletions and relabellings turn the prediction into the ground truth, normalised by the cost of building the ground truth from an empty tree. The first gives no credit for partial matches and cannot tell which item a field was grouped with (its flattened key paths do record nesting); the second rewards partial matches and scores the grouping.

      
field-level F1, micro-averaged over the test set (flattened (path, value) pairs, exact match)F1 = TP / (TP + (FP + FN) / 2)
TED-based accuracy (the paper's definition; gt, pr, ∅ = ground truth, prediction, empty tree)acc = max(0, 1 − TED(pr, gt) / TED(∅, gt))
edit costs in the released evaluator (zss tree edit distance, Zhang–Shasha)insert / delete: key node 1, leaf "v" = len(v)        relabel: key↔key 0 or 1,  leaf↔leaf = Levenshtein(v, v′),  key↔leaf = 1 + len(leaf)

      
How a record becomes a tree

      
The evaluator first normalises both records — keys sorted by (length, name), every value wrapped in a list, empty values dropped — then builds a tree: a root, one node per key, a <subtree> node for each dictionary inside a list, and one leaf per string value. Because leaves cost their length to insert, a long missing string costs more than a short one, and a one-character misread costs 1.

19 Ablations: backbone, resolution, OCR engine and data size

      
The paper's further studies isolate four factors: which vision backbone to use, how large the input canvas must be, how much the OCR-based baselines depend on their OCR engine, and how the models behave with few training examples. Most are reported as bar or line charts without printed values; the numbers below are the ones the text states.

      
Backbone

      
Figure 7(b) compares ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-152, EfficientNetV2, ViT-B and Swin-B with 18 and with 14 stage-3 blocks, on CORD and DocVQA. EfficientNetV2 and Swin outperform the others; Swin is chosen for its scalability and higher score. The paper attributes the gap to "the high expressiveness of the backbones", not to a document-specific property.

      
Resolution

      
Figure 7(c) shows performance growing rapidly with canvas size across 640 × 640, 960 × 960, 1280 × 960 and 2560 × 1920, most clearly on DocVQA, "where the images are larger with many tiny texts". The stated CORD numbers are 91.1 TED accuracy at 0.7 s per image with 1280 × 960 against 90.9 at 1.2 s with 2560 × 1920 (Table 2) — on receipts the larger canvas costs 70% more time for no gain in full-data accuracy, but the paper reports it was more robust with only 80 training examples. The repository lists the released 1280 × 960 CORD-v2 checkpoint at 91.3. The authors note an efficient attention mechanism could reduce the cost but keep the original Transformer for simplicity.

20 Handwriting, robustness and failure cases

      
On DocVQA's handwritten questions Donut scores 72.1 ANLS, above LayoutLMv2-Large-QG's 67.3, although its overall score is 19.2 points lower (67.5 vs 86.7). The paper's explanation is the one it opened with: handwriting is where OCR is weakest, and an OCR-based model cannot answer better than its OCR reads.

Where the robustness comes from, and where it stops

      
    
        
  • Joint reading and understanding. A plausible mechanism, not one the paper tests: the decoder knows the question while it reads, so it can use context ("the passenger's name") to resolve an ambiguous scrawl, where an OCR engine must commit to a transcription first.
    • 
        
  • No dedicated handwriting data. Donut's pretraining corpora are IIT-CDIP scans (mostly print, with some handwritten documents — RVL-CDIP's "handwritten" class is drawn from the same collection) and rendered fonts; its handwriting ability presumably comes from those scans.     • 
        
  • Low overall DocVQA score. 67.5 ANLS is below even LayoutLM-base with OCR (69.8). Most DocVQA pages are printed, dense and small-fonted — plausibly the setting where a commercial OCR engine reading the full-resolution scan has the advantage over Donut reading a 2560 × 1920 canvas.
    • 
      

      
Takeaway: OCR-free models degrade differently from pipelines. A pipeline fails where OCR fails — handwriting, unusual scripts, degraded scans; Donut fails where resolution fails — small print on large pages — and fails fluently, with confident answers that may come from the wrong place.

    


    
    

      
21 Limitations

      
Donut's design trades an external dependency for constraints inside the model: a fixed canvas that bounds legibility and cost, a single page per input, an output budget, and a generative decoder whose answers are not tied to locations on the page. Some of these are fixable engineering; others are properties of the approach.

      
Resolution against memory and time

      
Legibility needs pixels: the fine print that OCR engines read at scan resolution must survive resizing onto the canvas. Doubling both canvas sides quadruples the stage-1 tokens and roughly quadruples encoder cost; activations grow the same way. The paper's own training shows the effect — about half an hour on one A100 for CORD at 1280 × 960 against about a day on 64 A100s for DocVQA at 2560 × 1920 (on a much larger dataset).

      
Fixed aspect ratio and single pages

      
    
        
  • Aspect ratio. Every page is fitted into one rectangle. Very tall or wide documents lose most of the canvas to padding. The Pix2Struct paper fine-tuned Donut on InfographicVQA, whose images are large with extreme aspect ratios, and measured 11.6 ANLS against Pix2Struct-Large's 40.0, speculating that Donut's fixed-aspect scaling is the cause.
    • 
        
  • One page. The model reads one image. Multi-page documents need splitting and a merge step outside the model, or page tiling, neither of which the paper addresses.
    • 
        
  • Output budget. Generation stops at max_length (1,536 in pretraining, 768 for CORD). A long invoice with many line items can exceed it; the truncated sequence loses its closing tokens and, by the parser's rule, the fields they close.
    • 
      

      
Hallucination and grounding

      
    
        
  • Generated, not extracted. An OCR-based tagger can only label words that exist; Donut can write any string in its vocabulary. A misread becomes a plausible wrong value, and a value can come from the wrong place on the page (the "943" example).
    • 
        
  • No boxes, no confidences by default. The output carries no location. Cross-attention maps give a hint; token probabilities give a rough confidence; neither is calibrated or supervised.
    • 
        
  • Repetition. Autoregressive decoders can loop. Nougat, which uses the same encoder–decoder design for long academic pages, reports repetition as a major failure mode and adds anti-repetition augmentation and a logit-variance detector; Donut's short targets make this rarer but not impossible.
    • 
      

      
Coverage

      
    
        
  • Languages are bounded by the four-language vocabulary and the pretraining data; a new script needs vocabulary changes and new SynthDoG data.
    • 
        
  • Pseudo-labels. The 11M real pretraining pages were transcribed by an OCR API, so systematic OCR mistakes on real scans could be learned during pretraining.
    • 
        
  • Dense-page reading lags OCR: DocVQA 67.5 against 78.1 for LayoutLMv2 on the same training data.
    • 
      

      
Takeaway: use Donut where its failure modes are acceptable — short structured outputs from documents of known format and size — and add what it lacks outside the model: schema validation, per-field confidence thresholds, and a human check for fields that matter.

23 CKA: comparing two networks' representations

      
Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA; "Similarity of Neural Network Representations Revisited", Kornblith et al., 2019) measures how similar two layers' representations are by comparing the similarity structure they impose on the same m examples. It is invariant to rotations and isotropic scaling of either representation, and does not require the two layers to have the same width, which is why DONUT-hole can compare a pruned or Swin-T-based student with the original.

      
two layers' activations on the same m inputs: X ∈ ℝ^(m × d₁), Y ∈ ℝ^(m × d₂); Gram matrices and centringK = XXᵀ,   L = YYᵀ,   H = Iₘ − (1/m) 11ᵀ,   K′ = HKH,   L′ = HLH
Hilbert–Schmidt independence criterion, as DONUT-hole writes itHSIC₀(K, L) = vec(K′) · vec(L′) / (m − 1)²
CKA normalises HSIC; with linear kernels it equals a feature-space formCKA(K, L) = HSIC₀(K, L) / √(HSIC₀(K, K) · HSIC₀(L, L))  =  ‖Yᶜᵀ Xᶜ‖²_F / (‖Xᶜᵀ Xᶜ‖_F · ‖Yᶜᵀ Yᶜ‖_F)

      
Worked example

      
Four inputs, a teacher layer with two features and a student layer with one: X = [[2, 0], [0, 1], [−2, 0], [0, −1]] and Y = [[2], [0], [−2], [0]] — the student kept the teacher's high-variance feature and dropped the other. Both are already centred (column means are zero). Then Xᵀ X = [[8, 0], [0, 2]], so ‖XᵀX‖_F = √(64 + 4) = 8.246; Yᵀ Y = [[8]], so ‖YᵀY‖_F = 8; and Yᵀ X = [8, 0], so ‖YᵀX‖²_F = 64. CKA = 64 / (8.246 · 8) = 0.970. Had the student kept the low-variance feature instead, Y = [[0], [1], [0], [−1]], then ‖YᵀX‖²_F = 4, ‖YᵀY‖_F = 2, and CKA = 4 / (8.246 · 2) = 0.243. Rotating X by any angle, or multiplying it by 3, leaves CKA with the original at exactly 1.

      
The kernel form gives the same answer: here K′ = K, and HSIC₀(K, L) = 64 / 9 = 7.11, HSIC₀(K, K) = 68 / 9 = 7.56, HSIC₀(L, L) = 64 / 9 = 7.11, so CKA = 7.11 / √(7.56 · 7.11) = 0.970. The example shows why magnitude pruning and CKA fit together: keeping the directions that carry most of the variance keeps most of the similarity structure.

      
How DONUT-hole uses it

      
The paper computes layer-wise CKA between each trained model and DONUT-base-11M, and a global index by averaging layer scores (for decoders of different depth, a student layer is compared with the corresponding teacher layer). Findings it reports: the first encoder and decoder layers are highly similar across all models; encoder layers stay similar whatever the backbone (Swin-T, Swin-B or pruned Swin-B); the largest differences are in the last decoder layer; and DONUT-hole's 0.79, lower than the unpruned models', coexists with the best task scores — which the authors read as a more compact representation, and which is a reminder that CKA measures similarity, not quality.

      

        

          Linear CKA between a teacher and a transformed student
          pick a transformation · add noise · watch the Gram matrices and the score
        

        

          
          
          
        

        
        

        
The teacher is a fixed 8 × 3 activation matrix (illustrative, listed in the script) with column variances in a 16 : 4 : 1 ratio. The student is computed from it by the chosen operation; "magnitude-prune" multiplies the teacher by a random 3 × 3 map whose smallest-magnitude entries are zeroed at the chosen sparsity; noise adds seeded Gaussian noise with standard deviation noise/10 times the teacher's largest column standard deviation. The heatmaps are the centred Gram matrices K′ and L′; CKA is computed from them with the HSIC formula above, and cross-checked against the feature-space formula.

      

      
Takeaway: CKA asks "do these two layers arrange the same inputs the same way?", ignoring rotation and scale. It is a diagnostic of how much of the teacher's internal structure a compressed model kept, not a score of how well the compressed model works.

    


    
    

      
24 Relatives: Dessurt, Pix2Struct, Nougat and VLM readers

      
Donut, with the concurrent Dessurt, opened a line of image-to-text document models that read pixels and generate text. Dessurt appeared at nearly the same time with a different way of mixing image and task; Pix2Struct generalised the idea to web screenshots and variable resolution; Nougat specialised Donut's architecture to academic PDFs. General vision-language models have since absorbed the approach. These models have no pages of their own in these notes; the hub compares them at category level (the members compared).

      
Dessurt (arXiv 2203.16618)

      
"End-to-end Document Recognition and Understanding with Dessurt" (Brigham Young University and Adobe Research) describes itself as sharing "an architecture similar in design" with Donut, differing "primarily in how the cross attention occurs and in pre-training". It takes a 1152 × 768 two-channel image (the grayscale page and a highlight mask used by some tasks) and a query string, and processes three token streams — visual, query and response — through eight cross-attention layers in which the Swin-style visual layers also attend to the query, the query attends to the whole visual array, and the response attends to both. It is trained from scratch (no pretrained weights fit its architecture) on IIT-CDIP plus three synthetic sets: Wikipedia text, 800,000 lines of synthetic handwriting, and synthetic forms whose label–value pairs were generated with GPT-2. With 127M parameters it reports 93.6% on RVL-CDIP and 63.2 ANLS on DocVQA, and it covers tasks Donut's paper does not, including full-page handwriting recognition and named-entity recognition on the IAM handwriting database.

      
Pix2Struct (arXiv 2210.03347)

      
"Pix2Struct: Screenshot Parsing as Pretraining for Visual Language Understanding" (Google Research and collaborators) keeps the pixels-in, text-out interface but changes the pretraining and the input. It is pretrained on 80M screenshots of web pages (URLs from the C4 corpus) to generate a simplified HTML parse of a partly masked screenshot, after a short "warm-up" reading curriculum. Its ViT encoder takes a variable-resolution input: the image is scaled, preserving aspect ratio, to the largest size whose 16 × 16 patches fit a sequence budget (2,048 in pretraining), with 2-D absolute position embeddings for each patch's row and column. Task text such as a question is rendered onto the image as a header. Base has 282M parameters (12 encoder and 12 decoder layers), Large 1.3B. On DocVQA it reports 72.1 (Base) and 76.6 (Large) ANLS against Donut's 67.5, and on InfographicVQA 38.2 and 40.0 against 11.6 for a Donut the authors fine-tuned; across nine benchmarks it reports gains over Donut of 9 to 53 points.

      
Nougat (arXiv 2308.13418)

      
"Nougat: Neural Optical Understanding for Academic Documents" (Meta AI) builds directly "on the Donut architecture" to convert rendered pages of scientific papers into Markdown with LaTeX mathematics and tables. Pages are rendered at 96 dpi onto an 896 × 672 canvas; the encoder is a Swin-B initialised from pretrained weights; the mBART-style decoder has 10 layers and a maximum length of 4,096 tokens (350M parameters), or 4 layers and 3,584 tokens in a 250M "small" variant. It is trained on 8,204,754 pages — 7,511,745 from arXiv, whose LaTeX source provides aligned targets, 536,319 from PubMed Central and 446,777 from the Industry Documents Library — and decodes greedily. On its arXiv test set it reports a normalised edit distance of 0.071 (base) against 0.255 for the text embedded in the PDF and 0.312 for GROBID. It also documents the design's weaknesses at length: repetition loops, handled with anti-repetition augmentation and a detector on the variance of top logits, and failure on non-Latin scripts, which "result in instant repetitions".

Vision-language models as document readers

      
From 2023 onward, general vision-language models — a vision encoder feeding a large language model — took over much of what Donut was built for: reading a page, answering questions about it, emitting JSON on request, usually at dynamic resolution rather than on a fixed canvas. 26 Practical usage

      
Donut is available in the Hugging Face Transformers library as a VisionEncoderDecoderModel with a DonutProcessor (image processor + tokenizer), and in the original donut-python package. The checkpoints under the naver-clova-ix organisation — donut-base, and fine-tunes for CORD, train tickets, RVL-CDIP and DocVQA — are MIT-licensed like the code. Using one correctly comes down to four things: the processor, the prompt, the generation settings, and the parsing.

      
The steps

      
    
        
  1. Preprocess with the checkpoint's own processor. processor(image, return_tensors="pt").pixel_values applies that checkpoint's canvas size, long-axis setting and normalisation (preprocessing). Changing the canvas from the fine-tuning size can hurt accuracy; measure it before relying on it.
    2. 
        
  2. Build the prompt exactly as in training and tokenize it without extra special tokens: <s_cord-v2>, <s_rvlcdip>, or <s_docvqa><s_question>{question}</s_question><s_answer>.
    3. 
        
  3. Generate with decoder_input_ids = the prompt, max_length = the decoder's max_position_embeddings (or a task-appropriate bound), eos_token_id and pad_token_id from the tokenizer, use_cache=True, and bad_words_ids=[[unk_token_id]] to forbid the unknown token — the settings in the library's documented examples.
    4. 
        
  4. Parse: decode, remove </s> and padding, strip the first task token, and call processor.token2json(sequence).
    5.

Beam search can help when a field's reading is genuinely ambiguous, but it does not fix misreads caused by insufficient resolution and it multiplies decoding time by roughly the beam width; the released inference code and Nougat both use greedy decoding. A practical confidence signal is the minimum or mean token probability inside each field's span, thresholded on validation data, with low-confidence fields routed to review.

      
Fine-tuning on your own schema

      
    
        
  • Write each training target as JSON in one consistent key order and value format; add the key tokens and a task token to the tokenizer and resize the decoder's embedding.
    • 
        
  • Choose the canvas from your documents' size and smallest text; choose max_length from the longest serialised target.
    • 
        
  • Start from donut-base, use a learning rate around 1 × 10⁻⁵ – 1 × 10⁻⁴ with warm-up, and select checkpoints by validation edit distance or TED accuracy of generated outputs.
    • 
        
  • Quantisation is supported by the library (the documentation shows int4 weight-only quantisation with torchao); measure the accuracy change on your data.
    • 
      

      
Takeaway: most failed Donut deployments fail on plumbing — wrong processor, wrong prompt, too small a max_length — rather than on the model. Match the training-time preprocessing and prompt exactly, decode greedily, and validate the parsed JSON.

    


    
    

      
27 Pitfalls

      
The mistakes below each produce plausible-looking output, which is what makes them costly: a generative model rarely crashes on bad input, it writes something.

      
Input and preprocessing

      
    
        
  • Wrong canvas orientation. A landscape document on the 2560 × 1920 portrait canvas without long-axis alignment is shrunk to fit the 1920-pixel width, losing up to a quarter of its linear resolution relative to a matched canvas (a quarter for pages at least 4:3 wide).
    • 
        
  • Mixing normalisations. The Hub processors (mean 0.5, std 0.5) and the original repository (ImageNet statistics) differ; pairing a checkpoint with the other preprocessing shifts every input pixel.
    • 
        
  • Size lists read in the wrong order. [1920, 2560] in a processor file is width, height; [2560, 1920] in a model or training config is height, width.
    • 
        
  • Downscaled scans. Feeding a 100-dpi thumbnail loses information before the model sees it; the canvas cannot restore resolution.
    • 
      

      
Targets and prompts

      
    
        
  • Prompt mismatch. <s_cord> instead of <s_cord-v2>, or the paper's <parsing> instead of the code's names, starts generation from a prompt the model was never trained on.
    • 
        
  • Key order drift. Training targets must use one key order; with sort_json_key on, keys are written reverse-alphabetically, so "total" precedes "menu".
    • 
        
  • Value formatting drift. "9,000", "9000" and "9.000" are different targets and different token sequences (and number strings split irregularly: "14,000" is ▁1 · 4,000 but "28,000" is ▁28, · 000). Normalise values before training or after parsing, not inconsistently.
    • 
        
  • Truncated targets. A training target longer than max_length is cut, teaching the model to stop mid-record.
    • 
      

      
Evaluation

      
    
        
  • Comparing F1 across protocols. Donut's field-level F1 over parsed trees is not the entity F1 of OCR-based papers.
    • 
        
  • Reporting only one metric. F1 hides structure errors and TED accuracy hides exact-field failures; report both.
    • 
        
  • Trusting silent drops. The parser discards malformed fields without warning; count them.
    • 
        
  • Ignoring the OCR-free model's own "OCR" errors. Donut can misread; measure character error rate on the fields that matter, not just record-level scores.
    • 
      

      
Takeaway: most Donut pitfalls are inconsistencies between training and inference — in pixels, prompts, key order or value format. Write them down once as a data contract and test that inference follows it.

29 Takeaways

      
Donut showed in 2021 that a plain encoder–decoder Transformer, pretrained only to read, could replace the OCR stage of document understanding for classification and structured extraction, with competitive accuracy and lower end-to-end latency. Its limits — resolution, grounding, single pages — defined the agenda for the models that followed.

      
What to remember

      
    
        
  • Design: a hierarchical, windowed vision encoder makes a 2560 × 1920 input affordable; a short, wide decoder with key tokens turns generation into records.
    • 
        
  • Training: one next-token loss for every stage; reading as the pretraining task; synthetic pages (SynthDoG) to reach languages without labelled scans; real scans still matter for real-scan tasks.
    • 
        
  • Evaluation: field F1 and TED accuracy measure different things; the Donut paper's protocol re-scores OCR-based baselines end to end, so its numbers are not comparable with those papers' own.
    • 
        
  • Strengths: structure-heavy extraction (CORD TED 90.9 vs 82.4), handwriting (72.1 vs 67.3 ANLS), language flexibility, speed without an OCR call.
    • 
        
  • Weaknesses: dense small print (DocVQA 67.5), fixed aspect ratio (InfographicVQA 11.6 in Pix2Struct's fine-tune), no grounding, silent parse failures.
    • 
        
  • Compression: DONUT-hole's prune-then-distil recipe recovered a model destroyed by pruning; measure its speed rather than assume it, and read CKA as similarity, not quality.
    • 
        
  • Legacy: Pix2Struct, Nougat and today's vision-language document readers keep Donut's interface — pixels in, tokens out.
    • 
      

      
Takeaway: Donut is the reference design for OCR-free document understanding. Read the Document AI Models hub for where it sits among the alternatives, LayoutLM for the OCR-based design it was measured against, and Document Intelligence for how either fits into a working pipeline.
