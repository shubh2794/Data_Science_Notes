EfficientNet

A family of convolutional image classifiers, B0 to B7, built in two moves: a small baseline network found by architecture search, and one rule, compound scaling, that grows its depth, width and input resolution together by fixed ratios. The paper (“EfficientNet: Rethinking Model Scaling for Convolutional Neural Networks”, arXiv 1905.11946, 2019) matched the best ImageNet accuracy of its time with about an eighth of the parameters. This page covers the scaling rule and its arithmetic, the MBConv block, the B0 architecture counted layer by layer, the training recipe, the results, and what later work showed about the gap between FLOPs and real speed.

01 What EfficientNet is

EfficientNet is a baseline convolutional network (B0) plus a rule for scaling it. The rule multiplies the number of layers by d, the number of channels by w and the input side length by r, and ties all three to one knob, the compound coefficient φ. The family B1–B7 is B0 scaled by larger values of φ.

compound scaling, the whole idea in four linesdepth: d = αᵠ width: w = βᵠ resolution: r = γᵠ subject to α · β² · γ² ≈ 2, α ≥ 1, β ≥ 1, γ ≥ 1 found once, by a small grid search on B0α = 1.2 β = 1.1 γ = 1.15 → α·β²·γ² = 1.92027

The two separable contributions

  • A method for scaling. FLOPs of a convolutional network grow roughly as d · w² · r², so the constraint α·β²·γ² ≈ 2 means each step of φ roughly doubles the compute. φ says how much bigger; α, β, γ say how to split the extra compute between depth, width and resolution. You search α, β, γ once on the small model and then reuse them at every size.
  • A good thing to scale. Scaling does not change the layer types, so the baseline matters. B0 came from a neural architecture search over a MobileNet-style space. It is built from MBConv blocks (inverted residual bottlenecks with depthwise convolutions) with squeeze-and-excitation and the Swish/SiLU activation, and it costs about 0.39 B multiply-adds and 5.3 M parameters at 224×224.

What it achieved, stated with its caveats

  • EfficientNet-B7 reached 84.3% ImageNet top-1 (validation, single crop) with 66 M parameters and 37 B FLOPs. GPipe reported the same 84.3% with 557 M parameters, so B7 used about 8.4× fewer parameters.
  • EfficientNet-B4 reached 82.9% at 4.2 B FLOPs, close to ResNet-50's 4.1 B FLOPs and 76.0%. (The introduction quotes 83.0% and +6.7 for this comparison; Table 2 says 82.9%. §33 lists the mismatches found.)
  • The efficiency is measured in parameters and FLOPs. On GPUs and TPUs, where depthwise convolutions and large activation maps are memory-bound, the wall-clock advantage is much smaller, and later work found it can reverse. §28–§31 cover this.
Takeaway: EfficientNet = an NAS-found MBConv baseline + one knob φ that raises depth, width and resolution together as 1.2ᵠ, 1.1ᵠ, 1.15ᵠ, which roughly doubles FLOPs per unit of φ. The scaling rule is the transferable idea. The specific B0–B7 checkpoints are one application of it.

02 Why scaling needed a rule

Before 2019, a bigger model usually meant a model that had been scaled along one axis. Deeper ResNets (18 → 200 layers), wider WideResNets, or larger inputs as in GPipe (480×480). How to pick among these, or combine them, was left to manual tuning.

The status quo the paper was reacting to

  • Depth was the default axis because residual connections made it trainable. Returns diminish, though: the paper cites a ResNet-1000 that is about as accurate as a ResNet-101.
  • Width (channels per layer) was the usual axis for small models. MobileNet's “width multiplier” shrinks or grows every layer's channel count by one factor. The paper points out that some literature calls this a “depth multiplier”, which means the same as its width coefficient w. The naming is a common source of confusion.
  • Resolution had drifted upward: 224 in early networks, 299 for Inception-v3, 331 for NASNet, 480 for GPipe. It was rarely treated as a design variable in its own right.
  • Each axis alone saturates near 80% on the paper's baseline (§06). Tuning two or three at once by hand is expensive, because every candidate is a full ImageNet training run.

The central question, as the paper frames it

  • Is there a principled way to scale a ConvNet that gives better accuracy for the same added compute than scaling one dimension? The answer offered is empirical, not theoretical. Balance the three dimensions, and a constant ratio between them, found on the small model, keeps working as the model grows.
  • A second practical motive: large models were hitting accelerator memory limits (GPipe needed pipeline parallelism across devices). Further accuracy gains needed better efficiency, not just more parameters.
the budget arithmetic that motivates a ruleyou have 2× the FLOPs. options: depth only d = 2.00, w = 1, r = 1 → FLOPs × 2.00 width only d = 1, w = 1.414, r = 1 → FLOPs × 2.00 resolution only d = 1, w = 1, r = 1.414 → FLOPs × 2.00 compound (φ = 1) d = 1.2, w = 1.1, r = 1.15 → FLOPs × 1.92
Takeaway: the contribution is less a new layer than a budget allocation policy. For a given increase in compute, it tells you how much to spend on depth, how much on width and how much on pixels, and claims one fixed split works across a 100× range of model sizes.

04 The scaling problem, formally (equations 1–2)

The paper first writes a ConvNet as a list of stages. Then it restricts scaling to changes that leave every layer's type alone. That restriction is what makes the problem small enough to search.

A network as stages of repeated layers

A layer is a function Yᵢ = Fᵢ(Xᵢ) on a tensor of shape ⟨Hᵢ, Wᵢ, Cᵢ⟩ (batch omitted). A network is their composition. Real ConvNets group layers into stages in which one layer type repeats, apart from the first layer, which often downsamples. ResNet has five such stages, and B0 has nine rows in Table 1. So:

equation 1: a ConvNet as s stagesN = ⨀ᵢ₌₁…ₛ Fᵢᴸⁱ ( X⟨Hᵢ, Wᵢ, Cᵢ⟩ ) Fᵢᴸⁱ : layer type Fᵢ repeated Lᵢ times in stage i ⨀ : composition over the stages, first to last ⟨Hᵢ, Wᵢ, Cᵢ⟩ : input shape of stage i

Model scaling as an optimisation over three scalars

Architecture design searches over Fᵢ. Model scaling keeps every F̂ᵢ of a baseline fixed and only changes how many times each is repeated (Lᵢ), how many channels it has (Cᵢ), and at what resolution it runs (Hᵢ, Wᵢ). Choosing those per stage is still a large space. The paper therefore requires all stages to scale by the same ratio, which leaves three scalars:

equation 2: the constrained scaling problemmax over d, w, r Accuracy( N(d, w, r) ) s.t. N(d, w, r) = ⨀ᵢ₌₁…ₛ [ F̂ᵢ repeated d·L̂ᵢ times ] ( X⟨ r·Ĥᵢ , r·Ŵᵢ , w·Ĉᵢ ⟩ ) Memory(N) ≤ target memory FLOPS(N) ≤ target flops hatted symbols are the baseline's (Table 1)F̂ᵢ, L̂ᵢ, Ĥᵢ, Ŵᵢ, Ĉᵢ fixed; d, w, r free

What the uniform-ratio assumption gives up

  • Per-stage allocation. Some stages might benefit from extra depth more than others. EfficientNetV2 later argued exactly this and added layers mainly to its later stages (§29). Equation 2 cannot represent that.
  • Integer effects. d·L̂ᵢ and w·Ĉᵢ must be integers, and the reference code rounds them (§13). The realised network is therefore not exactly N(d, w, r). For small L̂ᵢ the realised depth ratio can be far from d.
  • Memory is a constraint in the formulation but not in the method. Equation 2 includes a memory bound, but the rule in equation 3 only controls FLOPs. Activation memory grows with d·w·r², and it is what later limited the large models in practice (§28).
Takeaway: by fixing the operators and forcing one ratio per dimension across all stages, the paper turns “design a bigger network” into “choose three numbers”. The rest of the method is about choosing them well.

05 Why FLOPs grow as d · w² · r², and why only approximately

The constraint α·β²·γ² ≈ 2 comes from one cost formula. The exponents are exactly 1, 2 and 2 for an ordinary convolution, and they are not exact for the blocks EfficientNet actually uses.

Derivation for one ordinary convolution

A k×k convolution from C_in to C_out channels, producing an H′×W′ map, does one multiply-add per weight per output position (full derivation on counting one layer exactly):

cost of one ordinary conv layerMACs = H′ · W′ · k² · C_in · C_out scale width by w and resolution by rC_in → w·C_in, C_out → w·C_out, H′ → r·H′, W′ → r·W′ MACs → (r·H′)(r·W′) · k² · (w·C_in)(w·C_out) = w² · r² · MACs scale depth by dd times as many such layers → d · MACs togethertotal MACs ∝ d · w² · r²

Width enters squared because it multiplies both the input and output channel counts. Resolution enters squared because it multiplies both spatial sides. Depth enters linearly because it only adds more layers of the same cost. So doubling depth doubles FLOPs, and doubling width or resolution quadruples them.

baseline56 · 56 · 9 · 64 · 64 = 3 136 · 36 864 = 115 605 504 MACs ≈ 115.6 M w = 2 (128 → 128)56 · 56 · 9 · 128 · 128 = 462 422 016 = 4.00 × baseline r = 2 (112 × 112 output)112 · 112 · 9 · 64 · 64 = 462 422 016 = 4.00 × baseline d = 2 (two such layers)2 · 115 605 504 = 231 211 008 = 2.00 × baseline

Why it is only approximate for EfficientNet

  • Depthwise convolutions are linear in width. A depthwise k×k on C channels costs H′W′·k²·C, which is one C, not two. Scaling width by w multiplies it by w · r², not w² · r².
  • The SE MLP ignores resolution. Its two small dense layers act on a pooled vector, so they scale like w² with no r². The final FC layer scales like w only (1280·w inputs, 1000 outputs).
  • The stem's input has 3 channels whatever the width, so the stem is linear in w.
  • Rounding of channels to multiples of 8 and of repeats up to integers (§13) moves every scaled model off the ideal curve. The paper's footnote 3 says “FLOPS may differ from theoretical value due to rounding.”
  • Why the approximation is still good: in B0, counted below, the 1×1 convolutions (expand, project, head), which really do scale as w²·r², account for about 87% of all MACs, and about 94% in B7. The linear-in-width terms are a small correction.
How each multiplier moves the bill the ideal law (lines) against an exact recount of B0 scaled one axis at a time (dots)

Takeaway: FLOPs ∝ d·w²·r² is exact for plain convolutions. For EfficientNet it holds for the 1×1 convolutions, which are about 87% (B0) to 94% (B7) of the compute, and the rest grows more slowly, so realised costs land a little below the law. The exponents are why β and γ appear squared in the constraint and α does not.

07 What each axis buys, and why each one runs out

The saturation has a different cause on each axis, and seeing the three causes is what makes the balancing argument plausible.

Depth: more composition, harder optimisation

  • Buys: more nonlinear stages, so richer and more abstract features, plus a larger receptive field (each extra k×k layer adds (k−1)·jump pixels).
  • Runs out because: optimisation gets harder with depth (vanishing gradients), and residual connections and BatchNorm only partly fix it. The paper's example is ResNet-1000 matching ResNet-101. The mechanics are on Residual & Skip Connections.

Width: finer features, shallow abstraction

  • Buys: more filters per layer, so more fine-grained patterns at each level of abstraction. Wide networks are also easier to train, as the WideResNet results showed.
  • Runs out because: a very wide but shallow network has many detectors at each level and too few levels. It struggles to build high-level features.

Resolution: more pixels, same field of view

  • Buys: more pixels per object, so fine texture and small objects survive downsampling. Detection networks run at 600+ px for this reason.
  • Runs out because: with a fixed network, each unit's receptive field, measured in pixels, stays the same while objects grow in pixels. A unit that covered a whole dog's face at 224 px covers part of it at 560 px. The network has more detail but no more capacity or context to use it.

So “add depth so the receptive field covers the bigger image” cannot be literally about the theoretical field. What depth increases is the effective receptive field, the central region that actually carries gradient mass, which is much smaller and grows roughly with the square root of depth (see effective vs theoretical receptive field). The paper's intuition is best read in that effective sense.

Takeaway: depth runs out on optimisation, width on abstraction, resolution on context. Each axis is limited by what the other two supply, and that points to scaling them together.

Why interaction is the expected result

  • Higher resolution puts more pixels on each object, so you need more depth to grow the effective receptive field over it and more width to hold the extra fine-grained patterns it exposes.
  • This is a statement about the marginal value of each axis: it depends on where the other two are. With interaction like this, scaling one axis at a time with the others fixed will usually miss the optimum. It is the same reason one-at-a-time hyperparameter search misses the optimum when parameters interact (see why random beats grid).
Takeaway: width scaling on a deeper, higher-resolution base beats width scaling on the plain base at equal FLOPs. That result motivates spending compute on all three axes at once.

09 Compound scaling (equation 3)

Having argued for balance, the paper chooses the simplest balanced rule: scale each axis geometrically in one shared exponent φ.

equation 3depth d = αᵠ width w = βᵠ resolution r = γᵠ s.t. α · β² · γ² ≈ 2, α ≥ 1, β ≥ 1, γ ≥ 1 consequence, from section 05FLOPs(φ) ≈ FLOPs(0) · (α·β²·γ²)ᵠ ≈ FLOPs(0) · 2ᵠ
Five ways to grow the same network bar height = channels, bar count = layers, input square = resolution · every mode is matched to the compound model's compute

How to read the rule

  • φ is the budget knob, set by the user: “I have about 2ᵠ times the FLOPs of B0.”
  • α, β, γ are the split, set once by search. Taking logs makes the split explicit: log(FLOP ratio) = φ·(log α + 2 log β + 2 log γ), so a fixed fraction of each extra doubling goes to each axis. §10 computes that fraction.
  • Geometric, not linear. Equal ratios per unit φ, not equal increments. That matches how the three terms enter the cost multiplicatively.
  • Why the constraint equals 2 rather than anything else: convenience. It makes φ read as log₂ of the FLOP ratio. Any constant above 1 would define the same family with φ rescaled.

Hand-worked: φ = 2 from the formula

α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15, φ = 2d = 1.2² = 1.44 w = 1.1² = 1.21 r = 1.15² = 1.3225 → 224·1.3225 = 296.2 px ideal FLOP ratio = 1.44 · 1.21² · 1.3225² = 1.44 · 1.4641 · 1.74901 = 3.6874 ( = 1.92027² )

The paper's Table 3 and Table 7 “compound scale” rows use d=1.4, w=1.2, r=1.3. That is close to this φ = 2 point, rounded, and not the φ = 1 point (1.2, 1.1, 1.15). They are compared against single-axis models of about 4× FLOPs (d=4, w=2, r=2), so the comparison is at roughly the φ ≈ 2 budget.

Takeaway: compound scaling fixes the direction of growth in (log d, log w, log r) space, and φ moves along that ray. Everything else in the method is about choosing the ray.

10 The grid search, and α·β²·γ² = 1.92, not 2

Step 1 of the method fixes φ = 1 and searches α, β, γ on B0 under the constraint. Step 2 freezes them and scales. The reported optimum is α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15.

The product, computed exactly

α · β² · γ²1.2 · 1.1² · 1.15² = 1.2 · 1.21 · 1.3225 = 1.452 · 1.3225 = 1.920270 what that does to the family's budgetφ = 1 : 1.920 vs 2 ( −4.0% ) φ = 3 : 7.08 vs 8 ( −11.5% ) φ = 7 : 96.3 vs 128 ( −24.8% )

So “≈ 2” allows about a 4% shortfall per unit of φ, which compounds. A φ = 7 model would ideally cost about 96× B0, not 128×. The released B7, counted exactly in §12, costs 97.6× B0. That is very close to 1.92⁷, although, as §11 shows, B7's individual multipliers are not the formula's.

What the paper does not report about the search

  • The grid's range, step, the tolerance on “≈ 2”, how many candidates were trained, and for how long. The figure's 0.05 step and ±5% band are illustrative choices, not the paper's.
  • Whether the optimum was sharp or the neighbourhood was flat. With 0.05 steps, many (α, β, γ) triples satisfy the constraint to within a few percent, and small ImageNet accuracy differences between them may be within run-to-run noise.
  • The paper notes that searching α, β, γ directly on a large model could do better, but is too expensive. Searching once on the small model and reusing the result is the cost-saving assumption. Nothing in the paper shows the optimum is the same at every size.
Takeaway: α·β²·γ² is 1.92, so each φ step buys about 1.92× compute, not 2×. About 43% of the log-compute growth goes to resolution, the largest share of the three.

14 The architecture search that produced B0

Scaling cannot fix a weak baseline, because scaling never changes F̂ᵢ. B0 was found with the MnasNet search space and a MnasNet-style multi-objective reward. The one difference the paper names is FLOPs instead of measured phone latency in the reward.

the multi-objective rewardreward(m) = ACC(m) · [ FLOPS(m) / T ]ʷ T = 400 M (target FLOPs) w = −0.07 where −0.07 comes from (MnasNet's reasoning)“doubling the cost buys about 5% relative accuracy” → 2ʷ = 1/1.05 w = −ln 1.05 / ln 2 = −0.0704

What the exponent means

  • w is the exchange rate between accuracy and cost. With w = −0.07, a model that doubles FLOPs must raise accuracy by a factor of 2⁰·⁰⁷ = 1.0497, about 5% relative, just to keep the same reward. Halving FLOPs is rewarded by the same factor.
  • It is a soft constraint. Nothing forbids 600 M FLOPs, it is just discounted. MnasNet defined the exponent piecewise, one value below T and another above. Setting 0 below and −1 above gave a hard constraint, and −0.07 on both sides gave the soft one. EfficientNet uses a single exponent, the symmetric soft setting.
  • The objective is scale-free in cost. Only the ratio FLOPS/T matters, so the reward traces a smooth Pareto front rather than a cliff at T.

Hand-worked: three candidates

T = 400 M, w = −0.07A: 76.0% at 400 M → 0.760 · pow(1.0, −0.07) = 0.760 · 1.00000 = 0.7600 B: 77.5% at 800 M → 0.775 · pow(2.0, −0.07) = 0.775 · 0.95264 = 0.7383 C: 74.0% at 200 M → 0.740 · pow(0.5, −0.07) = 0.740 · 1.04971 = 0.7768 break-even accuracy for B to tie A0.760 · pow(2, 0.07) = 0.760 · 1.04971 = 79.78%

B's +1.5 points is not enough, because at double the cost it would need about +3.8. C wins, trading 2 points for half the compute. With the reward symmetric around T, being cheaper is rewarded as much as being more expensive is penalised. The target is where the search is centred, not a ceiling. B0 landed at 0.39 B, within 3% of T.

Reward landscape ACC · (FLOPS/T)ʷ iso-reward curves in (FLOPs, accuracy) space; drag the candidate with the sliders

The search space and procedure (inherited from MnasNet)

  • Factorised hierarchical space. The network is split into a fixed number of blocks (stages). Each stage independently chooses its convolution type (regular, depthwise, or mobile inverted bottleneck with expansion 3 or 6), kernel size (3×3 or 5×5), squeeze-and-excitation ratio (0 or 0.25), skip operation, output channels, and number of repeated layers. Layers within a stage are identical, which is exactly the Fᵢᴸⁱ structure equation 1 assumes.
  • Search algorithm. MnasNet used a recurrent controller trained with reinforcement learning (policy gradient via PPO). Each sampled child network was trained briefly on a proxy ImageNet task and scored with the reward above. The EfficientNet paper states only that it used “the same search space” and a multi-objective search. It does not report compute, number of samples or proxy settings for the B0 search.
  • Why FLOPs and not latency: “we are not targeting any specific hardware device.” That choice is what makes B0 hardware-neutral on paper, and it is also the root of the later speed critiques (§28).
Takeaway: B0 is, as far as the paper says, MnasNet's search setup rerun with a FLOP budget of 400 M and a 5%-per-doubling exchange rate. Because the reward is soft, the result sits at the target by choice, not by force. Because it is FLOP-based, it inherits no knowledge of how fast any particular chip runs it.

Design features worth noticing

  • Mixed kernel sizes. The search chose 5×5 depthwise kernels in stages 4, 6 and 7. In depthwise form a 5×5 costs 25/9 ≈ 2.8× a 3×3 on the depthwise term only, and the depthwise term is under 10% of the network's MACs (§05), so larger kernels are cheap here in a way they are not in ordinary convolutions.
  • Stage 2 has no expansion (MBConv1). The search chose this and the paper gives no reason. A likely one: with 32 input channels at 112×112, expanding 6× would create a 192-channel map at the highest resolution in the network, which is expensive in both FLOPs and memory.
  • Channel counts are small. The widest block output is 320, while ResNet-50 reaches 2048. Capacity comes from the 6× expanded hidden width (up to 1152) inside blocks and from the 1280-wide head.
  • SE in every block, with the squeeze width set to a quarter of the block's input channels (§19).
  • Head: 1×1 conv 320 → 1280, global average pooling, dropout (0.2 for B0), then a 1280 × 1000 fully connected layer. The head holds 32% of B0's parameters and 5.5% of its MACs.
Takeaway: B0 is a 3×3 stride-2 stem, seven MBConv stages at expansion 6 (except the first), mixed 3×3/5×5 kernels, SE in every block, and a 1280-channel head. The paper calls it “similar to MnasNet”. It differs from MnasNet-A1, which uses SE in only some stages, an expansion-3 stage, and different stage widths. It is a mobile network that happens to scale well.

Conventions behind the count

  • Convolutions have no bias (BatchNorm follows each one). Each BatchNorm contributes 2 learned parameters per channel (scale γ, shift β). The two SE convolutions have biases. The classifier has a bias.

17 The MBConv block: inverted residual with a linear bottleneck

Every stage after the stem is a stack of one block type, the mobile inverted bottleneck from MobileNetV2 with squeeze-and-excitation added. Four operations in a fixed order, plus a skip connection when the shapes allow.

MBConv(e, k), input x of shape H × W × C_in1. expand h₁ = swish( BN( Conv1×1(x) ) ) C_in → e·C_in (skipped when e = 1) 2. depthwise h₂ = swish( BN( DWConv k×k, stride s (h₁) ) ) e·C_in → e·C_in, H → H/s 3. SE h₃ = h₂ ⊙ σ( W₂ · swish( W₁ · GAP(h₂) + b₁ ) + b₂ ) 4. project h₄ = BN( Conv1×1(h₃) ) e·C_in → C_out ← NO activation 5. skip y = x + DropPath(h₄) if s = 1 and C_in = C_out, else y = h₄
One MBConv block, live bar height ∝ channels · the counts below are computed from the settings, term by term

Why each design choice

  • “Inverted”: a ResNet bottleneck goes wide → narrow → wide and puts the skip on the wide ends. MBConv goes narrow → wide → narrow and puts the skip on the narrow ends. The tensors that cross block boundaries, and are kept for the skip, are small. The expensive spatial filtering happens in the wide space, where a depthwise conv is cheap because it costs k² per channel instead of k²·C.
  • “Linear bottleneck”: the projection has no activation. MobileNetV2's argument is that a ReLU applied in a low-dimensional space destroys information: once the manifold of features is squeezed into few channels, zeroing half of each coordinate is lossy. A linear projection keeps it. EfficientNet uses Swish rather than ReLU, but keeps the projection linear.
  • Expansion 6 is MobileNetV2's default. The depthwise stage works on six times the input channels. Expansion 1 (stage 2) drops the expand conv entirely.
  • Skip only when shapes match. The reference code adds the residual only if stride is 1 and input channels equal output channels. There is no projection shortcut, unlike ResNet's. The first block of every stage therefore has no skip. B0 has 9 skip-connected blocks out of 16.
  • DropPath (stochastic depth) is applied only on skip-connected blocks, because only there does dropping the branch leave a valid identity path. See §21 for the per-block rate.
Takeaway: MBConv = 1×1 expand (×e) → k×k depthwise (stride here) → SE gate → 1×1 linear project → add input if the shape is unchanged. Wide in the middle, narrow at the ends, no activation after the projection.

The general pattern, from the formula

  • For stride-1 blocks with C_in = C_out = C, expand and project each cost H²·e·C² and depthwise costs H²·k²·e·C. The ratio of depthwise to 1×1 work is k² / (2C): 9/160 = 5.6% at C = 80 with 3×3. The depthwise term matters only when channels are very few, as in stage 2.

19 Squeeze-and-excitation inside MBConv

SE turns each block's channels into a context-dependent reweighting. It pools the whole map to one number per channel, passes that vector through a tiny two-layer MLP, and multiplies every channel by the resulting gate in (0, 1).

squeeze-and-excitation on a map h of shape H × W × Esqueeze zᶜ = (1 / HW) · Σᵢⱼ h[i, j, c] global average pool → vector z ∈ ℝᴱ excite g = σ( W₂ · swish( W₁ z + b₁ ) + b₂ ) W₁ : S × E, W₂ : E × S scale h′[i, j, c] = gᶜ · h[i, j, c] EfficientNet's choice of squeeze width SS = max(1, floor(0.25 · C_in)) ← a quarter of the BLOCK INPUT channels

The ratio is of the input channels, not the expanded ones

  • The reference code computes num_reduced_filters = int(input_filters · se_ratio) with se_ratio = 0.25, where input_filters is the block's input width, not the expanded width it gates. Torchvision's implementation does the same (max(1, input_channels // 4)).
  • With expansion 6, a squeeze of C_in/4 is E/24 of the gated width. So relative to the channels it gates, EfficientNet's SE bottleneck is narrower than SENet's original E/16, not wider as “0.25” suggests.
  • The inner activation is Swish in EfficientNet, where SENet used ReLU, and the gate is a sigmoid.

Cost, and where it bites

  • Compute is negligible (0.8% of B0's MACs), but the parameters are 12% of B0 (§16).
  • It is a global synchronisation point. Every spatial position must be pooled before any output can be scaled. That needs a full pass over the expanded map, which makes it harder to fuse the block into one streaming kernel and adds memory traffic. EfficientNet-Lite removes SE; its release notes give the reason as poor support on some mobile accelerators (§32).
Takeaway: SE = global pool → tiny MLP (squeeze to a quarter of the block's input channels) → sigmoid gates on every channel. It is cheap in FLOPs, 12% of the parameters, and it adds a global synchronisation point inside every block.

20 The activation: Swish, also called SiLU

EfficientNet replaces ReLU with x · σ(x). The function was found by an automated search over activation formulas, and the same function had been proposed earlier as the sigmoid-weighted linear unit (SiLU). It is smooth, non-monotonic, and unbounded above.

What it changes, and what it costs

  • A small but nonzero gradient for negative inputs (−0.088 at x = −3, 0.072 at x = −1), so units do not “die” the way ReLU units can (see the dying ReLU).
  • The derivative exceeds 1 for x above 1.2785, the mirror image of the minimum. It peaks at 1.0998 near x ≈ 2.40 and then decays back towards 1. This is harmless in a BatchNormed network but worth knowing.
  • Memory: the backward pass needs σ(x), so a naive implementation stores extra tensors. The reference code's native swish uses a custom gradient to save memory, and frameworks now ship fused SiLU kernels.
  • Quantisation: the sigmoid is awkward in int8. EfficientNet-Lite switches to ReLU6. MobileNetV3's “hard-swish” x · ReLU6(x+3)/6 is a piecewise-linear approximation (the reference builder includes it as an option).
  • How much Swish alone contributes to EfficientNet's accuracy is not ablated in the paper. The search paper reported gains of a few tenths to about a point over ReLU on ImageNet models.
Takeaway: Swish = x·σ(x): ReLU-like for large |x|, smooth near zero, slightly negative with a minimum of −0.2785 at x ≈ −1.28. It helps accuracy a little and costs something in memory and quantisation friendliness.

Why each piece is there

  • RMSProp with momentum and a tiny weight decay is inherited from the MnasNet/MobileNet recipes, which are tuned for depthwise-heavy networks. 28 FLOPs are not latency: depthwise convolutions, memory traffic and activation size

EfficientNet optimised FLOPs because FLOPs are hardware-neutral. The price is that the resulting network does little arithmetic per byte of data it moves, and on GPUs and TPUs data movement, not arithmetic, often sets the speed.

The roofline argument in one paragraph

An operation is compute-bound if it performs more arithmetic per byte moved than the hardware's ratio of peak compute to memory bandwidth (the “ridge point”), and memory-bound otherwise. A V100, for example, offers about 125 TFLOP/s of fp16 tensor-core arithmetic against about 900 GB/s of memory bandwidth, so the ridge is roughly 139 FLOPs, or about 70 MACs, per byte. Below that, adding FLOPs is nearly free and removing them saves nearly nothing. What matters is bytes moved.

Hand-worked: arithmetic intensity of the two halves of an MBConv

stage-5 repeated block, 14×14, E = 480, fp16 (2 bytes), batch 1, minimum trafficdepthwise 3×3: MACs 14²·9·480 = 846 720 bytes ≥ (in 94 080 + out 94 080 + weights 4 320) · 2 = 384 960 → 2.2 MACs/byte project 1×1 480 → 80: MACs 14²·480·80 = 7 526 400 bytes ≥ (in 94 080 + out 15 680 + weights 38 400) · 2 = 296 320 → 25.4 MACs/byte upper bounds as batch grows (weights amortised)depthwise k×k: k² MACs per output element, ≥ 2 elements moved → ≤ k²/4 MACs/byte (2.25 for 3×3) 1×1 C_in → C_out: C_in·C_out MACs per pixel, (C_in + C_out)·2 bytes → 480·80 / 1120 = 34.3 MACs/byte

The depthwise convolution sits 30× below the ridge regardless of batch size, and even the 1×1 convolutions are below it at these narrow widths. A ResNet bottleneck's 3×3 convolution on 256 channels does 9·256 = 2 304 MACs per output element and is comfortably compute-bound. FLOPs undercount EfficientNet's real cost, and the undercount grows with the hardware's ratio of arithmetic to bandwidth.

Activation memory: what large resolution does to training

Stored activations per training image rough count of the feature maps kept for the backward pass (each conv, BN and activation output), computed from the spec at every resolution
  • Counted this way, B0 at 224 stores about 21.9 M activation elements per image (84 MiB in fp32), less than ResNet-50's 32.0 M (122 MiB). B7 at 600 stores about 962 M (3.6 GiB fp32), 30× ResNet-50, while B7 has 2.6× ResNet-50's parameters. Real frameworks fuse or recompute some of these tensors, so treat the numbers as an upper-side estimate that is right in ratio.
  • Activation memory grows like d · w · r² (one factor of width, not two), so the 43% share of compute given to resolution (§10) turns into an even larger share of memory. This is why B7 trains at small per-device batch sizes, and why small batches then slow training and interfere with BatchNorm statistics.
  • Arithmetic per stored element is low. B0 does 17.7 MACs per activation element and B7 39.4, against ResNet-50's 127.6. Each byte written to memory gets 3–7× less use.

29 EfficientNetV2 (2021): the authors' own revision

“EfficientNetV2: Smaller Models and Faster Training” (arXiv 2104.00298, ICML 2021) keeps the idea of searching a baseline and scaling it, but optimises training speed and parameters rather than FLOPs. It diagnoses three specific problems in V1.

The three bottlenecks, with the paper's measurements

  • Very large images train slowly. On B6, training at 512 px reaches 84.3% top-1 and fits only batch 32 per TPUv3 core (42 images/s/core, out of memory at 128). Training at 380 px reaches 84.6% and fits batch 128 (93 images/s/core). Training smaller was both faster (up to 2.2×) and slightly more accurate. That is the train/test resolution effect of §30.
  • Depthwise convolutions are slow in early layers. Replacing MBConv with Fused-MBConv (the 1×1 expand and 3×3 depthwise merged into one regular 3×3 convolution) in the early stages of B4 increases FLOPs from 4.5 B to 7.5 B, yet raises TPU training throughput from 262 to 362 images/s/core. Fusing every stage is worse: 34.4 B FLOPs, 132 M parameters, 1.1 points less accurate, and slower on TPU than no fusion at all (254 vs 262 images/s/core), though still faster on V100 (206 vs 155).
  • Scaling every stage equally is sub-optimal. V2 adds layers mostly to later stages and caps inference resolution at 480.

What else changed

  • Training-aware NAS. The search space adds Fused-MBConv as an operator, with kernel sizes 3×3 or 5×5 and expansion ratios 1, 4 or 6. The network it found uses only 3×3 kernels and expansion 4 in several stages. The reward combines accuracy, normalised training step time and parameter size, with the step time measured on real hardware. The found V2-S also drops V1's last stride-1 stage.
  • Progressive learning with adaptive regularisation. Training is split into four stages of about 87 epochs each. Image size rises from 128 px to 300 px (S) or 380 px (M, L), and regularisation strength rises with it: RandAugment magnitude 5 → 15/20/25, mixup α 0 → 0/0.2/0.4, dropout 0.1 → 0.3/0.4/0.5. The rationale is that small images mean small effective capacity, which needs weak regularisation. Fixed regularisation across sizes was, the authors argue, why earlier progressive resizing lost accuracy.
  • Results (ImageNet-1k only, from Table 7 there): V2-S 83.9%, 22 M params, 8.8 B FLOPs, 7.1 h training. V2-M 85.1%, 54 M, 24 B, 13 h. V2-L 85.7%, 120 M, 53 B, 24 h. In the same table, B7 is listed at 84.7% with 66 M, 38 B FLOPs, 139 h. That 84.7% matches the RandAugment-trained B7 checkpoint in the reference repository, not the paper's 84.3%. V2-M matches B7's accuracy in about 11× less training time (139 / 13 = 10.7), and V100 inference is 57 ms against 170 ms. With ImageNet-21k pretraining, V2-L reaches 86.8% and V2-XL 87.3%.

Evidential status: measured by the original group, with training times normalised to 32 TPU cores and inference timed in a common third-party codebase (timm, V100, batch 16). The comparisons with other families use each model's published or reproduced accuracy. The within-family comparisons (V1 against V2) are the most reliable.

Takeaway: V2 addresses V1's weaknesses directly. It uses fewer depthwise convolutions early in the network, caps resolution, scales later stages more, and changes image size and regularisation together during training. It gets B7's accuracy in a tenth of the training time.

30 The train/test resolution discrepancy

EfficientNet trains and tests at the same nominal resolution. “Fixing the train-test resolution discrepancy” (FixRes, arXiv 1906.06423) showed that the standard augmentation makes those two resolutions effectively different, and that evaluating at a higher resolution than training improves accuracy.

The mechanism

  • Training uses random-resized crops: a random region, typically 8–100% of the image area, is resized up to the training size. On average objects therefore appear larger in training images than in the evaluation centre crop, which shows most of the image at the same pixel size.
  • So at equal nominal resolution the network sees objects at a smaller apparent size at test time than it learned on. Testing at a higher resolution restores the apparent object size, at the cost of shifting activation statistics (pooled features get sparser and larger in magnitude), which FixRes corrects by fine-tuning the classifier and BatchNorm at the test resolution.
  • The reference EfficientNet evaluation takes a centre crop of res / (res + 32) of the shorter side (0.875 for B0 at 224, 0.949 for B7 at 600) and resizes it to res.

31 Critiques of the scaling rule itself

The strongest later critique does not say compound scaling is wrong. It says the best allocation depends on training length and data regime, and that EfficientNet's comparisons mixed the scaling rule with a much better training recipe.

“Revisiting ResNets: Improved Training and Scaling Strategies” (Bello et al., arXiv 2103.07579)

  • Recipe beats architecture, at this scale. A ResNet-200 goes from 79.0% to 82.2% (+3.2) with training changes alone: longer training, cosine schedule, label smoothing, RandAugment, stochastic depth, dropout, EMA, and lower weight decay. Adding two small architecture changes (ResNet-D and SE) gives 83.4%. That is a larger gain than many architecture papers of the period report.
  • The best scaling direction depends on the regime. Over depths 26–400 and resolutions 128–448, depth scaling beat width scaling when training was long enough to overfit (350 epochs), and width scaling was preferable in short runs. A fixed α, β, γ found on a small model in one regime cannot capture this.
  • Resolution should grow more slowly. Returns diminished beyond about 320 px, while EfficientNet goes to 600 (and L2 to 800). Slower resolution scaling improved EfficientNets as well on a speed-accuracy basis.
  • FLOPs fail to predict accuracy for large models in the bounded-data regime. Error against FLOPs follows a power law for small models and breaks for large ones, so FLOP-matched comparisons are less informative at the top end.

Evidential status: a large, controlled sweep on one architecture family (ResNets) with a strong fixed recipe. It shows the regime-dependence well. How far it transfers to MBConv networks is argued, not shown to the same depth.

Other results that bear on the scaling rule

  • RegNet (“Designing Network Design Spaces”, arXiv 2003.13678) derives simple quantised-linear width/depth rules from design-space statistics and reports GPU speed advantages of up to 5× at matched accuracy under a common recipe. On GPUs, it finds activation count predicts runtime better than FLOPs do.
  • NFNets (“High-Performance Large-Scale Image Recognition Without Normalization”, arXiv 2102.06171) report matching B7's accuracy while being up to 8.7× faster to train. That is another data point that FLOP-efficient is not train-efficient.
  • What survives: scaling several axes together beats scaling one, a view later work largely shares; ResNet-RS itself scales depth and resolution together, just with different ratios. What is contested: the specific ratios, their constancy across sizes, and the aggressive growth in resolution.
Takeaway: “balance depth, width and resolution” is well supported. “Use α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15 at every size” is not: the optimal split depends on training length and data. Part of EfficientNet's lead over 2019 ResNets was recipe, not architecture.

34 Using EfficientNet in practice

EfficientNet is still a common pretrained backbone, especially B0–B4. Using it well mostly comes down to resolution, preprocessing, BatchNorm, and memory.

Resolution and preprocessing

  • Match the checkpoint's resolution (B3 = 300, B4 = 380, …) or train at slightly less and test at the native size (§30). Feeding B7 224-px images wastes most of what makes it B7. Feeding B0 600-px images gives a train/test mismatch in apparent object size.
  • Normalisation differs by implementation. The TF reference normalises with ImageNet mean and standard deviation on the 0–255 scale. The Keras application models include rescaling and normalisation inside the model and expect raw 0–255 pixels. PyTorch implementations expect 0–1 tensors normalised with ImageNet statistics. Use the transforms bundled with the weights you load, since resize size and interpolation (often bicubic) differ per model.
  • Evaluation crop: the reference takes a centre crop of res/(res+32) of the short side. Other codebases use other ratios, which shifts accuracy by a few tenths.

Fine-tuning notes specific to this architecture

  • BatchNorm momentum conventions are opposite between frameworks: TF 0.99 corresponds to PyTorch momentum=0.01. Porting the number literally makes running statistics track single batches. With small batches, freeze the BN statistics (see BatchNorm when frozen).
  • Keep drop-path and dropout near the checkpoint's values (0.8 survival floor, 0.2–0.5 dropout) as a starting point when fine-tuning the whole network on small data. They were set for these model sizes, though not tuned for your dataset.
  • Optimiser: the RMSProp recipe is tuned for ImageNet training from scratch. For fine-tuning, SGD with momentum or AdamW at a small learning rate is the usual choice. Tiny weight decay (1e-5) was chosen for depthwise weights. Large decay on depthwise and SE parameters can hurt.
  • Memory: by §28's estimate, B7 at 600 px stores about 3.6 GiB (fp32) or 1.8 GiB (fp16) of activations per image, so a 16 GiB device holds only single-digit batches. Use mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, a lower training resolution, or a smaller model.
Takeaway: B0–B4 at their native resolution, the checkpoint's own preprocessing, BN handled with the right momentum convention, and a memory plan before choosing B5+. For accelerator throughput, start from V2 instead.

35 Pitfalls

Most EfficientNet surprises come from treating a proxy (FLOPs, a nominal multiplier, a caption number) as the real quantity.

Architecture and configuration

  • Equating FLOPs with speed. B4 has about ResNet-50's FLOPs but, in commonly published GPU benchmarks, much lower throughput. Benchmark on the target hardware and batch size.
  • Computing B-level architectures from αᵠ, βᵠ, γᵠ. The released models use hand-set triples (§11). A “B4” built from φ = 4 has depth 2.07, not 1.8, and resolution 392, not 380.
  • Assuming the multipliers are exact. Channel rounding moves individual stages by as much as 20% at w = 1.1 (40 → 48) and repeat rounding makes B1 44% deeper, not 10%.
  • Quoting SE's ratio as “0.25 of the expanded channels”. It is 0.25 of the block's input channels, which is 1/24 of the expanded width at expansion 6.
  • Adding a nonlinearity after the projection in a re-implementation. The linear bottleneck is deliberate.
  • Adding a projection shortcut to the first block of a stage. The reference has no skip there at all, and a shortcut changes the parameter count and behaviour.

Training, preprocessing and deployment

  • Porting BN momentum literally across frameworks (0.99 vs 0.01).
  • Wrong preprocessing: double-normalising Keras models (which normalise internally), or using a 224-px pipeline on a B3+ checkpoint.
  • Out-of-memory at B6/B7 from not budgeting activations. Parameter count (43–66 M) says nothing about activation memory.
  • Comparing against a weakly trained baseline and crediting the architecture. Much of the 2019 gap was the recipe (§31).
  • Mixing MAC and FLOP conventions across papers, which gives a spurious 2× (§33).
Takeaway: read the config, not the formula. Count activations, not parameters. Time on your hardware, not in FLOPs.

37 What to remember

The ideas that outlived the checkpoints.

The method

  • Scaling is an allocation problem. Depth, width and resolution interact. Growing them together beat growing any one by about 0.6–2.2 points at roughly 4× compute in the paper's comparisons (single runs, no error bars).
  • The compound rule fixes a direction in (log d, log w, log r) space, found once on a small model. φ moves along it, and each unit of φ multiplies FLOPs by about 1.92.
  • The released family only loosely follows the rule. B2 ≈ φ = 1 and B3 ≈ φ = 2. Above that, depth lags, and rounding dominates at small sizes. Use the config table.
  • B0 is MnasNet's search rerun for FLOPs: MBConv + SE + Swish, 5.29 M parameters, 0.388 B MACs, 87% of it in 1×1 convolutions.

The caveats

  • The recipe matters as much as the design: RMSProp, AutoAugment, stochastic depth, stepped dropout, 350 epochs. Later work showed old architectures closing much of the gap with the same care.
  • FLOPs are not time. Depthwise convolutions and large activation maps are memory-bound. On accelerators, networks with similar or up to about 2× the FLOPs have been measured 2–5× faster, and EfficientNetV2 is the authors' own correction.
  • Train smaller than you test, and scale resolution more cautiously than 1.15ᵠ.
  • Related pages: generic mechanics on Convolutional Networks; skips and stochastic depth on Residual & Skip Connections; adapting the backbone on Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning; the transformer alternative on Vision Transformers; deployment compression on Model Acceleration.
Takeaway: keep the principle (balanced, rule-based scaling of a searched baseline). Discard the specifics when your hardware, training length or data regime differs from the paper's, and verify every number against a recount.
