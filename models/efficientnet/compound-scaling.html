EfficientNet: Compound Scaling

The compound scaling rule itself: depth, width and resolution grown geometrically in one exponent φ, the grid search on B0 that fixes α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15 under α·β²·γ² ≈ 2, the φ the paper never lists, the hard-coded B0–B7 configs, and channel and repeat rounding.

09 Compound scaling (equation 3)

Having argued for balance, the paper chooses the simplest balanced rule: scale each axis geometrically in one shared exponent φ.

equation 3depth d = αᵠ width w = βᵠ resolution r = γᵠ s.t. α · β² · γ² ≈ 2, α ≥ 1, β ≥ 1, γ ≥ 1 consequence, from section 05FLOPs(φ) ≈ FLOPs(0) · (α·β²·γ²)ᵠ ≈ FLOPs(0) · 2ᵠ
Five ways to grow the same network bar height = channels, bar count = layers, input square = resolution · every mode is matched to the compound model's compute

How to read the rule

  • φ is the budget knob, set by the user: “I have about 2ᵠ times the FLOPs of B0.”
  • α, β, γ are the split, set once by search. Taking logs makes the split explicit: log(FLOP ratio) = φ·(log α + 2 log β + 2 log γ), so a fixed fraction of each extra doubling goes to each axis. §10 computes that fraction.
  • Geometric, not linear. Equal ratios per unit φ, not equal increments. That matches how the three terms enter the cost multiplicatively.
  • Why the constraint equals 2 rather than anything else: convenience. It makes φ read as log₂ of the FLOP ratio. Any constant above 1 would define the same family with φ rescaled.

Hand-worked: φ = 2 from the formula

α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15, φ = 2d = 1.2² = 1.44 w = 1.1² = 1.21 r = 1.15² = 1.3225 → 224·1.3225 = 296.2 px ideal FLOP ratio = 1.44 · 1.21² · 1.3225² = 1.44 · 1.4641 · 1.74901 = 3.6874 ( = 1.92027² )

The paper's Table 3 and Table 7 “compound scale” rows use d=1.4, w=1.2, r=1.3. That is close to this φ = 2 point, rounded, and not the φ = 1 point (1.2, 1.1, 1.15). They are compared against single-axis models of about 4× FLOPs (d=4, w=2, r=2), so the comparison is at roughly the φ ≈ 2 budget.

Takeaway: compound scaling fixes the direction of growth in (log d, log w, log r) space, and φ moves along that ray. Everything else in the method is about choosing the ray.

10 The grid search, and α·β²·γ² = 1.92, not 2

Step 1 of the method fixes φ = 1 and searches α, β, γ on B0 under the constraint. Step 2 freezes them and scales. The reported optimum is α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15.

The product, computed exactly

α · β² · γ²1.2 · 1.1² · 1.15² = 1.2 · 1.21 · 1.3225 = 1.452 · 1.3225 = 1.920270 what that does to the family's budgetφ = 1 : 1.920 vs 2 ( −4.0% ) φ = 3 : 7.08 vs 8 ( −11.5% ) φ = 7 : 96.3 vs 128 ( −24.8% )

So “≈ 2” allows about a 4% shortfall per unit of φ, which compounds. A φ = 7 model would ideally cost about 96× B0, not 128×. The released B7, counted exactly in §12, costs 97.6× B0. That is very close to 1.92⁷, although, as §11 shows, B7's individual multipliers are not the formula's.

How the extra compute is split

share of each doubling spent on each axis, in log-FLOPslog α = ln 1.2 = 0.1823 → 27.9% to depth 2 log β = 2 ln 1.1 = 0.1906 → 29.2% to width 2 log γ = 2 ln 1.15 = 0.2795 → 42.8% to resolution sum = 0.6525 = ln 1.92027

Resolution gets the largest share of the compute growth. Later critiques target this choice: ResNet-RS argues resolution should grow more slowly, and EfficientNetV2 capped it at 480 (§29, §31).

Constraint explorer choose α; the plane shows (β, γ) with the α·β²·γ² = 2 curve, a ±5% band, and a 0.05-step grid

What the paper does not report about the search

  • The grid's range, step, the tolerance on “≈ 2”, how many candidates were trained, and for how long. The figure's 0.05 step and ±5% band are illustrative choices, not the paper's.
  • Whether the optimum was sharp or the neighbourhood was flat. With 0.05 steps, many (α, β, γ) triples satisfy the constraint to within a few percent, and small ImageNet accuracy differences between them may be within run-to-run noise.
  • The paper notes that searching α, β, γ directly on a large model could do better, but is too expensive. Searching once on the small model and reusing the result is the cost-saving assumption. Nothing in the paper shows the optimum is the same at every size.
Takeaway: α·β²·γ² is 1.92, so each φ step buys about 1.92× compute, not 2×. About 43% of the log-compute growth goes to resolution, the largest share of the three.

11 What φ gives each B-level, and what the released models actually use

The paper says B1–B7 are obtained by scaling B0 “with different φ”, but it never lists the φ values. The released code does not use φ at all. It hard-codes a (width, depth, resolution) triple per model, and those triples are not integer powers of (1.1, 1.2, 1.15).

The last column builds the network the formula describes, with channels rounded to multiples of 8 and repeats rounded up (§13), and counts it. At small φ, rounding inflates the cost a lot. At φ = 1 the ceiling turns the repeats (1,2,2,3,3,4,1) into (2,3,3,4,4,5,2), 44% more blocks for a nominal 20%, and the 40-channel stage rounds up to 48. The realised model is 2.57× B0, not 1.92×. The paper's own numbers agree: the released B2, which is φ = 1 in width and depth, is listed at 1.0 B FLOPs against B0's 0.39 B, a ratio of 2.56. This is not a contradiction in the paper, whose footnote 3 warns that rounding moves FLOPs off the theoretical value. It is a measure of how large that effect is at small φ. At large φ the rounding errors are relatively small and the realised cost approaches the ideal.

Reading the table

  • B2 is φ = 1 exactly on width and depth (1.1 = β, 1.2 = α), with resolution rounded from 257.6 to 260. B3 is close to φ = 2 (1.2 ≈ 1.21, 1.4 ≈ 1.44, 300 ≈ 296).
  • B1 is not on the ray. It keeps width at 1.0 and moves only depth and resolution about half a step. It is a hand-placed intermediate point.
  • From B4 up, width and resolution track φ ≈ 3.5–7, and depth lags by about one unit. B7's width 2.0 is 1.1⁷·³ and its resolution 600 is 1.15⁷·⁰, but its depth 3.1 is only 1.2⁶·². The released large models are relatively shallower than the formula would make them.
  • Measured by compute, B5, B6 and B7 sit at φ ≈ 5.0, 6.0 and 7.0 (log base 1.92 of their FLOP ratios), and B1–B4 somewhat below n. B7 costs 97.6× B0 against 1.92027⁷ = 96.3×. The total budget follows the rule well, while the split between the axes was adjusted, presumably by hand, and with round numbers.
The formula's ray against the released points lines: αᵠ, βᵠ, γᵠ (normalised to B0) · dots: released coefficients placed at their FLOP-implied φ
Takeaway: “Bn = B0 scaled by φ = n” is not how the released models were built. B2 and B3 are close to φ = 1 and 2. Above that, width and resolution roughly follow the formula and depth is held back. Use the released triples, not αᵠ, βᵠ, γᵠ, when you need the real architecture.

The recount includes every convolution, both SE layers with biases, BatchNorm's trainable scale and shift, and the 1000-way classifier. It excludes BatchNorm running statistics, which are buffers, not learned parameters. The parameter counts equal torchvision's published num_params for its ports of these checkpoints (for example 19 341 616 for B4 and 30 389 784 for B5). They agree with Table 2 to rounding except B2, which counts 9.11 M against a printed 9.2 M (9.18 M if BatchNorm buffers are included, though that convention would put B1 at 7.9 M rather than the printed 7.8 M). MACs agree within about 2% for B0–B3, B6 and B7, but B4 (4.41 vs 4.2 B, +5%) and B5 (10.31 vs 9.9 B, +4%) come out higher. An independent counter gives nearly the same values: torchvision's model documentation lists 4.39 and 10.27 GFLOPS (its unit is multiply-adds here) for B4 and B5. The EfficientNetV2 paper, from the same authors, lists B5 at 10 B. Counting conventions differ (whether the SE rescale is counted, whether odd maps round up or down at a stride), but the variants tried here (with or without the rescale, ceil or floor sizing) do not reproduce 4.2 B and 9.9 B while also matching the other six models. The paper does not state its counter, so its FLOPs are best treated as ±5%.

Things the table shows that the paper does not say

  • Dropout is a stepped approximation of linear. The text says dropout rises “linearly” from 0.2 (B0) to 0.5 (B7). The config gives 0.2, 0.2, 0.3, 0.3, 0.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.5: it does rise at a constant average rate, but in steps of 0.1 every two models. An exactly linear schedule would be 0.2, 0.243, 0.286, … , 0.5. This is loose wording, not a substantive disagreement.
  • Depth is quantised coarsely. B1 and B2 have the same 23 blocks despite depth coefficients 1.1 and 1.2, because ceil(1.1·r) and ceil(1.2·r) agree for every stage repeat r ∈ {1,2,3,4}. B1's nominal 10% depth increase is really 44% more blocks.
  • Parameters grow more slowly than FLOPs. B7 has 12.5× B0's parameters but 97.6× its compute. Resolution adds compute and no parameters, which is the point of spending about 43% of the budget there.
  • Two larger members exist in the same file: B8 (width 2.2, depth 3.6, 672 px) and L2 (width 4.3, depth 5.3, 800 px). L2 was the student in the later Noisy Student work (§32). Neither appears in the 2019 paper.
Takeaway: the parameter column agrees with the paper to within about 1% (and with torchvision to the unit), and the FLOP column within about 5%. The dropout schedule is stepped, and the depth multiplier is heavily quantised at the small end. When you need these models precisely, use this table, not the paper's prose.

13 Channel rounding (round_filters) and repeat rounding (round_repeats)

A width multiplier of 1.1 turns 40 channels into 44, and hardware prefers multiples of 8. A depth multiplier of 1.1 turns 3 layers into 3.3, and a network cannot have 0.3 of a layer. Two small functions decide what is actually built, and at small φ they matter more than the formula.

round_filters (divisor 8, as in the reference code)x = filters · w new = max( 8 , floor( (x + 4) / 8 ) · 8 ) ← nearest multiple of 8, ties up if new < 0.9 · x : new = new + 8 ← never round down by more than 10% round_repeatsrepeats' = ceil( d · repeats ) ← always rounds UP

Takeaway: channels round to the nearest multiple of 8, but never more than 10% down. Repeats always round up. The two together are why realised FLOPs overshoot 1.92ᵠ at small φ, and why B1 is 44% deeper than its 1.1 suggests.
