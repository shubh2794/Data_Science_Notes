Two entries do not follow from the table's own columns. PNASNet's FLOP ratio is printed as 6.0×, but 23 B / 4.2 B = 5.5×, the same as AmoebaNet-A's identical 23 B. Either the ratio or the 23 B is wrong, and the PNASNet paper (“Progressive Neural Architecture Search”, arXiv 1712.00559) points to the 23 B: it reports PNASNet-5 at 25.0 B multiply-adds, and 25 / 4.2 = 5.95 ≈ 6.0×. ResNet-152's parameter ratio is printed as 7.6× here and in Figure 1's caption (“7.6x smaller”), while 60 / 7.8 = 7.69 rounds to 7.7. A few others (DenseNet-169 8.9 vs 8.97, DenseNet-264 4.3 vs 4.36) are one unit low in the last digit, which could be truncation or could come from unrounded inputs that the table does not show.

Accuracy against size and compute (Table 2) EfficientNet frontier in colour, comparison ConvNets in grey · hover a point

What the table does and does not show

  • It shows a consistent frontier: at every accuracy level from 77% to 84%, an EfficientNet uses 2.6–8.4× fewer parameters and 4.1–19× fewer FLOPs than the comparison ConvNets grouped with it.
  • It does not control the recipe. The EfficientNets were trained with AutoAugment, Swish, stochastic depth and a 350-epoch RMSProp schedule, and the comparison numbers are as published by their authors, often with simpler recipes. ResNet-50 retrained with a modern recipe reaches about 80% (80.4% in “ResNet strikes back: An improved training procedure in timm”, arXiv 2110.00476), and §31 shows the same effect for ResNet-200. The table measures architecture + scaling + recipe together, as the paper acknowledges.
  • It does not measure speed. FLOPs and parameters are proxies, and §24 and §28 show how far they are from wall-clock time on some hardware.
  • It excludes ensembles, multi-crop evaluation and models pretrained on extra data (e.g. 3.5 B Instagram images), as the footnote says.
Takeaway: Table 2's frontier is real in parameters and FLOPs. Recomputed from the table's own columns, the ratios hold, except PNASNet's FLOP ratio (5.5× from the printed 23 B; the 6.0× matches PNASNet's own 25 B). The comparison mixes architecture with training recipe.

How to read it critically

  • In all three groups the compound model has slightly more FLOPs than the single-axis models it beats: 2.3 vs 2.2 B, 1.3 vs 1.2 B, 16.7 vs 16.4 B, that is +2% to +8%. At these slopes (§06 measured about 0.4–1.5 points per doubling), 8% more compute (0.12 doublings) is worth under two tenths of a point. The +0.6 to +1.4 margins are much larger than that, so the conclusion survives the mismatch.
  • Only one compound setting is given (for MobileNetV1). The coefficients used for MobileNetV2 and ResNet-50 are not reported, nor whether α, β, γ were re-searched for each base.
  • Single runs. No error bars. ImageNet seed-to-seed spread is commonly reported at about ±0.1–0.2 points, so +0.6 is probably real and small, and +1.4 is clearly real.
  • Resolution-only is the weakest in every group. That fits §07: pixels without the capacity to use them saturate fastest.
Takeaway: compound scaling beats the best single axis on three different base networks at about 4× FLOPs, by +0.6 to +1.4 points, with a slight FLOP advantage that is too small to explain the margins. The idea is not specific to B0.

What to take from it

  • The speedup is much smaller than the FLOP reduction. B1 has 15.7× fewer FLOPs than ResNet-152 but is only 5.7× faster, so it achieves about a third of ResNet's FLOPs per second on this CPU. Depthwise and SE operations do little arithmetic per byte moved, so they run below peak (§28).
  • Single-core CPU at batch 1 is among the regimes most favourable to low-FLOP networks. One core has modest peak arithmetic relative to its memory bandwidth, so arithmetic tends to dominate runtime. On GPUs and TPUs at large batch the picture changes, and the paper did not measure those.
  • The accuracy column disagrees with Table 2. B1 is 79.1% in Table 2 and Figure 1 but 78.8% here. B7 is 84.3% in Table 2 and the abstract but 84.4% here. The paper does not say whether Table 4 used different checkpoints or evaluation. The reference repository's README also quotes B7 at 84.4% top-1, so two B7 numbers circulate from the authors. The latency comparison is unaffected, but quote Table 2's accuracies.
Takeaway: on one CPU core at batch 1, B1 is 5.7× faster than ResNet-152 and B7 6.1× faster than GPipe. That is real, but it is about a third of what the FLOP ratio would predict, and it says nothing about accelerators.

The parameter counts, and one ratio that does not add up

  • Transfer models have smaller heads: B7's 2560 × 1000 classifier (2.56 M) becomes 2560 × 100 on CIFAR-100. Recounting with each dataset's class count gives B0 4.02 M (CIFAR-10), B7 about 64.0 M, B6 40.8 M (Pets). These match the table's 4 M, 64 M and 41 M.
  • The recount gives B3 10.8–11.0 M where the table says 10 M, B4 17.6–17.7 M (table 17 M), and B5 28.5 M (Flowers) and 29.4 M (Birdsnap, 500 classes) where the table says 28 M for both. These are truncations and small gaps, not errors in substance.
  • Pets' printed ratio is wrong on its face: ResNet-152's 58 M against B4's 17 M is 3.4×, but the table says 5.6×. The “4.7×” geometric mean over the public column reproduces only if 5.6 is used (with 3.4 it is 4.4×). The “9.6×” mean over the best-reported column does reproduce: the geometric mean of 8.7, 8.7, 8.7, 14 and 8.7 is 9.57. Using the recomputed parameter counts for both public-column ratios (figure below), the geometric mean is 4.2×.

What the recount adds

  • The paper prints all four scaled models at 1.8–1.9 B. Recounted with the reference rounding, the compound model is 1.81 B, matching, but the single-axis models come out at 1.46–1.59 B, 12–20% cheaper. A bound that does not depend on the counter makes the point sharper: width and resolution scaling can at most quadruple a network's cost, so with B0 at 0.39 B (Table 2), w = 2 and r = 2 cannot exceed 1.56 B, yet Table 7 prints 1.8 and 1.9 B. Table 7 (like Figure 3, §06) therefore seems to use a counter that puts the baseline nearer 0.45–0.5 B, and under such a counter the compound model would also be above the printed 1.8 B. Which models were really cheaper cannot be settled from the paper. At §06's slopes (roughly 0.4–1 point per doubling in this range), a 15–25% compute difference is worth about 0.1–0.3 points, well below the 2.0–2.2 point gap, so the conclusion survives either way.
  • The gap in the table is +2.0 to +2.2 points (81.1 against 79.1, 79.0 and 78.9). The text says compound scaling improves accuracy “by up to 2.5%” over single-dimension methods. That sentence describes Figure 8's curves, not Table 7, so it is not a contradiction; it just cannot be checked against the tables.
  • The baseline here is 77.3%, while Table 2 gives B0 77.1% and Figure 3's baseline sits near 76.1%. There are three different numbers for the same starting network.

Figure 8: whole curves, not points

Figure 8 plots accuracy against FLOPs (0–5 B) for width, depth, resolution and compound scaling of B0. The compound curve lies above all three single-axis curves across the range, and the gap widens as FLOPs grow. That matches Observation 1 (single axes saturate) and Observation 2 (balance helps).

Figure 7: class activation maps

  • The paper shows CAMs (the class-weighted sum of the final feature maps) for two validation images, “bakeshop” and “maze”, under the five models of Table 7. The “bakeshop” image is several macarons on a plate. The baseline and wider models put one blob in the middle, and the higher-resolution model scatters small spots. The compound model lights up the individual macarons. The “maze” image is a circular maze. The compound model's map is a ring following the maze's circular shape, while the others highlight two lobes or a partial arc.
  • Evidential weight: illustrative only. The paper says the images are randomly picked, but two images are anecdotes, there is no quantitative localisation metric, and CAM quality is a debated proxy for what a network “uses”. Read it as a picture consistent with the accuracy numbers, not as independent evidence.
Takeaway: with the architecture held fixed, compound scaling beats the best single axis by about 2 points at about 1.8 B FLOPs. Table 7's FLOPs are not consistent with Table 2's 0.39 B baseline, so the exact compute match is uncertain, but the margin is far larger than any plausible mismatch could explain. The CAM figure is illustration, not evidence.

28 FLOPs are not latency: depthwise convolutions, memory traffic and activation size

EfficientNet optimised FLOPs because FLOPs are hardware-neutral. The price is that the resulting network does little arithmetic per byte of data it moves, and on GPUs and TPUs data movement, not arithmetic, often sets the speed.

The roofline argument in one paragraph

An operation is compute-bound if it performs more arithmetic per byte moved than the hardware's ratio of peak compute to memory bandwidth (the “ridge point”), and memory-bound otherwise. A V100, for example, offers about 125 TFLOP/s of fp16 tensor-core arithmetic against about 900 GB/s of memory bandwidth, so the ridge is roughly 139 FLOPs, or about 70 MACs, per byte. Below that, adding FLOPs is nearly free and removing them saves nearly nothing. What matters is bytes moved.

Hand-worked: arithmetic intensity of the two halves of an MBConv

stage-5 repeated block, 14×14, E = 480, fp16 (2 bytes), batch 1, minimum trafficdepthwise 3×3: MACs 14²·9·480 = 846 720 bytes ≥ (in 94 080 + out 94 080 + weights 4 320) · 2 = 384 960 → 2.2 MACs/byte project 1×1 480 → 80: MACs 14²·480·80 = 7 526 400 bytes ≥ (in 94 080 + out 15 680 + weights 38 400) · 2 = 296 320 → 25.4 MACs/byte upper bounds as batch grows (weights amortised)depthwise k×k: k² MACs per output element, ≥ 2 elements moved → ≤ k²/4 MACs/byte (2.25 for 3×3) 1×1 C_in → C_out: C_in·C_out MACs per pixel, (C_in + C_out)·2 bytes → 480·80 / 1120 = 34.3 MACs/byte

The depthwise convolution sits 30× below the ridge regardless of batch size, and even the 1×1 convolutions are below it at these narrow widths. A ResNet bottleneck's 3×3 convolution on 256 channels does 9·256 = 2 304 MACs per output element and is comfortably compute-bound. FLOPs undercount EfficientNet's real cost, and the undercount grows with the hardware's ratio of arithmetic to bandwidth.

Activation memory: what large resolution does to training

Stored activations per training image rough count of the feature maps kept for the backward pass (each conv, BN and activation output), computed from the spec at every resolution
  • Counted this way, B0 at 224 stores about 21.9 M activation elements per image (84 MiB in fp32), less than ResNet-50's 32.0 M (122 MiB). B7 at 600 stores about 962 M (3.6 GiB fp32), 30× ResNet-50, while B7 has 2.6× ResNet-50's parameters. Real frameworks fuse or recompute some of these tensors, so treat the numbers as an upper-side estimate that is right in ratio.
  • Activation memory grows like d · w · r² (one factor of width, not two), so the 43% share of compute given to resolution (§10) turns into an even larger share of memory. This is why B7 trains at small per-device batch sizes, and why small batches then slow training and interfere with BatchNorm statistics.
  • Arithmetic per stored element is low. B0 does 17.7 MACs per activation element and B7 39.4, against ResNet-50's 127.6. Each byte written to memory gets 3–7× less use.
