29 EfficientNetV2 (2021): the authors' own revision

“EfficientNetV2: Smaller Models and Faster Training” (arXiv 2104.00298, ICML 2021) keeps the idea of searching a baseline and scaling it, but optimises training speed and parameters rather than FLOPs. It diagnoses three specific problems in V1.

The three bottlenecks, with the paper's measurements

  • Very large images train slowly. On B6, training at 512 px reaches 84.3% top-1 and fits only batch 32 per TPUv3 core (42 images/s/core, out of memory at 128). Training at 380 px reaches 84.6% and fits batch 128 (93 images/s/core). Training smaller was both faster (up to 2.2×) and slightly more accurate. That is the train/test resolution effect of §30.
  • Depthwise convolutions are slow in early layers. Replacing MBConv with Fused-MBConv (the 1×1 expand and 3×3 depthwise merged into one regular 3×3 convolution) in the early stages of B4 increases FLOPs from 4.5 B to 7.5 B, yet raises TPU training throughput from 262 to 362 images/s/core. Fusing every stage is worse: 34.4 B FLOPs, 132 M parameters, 1.1 points less accurate, and slower on TPU than no fusion at all (254 vs 262 images/s/core), though still faster on V100 (206 vs 155).
  • Scaling every stage equally is sub-optimal. V2 adds layers mostly to later stages and caps inference resolution at 480.
Fused-MBConv in B4 (as reported in EfficientNetV2, Table 3) reported measurements, not recomputed · pick the metric

What else changed

  • Training-aware NAS. The search space adds Fused-MBConv as an operator, with kernel sizes 3×3 or 5×5 and expansion ratios 1, 4 or 6. The network it found uses only 3×3 kernels and expansion 4 in several stages. The reward combines accuracy, normalised training step time and parameter size, with the step time measured on real hardware. The found V2-S also drops V1's last stride-1 stage.
  • Progressive learning with adaptive regularisation. Training is split into four stages of about 87 epochs each. Image size rises from 128 px to 300 px (S) or 380 px (M, L), and regularisation strength rises with it: RandAugment magnitude 5 → 15/20/25, mixup α 0 → 0/0.2/0.4, dropout 0.1 → 0.3/0.4/0.5. The rationale is that small images mean small effective capacity, which needs weak regularisation. Fixed regularisation across sizes was, the authors argue, why earlier progressive resizing lost accuracy.
  • Results (ImageNet-1k only, from Table 7 there): V2-S 83.9%, 22 M params, 8.8 B FLOPs, 7.1 h training. V2-M 85.1%, 54 M, 24 B, 13 h. V2-L 85.7%, 120 M, 53 B, 24 h. In the same table, B7 is listed at 84.7% with 66 M, 38 B FLOPs, 139 h. That 84.7% matches the RandAugment-trained B7 checkpoint in the reference repository, not the paper's 84.3%. V2-M matches B7's accuracy in about 11× less training time (139 / 13 = 10.7), and V100 inference is 57 ms against 170 ms. With ImageNet-21k pretraining, V2-L reaches 86.8% and V2-XL 87.3%.

Evidential status: measured by the original group, with training times normalised to 32 TPU cores and inference timed in a common third-party codebase (timm, V100, batch 16). The comparisons with other families use each model's published or reproduced accuracy. The within-family comparisons (V1 against V2) are the most reliable.

Takeaway: V2 addresses V1's weaknesses directly. It uses fewer depthwise convolutions early in the network, caps resolution, scales later stages more, and changes image size and regularisation together during training. It gets B7's accuracy in a tenth of the training time.

30 The train/test resolution discrepancy

EfficientNet trains and tests at the same nominal resolution. “Fixing the train-test resolution discrepancy” (FixRes, arXiv 1906.06423) showed that the standard augmentation makes those two resolutions effectively different, and that evaluating at a higher resolution than training improves accuracy.

The mechanism

  • Training uses random-resized crops: a random region, typically 8–100% of the image area, is resized up to the training size. On average objects therefore appear larger in training images than in the evaluation centre crop, which shows most of the image at the same pixel size.
  • So at equal nominal resolution the network sees objects at a smaller apparent size at test time than it learned on. Testing at a higher resolution restores the apparent object size, at the cost of shifting activation statistics (pooled features get sparser and larger in magnitude), which FixRes corrects by fine-tuning the classifier and BatchNorm at the test resolution.
  • The reference EfficientNet evaluation takes a centre crop of res / (res + 32) of the shorter side (0.875 for B0 at 224, 0.949 for B7 at 600) and resizes it to res.

Training noticeably below it (EfficientNetV2 used 380 for B6's 528, about 72%) was faster and at least as accurate in the reported experiments. It is one of the cheapest changes to try in an EfficientNet training run.

31 Critiques of the scaling rule itself

The strongest later critique does not say compound scaling is wrong. It says the best allocation depends on training length and data regime, and that EfficientNet's comparisons mixed the scaling rule with a much better training recipe.

“Revisiting ResNets: Improved Training and Scaling Strategies” (Bello et al., arXiv 2103.07579)

  • Recipe beats architecture, at this scale. A ResNet-200 goes from 79.0% to 82.2% (+3.2) with training changes alone: longer training, cosine schedule, label smoothing, RandAugment, stochastic depth, dropout, EMA, and lower weight decay. Adding two small architecture changes (ResNet-D and SE) gives 83.4%. That is a larger gain than many architecture papers of the period report.
  • The best scaling direction depends on the regime. Over depths 26–400 and resolutions 128–448, depth scaling beat width scaling when training was long enough to overfit (350 epochs), and width scaling was preferable in short runs. A fixed α, β, γ found on a small model in one regime cannot capture this.
  • Resolution should grow more slowly. Returns diminished beyond about 320 px, while EfficientNet goes to 600 (and L2 to 800). Slower resolution scaling improved EfficientNets as well on a speed-accuracy basis.
  • FLOPs fail to predict accuracy for large models in the bounded-data regime. Error against FLOPs follows a power law for small models and breaks for large ones, so FLOP-matched comparisons are less informative at the top end.

Evidential status: a large, controlled sweep on one architecture family (ResNets) with a strong fixed recipe. It shows the regime-dependence well. How far it transfers to MBConv networks is argued, not shown to the same depth.

Other results that bear on the scaling rule

  • RegNet (“Designing Network Design Spaces”, arXiv 2003.13678) derives simple quantised-linear width/depth rules from design-space statistics and reports GPU speed advantages of up to 5× at matched accuracy under a common recipe. On GPUs, it finds activation count predicts runtime better than FLOPs do.
  • NFNets (“High-Performance Large-Scale Image Recognition Without Normalization”, arXiv 2102.06171) report matching B7's accuracy while being up to 8.7× faster to train. That is another data point that FLOP-efficient is not train-efficient.
  • What survives: scaling several axes together beats scaling one, a view later work largely shares; ResNet-RS itself scales depth and resolution together, just with different ratios. What is contested: the specific ratios, their constancy across sizes, and the aggressive growth in resolution.
Takeaway: “balance depth, width and resolution” is well supported. “Use α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15 at every size” is not: the optimal split depends on training length and data. Part of EfficientNet's lead over 2019 ResNets was recipe, not architecture.

  • BiFPN is a feature pyramid with top-down and bottom-up paths, repeated D times, that fuses features with learned non-negative weights (normalised by their sum), since inputs at different resolutions contribute unequally.
  • The published table departs from its own formula in places, as the classification family does. D5 uses 1280 px where 512 + 640 = 1152. BiFPN depth for D5, D6 and D7 is 7, 8, 8 where 3 + φ gives 8, 9, 10. D7 uses 1536 px where the rule gives 1408, and reuses the B6 backbone. Widths are rounded (86.4 → 88, 116.6 → 112, 212.6 → 224, 387.4 → 384).
  • The final arXiv version reports 55.1 AP on COCO test-dev with 77 M parameters and 410 B FLOPs (the abstract names the model D7; the results table lists this configuration as D7x), and claims 4–9× fewer parameters and 13–42× fewer FLOPs than earlier detectors. Earlier versions of the paper reported lower headline numbers. The same FLOPs-versus-latency caveat (§28) applies.

Larger checkpoints trained after the paper

  • RandAugment (arXiv 1909.13719) replaces AutoAugment and reports B7 at 85.0% in its own paper. The RandAugment B7 checkpoint released in the reference repository is listed at 84.7%, and that is the B7 figure EfficientNetV2 later compares against.
  • AdvProp (“Adversarial Examples Improve Image Recognition”, arXiv 1911.09665) uses adversarial examples with separate BatchNorm statistics and reports B8 at 85.5% without extra data.
  • Noisy Student (“Self-training with Noisy Student improves ImageNet classification”, arXiv 1911.04252) trains an EfficientNet-L2 student (width 4.3, depth 5.3, 800 px) on ImageNet plus 300 M pseudo-labelled images and reports 88.4% top-1. That was state of the art at the time, and it came from data and training, not architecture.
Takeaway: “search a small model, then scale by fixed ratios” reappears in Lite, EfficientDet and V2. Every one of them rounds or overrides its own rule somewhere, which suggests treating the rule as a strong default rather than a law.

Checked and not counted as inconsistencies

  • α·β²·γ² = 1.9203, not 2. The paper states the constraint as “≈ 2”, and 1.92 satisfies it. §10 works out what the 4% shortfall does to the family's budget, but it is not a contradiction.
  • “Up to 2.5%” in the Discussion against Table 7's largest gap of 2.2 points (81.1 − 78.9). The sentence describes Figure 8's curves, so it cannot be checked against Table 7 and does not contradict it.
  • Realised FLOPs above 1.92ᵠ at small φ (2.57× B0 at φ = 1 after rounding, §11). The paper's footnote 3 says FLOPs may differ from theory because of rounding, and its own B2 (1.0 B vs 0.39 B) shows the same ratio.

Deliberate choices on this page

  • All costs are MACs, called FLOPs where quoting the paper, following its convention. Papers that count 2 FLOPs per MAC (e.g. ResNet-RS's Table 3) show EfficientNet values twice as large.
  • Parameter counts are trainable parameters only. BatchNorm running statistics are excluded, which is why Keras's “total” is 42 023 higher for B0.
  • Where accuracies come from plots (Figures 3 and 4), they are marked as read-offs with their uncertainty.
Takeaway: when quoting EfficientNet numbers, use Table 2 for accuracy, parameters and FLOPs, Table 8 for exact validation and test values, and the released config for architecture. Treat the prose, captions and legends as secondary.

34 Using EfficientNet in practice

EfficientNet is still a common pretrained backbone, especially B0–B4. Using it well mostly comes down to resolution, preprocessing, BatchNorm, and memory.

Which size for which budget

SituationReasonable starting pointWhy
Mobile / CPU inference, tight latencyB0, or Lite0–Lite2 if you will quantise to int8Low FLOPs pay off on CPUs at batch 1. Lite avoids SE and Swish, which are awkward in int8.

Dense prediction (detection, segmentation)EfficientNet backbone + BiFPN (EfficientDet), or a feature pyramid on B3–B5Multi-scale features. Watch activation memory at detection resolutions.

Resolution and preprocessing

  • Match the checkpoint's resolution (B3 = 300, B4 = 380, …) or train at slightly less and test at the native size (§30). Feeding B7 224-px images wastes most of what makes it B7. Feeding B0 600-px images gives a train/test mismatch in apparent object size.
  • Normalisation differs by implementation. The TF reference normalises with ImageNet mean and standard deviation on the 0–255 scale. The Keras application models include rescaling and normalisation inside the model and expect raw 0–255 pixels. PyTorch implementations expect 0–1 tensors normalised with ImageNet statistics. Use the transforms bundled with the weights you load, since resize size and interpolation (often bicubic) differ per model.
  • Evaluation crop: the reference takes a centre crop of res/(res+32) of the short side. Other codebases use other ratios, which shifts accuracy by a few tenths.

Fine-tuning notes specific to this architecture

  • BatchNorm momentum conventions are opposite between frameworks: TF 0.99 corresponds to PyTorch momentum=0.01. Porting the number literally makes running statistics track single batches. With small batches, freeze the BN statistics (see BatchNorm when frozen).
  • Keep drop-path and dropout near the checkpoint's values (0.8 survival floor, 0.2–0.5 dropout) as a starting point when fine-tuning the whole network on small data. They were set for these model sizes, though not tuned for your dataset.
  • Optimiser: the RMSProp recipe is tuned for ImageNet training from scratch. For fine-tuning, SGD with momentum or AdamW at a small learning rate is the usual choice. Tiny weight decay (1e-5) was chosen for depthwise weights. Large decay on depthwise and SE parameters can hurt.
  • Memory: by §28's estimate, B7 at 600 px stores about 3.6 GiB (fp32) or 1.8 GiB (fp16) of activations per image, so a 16 GiB device holds only single-digit batches. Use mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, a lower training resolution, or a smaller model.
Takeaway: B0–B4 at their native resolution, the checkpoint's own preprocessing, BN handled with the right momentum convention, and a memory plan before choosing B5+. For accelerator throughput, start from V2 instead.
