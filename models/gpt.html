GPT

Generative Pre-trained Transformer — the decoder-only, autoregressive model family (GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3, InstructGPT/ChatGPT, GPT-4) that turned "predict the next token" into a general-purpose interface. This note follows the family itself: the objective, the exact block each generation used, the published configurations and their parameter and compute arithmetic, how each generation was adapted to tasks, how text is actually decoded, and what the later reports do and do not disclose. Where BERT reads bidirectionally to produce representations, GPT reads left to right to produce text.

01 What GPT is

GPT is a stack of causally masked Transformer decoder blocks trained to maximise the probability of each token given the tokens before it. Every model in the family uses that same objective; the generations differ in scale, normalisation placement, tokeniser, data, and — above all — in how the trained model is pointed at a task.

the objective every GPT is pretrained onP(x₁, …, xₙ) = ∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | x₁, …, xᵢ₋₁) minimised as next-token cross-entropy, parameters θℒ(θ) = − (1/n) ∑ᵢ log P_θ(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎)

The shape of the computation

  • Input: a sequence of n token ids. Each id is looked up in a token-embedding table, a learned position vector is added, and the result passes through L identical blocks of masked self-attention plus a position-wise feed-forward network.
  • Output: at every position i, a vector of |V| logits — a full distribution over the next token. During training all n distributions are scored at once; during generation only the last one is used, a token is chosen, appended, and the model runs again.
  • There is no encoder and no cross-attention. The same stack reads the prompt and writes the continuation, which is why the family is called decoder-only (see Encoder–Decoder Models for the other two wirings).

Why the family matters

  • GPT-1 (2018) showed generative pretraining followed by supervised fine-tuning transfers well; GPT-2 (2019) showed a larger model does many tasks zero-shot if the task is phrased as text; GPT-3 (2020) showed few-shot in-context learning from examples placed in the prompt; InstructGPT (2022) showed a small amount of preference-based post-training makes the model follow instructions; GPT-4 (2023) added image input and reported predicting its final loss from runs using up to 10,000× less compute.
  • The architecture changed remarkably little across those five steps. Most of the story is scale, data, and the adaptation interface.
Takeaway: GPT is a causal Transformer decoder trained on next-token cross-entropy. One objective, one block design, and a sequence of increasingly cheap ways to adapt it: fine-tune (GPT-1), prompt zero-shot (GPT-2), prompt with examples (GPT-3), and instruction-tune with human preferences (InstructGPT).

02 Why generative pretraining

Before 2018 most NLP systems were trained from scratch per task on labelled data, with at most pretrained word vectors as input. The GPT-1 bet was that predicting raw text is a task hard enough to force a model to learn syntax, semantics and some world knowledge — and that this knowledge would transfer.

The problem it addressed

  • Labelled data is scarce; raw text is not. A sentiment dataset may have tens of thousands of examples, while a book corpus has hundreds of millions of tokens, each of which is a free supervised target for next-token prediction.
  • Word vectors transfer only the first layer. Earlier transfer (word2vec, GloVe, and contextual features such as ELMo) handed downstream models a representation, but the task architecture still had to be designed and trained. GPT-1 transferred the whole network and added only a linear head.
  • Recurrent language models struggled with long-range structure. The GPT-1 paper chose a Transformer explicitly for its "more structured memory", and its ablation replaced it with a single-layer 2048-unit LSTM in the same framework: the average score across its benchmark suite fell by 5.6 points.

Why "generative" and not some other pretext task

  • Next-token prediction supplies a training signal at every position of every document. A masked objective such as BERT's scores only the ~15% of positions it corrupted; the decoder objective is denser per token processed.
  • The same objective is also a sampler: a model that assigns P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) can generate text, score text, and — as GPT-2 argued — perform any task whose input and output can be written as a string.
  • The cost is unidirectionality: a token's representation never sees tokens to its right, which is a real handicap for pure understanding tasks at small scale. That trade is examined in GPT vs BERT vs encoder–decoder.
Takeaway: the motivating claim was transfer, not generation. Generation came for free from the objective and turned out, at larger scale, to be the more important property.

04 The autoregressive factorisation

The chain rule of probability lets any joint distribution over a sequence be written as a product of conditionals. GPT models exactly those conditionals, in left-to-right order, with one network shared across positions.

chain rule — exact, no approximationP(x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄) = P(x₁) · P(x₂ | x₁) · P(x₃ | x₁, x₂) · P(x₄ | x₁, x₂, x₃) the modelling step — one network, every prefixP_θ(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) = softmax( W_U · h_L,ᵢ )[xᵢ] GPT-1's form, with a finite window of k tokensL₁(U) = ∑ᵢ log P(uᵢ | uᵢ₋ₖ, …, uᵢ₋₁; Θ)

What the factorisation buys

  • It is exact. Nothing is assumed about independence; any distribution over strings can in principle be represented. Apart from the network's finite capacity, the one structural truncation is the finite context window (512, 1024 or 2048 tokens for GPT-1/2/3), which cuts off the conditioning set.
  • It gives a proper likelihood. The model can score any string, so perplexity is well defined and comparable across models that share a tokeniser — the GPT-2 paper names, as a benefit of its byte-level tokeniser, that the model can be evaluated on any benchmark regardless of its preprocessing.
  • It gives ancestral sampling. Draw x₁, then x₂ given it, and so on. Every decoding strategy in Decoding is a modification of this loop.

What it costs

  • The order is fixed. The model never conditions on the right context, so infilling ("fill the middle") needs either a special data format or a different model.
  • Generation is sequential in n: each token needs a forward pass that depends on the previous token. Training is not — see Causal masking.
  • Errors compound: a poor early token becomes conditioning for every later one, and the model was trained only on human-written prefixes, never its own mistakes (the "exposure bias" argument, whose practical size is debated).
Takeaway: the chain rule is an identity, so the modelling choice in GPT is not the factorisation but the ordering (left to right) and the parameter sharing (one network for every prefix).

05 Next-token cross-entropy, worked by hand

The training loss is the average negative log-probability the model assigns to the token that actually came next. Perplexity is its exponential. Both are easiest to understand on four numbers.

per-sequence loss (nats) and perplexityℒ = − (1/n) ∑ᵢ ln P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) PPL = exp(ℒ) logits to probabilities over a vocabulary VP(v) = exp(zᵥ) / ∑ᵤ exp(zᵤ)

  • Mean loss 1.681 nats, so perplexity exp(1.681) ≈ 5.37: on average the model was as uncertain as a uniform choice among about 5.4 tokens. In bits, divide by ln 2: 2.43 bits per token.
  • The early tokens dominate: the first two steps contribute 5.30 of the 6.73 total. That pattern — high loss where context is short, low loss once the topic is set — is typical of real language models too.
  • A uniform guess over GPT-2's 50,257 tokens would score ln 50,257 ≈ 10.82 nats; that is the loss at initialisation if the output logits start near zero.

The gradient is simple

  • With respect to the logits at one position, the gradient of cross-entropy is p − onehot(true): push the true token's logit up by 1 − p_true, push every other logit down by its own probability.
  • Because every position is a target, one 2048-token GPT-3 training sequence yields 2048 of these gradients in one forward and backward pass.
Takeaway: the loss is "how surprised was the model by what actually came next", averaged over every position. Perplexity is that surprise expressed as an effective branching factor, and it is only comparable between models that share a tokeniser.

06 Causal masking: parallel training, sequential generation

If every position predicts its successor, position i must not see position i+1. A lower-triangular mask on the attention scores enforces that — and lets all n predictions be trained in a single pass.

The n = 4 mask, written out

k₁ k₂ k₃ k₄ q₁ [ 0 −∞ −∞ −∞ ] q₂ [ 0 0 −∞ −∞ ] q₃ [ 0 0 0 −∞ ] q₄ [ 0 0 0 0 ]

Why training is parallel

  • With teacher forcing, the true sequence is the input. Position 3 predicts x₄ from x₁…x₃, position 4 predicts x₅ from x₁…x₄, and so on — every prefix is already present, so all n predictions come from one masked matrix multiply.
  • The mask must hold at every layer. If any layer let position i read i+1, information about the target would leak upward through the residual stream and the training loss could fall toward zero without the model learning to predict unseen text.

Why generation is sequential

  • At inference the next token does not exist until it is chosen. Step t needs the token from step t−1, so m new tokens need m dependent forward passes.
  • The mask also means past positions' keys and values never change when a token is appended — which is exactly what makes the KV cache valid.
  • The attention maths (scaling, the −∞ versus large-negative constant, padding masks) is covered in Attention — masking.
Takeaway: the causal mask turns one sequence into n training examples at the cost of one pass, and guarantees that cached keys and values stay valid during generation. Relative to BERT, it is the only structural difference in a GPT-1 block; from GPT-2 on, the LayerNorm placement differs as well (pre-LN versus BERT's post-LN).

07 Generation, step by step

Generation is a loop: run the model on the current sequence, take the last position's distribution, choose a token, append it, repeat. The figure runs that loop on a toy model with fixed logits per step so every probability can be checked.

Autoregressive loop on a toy model each step: logits → softmax → pick → append · running log-probability and perplexity update

What the loop does and does not do

  • Each step conditions on the whole prefix, including tokens the model itself produced. Forcing a different token at step 1 changes every later distribution — that is the branching that beam search and sampling explore.
  • The model has no notion of "done" except a special token. GPT-2 and GPT-3 use <|endoftext|> as a document separator; generation stops when it is emitted or a length limit is reached.
  • The sequence log-probability is the sum of the per-step log-probabilities of the chosen tokens. Greedy decoding maximises each term locally, which does not in general maximise the sum — a point made concrete in Decoding.
Takeaway: generation reuses the training-time distribution one position at a time. Given a fixed model, the decoding choices all live in the "choose a token" step.

08 The decoder block as GPT uses it

A GPT block is the original Transformer decoder block with the cross-attention sub-layer deleted: masked multi-head self-attention, then a two-layer feed-forward network, each wrapped in a residual connection and a LayerNorm. The details below are what the GPT papers state; where a later paper only says it "largely follows" or uses "the same model and architecture" as its predecessor, the entry is inherited, and weight tying for GPT-2 is confirmed by its released checkpoints rather than its text.

Three choices worth understanding

  • GELU (x · Φ(x), with Φ the standard normal CDF) replaced the original ReLU. It is smooth near zero and lets small negative inputs through with a small negative output. GPT-1 was an early large-scale adopter (BERT, released months later, also used it); later families moved to gated variants (see Transformers — activations and gated units).
  • Learned absolute positions. GPT-1 explicitly chose "learned position embeddings instead of the sinusoidal version". The table has one row per position, so the context length is a hard ceiling: GPT-2 cannot attend to position 1025 because row 1025 does not exist. Rotary and relative schemes, which later decoders adopted, are covered in Transformers — positional encoding.
  • Weight tying. The output projection reuses the input embedding matrix transposed. For GPT-2 small that saves 50,257 × 768 ≈ 38.6M parameters — about 31% of the model — and couples "what a token means as input" to "what it takes to predict it as output".
Takeaway: nothing in the GPT block is exotic. Its specific choices — GELU, learned positions, tied output, 4× FFN — were shared with BERT and became common defaults by 2019. The consequential change inside the block across generations was where the LayerNorm sits.

09 Post-LN to pre-LN, and the scaled residual init

GPT-2's paper lists its architecture changes in three sentences: LayerNorm moved to the input of each sub-block, one extra LayerNorm after the final block, and residual-layer weights scaled at initialisation by 1/√N. Together they are widely credited with making the 48-layer GPT-2 and the 96-layer GPT-3 stacks train stably.

Where the LayerNorm sits — and what the residual stream does with depth toggle placement · slide depth · the growth curve is computed from a random-sum model
post-LN (GPT-1)h′ = LN( h + F(h) ) pre-LN (GPT-2, GPT-3)h′ = h + F( LN(h) ) output = LN_f( h_L ) · W_eᵀ scaled init of residual projectionsstd = 0.02 / √N N = number of residual layers

Why the move helps

  • In post-LN, every residual sum is renormalised, so the gradient to early layers passes through 2L LayerNorm Jacobians (one after each of the two sub-layers in every block). Deep post-LN stacks are known to need learning-rate warm-up and can diverge without it.
  • In pre-LN, the residual path from input to output is a plain sum: h_L = h₀ + ∑ F_ℓ(…). There is always an identity route for the gradient, which is the same argument as for residual connections in general.
  • The cost is that nothing normalises the stream itself, so its magnitude grows with depth — hence the extra final LayerNorm before the output projection, and hence the scaled initialisation.

The 1/√N scaling, worked

  • If each of N sub-blocks adds an independent update of variance s² to a stream of variance 1, the stream variance after all of them is 1 + N·s². Scaling each update's weights by 1/√N divides s² by N, so the total added variance stays about s² regardless of depth.
  • The paper says only "N is the number of residual layers". Common reimplementations take N = 2L (two residual sub-blocks per block) and apply it to the attention and FFN output projections. For GPT-2 small, N = 24: 1/√24 = 0.204, so the output-projection std becomes 0.02 × 0.204 ≈ 0.0041. For GPT-2 XL, N = 96 and the factor is 0.102.
  • The trade-offs of norm placement in general (including RMSNorm and the later "sandwich" variants) live in Transformers — pre-norm against post-norm.
Takeaway: pre-LN gives the gradient a clean identity path; the final LN and the 1/√N init compensate for the stream growth that pre-LN allows. All three arrived together in GPT-2 and were inherited unchanged by GPT-3.

10 Tokenisation across generations

Every GPT reads subword units produced by byte-pair encoding (BPE): start from small symbols, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair. What changed between generations is the base alphabet and the number of merges. General tokenisation theory is in Tokenization — BPE; this section covers the GPT-specific choices.

Why byte-level mattered

  • A code-point BPE would need a base vocabulary of over 130,000 symbols to cover Unicode; a byte-level base needs 256. GPT-2 got universality without an enormous base.
  • Naive byte-level BPE wastes merges on variants such as dog., dog!, dog?. GPT-2 blocked merges across character categories (letters, digits, punctuation), with an exception for a leading space — which is why GPT-2 tokens so often look like Ġthe (a space-prefixed word).
  • Because the model can assign a probability to any string, GPT-2 could be evaluated on benchmarks with their own preprocessing without converting them — which is how its zero-shot language-modelling table was produced.
Takeaway: GPT-1 used a character-level BPE over pre-tokenised text; GPT-2 introduced byte-level BPE with 50,257 entries and GPT-3 kept it. Later tokenisers are larger but are documented only through the public tokeniser library, not the papers.

13 Compute: 2N FLOPs per token, 6ND to train

Each parameter takes part in one multiply and one add per token in the forward pass; the backward pass costs about twice the forward. That gives the two estimates every GPT-scale budget starts from. The derivation in multiply-accumulates is in Transformers — 6·N·D, derived; here it is applied to the family.

forward, per token (ignoring the attention n² term)C_fwd ≈ 2 · N training, over D tokensC_train ≈ 6 · N · D (2ND forward + 4ND backward) attention correction per token, per layer≈ 2 · n_ctx · d (forward) — small unless n_ctx approaches 12·d

GPT-3 175B, worked

  • N = 174.6 × 10⁹, D = 300 × 10⁹ tokens. 6 · N · D = 6 × 1.746×10¹¹ × 3×10¹¹ = 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs — the paper's own figure, 3.14E+23. That agreement checks the arithmetic, not the approximation: the paper's Appendix D computes its compute column the same way (one multiply and one add per active parameter per token forward, times 3 for the backward pass, ignoring attention).
  • A petaflop/s-day is 10¹⁵ × 86,400 = 8.64 × 10¹⁹ FLOPs, so the run is 3.14×10²³ / 8.64×10¹⁹ ≈ 3,640 petaflop/s-days, again matching the paper's 3.64E+03.
  • Per generated token at inference: 2N ≈ 3.5 × 10¹¹ FLOPs. The attention term, 2 · 2048 · 12,288 · 96 ≈ 4.8 × 10⁹ at full context, adds under 2%.
  • The GPT-3 paper notes that GPT-3 2.7B and RoBERTa-Large used roughly the same ~50 petaflop/s-days despite a ~10× difference in parameters — it trained larger models on fewer tokens, which is the choice later revisited in Scaling laws.
Training-compute explorer: C ≈ 6·N·D choose model size and token count · compare with 20 tokens per parameter · wall-clock at an assumed sustained throughput
Takeaway: 6ND is the same approximation the GPT-3 paper used for its published compute, so it reproduces that figure by construction; it is a good first estimate for a dense GPT whenever the attention term (n_ctx small next to 12·d) and recomputation overheads are small. Inference is cheaper per token (2N) but runs forever; training is paid once.

15 GPT-1: the fine-tuning recipe and input transformations

GPT-1 adapted one pretrained network to classification, entailment, similarity and multiple-choice tasks without changing its architecture. The trick was to turn every structured input into a single token sequence, and to keep the language-modelling loss switched on during fine-tuning.

The four transformations

  • Every input gets randomly initialised start and extract tokens, ⟨s⟩ … ⟨e⟩; the final state at ⟨e⟩ is what the linear head reads.
  • Entailment: premise, delimiter $, hypothesis — one sequence.
  • Similarity: the pair has no natural order, so both orders are run separately and their final states are added element-wise before the head.
  • Multiple choice / QA: context and question, delimiter, then one candidate answer — one sequence per candidate, with a softmax across candidates.
  • The only new parameters are W_y and the delimiter embeddings.

Hyperparameters and results

  • Fine-tuning reused pretraining settings except: learning rate 6.25e-5, batch 32, typically 3 epochs, linear decay with warm-up over 0.2% of training, classifier dropout 0.1, λ = 0.5.
  • It improved the state of the art on 9 of the 12 datasets studied, with absolute gains of 8.9% on Story Cloze, 5.7% on RACE, 1.5% on MultiNLI, and a GLUE score of 72.8 against the previous best of 68.9.
  • The auxiliary LM loss is often remembered as uniformly helpful. The paper's own ablation is more mixed: removing it lowered scores on the NLI tasks and QQP, but the unweighted average was 75.0 without it against 74.7 with it. The authors conclude that larger datasets benefit and smaller ones do not.
  • Removing pretraining altogether cost 14.8% on average — the headline result the paper was built around.
Takeaway: GPT-1's adaptation interface was "serialise the task, fine-tune everything, read the last token". The same serialisation idea, without the fine-tuning, is what GPT-2 and GPT-3 pushed into the prompt.

16 GPT-2: zero-shot, with the task written as text

GPT-2's argument: a task is a conditional distribution p(output | input, task), and natural language can specify all three. A good enough language model trained on diverse text should already have learned many tasks, because the web contains their demonstrations — "TL;DR:" before summaries, "A:" before answers, English sentences next to French ones.

How tasks were induced

  • Summarisation: append TL;DR: to an article, sample 100 tokens with top-k (k = 2), keep the first three sentences.
  • Translation: condition on example pairs formatted english sentence = french sentence, then a final english sentence =, decode greedily.
  • Reading comprehension (CoQA): document, conversation history, then a final A:; greedy decoding.
  • Winograd / Children's Book Test: score each candidate by the probability the model assigns, rather than generating.
  • LAMBADA: predict the final word of the passage; accuracy rose from 52.66% to 63.24% when a stop-word filter approximated the constraint that the word must end the sentence.

17 GPT-3: the model and how it was trained

GPT-3 is GPT-2's architecture — "including the modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization" — made 100× larger, with one change inside the stack: alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention layers. The paper's contribution is less the model than the systematic evaluation across eight sizes and three prompting settings.

The architectural change

  • Layers alternate between ordinary dense causal attention and a locally banded sparse pattern "similar to the Sparse Transformer", in which each query attends only to a band of nearby earlier positions.
  • The paper does not state the band width, the exact alternation, or the measured effect; it is one sentence long. Reproductions of GPT-3-sized models (OPT-175B, BLOOM) generally used dense attention throughout, so the effect of this choice on GPT-3's results is not publicly isolated.
  • The model was partitioned across GPUs along both depth and width, and "the precise architectural parameters for each model are chosen based on computational efficiency and load-balancing" — which plausibly explains the d_head values of 64, 80, 96 and 128 across sizes (the paper gives no per-size rationale).

Why eight sizes, all on 300B tokens

  • Training every size on the same 300B tokens lets the paper plot each capability against parameter count with data held fixed — a controlled size sweep from 125M to 175B, over three orders of magnitude.
  • The token count was chosen under the scaling-law advice of the time (Scaling laws), which favoured large models trained short of convergence. Measured by the later compute-optimal analysis, 175B on 300B tokens is about 1.7 tokens per parameter, far below the ~20 that analysis implies.
  • Validation loss followed a smooth power law in compute across the eight models (the paper's Figure 3.1), and the train–validation gap grew only minimally with model size and training time (its Figure 4.1).

20 GPT-3: failure cases and the contamination analysis

The GPT-3 paper is unusually explicit about where its model failed and about a data-leak bug it could not afford to fix. Both are worth knowing because they recur in every later large model evaluation.

Where it was weak

  • Sentence-pair comparison. WiC (does a word have the same meaning in two sentences) was at chance: 49.4% few-shot. ANLI rounds 1 and 2 stayed near chance for every size.
  • Some reading comprehension. Few-shot RACE-high 46.8% (fine-tuned best 90.0%), QuAC 44.3 F1 (74.4), DROP 36.5 F1 (89.1). The paper notes these tasks need comparing or re-reading passages, which a single left-to-right pass handles poorly.
  • Arithmetic beyond three digits. Few-shot 175B: 2-digit addition 100%, 3-digit 80.4%, 4-digit 25.5%, 5-digit 9.3%, 2-digit multiplication 29.2%.
  • Generation quality. Samples "sometimes repeat themselves semantically at the document level, start to lose coherence over sufficiently long passages, contradict themselves". Common-sense physics questions such as "If I put cheese into the fridge, will it melt?" were a named weakness.
  • Bias. Among 388 occupations, 83% were more likely to be followed by a male identifier; the paper also reports race and religion associations in co-occurrence tests.
  • Detectability. Human judges identified ~200-word GPT-3 175B news articles as machine-written with about 52% accuracy — near chance — versus 86% for a deliberately bad control model; for ~500-word articles the figures were 52% and 88% (the paper's Tables 3.11 and 3.12).

Contamination: the bug and the audit

  • The authors tried to remove every benchmark test/dev example from the training data, but "a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model".
  • Instead they built a "clean" subset of each benchmark by dropping any example with a 13-gram overlap with the pretraining data, then compared scores on clean versus full sets. A quarter of benchmarks were flagged as over 50% potentially contaminated, yet for most the score barely changed.
  • They flagged PIQA (29% of examples flagged; 3-point drop on the clean subset) and Winograd (45% flagged; 132 schemas actually present in training data; 2.6% drop), and marked those results with asterisks. For QuAC, SQuAD2 and DROP over 90% of examples were flagged, but manual inspection found the source passages in training data, not the question–answer pairs.
  • The conservative 13-gram method probably over-flags; it cannot prove that the remaining results are uncontaminated. The same limitation applies to later web-scale models evaluated this way.
Takeaway: GPT-3's strengths were cloze, closed-book QA and pattern-following; its weaknesses were pairwise comparison, multi-step reading and long arithmetic. Its contamination audit set the template — clean-subset comparison — for later model reports.

21 In-context learning: what it is and what might explain it

In-context learning (ICL) is a frozen model performing a task better because demonstrations of it appear in the prompt. The GPT-3 paper framed pretraining as an outer loop that builds general skills and ICL as an inner loop that uses them at inference. What happens inside the forward pass is still an open research question.

ICL as conditioning, no parameter changeŷ = argmaxᵧ P_θ( y | (x₁, y₁), …, (x_K, y_K), x_query ) θ fixed

Evidence about what the demonstrations do

  • Format and label space matter a lot; correct labels sometimes matter little. "Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations" (arXiv 2202.12837) found that replacing demonstration labels with random ones "barely hurts" on many classification and multiple-choice tasks across 12 models including GPT-3. The demonstrations appeared to convey the input distribution, the label set and the format.
  • But larger models can learn the mapping. Follow-up work ("Larger language models do in-context learning differently", arXiv 2303.03846) found that sufficiently large models follow flipped labels in the prompt, overriding their priors — so the "labels do not matter" result depends on model size and task.
  • ICL is fragile. "Calibrate Before Use" (arXiv 2102.09690) showed GPT-3's few-shot accuracy swinging widely with the choice and order of examples, driven by biases toward the majority label, the most recent label, and common tokens. Order sensitivity alone can move accuracy from near-chance to near-state-of-the-art on some tasks (arXiv 2104.08786).

Takeaway: ICL is well established as a behaviour and poorly understood as a mechanism. Treat single-prompt few-shot numbers as having high variance, and treat any one-line explanation of why it works as a hypothesis.

22 The "emergence" debate

An ability is called emergent if it is near zero in smaller models and appears sharply in larger ones, so that it could not have been predicted by extrapolation. GPT-3's arithmetic curves are a canonical example. Whether such jumps are properties of the models or of the metrics is disputed.

The two positions

  • For: "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" (arXiv 2206.07682) catalogued dozens of tasks — multi-digit arithmetic, some BIG-Bench tasks, chain-of-thought benefits — where performance is flat near chance and then rises steeply past a model scale.
  • Against: "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" (arXiv 2304.15004) argued that most such jumps come from nonlinear or discontinuous metrics such as exact match. When the same outputs are scored with a continuous metric (per-token accuracy, edit distance, log-likelihood of the correct answer), the curves become smooth and predictable. It tested this on the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family directly.

The metric argument, by hand

Suppose a model must emit a 4-token answer and gets each token right independently with probability p. Exact-match accuracy is p⁴.

  • A smooth improvement in p across model sizes produces a curve in exact match that looks flat, then sudden. A multi-digit sum scored by exact match is exactly this kind of all-or-nothing metric, so part of GPT-3's 8.4% → 80.4% three-digit-addition jump between 13B and 175B is consistent with this mechanism.
  • The counter-argument: not every jump disappears under continuous metrics, only 8 size points are available, and the practical question — "can this model do the task?" — often is an exact-match question. GPT-4's report treats the related problem as forecasting and shows some capabilities predicted in advance (see GPT-4).
Takeaway: "emergence" is best stated as an observation about a metric at a set of scales, not as a property of the model. The evidence that metric choice explains many reported jumps is strong; the claim that it explains all of them is not established.

23 Scaling laws, as they bear on GPT

Two papers frame the GPT-3 era: "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (arXiv 2001.08361), which shaped GPT-3's choices, and "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (arXiv 2203.15556), which argued those choices undertrained it. The general theory is in Large Language Models — scaling; this is the GPT-specific slice.

loss vs non-embedding parameters (2001.08361, data not limiting)L(N) = (N_c / N)^0.076 N_c ≈ 8.8 × 10¹³ compute-efficient allocation, same paperN_opt ∝ C^0.73 D_opt ∝ C^0.27 compute-optimal allocation (2203.15556)N_opt ∝ C^a, D_opt ∝ C^b, a ≈ b ≈ 0.5 (a = 0.46–0.50 across its three methods) ≈ 20 tokens per parameter

Worked against GPT-2 and GPT-3

  • Plugging non-embedding counts into L(N): GPT-2 XL (1.47 × 10⁹) gives about 2.31 nats per token; GPT-3 175B (1.74 × 10¹¹) about 1.61. These are loss values on the scaling-law paper's own WebText2 test set and tokeniser, useful for the ratio, not as a prediction of GPT-3's reported loss.
  • Under the 2020 allocation (N ∝ C^0.73), most extra compute should go to parameters — hence 175B trained on only 300B tokens, about 1.7 tokens per parameter.
  • The compute-optimal analysis found parameters and tokens should grow equally. Its 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens (20 per parameter) with the same compute as the 280B Gopher outperformed Gopher and GPT-3 on a large majority of evaluated tasks. At 20 tokens per parameter, a compute-optimal 175B model would need about 3.5T tokens — nearly 12× what GPT-3 used.
  • Inference economics push further: a smaller model trained on more tokens than compute-optimal costs more to train but less to serve, which is why many post-2022 GPT-style open models train well beyond 20 tokens per parameter.
Takeaway: GPT-3's shape (huge N, modest D) was a faithful application of the 2020 scaling advice, and the 2022 re-analysis showed that advice over-weighted parameters. Both results are empirical fits over specific ranges, not laws of nature.

Greedy and beam, and why neither is the default for open-ended text

  • Greedy is not the most probable sequence. If step 1 offers A (0.6) or B (0.4), and the best continuation after A has probability 0.3 but after B has 0.9, greedy takes A for a sequence probability 0.18, while B's path scores 0.36. A beam of width 2 finds it.
  • Beam search keeps the b best partial sequences; a length penalty stops it favouring short outputs. It suits tasks with one right answer (GPT-3's free-form QA and translation).
  • For open-ended text, maximising probability produces bland, repetitive output — the observation behind nucleus sampling ("The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", arXiv 1904.09751), which showed human text is not the highest-likelihood text a model can produce. Truncated sampling keeps diversity while cutting the unreliable tail.
  • Repetition controls: the CTRL paper (arXiv 1909.05858) divides the logits of already-generated tokens by a penalty (1.2 in that paper); API-style presence/frequency penalties subtract from logits instead. Worked: logit 2.0 for a token already used twice, with α_freq = 0.5 and α_pres = 0.3, becomes 2.0 − 1.0 − 0.3 = 0.7.
Takeaway: temperature reshapes, top-k and top-p truncate, penalties discourage repeats, and beam search optimises. Use beam or greedy when there is one correct answer; use truncated sampling when you want text that reads like a person wrote it.

25 The KV cache, prefill and decode

Because of the causal mask, the keys and values of earlier tokens never change when a token is appended. Storing them turns each generation step from "re-run the whole prefix" into "run one token against a cache". That cache, rather than the arithmetic, is often what limits GPT-scale serving at long contexts and large batches. The general analysis is in Attention — the KV cache.

cache size (multi-head attention, as in GPT-1/2/3)bytes = 2 (K and V) · L · d_model · bytes_per_value · n_tokens · batch GPT-3 175B, 16-bit valuesper token = 2 · 96 · 12,288 · 2 B = 4,718,592 B = 4.5 MiB 2,048 tokens = 9.0 GiB per sequence

Prefill and decode are different workloads

  • Prefill processes the whole prompt in one parallel pass (like training, without the backward). For a P-token prompt it costs about 2·N·P FLOPs and is compute-bound: every weight loaded is reused across P tokens.
  • Decode produces one token per step. Each step does about 2N FLOPs but must read every weight and the whole cache from memory, so at small batch it is memory-bandwidth-bound: roughly 2 FLOPs per 2-byte weight, far below what accelerators need to stay busy.
  • Worked, as an illustration only: GPT-3's 16-bit weights are 174.6×10⁹ × 2 B ≈ 349 GB. On hardware with an aggregate 2 TB/s of memory bandwidth, just streaming the weights once takes about 0.17 s — a floor on per-token latency at batch 1, regardless of FLOP rate. Batching amortises that read across sequences, which is why serving systems batch aggressively and why cache size bounds the batch.
KV-cache memory against context and batch pick a model · slide context and batch · compared with the weights' own size
  • At GPT-3 scale, a batch of 32 full-context sequences needs 32 × 9 GiB = 288 GiB of cache — most of the size of the weights again. Later architectures cut this with multi-query and grouped-query attention (fewer K/V heads) or latent compression; none of the documented GPT-1/2/3 models used them.
  • Speeding up decode without changing the model is the subject of Speculative Decoding; shrinking weights and cache, of Model Acceleration.
Takeaway: a GPT generates by re-reading its weights and its cache once per token. Prefill is limited by arithmetic, decode by memory bandwidth — and the cache grows linearly with context and batch until it, not the weights, sets the serving limit.

The three stages, as published

  • 1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). About 40 contractors wrote demonstrations for prompts drawn from the API and written by labellers; ~13k training prompts. SFT ran 16 epochs even though validation loss overfit after one, because reward-model score and human preference kept improving.
  • 2. Reward model (RM). Labellers ranked K = 4 to 9 outputs per prompt, giving C(K,2) comparisons (6 for K = 4, 36 for K = 9); ~33k training prompts. A 6B RM was used for all policy sizes, since the paper found 175B RM training could be unstable. Worked: a reward gap of 1.0 gives σ(1.0) = 0.731 predicted preference and loss −ln 0.731 = 0.313 for that pair.
  • 3. PPO. ~31k API prompts; the policy is rewarded by the RM with a per-token KL penalty to the SFT model; "PPO-ptx" adds pretraining gradients (γ > 0) to reduce regressions on public NLP benchmarks — the "alignment tax". Worked KL term: if the policy gives a token 0.30 where SFT gave 0.10, the log-ratio is ln 3 = 1.10; with β = 0.02 that token costs 0.022 reward.

Results the paper reports

  • Outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT were preferred to 175B GPT-3's on the API prompt distribution, despite over 100× fewer parameters. 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time, and to few-shot-prompted GPT-3 71 ± 4%.
  • TruthfulQA: truthful and informative about twice as often as GPT-3. Closed-domain hallucination: 21% versus 41%. About 25% fewer toxic outputs when prompted to be respectful. No significant improvement on bias benchmarks (Winogender, CrowS-Pairs).
  • Cost: training the 175B SFT model took 4.9 petaflop/s-days and the 175B PPO-ptx model 60, against 3,640 for GPT-3 pretraining — under 2%.
  • ChatGPT (November 2022) used "the same methods as InstructGPT, but with slight differences in the data collection setup", fine-tuned from a GPT-3.5-series model on dialogue-formatted data. No paper documents its size or data.
Takeaway: preference-based post-training changed user-rated quality more than a 100× increase in parameters, for under 2% of the pretraining compute. It did not fix bias, and without the pretraining mix it regressed on several public NLP benchmarks.

27 GPT-4: what the technical report states, and what it withholds

The GPT-4 report (arXiv 2303.08774) is explicit about its own scope: it "contains no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, training method, or similar", citing "the competitive landscape and the safety implications". Everything below is limited to what it does state; parameter counts that circulate elsewhere are not from the report.

Stated

  • Model: "a Transformer-style model pre-trained to predict the next token in a document", on publicly available and licensed data, then fine-tuned with RLHF. It accepts image and text inputs and produces text outputs; interleaved images and text are supported, and few-shot and chain-of-thought prompting work with images too.
  • Predictable scaling: the final loss on an internal codebase was predicted by fitting L(C) = a·C^b + c to models trained with at most 10,000× less compute, shortly after the run started. Pass rate on a subset of HumanEval was predicted from models with at most 1,000× less compute.
  • Exams and benchmarks: simulated Uniform Bar Exam 298/400 (around the top 10% of test takers; GPT-3.5 scored 213/400, around the bottom 10%). MMLU 86.4% (5-shot) versus 70.0% for GPT-3.5. HumanEval 67.0% versus 48.1%.
  • Inverse scaling: on the "hindsight neglect" task, where smaller models got worse with scale, GPT-4 reversed the trend.
  • Calibration: the pre-trained model was well calibrated on an MMLU subset; post-training "hurts calibration significantly".
  • Safety: an added set of safety-relevant RLHF prompts and rule-based reward models (RBRMs) — zero-shot GPT-4 classifiers that score outputs against rubrics. The report states an 82% reduction versus GPT-3.5 in responding to disallowed-content requests.
  • Limitations: it hallucinates, has a limited context window, does not learn from experience, and its knowledge largely stops at September 2021.

Withheld or not stated

  • Parameter count, depth, width, attention variant, any use of mixture-of-experts, tokeniser (only knowable from the public library, see Tokenisation), training tokens, data mixture, compute, and hardware.
  • Context length is not stated in the report; the launch API offered 8,192- and 32,768-token variants.
  • The contamination analysis exists (per-benchmark, in its appendices) and found part of BIG-bench mixed into training data; the report did not check RLHF data for TruthfulQA contamination.
Takeaway: GPT-4 is documented as a capability and safety report, not a model paper. Its main methodological contribution that transfers is loss and capability forecasting from much smaller runs; its architecture is not publicly documented.

Takeaway: open reproductions showed the GPT-3 recipe was reproducible from the paper plus engineering, that its sparse attention was optional, and that learned positions were the part most worth replacing.

Why the decoder-only design won at scale

  • BERT's paper compared directly against GPT-1 at the same size and attributed its gains largely to bidirectionality — a fair result at that scale. Why the decoder-only design later became the default at scale is not settled by any controlled comparison; the commonly cited reasons are a loss at every position, one stack for both reading and writing, and a prompt interface that needs no task-specific head.
  • For inputs that fit in context, a decoder-only model can emulate an encoder–decoder by putting the input in the prefix; the cost is that the "input" tokens are processed with a causal mask rather than bidirectionally.
Takeaway: GPT gives up bidirectional context in exchange for a dense loss, exact likelihoods and native generation. At small scale that trade loses on understanding benchmarks; at large scale the prompt interface made it the default.

30 Limitations of the GPT design

Some limitations come from the objective, some from the architecture, and some from scale. Each has its own page; the GPT-specific view is summarised here.

From the objective

  • Hallucination. Next-token prediction rewards plausible continuations, not true ones. GPT-3's closed-book QA and InstructGPT's closed-domain hallucination rates (41% for GPT-3 on the paper's API prompts) are direct measurements. Detection and mitigation: Hallucination & Factuality.
  • Frozen knowledge. Everything the model knows comes from a corpus with a cutoff (December 2017 links for WebText; September 2021 for most of GPT-4's data). Retrieval is the usual remedy: Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
  • Bias. The model reproduces associations in its data — GPT-3's occupation and religion probes, and InstructGPT's lack of improvement on bias benchmarks.

From the architecture

  • Hard context ceiling of 512, 1024 or 2048 tokens for GPT-1/2/3, fixed by the learned position table; and quadratic attention cost in context length. Remedies: Context-Length Extension.
  • Left-to-right only. Tasks that need comparing two passages or revising earlier output (WiC, ANLI, DROP for GPT-3) are harder in a single causal pass. Multi-step prompting and reasoning methods partly compensate: Reasoning in LLMs.

From scale

  • Cost. 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs to train GPT-3 and 349 GB of 16-bit weights to serve it; the GPT-3 paper itself notes generating 100 pages costs on the order of 0.4 kWh. Serving economics: LLMOps.
  • Evaluation contamination. Web-scale training data overlaps benchmarks; the GPT-2, GPT-3 and GPT-4 reports each include an overlap analysis, and none can rule it out.
  • Opacity. From GPT-4 on, the architecture and data are not public, so external claims about why the model behaves as it does cannot be checked against its design.
Takeaway: the objective explains hallucination and bias, the architecture explains the context ceiling and weakness at comparison tasks, and scale explains cost and contamination. Fixing one layer rarely fixes the others.

31 Pitfalls

Mistakes that recur when reasoning about, reproducing, or using GPT-family models.

When reading or quoting the papers

  • Quoting "117M" and "345M". The code repository itself calls them errors; the checkpoints are 124M and 355M. Use the formula in §12 when a number matters.
  • Assuming GPT-3 used dense attention in every layer. The paper says alternating dense and locally banded sparse — without the details, which is why exact reproduction is impossible from the paper alone.
  • Citing a GPT-4 parameter count. The report gives none; any number is from outside the report and should be labelled as such.
  • Comparing perplexities across tokenisers. Per-token perplexity depends on how many tokens a text becomes. Compare bits per byte or per character instead, as GPT-2 did for enwik8 and text8.
  • Treating one few-shot number as the model's ability. Example choice and order can move accuracy by tens of points; report means over several prompts.

When implementing or serving

  • Off-by-one targets. Logits at position i predict token i+1; shift labels by one and drop the last logit. Forgetting the shift trains a copy model with near-zero loss.
  • Porting pre-LN weights without the final LayerNorm, or applying post-LN order to GPT-2/3 weights. Outputs degrade badly, often into repetition or noise, without any error being raised.
  • Exceeding the position table. GPT-2 has no row 1025; silently truncating or wrapping positions produces garbage beyond the limit.
  • Packing documents without the end-of-text separator. GPT-3 relies on it (not a mask) to tell unrelated documents apart.
  • Underestimating the KV cache. At GPT-3 scale it is 4.5 MiB per token per sequence at 16 bits; a large batch of long contexts can exceed the weights' size.
  • Expecting temperature 0 to be bit-reproducible. Greedy decoding is deterministic in exact arithmetic, but batched floating-point kernels on accelerators often are not, so ties and near-ties can flip between runs.
Takeaway: most GPT mistakes are bookkeeping — a shifted label, a missing LayerNorm, a mislabelled size, an unfair comparison — rather than deep modelling errors. The formulas on this page catch most of them.

33 What to remember

Ten statements that summarise the family, each backed by a section above.

Ten statements

  • One objective. Every GPT is trained on next-token cross-entropy under a causal mask; the chain rule makes this an exact likelihood model.
  • Parallel training, sequential generation. The mask gives n training targets per pass and makes the KV cache valid, but generation needs one dependent step per token.
  • A nearly fixed block. GELU, learned positions and a tied output from GPT-1; pre-LN, a final LN and 1/√N residual init from GPT-2; alternating banded sparse attention in GPT-3.
  • Byte-level BPE with 50,257 entries arrived with GPT-2 and was kept by GPT-3.
  • Arithmetic you can check. 12·L·d² + V·d reproduces the reported sizes (and exposes the 117M label); 6ND gives GPT-3's published 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs (the paper computed it the same way).
  • The interface changed each generation: fine-tune with input transformations (GPT-1), write the task as text (GPT-2), add demonstrations (GPT-3), align to preferences (InstructGPT).
  • In-context learning is a robust behaviour with an unsettled mechanism and high prompt sensitivity; "emergence" is at least partly a property of the metric.
  • GPT-3 was undertrained by later compute-optimal standards — about 1.7 tokens per parameter against roughly 20.
  • Post-training beat scale for user preference: 1.3B InstructGPT over 175B GPT-3, for under 2% of pretraining compute.
  • GPT-4 is documented by capabilities, not design. Quote only what its report states.
Takeaway: GPT is the clearest case in modern ML of one simple objective, scaled with care and adapted through ever-lighter interfaces, turning into a general-purpose system. Understanding the arithmetic and the evidential status of each claim matters more than memorising benchmark scores.
