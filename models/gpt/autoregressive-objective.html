02 Why generative pretraining

Before 2018 most NLP systems were trained from scratch per task on labelled data, with at most pretrained word vectors as input. The GPT-1 bet was that predicting raw text is a task hard enough to force a model to learn syntax, semantics and some world knowledge — and that this knowledge would transfer.

The problem it addressed

  • Labelled data is scarce; raw text is not. A sentiment dataset may have tens of thousands of examples, while a book corpus has hundreds of millions of tokens, each of which is a free supervised target for next-token prediction.
  • Word vectors transfer only the first layer. Earlier transfer (word2vec, GloVe, and contextual features such as ELMo) handed downstream models a representation, but the task architecture still had to be designed and trained. GPT-1 transferred the whole network and added only a linear head.
  • Recurrent language models struggled with long-range structure. The GPT-1 paper chose a Transformer explicitly for its "more structured memory", and its ablation replaced it with a single-layer 2048-unit LSTM in the same framework: the average score across its benchmark suite fell by 5.6 points.

Why "generative" and not some other pretext task

  • Next-token prediction supplies a training signal at every position of every document. A masked objective such as BERT's scores only the ~15% of positions it corrupted; the decoder objective is denser per token processed.
  • The same objective is also a sampler: a model that assigns P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) can generate text, score text, and — as GPT-2 argued — perform any task whose input and output can be written as a string.
  • The cost is unidirectionality: a token's representation never sees tokens to its right, which is a real handicap for pure understanding tasks at small scale. That trade is examined in GPT vs BERT vs encoder–decoder.
Takeaway: the motivating claim was transfer, not generation. Generation came for free from the objective and turned out, at larger scale, to be the more important property.

04 The autoregressive factorisation

The chain rule of probability lets any joint distribution over a sequence be written as a product of conditionals. GPT models exactly those conditionals, in left-to-right order, with one network shared across positions.

chain rule — exact, no approximationP(x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄) = P(x₁) · P(x₂ | x₁) · P(x₃ | x₁, x₂) · P(x₄ | x₁, x₂, x₃) the modelling step — one network, every prefixP_θ(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) = softmax( W_U · h_L,ᵢ )[xᵢ] GPT-1's form, with a finite window of k tokensL₁(U) = ∑ᵢ log P(uᵢ | uᵢ₋ₖ, …, uᵢ₋₁; Θ)

What the factorisation buys

  • It is exact. Nothing is assumed about independence; any distribution over strings can in principle be represented. Apart from the network's finite capacity, the one structural truncation is the finite context window (512, 1024 or 2048 tokens for GPT-1/2/3), which cuts off the conditioning set.
  • It gives a proper likelihood. The model can score any string, so perplexity is well defined and comparable across models that share a tokeniser — the GPT-2 paper names, as a benefit of its byte-level tokeniser, that the model can be evaluated on any benchmark regardless of its preprocessing.
  • It gives ancestral sampling. Draw x₁, then x₂ given it, and so on. Every decoding strategy in Decoding is a modification of this loop.

What it costs

  • The order is fixed. The model never conditions on the right context, so infilling ("fill the middle") needs either a special data format or a different model.
  • Generation is sequential in n: each token needs a forward pass that depends on the previous token. Training is not — see Causal masking.
  • Errors compound: a poor early token becomes conditioning for every later one, and the model was trained only on human-written prefixes, never its own mistakes (the "exposure bias" argument, whose practical size is debated).
Takeaway: the chain rule is an identity, so the modelling choice in GPT is not the factorisation but the ordering (left to right) and the parameter sharing (one network for every prefix).

05 Next-token cross-entropy, worked by hand

The training loss is the average negative log-probability the model assigns to the token that actually came next. Perplexity is its exponential. Both are easiest to understand on four numbers.

per-sequence loss (nats) and perplexityℒ = − (1/n) ∑ᵢ ln P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) PPL = exp(ℒ) logits to probabilities over a vocabulary VP(v) = exp(zᵥ) / ∑ᵤ exp(zᵤ)

A hand-worked sequence

Suppose a model reads "<s> the cat sat on" and assigns these probabilities to the true next token at each step: P(the | <s>) = 0.05, P(cat | the) = 0.10, P(sat | the cat) = 0.40, P(on | the cat sat) = 0.60.

sum / mean6.725 / 1.681 nats
  • Mean loss 1.681 nats, so perplexity exp(1.681) ≈ 5.37: on average the model was as uncertain as a uniform choice among about 5.4 tokens. In bits, divide by ln 2: 2.43 bits per token.
  • The early tokens dominate: the first two steps contribute 5.30 of the 6.73 total. That pattern — high loss where context is short, low loss once the topic is set — is typical of real language models too.
  • A uniform guess over GPT-2's 50,257 tokens would score ln 50,257 ≈ 10.82 nats; that is the loss at initialisation if the output logits start near zero.

The gradient is simple

  • With respect to the logits at one position, the gradient of cross-entropy is p − onehot(true): push the true token's logit up by 1 − p_true, push every other logit down by its own probability.
  • Because every position is a target, one 2048-token GPT-3 training sequence yields 2048 of these gradients in one forward and backward pass.
Takeaway: the loss is "how surprised was the model by what actually came next", averaged over every position. Perplexity is that surprise expressed as an effective branching factor, and it is only comparable between models that share a tokeniser.

06 Causal masking: parallel training, sequential generation

If every position predicts its successor, position i must not see position i+1. A lower-triangular mask on the attention scores enforces that — and lets all n predictions be trained in a single pass.

The causal mask, cell by cell click a row to see which keys that query may attend to · change n
masked attention scoresSᵢⱼ = (qᵢ · kⱼ) / √dₖ + Mᵢⱼ Mᵢⱼ = 0 if j ≤ i, −∞ if j > i after softmax, future weights are exactly zeroAᵢⱼ = exp(Sᵢⱼ) / ∑ₘ≤ᵢ exp(Sᵢₘ) allowed (query, key) pairs out of n²n(n+1)/2 e.g. n = 4 → 10 of 16

The n = 4 mask, written out

k₁ k₂ k₃ k₄ q₁ [ 0 −∞ −∞ −∞ ] q₂ [ 0 0 −∞ −∞ ] q₃ [ 0 0 0 −∞ ] q₄ [ 0 0 0 0 ]

Why training is parallel

  • With teacher forcing, the true sequence is the input. Position 3 predicts x₄ from x₁…x₃, position 4 predicts x₅ from x₁…x₄, and so on — every prefix is already present, so all n predictions come from one masked matrix multiply.
  • The mask must hold at every layer. If any layer let position i read i+1, information about the target would leak upward through the residual stream and the training loss could fall toward zero without the model learning to predict unseen text.

Why generation is sequential

  • At inference the next token does not exist until it is chosen. Step t needs the token from step t−1, so m new tokens need m dependent forward passes.
  • The mask also means past positions' keys and values never change when a token is appended — which is exactly what makes the KV cache valid.
  • The attention maths (scaling, the −∞ versus large-negative constant, padding masks) is covered in Attention — masking.
Takeaway: the causal mask turns one sequence into n training examples at the cost of one pass, and guarantees that cached keys and values stay valid during generation. Relative to BERT, it is the only structural difference in a GPT-1 block; from GPT-2 on, the LayerNorm placement differs as well (pre-LN versus BERT's post-LN).

07 Generation, step by step

Generation is a loop: run the model on the current sequence, take the last position's distribution, choose a token, append it, repeat. The figure runs that loop on a toy model with fixed logits per step so every probability can be checked.

Autoregressive loop on a toy model each step: logits → softmax → pick → append · running log-probability and perplexity update

What the loop does and does not do

  • Each step conditions on the whole prefix, including tokens the model itself produced. Forcing a different token at step 1 changes every later distribution — that is the branching that beam search and sampling explore.
  • The model has no notion of "done" except a special token. GPT-2 and GPT-3 use <|endoftext|> as a document separator; generation stops when it is emitted or a length limit is reached.
  • The sequence log-probability is the sum of the per-step log-probabilities of the chosen tokens. Greedy decoding maximises each term locally, which does not in general maximise the sum — a point made concrete in Decoding.
Takeaway: generation reuses the training-time distribution one position at a time. Given a fixed model, the decoding choices all live in the "choose a token" step.
