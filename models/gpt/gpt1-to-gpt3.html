GPT: GPT-1 to GPT-3 & In-Context Learning

15 GPT-1: the fine-tuning recipe and input transformations

GPT-1 adapted one pretrained network to classification, entailment, similarity and multiple-choice tasks without changing its architecture. The trick was to turn every structured input into a single token sequence, and to keep the language-modelling loss switched on during fine-tuning.

Task-specific input transformations pick a task · each row is one forward pass · the final token's state feeds a linear head
supervised head on the last token's final stateP(y | x₁, …, xₘ) = softmax( h_Lᵐ · W_y ) L₂(C) = ∑₍ₓ,ᵧ₎ log P(y | x₁, …, xₘ) combined fine-tuning objective, weight λ = 0.5L₃(C) = L₂(C) + λ · L₁(C)

The four transformations

  • Every input gets randomly initialised start and extract tokens, ⟨s⟩ … ⟨e⟩; the final state at ⟨e⟩ is what the linear head reads.
  • Entailment: premise, delimiter $, hypothesis — one sequence.
  • Similarity: the pair has no natural order, so both orders are run separately and their final states are added element-wise before the head.
  • Multiple choice / QA: context and question, delimiter, then one candidate answer — one sequence per candidate, with a softmax across candidates.
  • The only new parameters are W_y and the delimiter embeddings.

Hyperparameters and results

  • Fine-tuning reused pretraining settings except: learning rate 6.25e-5, batch 32, typically 3 epochs, linear decay with warm-up over 0.2% of training, classifier dropout 0.1, λ = 0.5.
  • It improved the state of the art on 9 of the 12 datasets studied, with absolute gains of 8.9% on Story Cloze, 5.7% on RACE, 1.5% on MultiNLI, and a GLUE score of 72.8 against the previous best of 68.9.
  • The auxiliary LM loss is often remembered as uniformly helpful. The paper's own ablation is more mixed: removing it lowered scores on the NLI tasks and QQP, but the unweighted average was 75.0 without it against 74.7 with it. The authors conclude that larger datasets benefit and smaller ones do not.
  • Removing pretraining altogether cost 14.8% on average — the headline result the paper was built around.
Takeaway: GPT-1's adaptation interface was "serialise the task, fine-tune everything, read the last token". The same serialisation idea, without the fine-tuning, is what GPT-2 and GPT-3 pushed into the prompt.

16 GPT-2: zero-shot, with the task written as text

GPT-2's argument: a task is a conditional distribution p(output | input, task), and natural language can specify all three. A good enough language model trained on diverse text should already have learned many tasks, because the web contains their demonstrations — "TL;DR:" before summaries, "A:" before answers, English sentences next to French ones.

How tasks were induced

  • Summarisation: append TL;DR: to an article, sample 100 tokens with top-k (k = 2), keep the first three sentences.
  • Translation: condition on example pairs formatted english sentence = french sentence, then a final english sentence =, decode greedily.
  • Reading comprehension (CoQA): document, conversation history, then a final A:; greedy decoding.
  • Winograd / Children's Book Test: score each candidate by the probability the model assigns, rather than generating.
  • LAMBADA: predict the final word of the passage; accuracy rose from 52.66% to 63.24% when a stop-word filter approximated the constraint that the word must end the sentence.

  • It set the state of the art on 7 of 8 language-modelling datasets zero-shot — all except the One Billion Word Benchmark, whose sentence-level shuffling removes the long-range structure GPT-2 exploits.
  • Performance rose roughly log-linearly with size on the tasks in the paper's Figure 1, and the authors note the largest model "still underfits WebText".
  • Overlap check: 8-gram Bloom filters found WebText overlaps benchmark test sets by 1–6% (3.2% on average) — less than many benchmarks overlap their own training splits (5.9%). Removing overlapping LAMBADA examples moved perplexity from 8.6 to 8.7.
  • GPT-2 was released in stages over 2019, starting with the smallest model, because of concerns about misuse — a release policy that became part of the model's history.
Takeaway: GPT-2 did not beat supervised systems on most tasks. Its contribution was showing that task behaviour could be invoked by text with no gradient step, and that this improved smoothly with scale — the premise GPT-3 then tested at 100× the size.

17 GPT-3: the model and how it was trained

GPT-3 is GPT-2's architecture — "including the modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization" — made 100× larger, with one change inside the stack: alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention layers. The paper's contribution is less the model than the systematic evaluation across eight sizes and three prompting settings.

The architectural change

  • Layers alternate between ordinary dense causal attention and a locally banded sparse pattern "similar to the Sparse Transformer", in which each query attends only to a band of nearby earlier positions.
  • The paper does not state the band width, the exact alternation, or the measured effect; it is one sentence long. Reproductions of GPT-3-sized models (OPT-175B, BLOOM) generally used dense attention throughout, so the effect of this choice on GPT-3's results is not publicly isolated.
  • The model was partitioned across GPUs along both depth and width, and "the precise architectural parameters for each model are chosen based on computational efficiency and load-balancing" — which plausibly explains the d_head values of 64, 80, 96 and 128 across sizes (the paper gives no per-size rationale).

Why eight sizes, all on 300B tokens

  • Training every size on the same 300B tokens lets the paper plot each capability against parameter count with data held fixed — a controlled size sweep from 125M to 175B, over three orders of magnitude.
  • The token count was chosen under the scaling-law advice of the time (Scaling laws), which favoured large models trained short of convergence. Measured by the later compute-optimal analysis, 175B on 300B tokens is about 1.7 tokens per parameter, far below the ~20 that analysis implies.
  • Validation loss followed a smooth power law in compute across the eight models (the paper's Figure 3.1), and the train–validation gap grew only minimally with model size and training time (its Figure 4.1).
the headline numbers, recomputedN = 12 · 96 · 12288² + 50257 · 12288 + … ≈ 174.6 × 10⁹ C = 6 · N · D = 6 · 174.6×10⁹ · 300×10⁹ ≈ 3.14 × 10²³ FLOPs ≈ 3,640 PF/s-days
Takeaway: architecturally GPT-3 is GPT-2 at 100× scale plus an under-documented sparse-attention change. Its lasting contribution is methodological: a fixed-data size sweep, evaluated zero-, one- and few-shot on dozens of tasks.

18 Zero-, one- and few-shot prompting

GPT-3 defined three evaluation settings that differ only in what is placed in the context before the query. No gradients are taken in any of them; "learning" happens only through the forward pass.

Few-shot prompt builder with a 2048-token budget choose a task and K · pieces are counted with a GPT-2-style pre-tokeniser (a lower bound on BPE tokens)
SettingContext containsPaper's framing
Zero-shotnatural-language task description + querymost convenient; "in some cases unfairly hard", because the format may be ambiguous
One-shotdescription + one demonstration + queryclosest to how a task is explained to a human worker
Few-shotdescription + K demonstrations + query; K typically 10 to 100, "as many as fit" in 2048 tokensmost accurate; still needs a small amount of task data
Fine-tuning (not done for GPT-3)gradient updates on thousands of labelled examplesstrongest per-task; needs a dataset per task

How answers were scored

  • For multiple choice, each candidate completion is scored by its (length-normalised) log-likelihood given the context, and the highest is chosen; for a few datasets the paper normalises by the unconditional probability of the completion instead.
  • For free-form tasks, the model generates with beam search (width 4, length penalty 0.6) and the output is compared with the reference.
  • K is bounded by the context window. The figure's budget arithmetic shows why arithmetic tasks allow dozens of demonstrations while long reading-comprehension passages allow only a handful.
Takeaway: zero-, one- and few-shot are points on a spectrum of how much task data goes into the context. The context window is the hard constraint on K, and the prompt format is part of the model's input distribution — change it and results move.

19 The GPT-3 size sweep, as reported

Because all eight GPT-3 models saw the same 300B tokens, the paper's appendix table of every task × setting × size is one of the cleanest public records of how capability moved with parameters in this family. The figure plots values taken directly from that table (Table H.1 of arXiv 2005.14165).

Accuracy against model size — values reported in the GPT-3 paper pick a benchmark · three lines = zero-, one-, few-shot · log-scale x axis · hover points

What the sweep shows

  • The few-shot advantage grows with size. On TriviaQA the few-shot minus zero-shot gap is 2.8 points at 125M and 6.9 points at 175B; on LAMBADA few-shot is worse than zero-shot for several small models and 10.2 points better at 175B. The paper's reading: larger models are more efficient in-context learners.
  • Some curves are smooth, some are not. LAMBADA and TriviaQA rise steadily on a log axis. Three-digit addition sits near zero up to 6.7B, reaches 8.4% (few-shot) at 13B and 80.4% at 175B. ANLI round 3 hovers around chance (~33%) for every size until the 175B few-shot result of 40.2%.
  • The 175B headline results (few-shot): LAMBADA 86.4% (prior best 68.0%), TriviaQA 71.2% in the closed-book setting, CoQA 85.0 F1, SuperGLUE 71.8 on the test server against a fine-tuned state of the art of 89.0.
Takeaway: within one data budget, capability usually rose smoothly with log-parameters, and prompting with examples helped more the larger the model. The few abrupt curves are exactly the ones the emergence debate (below) is about.

20 GPT-3: failure cases and the contamination analysis

The GPT-3 paper is unusually explicit about where its model failed and about a data-leak bug it could not afford to fix. Both are worth knowing because they recur in every later large model evaluation.

Where it was weak

  • Sentence-pair comparison. WiC (does a word have the same meaning in two sentences) was at chance: 49.4% few-shot. ANLI rounds 1 and 2 stayed near chance for every size.
  • Some reading comprehension. Few-shot RACE-high 46.8% (fine-tuned best 90.0%), QuAC 44.3 F1 (74.4), DROP 36.5 F1 (89.1). The paper notes these tasks need comparing or re-reading passages, which a single left-to-right pass handles poorly.
  • Arithmetic beyond three digits. Few-shot 175B: 2-digit addition 100%, 3-digit 80.4%, 4-digit 25.5%, 5-digit 9.3%, 2-digit multiplication 29.2%.
  • Generation quality. Samples "sometimes repeat themselves semantically at the document level, start to lose coherence over sufficiently long passages, contradict themselves". Common-sense physics questions such as "If I put cheese into the fridge, will it melt?" were a named weakness.
  • Bias. Among 388 occupations, 83% were more likely to be followed by a male identifier; the paper also reports race and religion associations in co-occurrence tests.
  • Detectability. Human judges identified ~200-word GPT-3 175B news articles as machine-written with about 52% accuracy — near chance — versus 86% for a deliberately bad control model; for ~500-word articles the figures were 52% and 88% (the paper's Tables 3.11 and 3.12).

Contamination: the bug and the audit

  • The authors tried to remove every benchmark test/dev example from the training data, but "a bug in the filtering caused us to ignore some overlaps, and due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model".
  • Instead they built a "clean" subset of each benchmark by dropping any example with a 13-gram overlap with the pretraining data, then compared scores on clean versus full sets. A quarter of benchmarks were flagged as over 50% potentially contaminated, yet for most the score barely changed.
  • They flagged PIQA (29% of examples flagged; 3-point drop on the clean subset) and Winograd (45% flagged; 132 schemas actually present in training data; 2.6% drop), and marked those results with asterisks. For QuAC, SQuAD2 and DROP over 90% of examples were flagged, but manual inspection found the source passages in training data, not the question–answer pairs.
  • The conservative 13-gram method probably over-flags; it cannot prove that the remaining results are uncontaminated. The same limitation applies to later web-scale models evaluated this way.
Takeaway: GPT-3's strengths were cloze, closed-book QA and pattern-following; its weaknesses were pairwise comparison, multi-step reading and long arithmetic. Its contamination audit set the template — clean-subset comparison — for later model reports.

21 In-context learning: what it is and what might explain it

In-context learning (ICL) is a frozen model performing a task better because demonstrations of it appear in the prompt. The GPT-3 paper framed pretraining as an outer loop that builds general skills and ICL as an inner loop that uses them at inference. What happens inside the forward pass is still an open research question.

ICL as conditioning, no parameter changeŷ = argmaxᵧ P_θ( y | (x₁, y₁), …, (x_K, y_K), x_query ) θ fixed

Evidence about what the demonstrations do

  • Format and label space matter a lot; correct labels sometimes matter little. "Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations" (arXiv 2202.12837) found that replacing demonstration labels with random ones "barely hurts" on many classification and multiple-choice tasks across 12 models including GPT-3. The demonstrations appeared to convey the input distribution, the label set and the format.
  • But larger models can learn the mapping. Follow-up work ("Larger language models do in-context learning differently", arXiv 2303.03846) found that sufficiently large models follow flipped labels in the prompt, overriding their priors — so the "labels do not matter" result depends on model size and task.
  • ICL is fragile. "Calibrate Before Use" (arXiv 2102.09690) showed GPT-3's few-shot accuracy swinging widely with the choice and order of examples, driven by biases toward the majority label, the most recent label, and common tokens. Order sensitivity alone can move accuracy from near-chance to near-state-of-the-art on some tasks (arXiv 2104.08786).

Competing explanations, with their evidential status

ExplanationClaimStatus
Implicit Bayesian inference (arXiv 2111.02080)Pretraining on documents with latent topics teaches the model to infer the latent "concept" shared by the demonstrations and predict accordinglyProved under stated assumptions and demonstrated on a synthetic dataset (mixtures of HMMs); consistent with real-model behaviour but not directly verified in GPT-3
Implicit gradient descent (arXiv 2212.07677, 2211.15661)Attention layers can implement steps of gradient descent on a regression loss defined by the demonstrationsConstructive proofs and trained small models on linear regression; whether large LMs do this on language tasks is contested
Induction heads (arXiv 2209.11895)Pairs of attention heads that find a previous occurrence of the current token and copy what followed form during training, coinciding with a jump in ICL abilityStrong mechanistic evidence in small attention-only models; correlational evidence in larger ones
Task recognition vs task learningDemonstrations mostly select a skill already learned in pretraining; only some settings show genuinely new input–output mappings being acquiredSupported by the random-label and flipped-label studies above; the balance depends on scale

These are not mutually exclusive. The practical side — ordering, retrieval of demonstrations, prompt structure — lives in Context Engineering; the mechanism side, in Attention — induction heads.

Takeaway: ICL is well established as a behaviour and poorly understood as a mechanism. Treat single-prompt few-shot numbers as having high variance, and treat any one-line explanation of why it works as a hypothesis.

22 The "emergence" debate

An ability is called emergent if it is near zero in smaller models and appears sharply in larger ones, so that it could not have been predicted by extrapolation. GPT-3's arithmetic curves are a canonical example. Whether such jumps are properties of the models or of the metrics is disputed.

The two positions

  • For: "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" (arXiv 2206.07682) catalogued dozens of tasks — multi-digit arithmetic, some BIG-Bench tasks, chain-of-thought benefits — where performance is flat near chance and then rises steeply past a model scale.
  • Against: "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" (arXiv 2304.15004) argued that most such jumps come from nonlinear or discontinuous metrics such as exact match. When the same outputs are scored with a continuous metric (per-token accuracy, edit distance, log-likelihood of the correct answer), the curves become smooth and predictable. It tested this on the InstructGPT/GPT-3 family directly.

The metric argument, by hand

Suppose a model must emit a 4-token answer and gets each token right independently with probability p. Exact-match accuracy is p⁴.

Per-token accuracy p0.500.700.900.99
Exact match p⁴0.0630.2400.6560.961
  • A smooth improvement in p across model sizes produces a curve in exact match that looks flat, then sudden. A multi-digit sum scored by exact match is exactly this kind of all-or-nothing metric, so part of GPT-3's 8.4% → 80.4% three-digit-addition jump between 13B and 175B is consistent with this mechanism.
  • The counter-argument: not every jump disappears under continuous metrics, only 8 size points are available, and the practical question — "can this model do the task?" — often is an exact-match question. GPT-4's report treats the related problem as forecasting and shows some capabilities predicted in advance (see GPT-4).
Takeaway: "emergence" is best stated as an observation about a metric at a set of scales, not as a property of the model. The evidence that metric choice explains many reported jumps is strong; the claim that it explains all of them is not established.

23 Scaling laws, as they bear on GPT

Two papers frame the GPT-3 era: "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (arXiv 2001.08361), which shaped GPT-3's choices, and "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (arXiv 2203.15556), which argued those choices undertrained it. The general theory is in Large Language Models — scaling; this is the GPT-specific slice.

loss vs non-embedding parameters (2001.08361, data not limiting)L(N) = (N_c / N)^0.076 N_c ≈ 8.8 × 10¹³ compute-efficient allocation, same paperN_opt ∝ C^0.73 D_opt ∝ C^0.27 compute-optimal allocation (2203.15556)N_opt ∝ C^a, D_opt ∝ C^b, a ≈ b ≈ 0.5 (a = 0.46–0.50 across its three methods) ≈ 20 tokens per parameter

Worked against GPT-2 and GPT-3

  • Plugging non-embedding counts into L(N): GPT-2 XL (1.47 × 10⁹) gives about 2.31 nats per token; GPT-3 175B (1.74 × 10¹¹) about 1.61. These are loss values on the scaling-law paper's own WebText2 test set and tokeniser, useful for the ratio, not as a prediction of GPT-3's reported loss.
  • Under the 2020 allocation (N ∝ C^0.73), most extra compute should go to parameters — hence 175B trained on only 300B tokens, about 1.7 tokens per parameter.
  • The compute-optimal analysis found parameters and tokens should grow equally. Its 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens (20 per parameter) with the same compute as the 280B Gopher outperformed Gopher and GPT-3 on a large majority of evaluated tasks. At 20 tokens per parameter, a compute-optimal 175B model would need about 3.5T tokens — nearly 12× what GPT-3 used.
  • Inference economics push further: a smaller model trained on more tokens than compute-optimal costs more to train but less to serve, which is why many post-2022 GPT-style open models train well beyond 20 tokens per parameter.
Takeaway: GPT-3's shape (huge N, modest D) was a faithful application of the 2020 scaling advice, and the 2022 re-analysis showed that advice over-weighted parameters. Both results are empirical fits over specific ranges, not laws of nature.
