InstructGPT & RLHF

The paper that turned GPT-3 from a text continuer into an instruction follower using human feedback. The short version — three stages, two formulas, the headline results — is §26 of the GPT card, and the general machinery of reward models, PPO and their DPO-style alternatives is on Policy / Preference Optimization. This page reads the paper itself: where the prompts came from, who labelled them and how often they agreed, the exact settings of each stage, how the evaluation was built, every family of result, what alignment cost, and the paper's own account of whose preferences it encoded.

01 The problem: the pretraining objective is not the task

Predicting the next token of web text is not the same objective as "follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely". The paper calls a model trained only on the first misaligned with the second: it can make up facts, produce toxic or biased text, or simply ignore the instruction and continue the prompt.

  • The target behaviour is stated in three words: helpful (do the task the user intends), honest (do not fabricate; measured as truthfulness), and harmless (avoid causing physical, psychological or social harm).
  • The approach is to use human preferences as the reward signal: collect demonstrations and rankings from people, fit a model to them, and optimise the language model against that model. The objective-level view of why next-token likelihood and preference are different targets is on GPT: Autoregressive Objective & Causal Masking.
  • The starting point is GPT-3 at three sizes — 1.3B, 6B and 175B parameters (the architecture and its training are on GPT-1 to GPT-3).

02 The prompt dataset: what people actually asked

Most prompts came from real use of an API, not from academic benchmarks. That single choice explains much of the evaluation (§07) and why models fine-tuned on public NLP datasets did poorly on it (§08).

  • Sources. Prompts submitted to the API's playground by customers using an early instruction-tuned model, plus prompts written by labellers to bootstrap the process — plain arbitrary tasks, few-shot instruction/response sets, and prompts modelled on use cases from API waitlist applications.
  • Filtering. Heuristic de-duplication by long common prefix; at most 200 prompts per user ID; train, validation and test split by user ID so no user appears in two splits; personally identifiable information filtered out of the training prompts. The data is over 96% English.

03 The labellers, and how often they agreed

  • About 40 contractors, hired through two contracting platforms and chosen by a screening test that measured agreement with the researchers on flagging sensitive content and on ranking outputs, plus the quality of their written demonstrations.
  • What to optimise. During training data collection labellers were told to prioritise helpfulness to the user; in the final evaluations, to prioritise truthfulness and harmlessness. The paper notes the tension: a request for something harmful puts the two in conflict.
  • Agreement. Training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time; a separate group of held-out labellers, who produced no training data, agreed 77.3 ± 1.3%. For comparison, an earlier summarisation study measured researcher–researcher agreement of 73 ± 4%.
  • Who they were. In a voluntary survey of 19 labellers, about half identified as male and 44% as female; most were 18–34; the largest nationality groups were Filipino and Bangladeshi (22% each), then American (17%).
Why the agreement numbers matter. If people agree only about three times in four, no reward model can predict "the" preference much better than that — the label noise is a ceiling on reward-model accuracy, and the reward model's 72% (§05) is close to it.

The SFT models overfit on validation loss after one epoch, yet training longer kept improving both reward-model score and human preference ratings. Validation likelihood is therefore not the quantity to early-stop on when the goal is preference — one of the paper's quieter, practically important findings.

05 Stage 2 — the reward model, and the ranking batch

The reward model is the SFT model with its unembedding layer removed and a scalar head added: prompt and response in, one number out. It is trained to give the human-preferred response the higher score.

  • Size: 6B for every policy size. 175B reward models were tried; training "could be unstable", and a 175B model would be much more expensive to use as the value function in RL.
  • Rankings, not pairs. Labellers ranked K = 4 to 9 responses per prompt, giving C(K, 2) comparisons.
  • Training: a single epoch, learning rate 9 × 10⁻⁶, a batch of 64 prompts — up to 2 304 comparisons per batch (64 · C(9, 2) = 64 · 36, our check).
  • Normalisation. Before RL a bias is added so that the labeller demonstrations score 0 on average; only reward differences are learned, so the offset is free.
the pairwise loss, and why all C(K,2) pairs of a prompt go in one batch elementloss(θ) = − 1 / C(K,2) · E_(x, y_w, y_l) [ log σ( r_θ(x, y_w) − r_θ(x, y_l) ) ] naive: shuffle every pair into the dataset independently → each completion is seen in K − 1 different pairs within one epoch → the model overfits after a single epoch paper: all pairs of one prompt form ONE batch element → K forward passes instead of 2 · C(K,2) K = 9: 9 vs 72 (our arithmetic) → no overfitting, and it trains faster
  • Does it generalise to other people? Five reward models were trained by cross-validation over five groups of labellers. On the held-out group they predicted preferences with 69.6 ± 0.9% accuracy, against 72.4 ± 0.4% on the labellers they were trained on — a small drop.
  • A worked example of this loss for a chosen reward gap is the lower panel of the figure in §26.

06 Stage 3 — PPO against the reward model, held near SFT

The RL environment is a bandit: a random prompt arrives, the policy writes a response, the reward model scores it, the episode ends. A per-token KL penalty to the SFT model keeps the policy from drifting into text the reward model scores well but people would not. PPO itself is on PPO.

A toy: what the KL coefficient protects against drag β · eight candidate responses · two are "reward hacks" the reward model over-scores · per-response, not per-token, for clarity

The toy's numbers are invented; its shape is the point. As β falls the policy concentrates on whatever the reward model rates highest. While the reward model is right, true quality rises with it; once the policy reaches responses the reward model over-scores, reward keeps climbing while true quality falls — reward over-optimisation, the reason a KL anchor exists at all. The paper reports its useful range of β as roughly 0.01–0.02.

  • The main metric is a win rate: how often labellers prefer a model's output to the 175B SFT model's on held-out API prompts from customers not in the training set. The SFT model was chosen as the baseline because it sits in the middle of the pack.
  • Labellers also gave a 1–7 Likert score and binary metadata judgements: did it fail to follow the instruction, violate an explicit constraint, hallucinate, use language inappropriate for a customer assistant?
  • Public benchmarks measured truthfulness, toxicity, bias and the capability regressions (§09, §10). Judging outputs with models instead of people is the subject of LLM-as-a-Judge (beyond this paper).

Win rate against the 175B SFT baseline: InstructGPT 73.4 ± 2%, the FLAN model 29.8 ± 2%, the T0 model 26.8 ± 2%. Head to head, 175B InstructGPT was preferred to FLAN 78 ± 4% and to T0 79 ± 4%.
  • Metadata. Compared with GPT-3, InstructGPT outputs were rated more appropriate for a customer assistant, followed explicit constraints more often, failed to attempt the correct instruction less often, and hallucinated less in closed-domain tasks.
  • Why FLAN and T0 lost. The paper's explanation is the prompt distribution of §02: public NLP datasets are mostly classification, QA and similar tasks with automatic metrics, while over half of real API use is open-ended generation and brainstorming. And public datasets lack diversity of inputs. Fine-tuning on them builds a different skill.
  • The ordering. GPT-3 outputs did worst; a well-crafted few-shot prompt gave a clear step up, training on demonstrations (SFT) another, and RL (PPO) another. Adding the pretraining mix (PPO-ptx) did not change labeller preference much. The paper presents the per-size win rates in figures rather than a table, so no per-size numbers are quoted here.

  • Truthfulness. On TruthfulQA InstructGPT gave truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3. On closed-domain API tasks (summarise, answer from given text) it made up information 21% of the time against GPT-3's 41% — a measure of honesty to the input, which is what Hallucination & Factuality calls faithfulness.
  • Toxicity cuts both ways. Told to be respectful, InstructGPT produced about 25% fewer toxic outputs than GPT-3; with no such instruction the advantage disappeared; told to be toxic, it was more toxic than GPT-3 (highlighted row). It follows instructions — including bad ones.
  • Bias: no significant improvement on Winogender or CrowS-Pairs.

10 The alignment tax, and what pays it down

  • The regressions. RL fine-tuning without the pretraining mix (PPO) lost performance against GPT-3 on several public datasets: SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag and WMT 2015 French→English translation.
  • The fix. Mixing pretraining gradients into PPO (PPO-ptx, γ = 27.8) greatly reduced the regressions and even surpassed GPT-3 on HellaSwag, but still lagged it on DROP, SQuADv2 and translation. The paper reports that mixing in pretraining updates works better than the simpler alternative of raising the KL coefficient, and costs little in labeller preference.
  • The compute. Training the 175B SFT model took 4.9 petaflop/s-days and the 175B PPO-ptx model 60, against 3 640 for GPT-3's pretraining — the alignment stage is about 1.8% of the pretraining bill (our arithmetic: 64.9 / 3 640).

11 What generalised, and the mistakes it still made

generalised (qualitatively)
Non-English instructions and code
Although almost all training data was English and contained little code, InstructGPT could follow instructions in other languages and answer questions about code — though it often answered in English when asked in another language.
mistake 1
Accepts false premises
Asked a question that assumes something false, it may go along with the premise.
mistake 2
Over-hedges
It can give several possible answers when one is clear, or hedge a simple question — the paper suggests labellers rewarded epistemic humility.
mistake 3
Several constraints at once
Performance degrades when an instruction carries multiple explicit constraints, such as "list 10 movies made in the 1930's set in France".

12 Whose preferences, and the stated limits

The paper is explicit that "aligned" here means aligned to a particular group, not to people in general.

  • The behaviour reflects the labellers (a few dozen people, mostly English-speaking, based largely in the US or Southeast Asia), the researchers who wrote their instructions and resolved ambiguous cases, and the API customers whose prompts defined the tasks — a population drawn from a waitlist.
  • It states that aligning to everyone's preferences at once is impossible; there is no single set of values the method could target, and conditioning on the preferences of particular groups is left as future work.
  • The models remain neither fully aligned nor fully safe: they still produce toxic or biased outputs, make up facts, and generate harmful content when explicitly asked. Many of these concerns became the agenda of later preference-optimisation work (Policy / Preference Optimization, beyond this paper).

13 Common misconceptions

"RLHF made the model know more." It changed behaviour, not knowledge. The alignment stage is under 2% of pretraining compute, and capability benchmarks regressed slightly without the pretraining mix (§10).
"InstructGPT is PPO on the reward model." The released recipe is PPO-ptx: the KL penalty to SFT and a pretraining-likelihood term with γ = 27.8.
"A bigger reward model is always better." The paper used 6B for every policy size; 175B reward-model training could be unstable and is expensive as a value function.
"Instruction tuning on many public NLP tasks gives the same result." On real API prompts it did not: FLAN- and T0-tuned 175B models lost to plain SFT (§08).
"Aligned means safe." It means following the instruction. Asked to be toxic, InstructGPT was more toxic than GPT-3; bias did not improve (§09).

14 Takeaways

  • Data: real API prompts (filtered, split by user) — ~13k SFT, ~33k RM, ~31k PPO — written and ranked by ~40 screened labellers who agree with one another about 73–77% of the time.
  • Recipe: SFT for 16 epochs (selected by RM score, not validation loss) → a 6B reward model trained one epoch on whole rankings per batch element → PPO with β = 0.02 and a pretraining mix γ = 27.8.
  • Result: a 1.3B aligned model is preferred to 175B GPT-3; 175B InstructGPT wins 85 ± 3% against GPT-3 and beats FLAN/T0-style tuning on real prompts.
  • Safety: truthfulness roughly doubles and closed-domain hallucination halves (41% → 21%); toxicity improves only when asked to be respectful; bias does not improve.
  • Cost: about 2% of pretraining compute, plus an alignment tax on public benchmarks that the pretraining mix mostly removes.
  • Scope: aligned to a specific group of labellers, researchers and customers — by the paper's own account.
