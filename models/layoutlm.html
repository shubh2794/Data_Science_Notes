LayoutLM (v1 · v2 · v3 · LayoutXLM)

LayoutLM is a family of Transformer encoders from Microsoft Research that read a document page as OCR words plus the bounding box of every word, and — from v2 on — the page image too. Each coordinate is scaled to a 0–1000 grid and looked up in a learned embedding table, so a word's position on the page is part of its input vector. This note follows the family in detail: the benchmark landscape and the OCR front end it depends on, v1's four coordinate lookups and masked visual-language modelling, v2's 7 × 7 visual tokens, spatial-aware attention and text–image alignment tasks, the multilingual LayoutXLM and its XFUND benchmark, v3's linear patches with unified text and image masking, recomputed parameter counts, fine-tuning recipes, sequence budgets, licences, limitations and pitfalls.

01 What LayoutLM is

LayoutLM is a BERT-style bidirectional Transformer encoder whose input is a sequence of OCR tokens, each carrying the bounding box of the word it came from, scaled to integers between 0 and 1000. The box coordinates index learned embedding tables and are summed into the token's input vector, so self-attention can use where a word sits on the page as well as what it says. Later versions add the page image as extra tokens and a relative-position bias in attention.

What goes in and what comes out

  • Inputs: the words an OCR engine (or a PDF's text layer) found on one page, in some reading order, with one box per word; from v2 on, the page image resized to 224 × 224. LayoutLM does not read pixels to find words — an external engine does.
  • Outputs: one contextual vector per token (and per visual token in v2/v3). A linear or MLP head turns them into a label per token (form and receipt fields), start/end positions of an answer span (document question answering), or one class per page (document classification).
  • Training: self-supervised pretraining on the roughly 11 million scanned pages of the IIT-CDIP collection, then supervised fine-tuning on a small labelled dataset such as FUNSD's 149 training forms.

02 Why layout needs its own signal

In a form or an invoice, the meaning of a string often comes from where it is: the number to the right of "Total:" is the total, the text under "Ship to:" is an address. A text-only encoder sees only the order the OCR engine emitted words in, and that order can separate a key from its value or interleave two columns.

What a 1-D token stream loses

  • Key–value adjacency is two-dimensional. The v1 paper's example: for a key such as "Passport ID:", the value is much more likely to the right of it or below it than to the left or above. In a serialised stream, "below" can be dozens of tokens away.
  • Visual cues carry priority. Headings are larger or bold; totals are often boxed; table rules group cells. The v1 paper lists bold, underline and italic as useful hints for sequence labelling, and the whole-page appearance as a strong cue for classifying the document type.
  • Reading order is itself an OCR output. The text encoder's 1-D positions are only as good as the engine's line ordering, which tends to be weakest on the multi-column and tabular pages where layout matters most (see the OCR front end).

What existed before, and what LayoutLM changed

Before 2019, document AI was split between computer-vision pipelines (table or region detection with Faster R-CNN or Mask R-CNN), grid-based models such as Chargrid that painted characters onto a 2-D map, and graph-convolution models over OCR segments. The v1 paper names two gaps: these methods relied on small human-labelled datasets without using large unlabelled collections, and none jointly pretrained text and layout. LayoutLM's move was to take BERT's recipe — pretrain on a large unlabelled corpus, fine-tune on small labelled sets — and change only the input embedding, so the whole BERT toolkit (initialisation, masked-token objective, heads) carried over.

The payoff showed up where labels are scarcest. On FUNSD, whose training split has only 149 forms, the v1 paper reports BERT-base at 60.26 F1 and LayoutLM-base (text + layout) at 78.66 — the same encoder size, pretrained with coordinates.

Takeaway: layout is a second positional signal that a document's text order does not reliably carry. LayoutLM supplies it as extra embeddings rather than as a new architecture, which is why it could start from BERT's weights.

Text-centric and image-centric

The v3 paper divides these into text-centric tasks — FUNSD, CORD, DocVQA, where the answer is a set of OCR words — and image-centric tasks — RVL-CDIP and PubLayNet, where appearance dominates. The distinction explains the family's arc: v1 was built for the first group and needed image features bolted on for RVL-CDIP; v3 was the first member evaluated as a backbone for pure layout detection.

What the metrics measure

  • Entity-level F1 counts an entity correct only if its whole span and label match; word-level F1 (v1's FUNSD metric) scores each word, which is more forgiving. Numbers from the two protocols are not strictly comparable.
  • ANLS gives partial credit to near-miss answer strings (normalised Levenshtein similarity, zeroed below 0.5). Its formula and pitfalls are on the hub's benchmark section.
  • Test sets are small. FUNSD has 50 test forms and CORD 100 test receipts, so a gap of under a point may be within run-to-run variation; none of the four papers' result tables reports variance across seeds.
Takeaway: read every LayoutLM score together with its dataset's size, its metric's granularity, and whether the task is text-centric or image-centric. The same model can look state-of-the-art or ordinary depending on which column is read.

04 The OCR front end and reading order

Every LayoutLM input begins with an OCR pass (or a born-digital PDF's text layer) that returns words, boxes, and an order. The model inherits all three: misread characters become wrong token ids, loose boxes become wrong coordinates, and the engine's line order becomes the 1-D position of every token.

Which OCR the papers used

  • v1 pretraining: the IIT-CDIP collection ships OCR text without word boxes, so the authors re-ran OCR with Tesseract and stored results in hOCR, a hierarchical page → line → word format.
  • v2: word-level boxes from the Microsoft Read API for IIT-CDIP and DocVQA ("Microsoft OCR" for RVL-CDIP); the official annotations for FUNSD, CORD and SROIE.
  • v3: the Microsoft Read API for RVL-CDIP and DocVQA; the official OCR annotations for FUNSD and CORD; for IIT-CDIP pretraining the paper says only "an off-the-shelf OCR toolkit".
  • SROIE in v1: the authors used ground-truth OCR "to eliminate the effect of incorrect OCR results", so the 95.24 F1 is an extraction score, not an end-to-end one.
  • LayoutXLM pretraining: 22M born-digital PDFs parsed with PyMuPDF, so text and boxes come from the file rather than from recognition.

Reading order

An OCR engine typically emits text line by line, top to bottom, left to right within a detected block. On a two-column region, a naive "sort by y, then x" order interleaves the columns — "Bill to: Ship to: Jane Doe Same as billing" — while a block-aware order keeps each column together. The 2-D embeddings mean LayoutLM is not blind to the true geometry, but the 1-D position embedding (and, from v2, the 1-D relative attention bias) is computed from this order, so it pulls the model toward treating stream-adjacent tokens as related.

The block-aware order is a simple XY-cut: lines less than 100 px apart vertically form a band, a band is split into columns at its widest vertical whitespace gap if that gap exceeds 150 px, and bands are read top to bottom, columns left to right. Takeaway: LayoutLM's accuracy is bounded by its OCR. Check the engine's word accuracy, box tightness and reading order on your own documents before comparing to published scores, which were mostly measured with official or high-quality OCR.

05 From pixel boxes to the 0–1000 grid

Pages arrive at different sizes and scan resolutions, so v1 scales every box coordinate to a "virtual" page 1000 units on a side. The integers that result are indices into embedding tables with 1,024 rows (the released configurations' max_2d_position_embeddings), exactly as a token id indexes the vocabulary.

With the checkbox off the lookups are the v1 paper's four (two shared tables X, Y). With it on, the figure adds the two extra lookups W[ŵ] and H[ĥ] that the open-source v1 implementation adds (four full-width 1024-row tables summed) and that v2/v3 concatenate as narrower slices instead — see v2's layout embedding.

Why 1000, and what it costs

  • 1000 buckets across an A4 width of 210 mm is one bucket per 0.21 mm — finer than typical OCR box accuracy, so quantisation is rarely the bottleneck.
  • The tables have 1,024 rows, so ids up to 1023 are legal; an id of 1024 or more raises an index error in the reference implementation, which is the usual symptom of passing un-normalised pixel boxes.
  • Normalisation removes page size and resolution, not rotation, skew or perspective. A photographed receipt needs deskewing first, or its "rows" will not share a ŷ.
Takeaway: the layout input is four (or six) small integers per token. Getting them right — dividing by the original page size, not the resized image, and clamping into 0…1000 — is the most common place a LayoutLM pipeline silently goes wrong.

06 v1: the 2-D position embedding

LayoutLM v1 keeps BERT's architecture and adds, for each token, four position-embedding lookups from two tables: an X table indexed by x̂₀ and x̂₁ and a Y table indexed by ŷ₀ and ŷ₁. The embedding layers "representing the same dimension share the same embedding table", in the paper's words. The result is summed with BERT's word, 1-D position and segment embeddings.

v1 as the paper specifies iteᵢ = Tok(wᵢ) + Pos1D(i) + Seg(sᵢ) + X(x̂₀ᵢ) + Y(ŷ₀ᵢ) + X(x̂₁ᵢ) + Y(ŷ₁ᵢ) the widely used open-source v1 implementation adds two more tableseᵢ = … + X(x̂₀ᵢ) + Y(ŷ₀ᵢ) + X(x̂₁ᵢ) + Y(ŷ₁ᵢ) + H(ŷ₁ᵢ − ŷ₀ᵢ) + W(x̂₁ᵢ − x̂₀ᵢ) then LayerNorm and dropout, as in BERTxᵢ⁽⁰⁾ = Dropout(LayerNorm(eᵢ))

What two corners encode

The top-left and bottom-right corners determine a box's position and size, so width and height are implicitly available: a network can compute X(x̂₁) − X(x̂₀)-like features in its first layer. Sharing one table between x̂₀ and x̂₁ means "x = 665" has one meaning whether it is a left or a right edge, halving the layout parameters. The explicit width and height tables in the open-source code add those features directly; the paper does not describe them, and its reported 113M base parameter count happens to sit closer to the implementation's total (see parameter counts).

Special tokens

  • The v1 paper's architecture figure gives [CLS] the whole page, (0, 0, maxW, maxH), and every word its own box.
  • In common fine-tuning code [CLS] and [PAD] get (0, 0, 0, 0) and [SEP] gets (1000, 1000, 1000, 1000); the Transformers documentation's question-answering example uses this convention, and it is the LayoutLMv2 tokenizer's default. The conventions are not uniform across the family: the v2 paper assigns every special token the empty box (0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0), and the v3 paper and the LayoutLMv3 tokenizer's default give [SEP] zeros as well. What matters is using at fine-tuning time whatever convention the checkpoint was pretrained with, and the v1 paper does not specify [SEP].

Initialisation and what is new

All modules except the 2-D tables are initialised from BERT-base (or BERT-large); the 2-D tables start random. That is why the text side works from step one and the layout signal is learned during pretraining. Table 3 of the v1 paper shows the initialisation matters: with 1M pretraining pages, FUNSD F1 is 0.6130 from scratch, 0.7299 from BERT-base and 0.7514 from RoBERTa-base — though the released v1 checkpoints use BERT's uncased 30,522-token vocabulary, consistent with BERT initialisation.

Takeaway: v1 is literally BERT plus four extra table lookups per token. Everything that makes it work — masked-token pretraining, BERT initialisation, fine-tuning heads — is inherited unchanged.

07 Sub-words, and which box each one gets

OCR produces words; the model consumes sub-word tokens. v1 and v2 give every sub-word of a word the word's box (v3, its segment's box). LayoutXLM instead computes character-level boxes and merges the characters in each SentencePiece token, because "word" is not a well-defined unit in every language.

Worked example: "$1,240.00" in an uncased BERT tokenizer

BERT's basic tokenizer splits on punctuation before WordPiece runs, so the single OCR word "$1,240.00" with box (766, 826, 887, 843) becomes six tokens — "$", "1", ",", "240", ".", "00", each of which is in the released 30,522-entry uncased vocabulary — and all six carry (766, 826, 887, 843). Their 1-D positions differ by one each; their 2-D embeddings are identical. For token classification, the common convention (the Transformers LayoutLMv2 tokenizer's default) labels only the first sub-word with the word's tag and gives the other five the ignore index −100, so the loss counts each word once.

Three levels of box granularity

  • Word-level (v1, v2): each OCR word's own box, copied to its sub-words.
  • Character-merged (LayoutXLM): SentencePiece (unigram) tokens get the union of their characters' boxes, which works for Chinese and Japanese where OCR "words" may be whole lines.
  • Segment-level (v3): every word in an OCR segment (a line or text block) shares the segment's box, on the argument that words in one segment usually express one meaning. The v3 paper notes this may help FUNSD and makes its FUNSD scores not directly comparable with word-level models.
Takeaway: the box a sub-word receives is a design decision, not a detail. Match the granularity the checkpoint was pretrained with — word boxes for v1/v2, segment boxes for v3 if you want its published FUNSD behaviour — or expect a distribution shift.

Masked visual-language modelling (MVLM) is BERT's masked language modelling with one change: when a token is masked, its 2-D position embeddings are kept. The model must recover "Total" from the surrounding words and from the fact that a token sits at (665, 826, 745, 843), just left of a dollar amount.

The multi-label document classification task

IIT-CDIP pages carry metadata tags, so v1 optionally adds a multi-label classification loss (MDC) on [CLS]. The paper's Table 1 reports it helping FUNSD at 1M pages (0.7299 → 0.7372 F1), but it also flags MDC as optional "and may not be used for pretraining larger models in the future", since most corpora lack such tags; the best v1 FUNSD figure used MVLM alone. v2 and v3 drop it.

Takeaway: MVLM teaches the encoder to fill in words from both neighbours and geometry. Keeping the masked token's box is what forces the layout embeddings to become informative.

09 v1: image features, added only at fine-tuning

v1 is pretrained on text and layout alone. For fine-tuning, the paper optionally adds an image embedding per token: the page is cut into pieces along each word's OCR box, and a Faster R-CNN with a ResNet-101 backbone pretrained on Visual Genome turns each piece into a region-of-interest feature that is combined with that token's LayoutLM output.

image embedding for word i: ROI features from the word's own boxvᵢ = FasterRCNN_ROI( image, box_i ) [CLS]: ROI = the whole page combined with the pretrained LayoutLM output before the task headzᵢ = combine(hᵢ, vᵢ) (the paper's figure shows the image embeddings joining after the pretrained encoder, before task-specific layers)

What it bought

  • RVL-CDIP: text + layout alone reaches 91.78% (base, 11M pages); adding the image embedding reaches 94.42%. For classification the paper concatenates the LayoutLM output with the whole-image embedding before the softmax.
  • FUNSD: 78.66 → 79.27 F1 (base, 11M pages, 2 epochs).
  • SROIE: 94.38 → 94.67 F1 (base), while LayoutLM-large without images reaches 95.24.
  • Cost: the paper's parameter column rises from 113M to 160M, the Faster R-CNN's share.

Why this design was replaced

The image branch never interacts with the text during pretraining, so the model cannot learn cross-modal relations from IIT-CDIP's 11M pages — only from the labelled fine-tuning set (149 forms for FUNSD, though 320,000 pages for RVL-CDIP). It also needs an object detector at inference. The paper's own future-work section proposes "involving image embeddings in the pre-training step", which is exactly v2.

Takeaway: v1's image features are a late-fusion add-on: helpful for page classification, marginal for extraction, and absent from the pretraining that gives the family its strength.

10 v1: configurations, training and results

Two sizes, both initialised from BERT: base (12 layers, width 768, 12 heads) and large (24 layers, width 1024, 16 heads), with a 30,522-token uncased WordPiece vocabulary and 512 positions. The paper reports about 113M and 343M parameters.

Pretraining recipe

  • Data: IIT-CDIP Test Collection 1.0 — more than 6 million documents, more than 11 million scanned page images, re-OCR'd with Tesseract.
  • Hardware and optimiser: 8 NVIDIA V100 32 GB GPUs, total batch 80, Adam at 5 × 10⁻⁵ with linear decay.
  • Time: about 80 hours per epoch over 11M documents for base (roughly 38 pages per second) and nearly 170 hours for large. The best base model ran 2 epochs; the large model 1.
  • Fine-tuning: FUNSD 100 epochs, batch 16, learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁵; RVL-CDIP 30 epochs, batch 40, 2 × 10⁻⁵. Form and receipt labelling use {B, I, E, S, O} tags.

11 v2: one sequence, three modalities

LayoutLMv2 moves the image inside the Transformer. A CNN turns the page into 49 visual tokens that are concatenated with the text tokens into one sequence, every token — text or visual — gets a layout embedding, and all of them are pretrained together. The abstract calls it a two-stream multi-modal Transformer encoder; in the architecture section the two token streams are concatenated into one sequence, so text and visual tokens attend to one another in every layer.

text token i (0 ≤ i < L): WordPiece id, 1-D index, segment [A] or [B]tᵢ = TokEmb(wᵢ) + PosEmb1D(i) + SegEmb(sᵢ) visual token i (0 ≤ i < W·H): projected CNN feature, shared 1-D table, segment [C]vᵢ = Proj( VisTokEmb(I)ᵢ ) + PosEmb1D(i) + SegEmb([C]) first-layer input: concatenate, then add each token's layout embeddingX = { v₀ … v_(WH−1), t₀ … t_(L−1) } xᵢ⁽⁰⁾ = Xᵢ + lᵢ

What changed from v1

  • Initialisation: the encoder and text embeddings come from UniLMv2 rather than BERT (same WordPiece vocabulary size, 30,522); the visual backbone from a Mask R-CNN trained on PubLayNet; everything else is random.
  • Segments: [A] and [B] separate text segments (question and context in DocVQA); [C] marks visual tokens. In pretraining all text is [A].
  • Layout for everyone: visual tokens get boxes too — the grid cell each one pools — and special tokens get the empty box (0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0).
  • Attention gains relative biases for 1-D and 2-D offsets (spatial-aware self-attention).
  • Two new objectives tie the modalities together (TIA and TIM).

Where released code differs from the paper's description

  • The paper's Figure 1 puts the visual tokens first; the Transformers implementation appends them after the text tokens. Because attention is order-agnostic apart from position terms, this changes indexing, not capacity.
  • The released v2 configurations set has_visual_segment_embedding to false (the large configuration explicitly, the base by the library default), so the checkpoints carry no trained [C] segment vector. One exception in the Transformers code: LayoutLMv2ForQuestionAnswering switches the flag on by default (has_visual_segment_embedding=True), so for question answering that vector is created freshly initialised rather than loaded. The released LayoutXLM-base configuration sets the flag to true.
Takeaway: v2 feeds text tokens and image tokens through one encoder as a single concatenated sequence, with layout added to both. Pretraining sees all three modalities at once — the change v1's authors named as their next step.

12 v2: the visual backbone and the 7 × 7 visual tokens

The page is resized to 224 × 224 and passed through a ResNeXt-101-FPN (32 groups × 8 channels, the "MaskRCNN ResNeXt101 32x8d FPN 3X" Detectron2 setting). The backbone's weights are updated during pretraining and fine-tuning.

Practical details that bite

  • The Detectron2 backbone expects BGR channel order and normalises internally; LayoutLMv3 by contrast expects normalised RGB (the Transformers documentation states both).

13 v2: the six-part layout embedding

v2 keeps the 0–1000 normalisation but changes how coordinates enter: each token's box is described by six features — x_min, x_max, width, y_min, y_max, height — and the paper builds the layout embedding by concatenating an x-part and a y-part rather than summing full-width vectors.

Worked example: the parameter saving

In the open-source v1 code, four tables of 1,024 rows × 768 columns hold 4 · 1024 · 768 = 3,145,728 parameters. v2-base's four tables are 1,024 rows × 128 columns: 4 · 1024 · 128 = 524,288 — one sixth. The x table is still shared by x̂₀ and x̂₁ and the y table by ŷ₀ and ŷ₁; each lookup now fills its own 128-wide slice of the vector instead of being summed with the others, so a left edge and a right edge with the same value occupy different slices.

For "Total:" (665, 826, 745, 843): the vector is X[665] in dimensions 0–127, Y[826] in 128–255, X[745] in 256–383, Y[843] in 384–511, H[17] in 512–639 and W[80] in 640–767. It is then added to the token's text embedding.

Width and height are now explicit

This is where width and height embeddings enter the family in the papers: v1's paper uses corners only, v2's paper names width and height among the box features, and v3 embeds "x-axis, y-axis, width and height features separately". (The widely used v1 implementation already added width and height tables, which is why many descriptions of v1 include them.)

Takeaway: v2's layout embedding is the same coordinate lookup, made cheaper by concatenating narrow slices and made richer by explicit width and height.

14 v2: spatial-aware self-attention

Absolute embeddings tell a token where it is; a relative bias tells a pair of tokens how far apart they are. v2 adds three learned scalars to every attention logit — one for the 1-D offset in the token stream, one for the horizontal offset and one for the vertical offset between the two boxes — before the softmax. The biases differ across heads and are shared across layers.

Buckets, not raw offsets

Offsets range over hundreds of tokens and up to ±1000 grid units, so the implementation maps each offset to a bucket with the T5 scheme: half the buckets are for the sign, and within each sign half the small distances get one bucket each while larger distances share logarithmically wider buckets up to a maximum distance, beyond which everything shares the last bucket. The released configurations use 32 buckets up to 128 tokens for 1-D and 64 buckets up to 256 grid units for each 2-D axis.

A detail where code and paper disagree

The paper anchors each box by its top-left corner (x_min, y_min). The released implementation — in both v2 and v3 — uses the left edge x̂₀ and the bottom edge ŷ₁. Takeaway: spatial-aware attention adds three table lookups per head per token pair — 32 + 64 + 64 scalars per head in total — and lets attention prefer pairs by stream distance and by 2-D offset, which matches the "value is to the right of or below its key" regularity common in forms.

15 v2 pretraining: MVLM, text–image alignment and matching

With the image inside the model, masked-token prediction alone gives the network no loss that directly requires the pixels — though the v2 ablation shows an MVLM-only model with visual tokens still gains a little (0.6841 → 0.6915 ANLS), so it does use them. v2 therefore adds a fine-grained task, text–image alignment (TIA), which asks each token whether its own image region was covered, and a coarse task, text–image matching (TIM), which asks whether the image and the text come from the same page.

Design choices inside TIA

  • Line-level covering. At 224 × 224, a covered word-sized patch can be confused with signatures or bars in a figure, so the paper covers whole OCR lines and calls the operation "covering" to keep it distinct from MVLM's masking.
  • Masked tokens are excluded from the TIA loss, so the model cannot learn the shortcut "[MASK] implies Covered" (MVLM already blanks those tokens' regions).
  • In TIM negatives, the same masking and covering are applied to the substituted image so the model cannot simply spot negatives by their lack of covering, and every TIA label is set to Covered.

17 LayoutXLM: the v2 recipe in 53 languages, and XFUND

LayoutXLM keeps the v2 architecture and objectives and changes the data and the text side: the encoder is initialised from the multilingual InfoXLM, the vocabulary is a 250,002-entry SentencePiece model, and pretraining uses 30 million documents in 53 languages. Its paper also introduces XFUND, FUNSD-style forms in seven more languages.

Data pipeline

  • Collection: publicly available born-digital multilingual PDFs, downloaded "following the principles and policies of Common Crawl", parsed with PyMuPDF for text, layout and page images — no OCR needed.
  • Cleaning: discard documents under 200 characters; detect language with BlingFire and keep a document only if its language score exceeds 0.5.
  • Sampling: languages sampled with probability pₗ ∝ (nₗ / n)^0.7, the XLM-style smoothing with the exponent InfoXLM used, to lift low-resource languages. This yields 22M multilingual documents, plus 8M scanned English pages sampled from IIT-CDIP, 30M in total.
  • Boxes per token: character-level boxes merged per SentencePiece token (sub-words).
  • Compute and size: 64 V100 GPUs; about 345M (base) and 625M (large) parameters by the paper's count.

XFUND

Form templates in Chinese, Japanese, Spanish, French, Italian, German and Portuguese were collected from the web, emptied, filled with synthetic content by annotators (typed or handwritten, each template used once), scanned, OCR'd with the Microsoft Read API and labelled with FUNSD's entity types and key–value links. Each language has 199 forms, 149 for training and 50 for testing — 1,393 in all. Two tasks are defined: semantic entity recognition (SER, BIO labelling) and relation extraction (RE, a biaffine classifier over candidate entity pairs).

18 v3: linear patches instead of a CNN

LayoutLMv3 removes the convolutional backbone. The page image is resized to 224 × 224, cut into 16 × 16-pixel patches, and each patch is flattened and linearly projected to the model width — the ViT recipe. The v3 paper describes itself as the first multimodal document AI model that does not rely on a pre-trained CNN or Faster R-CNN for image features.

Worked example: what the patches cost

The projection is a 16 × 16 convolution with stride 16 from 3 to 768 channels: 3 · 16 · 16 · 768 weights + 768 biases = 590,592 parameters, the "0.6M" the v3 paper reports for adding image embedding. Other v3 architecture choices

  • Segment-level layout for text tokens (sub-words); image patches get their grid cell as a box, and special tokens get zeros.
  • RoBERTa initialisation and its byte-level BPE vocabulary (50,265 entries) replace BERT/UniLMv2 WordPiece; the maximum text length stays 512 (the configuration lists 514 positions because RoBERTa's position ids start after the padding index).

Takeaway: v3 spends 0.6M parameters on image input where v2 spent about 90M, and gets the image into the sequence at 196 patch tokens — more visual tokens than v2's 49, each looking at a quarter of the area.

19 v3 pretraining: MLM, MIM and word–patch alignment

v3's argument is that text and image objectives should look alike. Text uses masked language modelling over discrete word tokens; image uses masked image modelling over discrete visual tokens produced by an image tokenizer; and word–patch alignment ties the two by asking whether a word's image patches were masked. The total loss is the plain sum.

The image tokenizer

MIM's targets come from a discrete VAE image tokenizer, the approach of DALL·E and BEiT. The v3 paper initialises its tokenizer from the one in DiT (arXiv 2203.02378), a self-supervised document image Transformer; the DiT paper trained that dVAE on the 42 million page images of IIT-CDIP because the DALL·E tokenizer, trained on natural images, reconstructed documents poorly. It has an 8,192-entry codebook and downsamples by 8, so a 112 × 112 input — the released configuration's second_input_size — would yield a 14 × 14 grid of codes, one target per 16 × 16 patch of the 224 × 224 image (the v3 paper itself does not state the tokenizer's input size). The tokenizer is used only to make labels and is not counted in the model's parameters.

23 Fine-tuning recipes per task

All four members are fine-tuned end-to-end with a small task head. Three head shapes cover almost every use: a classifier per token for field extraction, start and end classifiers for extractive question answering, and a classifier on [CLS] (plus pooled visual features in v1/v2) for page classification.

Token classification with BIO labels (FUNSD, CORD, SROIE, XFUND SER)

Each entity type T gets two labels, B-T for the first word of an entity and I-T for the rest, plus O for words outside any entity. FUNSD's four types give 2·3 + 1 = 7 labels (its "other" class is usually mapped to O). CORD's 30 fields give 2·30 + 1 = 61 under plain BIO; SROIE's four keys give 9. v1 used the finer {B, I, E, S, O} scheme; the v2 and LayoutXLM papers state BIO, and the v3 paper does not name its tagging scheme.

Extractive question answering (DocVQA)

  • Input: [CLS] question [SEP] OCR words [SEP] (+ visual tokens). Question tokens are in segment [A], context in [B] (visual in [C] for v2).
  • Boxes: context tokens get their word boxes; a common convention gives question tokens and [CLS] (0, 0, 0, 0) and [SEP] (1000, 1000, 1000, 1000), as in the Transformers documentation's example.
  • Head: two linear classifiers over context tokens give start and end logits; the answer is the highest-scoring valid span, mapped back to OCR words.
  • Labels: DocVQA answers are free text, so training spans are found by heuristic matching in the OCR output; v2 reports the found spans reach over 97% ANLS on train and validation. Questions whose answer is not in the OCR text cannot be answered by this head at all.

Page classification (RVL-CDIP)

v1: LayoutLM output concatenated with a Faster R-CNN whole-page embedding, then softmax. v2: [CLS] output concatenated with a pooled pre-encoder visual feature and a pooled post-encoder visual feature. v3: an MLP on the [CLS] output. The papers' fine-tuning lengths range from 30 epochs (v1) to 20,000 steps at batch 64 (v3).

24 Input construction, the sequence budget, and long documents

Every member has 512 text positions. v2 and LayoutXLM add 49 visual tokens and v3 adds 197 (196 patches plus a visual [CLS] in the reference implementation), so one forward pass sees at most 561 or 709 tokens. A dense page, a long contract or a multi-page file does not fit, and the text must be windowed.

Worked example

A page with 450 OCR words at an average of 1.4 sub-words per word gives 630 text tokens. For token classification the capacity is c = 510; with a 128-token overlap, k = 1 + ceil(120 / 382) = 2 windows. For DocVQA with a 20-token question the capacity drops to 509 − 20 = 489 (488 with v3's tokenizer) and the answer can lie in either window, so both are scored and the best span kept. The v2 paper fine-tunes DocVQA at L = 384 rather than 512.

The sub-words-per-word ratio is an assumption to vary, not a measured constant: it depends on the tokenizer (WordPiece, BPE, SentencePiece), the language and how much of the page is numbers and codes. Visual token counts are from the released configurations (7 × 7 for v2; 14 × 14 plus a visual [CLS] for v3). Every window repeats the whole image's visual tokens. With a question, the LayoutLMv3 choice uses the RoBERTa-style pair (two separators, capacity 508 − q); the v2 / LayoutXLM choice uses the BERT-style count (509 − q), although LayoutXLM's XLM-R tokenizer is also RoBERTa-style and would lose one more position.

Strategies for long documents

  • Overlapping windows (the tokenizers' overflow-with-stride option): predict per window, and for tokens that appear in two windows keep the prediction from the window where the token is further from an edge.
  • Page by page for multi-page files: each page is its own example with its own image; cross-page fields need a merging step outside the model.
  • First page only for classification is a common shortcut, since RVL-CDIP labels are per page.
  • v2 pretraining itself took a random sliding window of the text when a page exceeded 512 tokens.
Takeaway: the 512-position limit is the family's hardest practical constraint. Plan windows and a merge rule before choosing a checkpoint, and remember that each window pays the full visual-token cost again.

26 Close relatives: MarkupLM, LiLT, LayoutReader and others

Several models reuse LayoutLM's idea — give each token a structural position and pretrain — while changing what "position" means or how it is wired in. They are context here, not full treatments; the hub's comparison table lists their scores.

From the same research group

  • MarkupLM ("MarkupLM: Pre-training of Text and Markup Language for Visually-rich Document Understanding", arXiv 2110.08518). For HTML and XML pages, where there are no pixel boxes but there is a DOM tree: each token gets an XPath embedding built from the tag names and sibling subscripts along its XPath, in place of the 2-D embedding. Evaluated on web reading comprehension (WebSRC) and structured web data extraction (SWDE).
  • LayoutReader ("LayoutReader: Pre-training of Text and Layout for Reading Order Detection", arXiv 2108.11591). Builds a sequence-to-sequence reading-order model on a LayoutLM encoder, with the ReadingBank dataset of 500,000 page images — a direct attack on the reading-order problem.
  • DiT (arXiv 2203.02378). An image-only document Transformer pretrained BEiT-style on 42M pages; its dVAE tokenizer supplies v3's MIM targets.

From other groups

  • LiLT ("LiLT: A Simple yet Effective Language-Independent Layout Transformer for Structured Document Understanding", arXiv 2202.13669). A separate, small layout Transformer runs in parallel with any RoBERTa-like text encoder and exchanges attention scores with it (bi-directional attention complementation, BiACM). Pretrained once on English IIT-CDIP, it can be paired with a monolingual or multilingual text model for another language — an alternative route to LayoutXLM's goal.
  • StructuralLM (arXiv 2105.11210). Introduced segment-level ("cell") layout, which v3 adopted, and a task that predicts a cell's position on the page.
  • BROS (arXiv 2108.04539). Encodes relative positions between text blocks and uses area masking; text + layout only.
  • DocFormer (arXiv 2106.11539) keeps separate visual and text streams that share spatial embeddings; UDOP (arXiv 2212.02623) unifies vision, text and layout in an encoder–decoder that generates outputs.
Takeaway: the LayoutLM idea generalises beyond page boxes: any structural coordinate — a DOM path, a table cell, a reading-order index — can be embedded and pretrained the same way.

27 LayoutLM versus Donut and vision-language document readers

LayoutLM is the clearest example of the OCR-based design: an external engine reads, the model interprets. Donut is the clearest OCR-free counterpart: a Swin encoder reads the pixels at 2560 × 1920 and a decoder generates the answer. General vision-language models now read documents the same way at dynamic resolution. The category-level trade-off is covered on the hub (pipeline versus end-to-end); here are the LayoutLM-specific consequences.

Where LayoutLM's design is strong

  • Every output is grounded. A token-classification or span answer is a set of OCR words with boxes, so it can be highlighted on the page and checked; a generative reader can produce text that is not on the page.
  • Cheap per page. A 125M-parameter encoder at 224 × 224 plus an OCR call, against thousands of image tokens for an OCR-free model (the hub's token-budget section works the numbers).
  • Born-digital PDFs have exact text and boxes, removing OCR error entirely — the setting where an OCR-based encoder has the least to lose.

Where it is weak

  • OCR errors are unrecoverable and compound with field length; handwriting, low-quality photos and unusual scripts are where OCR typically fails first. The Donut paper reports its model ahead of the LayoutLMv2-Large-QG leaderboard entry on DocVQA's handwritten questions (72.1 vs 67.3 ANLS) despite a much lower overall score (67.5 vs 86.7).
  • Outputs are limited to what the head can express: labels over existing words, or a single span. Nested records (line items), normalised values ("18 March 2024" → "2024-03-18"), yes/no or counting questions need post-processing or a generative model.
  • Protocols differ: when the Donut paper reruns LayoutLM and LayoutLMv2 on CORD under its end-to-end protocol they score 78.4 and 78.9, not the ≈95 entity F1 of the LayoutLM papers — different tasks, not contradictory results.
Takeaway: choose LayoutLM when text is reliable (good OCR or born-digital PDFs) and the task is labelling or extracting existing words; choose an OCR-free model or a VLM when reading itself is hard or the output must be generated. The hub's how-to-choose section gives the full decision sequence.

29 Limitations

Most of LayoutLM's limitations follow from two design facts: it consumes someone else's reading of the page, and it is a 512-position encoder with small task heads.

Inherited from the OCR interface

  • OCR dependence. Recognition errors, missed words and merged or split boxes pass straight into the input. Published scores mostly use official OCR annotations (FUNSD, CORD, SROIE) or a strong commercial engine, so they are optimistic for a pipeline built on a weaker engine.
  • Reading order. 1-D positions and the 1-D attention bias come from the engine's ordering; multi-column pages, tables and rotated text can serialise badly. The 2-D signals partly compensate, but nothing in the model reorders tokens.
  • Language coverage is set by the OCR engine as much as by the model; LayoutXLM covers 53 pretraining languages only if the OCR does.

Inherited from the encoder design

  • 512 text positions, so dense and multi-page documents need windowing and merge logic (input construction).
  • Low image resolution — 224 × 224 in v2 and v3 — gives layout and appearance cues but cannot read small print or fine marks such as ticked checkboxes reliably.
  • Extractive outputs only: answers must be spans or labels of existing tokens; no normalisation, arithmetic or free-form generation.
  • Pretraining domain: IIT-CDIP is overwhelmingly English office and legal documents released in tobacco-industry litigation and scanned decades ago; receipts, modern invoices, scientific papers and forms in other languages are largely out of its distribution, and the small labelled sets are what adapts the model.
Takeaway: LayoutLM is strong exactly where its assumptions hold — good OCR, one page's worth of text, answers that are words on the page — and brittle when any of the three fails.

30 Pitfalls

The failures below are silent: the model runs, the loss goes down, and the scores are simply worse than they should be. Each is cheap to check once you know to look.

Coordinates

  • Normalising by the wrong size. Divide pixel boxes by the original page width and height, not by the 224 × 224 resized image or by a PDF's point size when boxes are in pixels. The Transformers documentation says explicitly to use the size "before resizing the image".
  • Out-of-range ids. Values below 0 or above 1023 raise index errors; values between 1001 and 1023 do not, but should not occur in coordinates normalised the pretraining way. Clamp to 0…1000.
  • Swapped conventions. LayoutLM expects (x₀, y₀, x₁, y₁) with the origin at the top left; some OCR engines return (x, y, width, height) or polygons.
  • Degenerate boxes. x₁ < x₀ gives a negative width; v3's implementation clips width and height into 0…1023, but a wrong box is still wrong.

Tokens and labels

  • Sub-word boxes. Every sub-word must receive its word's box; building boxes per sub-word from character offsets without the checkpoint's convention creates a mismatch. For labels, use the first-sub-word convention (others −100) or label all sub-words — consistently in training and evaluation.
  • Box granularity. Feeding word boxes to a checkpoint fine-tuned on segment boxes (or the reverse) shifts the input distribution; v3's published FUNSD numbers used segment boxes.
  • Special-token boxes. Use the pretraining convention for [CLS], [SEP] and padding.

Images and processors

  • Channel order and normalisation. v2 expects BGR and normalises inside the model; v3 expects RGB normalised by its image processor. Feeding v3-style tensors to v2 (or the reverse) runs without error.
  • Mixing processors. Each version has its own processor: tokenizer (WordPiece, SentencePiece or BPE), image size and normalisation. The v3 resources on the Transformers documentation reuse v2 notebooks and warn to use the v2 processor there.
  • Built-in OCR. The v2/v3 image processors run Tesseract by default (apply_ocr=True); when you already have OCR, turn it off and pass words and normalised boxes, or you will train on one engine's output and infer on another's.
Takeaway: before training, plot a few examples' normalised boxes back over their page images and print the token–box–label triples of one example. Most LayoutLM bugs are visible in that one picture.

32 Takeaways

The family in eight statements, each backed by a section above. If only one thing is remembered, it should be that LayoutLM interprets an OCR engine's reading of the page: its strengths and its failures are both inherited from that interface.

The family in eight statements

  • Layout is an input embedding. Box corners on a 0–1000 grid index learned tables and are added to token embeddings; this costs 0.5M–3.1M parameters at base size (up to 4.2M in v1-large's implementation) and lifts FUNSD by about 18 F1 over same-size BERT in v1.
  • Masked-token pretraining with boxes kept (MVLM) is what makes the layout tables informative; more pretraining pages helped monotonically in v1.
  • v2 put the image into pretraining as 49 CNN tokens and added relative 1-D and 2-D attention biases; token-level text–image alignment (TIA) was worth more than page-level matching (TIM).
  • LayoutXLM carried the v2 recipe to 53 languages with character-merged token boxes and introduced XFUND; cross-lingual zero-shot transfer works partially, not fully.
  • v3 replaced the CNN with 0.6M parameters of linear patches and made text and image objectives symmetric (MLM, MIM, WPA); without MIM the patches help FUNSD slightly but hurt CORD and RVL-CDIP and break layout detection.
  • Numbers need their settings: word vs segment boxes on FUNSD, train vs train + dev + generated questions on DocVQA, and paper vs recomputed parameter counts all change the story.
  • 512 positions and extractive heads bound what the family can do; long documents need windows, and generated or normalised answers need another model.
  • Licensing — non-commercial weights for v2, v3 and LayoutXLM — often decides adoption before accuracy does.
Takeaway: LayoutLM is the reference design for OCR-based document understanding. Read the Document AI Models hub for where it sits in the field, and Donut for the design that removes the OCR step.
