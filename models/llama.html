Llama

Meta's open-weight family of decoder-only language models — LLaMA (February 2023), Llama 2 and Llama 2-Chat (July 2023), Llama 3, 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 (April–December 2024) and the mixture-of-experts Llama 4 (April 2025). The generic decoder-only story — next-token prediction, causal masking, the generation loop, KV-cache basics and decoding — is told on the GPT page and is not repeated here. This page owns what Llama changed: training small models far past compute-optimal on public data, the RMSNorm / SwiGLU / RoPE / bias-free block, grouped-query attention, the 128K vocabulary and 128K context, the Llama 2-Chat alignment recipe, the move to experts and interleaved NoPE layers, and every released configuration with its parameter, KV-cache and compute arithmetic recomputed from the configs.

01 What Llama is

Llama is a causal Transformer decoder trained on next-token cross-entropy — the same objective as GPT — with a block that swaps three GPT components for later alternatives and drops every bias. Its historical importance is less the block than the release: capable weights that anyone could download, fine-tune and quantise.

the objective (unchanged from GPT)ℒ = − (1/n) ∑ᵢ log P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎) one Llama block, pre-norma = h + Attn( RoPE, RMSNorm(h) ) h′ = a + W₂ · ( SiLU(W₁ · RMSNorm(a)) ⊙ W₃ · RMSNorm(a) ) outputlogits = W_out · RMSNorm(h_L) (W_out separate from the input embedding)

The four things that make a model "a Llama"

  • Pre-normalisation with RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm (§07).
  • SwiGLU feed-forward with a hidden width near 8d/3, rounded up (§08).
  • Rotary position embeddings applied to queries and keys in every layer, instead of a learned position table (§09).
  • No bias terms in any linear layer, and an untied output head (§10).

Everything else in the family's history — grouped-query attention, a bigger tokenizer, a larger RoPE base, a long-context stage, experts — was added on top of that template, one or two changes per generation.

Takeaway: Llama is GPT's objective with a modernised block, trained on far more tokens per parameter, and released as weights. The block became the default template for later open families (see LLM Families).

03 LLaMA 1: public data, trained past compute-optimal

The LLaMA paper's argument was about inference, not training. The compute-optimal recipe (roughly 20 training tokens per parameter) minimises the cost of training a model of given quality; if the model will then serve many requests, a smaller model trained longer is cheaper overall.

lifetime compute of a dense modelC_life ≈ 6·N·D (train) + 2·N·Q (serve Q tokens) compute-optimal size for a training budget C (≈20 tokens per parameter)D ≈ 20·N ⇒ N_opt ≈ √(C / 120)

What the paper claimed and showed

  • The inference-budget argument, in the paper's words: the compute-optimal work "disregards the inference budget"; given a target level of performance, "the preferred model is not the fastest to train but the fastest at inference". It sets the compute-optimal recommendation of a 10B model on 200B tokens against its own finding that a 7B model "continues to improve even after 1T tokens".
  • Public data only. Every source in the mix (§04) was publicly available, in contrast to the undocumented corpora of GPT-3, Chinchilla and PaLM — which made the recipe reproducible in principle.
  • Headline results: LLaMA-13B "outperforms GPT-3 (175B) on most benchmarks" despite being more than ten times smaller, and LLaMA-65B is "competitive with" Chinchilla-70B and PaLM-540B. On MMLU (5-shot) the 65B scored 63.4, and a briefly instruction-tuned variant (LLaMA-I) 68.9.
  • Where the tokens-per-parameter lever actually applies. The 7B and 13B saw 1.0T tokens (about 148 and 77 per parameter); the 33B and 65B saw 1.4T (about 43 and 21). The 65B is therefore close to compute-optimal; the "trained far past optimal" story is about the small models.

Takeaway: LLaMA made "train the small model much longer" the default for open models. By Llama 3 the 8B saw about 1,900 tokens per parameter, while the report sized its 405B to be roughly compute-optimal for the flagship budget.

Why the vocabulary grew

  • Compression. The Llama 3 report measures English compression rising from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token — about 24% more text per token, so the same training compute "reads" more text and the same context window holds more.
  • Multilinguality. The 28K extra tokens were chosen for non-English languages, improving compression and downstream scores "with no impact on English tokenization".
  • The cost is at small scale. Embedding matrices grow as V·d. For Llama 3.2 1B the (tied) embedding is 21% of all parameters; for Llama 3.1 405B the two untied matrices are about 1% (use the calculator in §15). This is part of why the smallest Llama 3.2 models tie their embeddings.
  • A tiktoken detail. The Transformers documentation notes the Llama 3 tokenizer "ignores BPE merge rules when an input token is part of the vocab": if a whole word is itself a vocabulary entry it is emitted directly, even if no merge sequence would produce it.
Takeaway: Llama 3's biggest single change apart from data volume was the tokenizer. Token counts before and after it are not comparable: "2T tokens" of Llama 2 and "15T tokens" of Llama 3 are measured in different units.

07 RMSNorm, worked by hand

LayerNorm subtracts the mean, divides by the standard deviation, then applies a gain and a bias. RMSNorm skips the mean and the bias: it divides by the root-mean-square and applies a gain. The placement (pre-norm, plus a final norm) is the same as GPT-2's; see Transformers — norms for the general comparison.

LayerNorm (GPT)LN(x) = γ ⊙ (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε) + β μ = mean(x), σ² = mean((x − μ)²) RMSNorm (Llama)RMSNorm(x) = g ⊙ x / √(mean(x²) + ε) epsilon in the released configsrms_norm_eps = 1e-6 (LLaMA 7B/13B/33B) · 1e-5 (LLaMA 65B, Llama 2 onward)

Why the simpler norm is enough

  • The RMSNorm paper argued that LayerNorm's benefit comes mainly from re-scaling invariance, not re-centring, and reported similar quality with less computation. RMSNorm needs one reduction per token instead of two.
  • One learned vector per norm instead of two: a Llama block has 2d norm parameters, a GPT block 4d. At any real width this is negligible; the saving that matters is the reduction, which is memory-bound on accelerators.
  • Because the residual stream is not mean-centred, a constant offset in the stream is not removed by the norm. Nothing in Llama relies on it being removed.
Takeaway: RMSNorm is LayerNorm without the mean and the bias. It removes scale but not shift, costs one reduction instead of two, and has one gain vector per norm.

08 SwiGLU and the (2/3)·4d hidden size

A SwiGLU feed-forward multiplies a SiLU-activated projection element-wise by a second, linear projection before projecting back. Three weight matrices instead of two, so the hidden width is cut by a third to keep the parameter count — then rounded up by the reference code.

SwiGLU FFN (three matrices, no biases)FFN(x) = W₂ · ( SiLU(W₁x) ⊙ W₃x ) SiLU(z) = z · sigmoid(z) parameter match with a 4d GELU FFN3 · d · f = 2 · d · 4d ⇒ f = 8d/3 ≈ 2.667·d the width rule in the reference codef₀ = ⌊2 · 4d / 3⌋ f₁ = ⌊ffn_dim_multiplier · f₀⌋ (if a multiplier is set) f = multiple_of · ⌈f₁ / multiple_of⌉

The gate, by hand

  • If for one hidden unit W₁x = 1.0 and W₃x = 2.0: SiLU(1.0) = 1.0 × 0.731 = 0.731, times 2.0 gives 1.462.
  • If instead W₁x = −2.0: SiLU(−2.0) = −2.0 × 0.119 = −0.238, times 2.0 gives −0.477. The gate is smooth and lets a small negative value through, unlike ReLU.
  • For LLaMA 7B (d = 4096): 4d = 16,384, ⌊2·16,384/3⌋ = 10,922, rounded up to a multiple of 256 gives 11,008 — the intermediate_size in the config, 2.6875·d.

09 RoPE, worked by hand

Rotary position embedding splits each query and key head vector into two-dimensional pairs and rotates pair i by an angle proportional to the token's position. The dot product of a rotated query and a rotated key then depends only on their relative offset. General theory and alternatives: Transformers — RoPE.

frequency of pair i (head dimension d_h, base θ)ωᵢ = θ^(−2i / d_h) i = 0, 1, …, d_h/2 − 1 rotation of a pair at position mR(mωᵢ) = [ cos mωᵢ −sin mωᵢ ] [ sin mωᵢ cos mωᵢ ] why only the offset matters(R(mω)q) · (R(nω)k) = qᵀ R(mω)ᵀ R(nω) k = qᵀ R((n − m)ω) k

A hand-worked pair

  • Pair i = 0 has ω₀ = θ⁰ = 1 radian per token for any base. Let q = k = (1, 0).
  • Query at m = 2: R(2)q = (cos 2, sin 2) = (−0.416, 0.909). Key at n = 0: unchanged, (1, 0). Score −0.416 = cos 2.
  • Move both by five tokens (m = 7, n = 5): q = (cos 7, sin 7), k = (cos 5, sin 5), score cos 7·cos 5 + sin 7·sin 5 = cos 2 = −0.416 again. Absolute position has cancelled.
  • The last pair (i = 63 of a 128-dimensional head) rotates at θ^(−126/128): with θ = 10,000 its wavelength is about 54,000 tokens; with θ = 500,000 about 2.56 million. Slow pairs carry long-range position; fast pairs carry local order.

Implementation facts that matter when porting weights

  • Only Q and K are rotated; V is not, so positions affect where attention goes but are not written into the values.
  • The rotation is applied in every layer, after the Q/K projections and before the dot product — a GQA key head is rotated once and shared by its query group.
  • Meta's reference code pairs adjacent dimensions (it views each head as complex numbers); the Transformers implementation pairs dimension j with j + d_h/2 (rotate_half). The two are equivalent only because the conversion script permutes the rows of the Q and K projection weights. Loading raw reference weights into the Transformers module without that permutation produces wrong outputs without an error (§30).
Takeaway: RoPE turns absolute positions into relative ones inside the attention score, with no learned table and no row limit. Its reach is set by the base θ: Llama 3 raised it from 10,000 to 500,000 so that more pairs have wavelengths longer than the training context (§20).

10 No biases, untied embeddings

Two small choices that the LLaMA paper does not discuss but the released weights make explicit: no linear layer has a bias, and the output projection is a separate matrix from the input embedding.

Biases

  • Every Llama config sets attention_bias = false and mlp_bias = false; the RMSNorms have no bias either. In a GPT block the biases total 13d per block — at d = 8192 about 106k parameters, 0.01% of a block. The saving is not the point.
  • The reasons usually given are simplicity and stability at scale (PaLM also dropped biases, reporting better training stability for large models). The Llama papers themselves do not state a reason.
  • Later families differ: Qwen kept Q/K/V biases through Qwen2.5, which is one of the few block-level differences from Llama (see LLM Families).

Untied output head

  • GPT ties W_out = W_embᵀ. Llama keeps two V × d matrices, so its embedding cost is 2·V·d: for Llama 3.1 8B that is 1.05B of 8.03B parameters (13%), for 405B 4.2B of 405.9B (1%).
  • Llama 3.2 1B and 3B tie (tie_word_embeddings = true). At 1.24B total, an untied 128,256 × 2048 head would add another 263M parameters — 21% more.
  • Llama 4 Scout and Maverick are untied again.
Takeaway: no biases anywhere and a separate output head, except where the embedding would be too large a share of a small model. Both show up only in the configs, which is why parameter counts should be computed from those, not from the papers' prose.

LLaMA 1's efficiency work

  • A memory-efficient causal attention (from the xformers library) that neither stores attention weights nor computes scores for masked positions.
  • Hand-written backward passes so that activation checkpointing saves the expensive activations (linear-layer outputs) and recomputes only cheap ones; model and sequence parallelism; overlapping GPU communication with computation.
  • Result reported for the 65B: about 380 tokens per second per GPU on 2,048 A100-80GB GPUs, so 1.4T tokens took about 21 days. That rate times 6·N is about 149 TFLOP/s per GPU — consistent with the GPU-hours in §17.
Takeaway: the recipe is plain AdamW with β₂ = 0.95, cosine decay and weight decay 0.1. Llama 3 added the practices that matter at 16K GPUs: growing batch and sequence length early, a separate long-context stage, and an annealing phase with checkpoint averaging.

The two ablations behind the changes (appendix A.2.1)

  • Context 2k → 4k, both trained for 150B tokens: large gains on long-context tasks (NarrativeQA F1 0.21 → 17.26, Qasper 0.71 → 18.52) and no loss on general tasks such as HellaSwag (75.1 → 74.8, within noise).
  • MHA vs MQA vs GQA at 30B, 150B tokens each: GQA "performs comparably to the MHA baseline on most evaluation tasks and is better than the MQA variant on average". Takeaway: Llama 2 pretraining is LLaMA 1 with more tokens, a longer window and GQA. Its lasting contributions are GQA as the default for large open models, a commercial-use licence, and a documented chat-alignment recipe (§18–§19).

13 Grouped-query attention in Llama

GQA keeps all query heads but lets groups of them share one key head and one value head. The KV cache and the K/V projection weights shrink by the group size; the attention computation per query head is unchanged. General mechanics: Attention — GQA.

headsh query heads, n_kv key/value heads, group size g = h / n_kv attention weights per layer (bias-free)W_Q, W_O: 2 · d · h · d_h W_K, W_V: 2 · d · n_kv · d_h KV cache per token2 · L · n_kv · d_h · bytes
Query heads sharing key/value heads choose a model · slide the number of KV heads through the divisors of its query-head count · cache and weights recomputed

Where Llama uses it

  • Llama 2: only 34B and 70B (64 query heads, 8 KV heads, group of 8). The 7B and 13B stay multi-head.
  • Llama 3 onward: 8 KV heads at every size, including 8B (32 query heads, group of 4), 405B (128 query heads, group of 16), 3.2 1B (32 query heads of dimension 64) and Llama 4 (40 query heads, group of 5).
  • Converting a multi-head checkpoint to GQA is possible by mean-pooling the heads in each group and continuing training briefly (the GQA paper's "uptraining"); Llama's GQA models were trained with GQA from scratch.
Takeaway: with 8 KV heads, a 70B Llama caches as much per token as a multi-head model an eighth its width would. That — not the few percent of saved weights — is why every Llama since 3 uses it.

18 Llama 2-Chat: SFT and two reward models

Llama 2-Chat follows the InstructGPT pipeline summarised on the GPT page — supervised fine-tuning, a learned reward, then reinforcement learning — with three departures: far fewer but better SFT examples, two separate reward models, and iterative data collection over five RLHF versions.

reward-model loss with a rating marginℒ = − log sigmoid( r(x, y_c) − r(x, y_r) − m(r) ) margin m(r) by preference rating (Table 27, "large" variant)significantly better 3 · better 2 · slightly better 1 · negligibly better 0

Supervised fine-tuning

  • Started from public instruction data, then replaced it: "SFT annotations in the order of tens of thousands was enough", and collection stopped at 27,540 vendor-written examples, with no Meta user data.
  • Settings: learning rate 2e-5 with cosine decay, weight decay 0.1, batch 64, sequence length 4,096, 2 epochs; prompts and answers concatenated to fill sequences, loss only on answer tokens.

Preference data and the two reward models

  • Annotators compared two responses and rated the preferred one as significantly better, better, slightly better or negligibly better/unsure. Meta collected 1,418,091 such comparisons in weekly batches, alongside existing open datasets.
  • Two reward models, Helpfulness and Safety, because the two objectives trade off and a single model struggled to do both. Both are initialised from pretrained chat-model checkpoints, with the next-token head replaced by a scalar regression head, so that the reward model "knows" what the chat model knows.
  • The margin term helped most on clearly separable pairs: average helpfulness accuracy 62.5% without a margin, 63.0% with the small margin (Table 28). Llama 3 later dropped the margin, observing "diminishing improvements after data scaling".
Takeaway: quality over quantity for SFT, and two reward models rather than one. Preference data is collected against the latest chat model each week so the reward models stay in distribution.

19 Rejection sampling, then PPO

Llama 2-Chat was optimised with two algorithms in sequence. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning draws K samples per prompt, keeps the one the reward model likes best, and fine-tunes on those; PPO then optimises the reward directly with a KL penalty. Up to RLHF-V4 only rejection sampling was used; for V5, PPO was applied on top of the rejection-sampling checkpoint.

The reward model here is a fixed random toy — prompt difficulty plus a temperature-dependent mean and spread — chosen only to reproduce the shape of the paper's Figures 7 and 8, not their values. Every curve is computed by sorting the simulated samples.

How the two methods differ (as the paper frames it)

  • Breadth: rejection sampling explores K samples per prompt; PPO generates one.
  • Depth: PPO samples from the policy as it is being updated; rejection sampling samples everything from the starting policy, then fine-tunes like SFT. Iterating the rounds blurs the difference.
  • Rejection sampling was run only on the 70B; smaller chat models were fine-tuned on the 70B's selected samples — a form of distillation.
  • The best temperature for K between 10 and 100 was 1.2–1.3, and it shifts as RLHF versions progress, so it was re-tuned each round.
  • Result: in human evaluation on about 4,000 prompts, Llama 2-Chat 70B had a 36% win rate and 31.5% tie rate against ChatGPT.
Takeaway: Llama 2-Chat's RLHF is mostly best-of-K filtering fed back into supervised fine-tuning, with PPO added last. Llama 3 kept the rejection sampling and replaced PPO with DPO (§21); the PPO and DPO mechanics are on Policy & Preference Optimization.

20 8K → 128K: RoPE base 500,000 and the llama3 rescaling

Llama 3 pretrained at 8,192 tokens; Llama 3.1 extends to 128K. Two mechanisms are involved: a larger RoPE base chosen before training, and — in the released code and configs, though not described in the report — a frequency-dependent rescaling of the RoPE frequencies. Other extension methods (position interpolation, NTK, YaRN) are on Context-Length Extension.

What the report does say

  • Base 500,000 "to better support longer contexts", citing work showing the value effective up to 32,768 tokens.
  • Long-context pretraining "in six stages, starting from the original 8K context window and ending in the final 128K", using about 800B tokens; each stage continued until short-context performance fully recovered and needle-in-a-haystack retrieval was perfect at that length.
  • A document mask that prevents attention across documents packed into one sequence: "limited impact" in standard pretraining, "important in continued pre-training on very long sequences".
  • An annealing stage over the final 40M tokens, still at 128K context. The attention compute at 128K was handled with context parallelism (16-way at 131,072 tokens, Table 4).
Takeaway: with base 500,000 and a 128-dimensional head, 29 of the 64 frequency pairs already have wavelengths above 8,192; the llama3 rule divides exactly those by the factor, blends six, and leaves the 29 fast pairs alone — so local word order keeps its trained resolution while the slow pairs cover the longer window.

21 Llama 3 and 3.1: data, scale and a simpler post-training loop

The Llama 3 report is explicit that its gains come "primarily" from "improvements in data quality and diversity as well as by increased training scale" — the architecture deviates little from Llama 2. It also explains choosing a dense model over MoE "to maximize training stability".

Pretraining (§3 of the report)

  • Architecture deltas from Llama 2: GQA with 8 KV heads at every size, the document mask, the 128K vocabulary, base 500,000.
  • Scaling laws were fitted on small runs to pick the flagship size: extrapolated to 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs they suggested about 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens; the final 405B on 15.6T is "approximately compute-optimal". The 8B and 70B were trained "much longer than is compute-optimal".
  • The data mix was adjusted during training: more non-English data, upsampled maths, more recent web data late in training.
  • Annealing on high-quality code and maths improved the 8B's GSM8k and MATH validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4%, but had negligible effect on the 405B.

Post-training (§4)

  • Each of six rounds: train a reward model (Llama 2's loss without the margin; some samples have a third, human-edited response ranked above the chosen one), rejection-sample responses, SFT on them (lr 1e-5, 8.5K–9K steps for the largest models), then DPO (lr 1e-5, β = 0.1).
  • Two DPO modifications: mask special formatting tokens out of the loss (they otherwise caused tail repetition or early termination tokens), and add an NLL term with weight 0.2 on the chosen response.
  • PPO was tried and dropped: DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better", especially on instruction-following benchmarks.
  • Models from different data or hyperparameter variants are averaged at each stage.
Takeaway: Llama 3's recipe is "same block, better and much more data, one big dense model, then SFT + rejection sampling + DPO in rounds". The change from PPO to DPO between Llama 2 and Llama 3 is the main alignment-method change in the family.

22 Llama 3.2 and 3.3: small models, vision adapters, a new 70B

Llama 3.2 (September 2024) added two kinds of model on either side of 3.1: small text models for on-device use, and vision models that bolt an image encoder onto the frozen 3.1 text models. Llama 3.3 (December 2024) is one 70B text model.

1B and 3B: pruned and distilled

  • Initialised by "structured pruning in a single shot manner from the Llama 3.1 8B", then trained with "logits from the Llama 3.1 8B and 70B models" as token-level targets — knowledge distillation during pretraining.
  • Configs: 1B is 16 layers × 2048 with 32 query heads of dimension 64; 3B is 28 layers × 3072 with 24 heads of 128; both have 8 KV heads, an 8192-wide FFN, tied embeddings, and llama3 rope scaling with factor 32 for a 128K window.

11B and 90B vision

  • An image encoder feeds a series of cross-attention layers inserted into Llama 3.1 8B and 70B; the adapter and encoder are trained on image–text pairs while the team "intentionally did not update the language-model parameters", so the models are drop-in replacements for 3.1 on text. This is the approach described in the Llama 3 report's vision section; the broader design space is on Vision-Language Models.
  • This is late fusion through cross-attention — the opposite of Llama 4's early fusion (§23).

3.3 70B

  • Same architecture as 3.1 70B (identical dimensions and RoPE settings in the config), 128K context, "15T+" pretraining tokens and a December 2023 knowledge cutoff per its model card; it differs in post-training. Its model card lists 7.0M H100 GPU-hours, the same figure as 3.1 70B.
Takeaway: 3.2's small models are distilled from bigger Llamas and tie their embeddings; its vision models leave the text weights frozen and add cross-attention. 3.3 is a post-training refresh of the 70B, not a new architecture.

23 Llama 4: mixture of experts, interleaved NoPE, early fusion

Llama 4 was released in April 2025 with a blog post and model cards rather than a technical paper. Everything below is either stated in that material or read from the released configs, and the two are distinguished. General MoE mechanics: Mixture of Experts.

What Meta published

  • Scout: 17B active, 16 experts, 109B total; context 10M tokens; "pre-trained and post-trained with a 256K context length"; fits one H100 with int4 quantisation. Maverick: 17B active, 400B total, 128 routed experts plus a shared expert; context 1M. Behemoth (288B active, 16 experts, nearly 2T total) was previewed, still in training, as the teacher used to distil the other two; it was not released.
  • Maverick uses "alternating dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers"; "each token is sent to the shared expert and also to one of the 128 routed experts".
  • Early fusion: text and vision tokens are integrated "into a unified model backbone"; the vision encoder is "based on MetaCLIP" and trained with a frozen Llama.
  • iRoPE: "interleaved attention layers without positional embeddings", plus "inference time temperature scaling" of attention, aimed at length generalisation.
  • Training: FP8 (390 TFLOP/s per GPU reported for Behemoth on 32K GPUs), a hyperparameter-transfer technique called MetaP, more than 30T tokens and 200 languages overall; post-training is lightweight SFT, then online RL, then lightweight DPO.
The 48-layer stack of Scout and Maverick, from the configs switch model · slide context · parameters and KV cache recomputed from the layer pattern

What the configs add

  • no_rope_layers marks every fourth layer (indices 3, 7, …, 47) as NoPE: 12 of 48 layers have no positional signal at all; the other 36 use RoPE with base 500,000.
  • attention_chunk_size = 8192: in the Transformers implementation the RoPE layers attend within 8,192-token chunks and the NoPE layers attend globally, which is what makes a 10M window's cache tractable.
  • Scout: MoE in all 48 layers, use_qk_norm = true, llama3 rope scaling with factor 16. Maverick: MoE in the 24 odd-indexed layers and a 16,384-wide dense SwiGLU in the others, no QK norm, no rope scaling.
  • Top-1 routing (num_experts_per_tok = 1), an 8,192-wide SwiGLU per expert and a shared expert of the same shape; a 202,048-row vocabulary.
Takeaway: Llama 4 changes three things at once — dense to MoE with a shared expert, RoPE everywhere to RoPE plus global NoPE layers, and adapter-based vision to early fusion. With both total and active counts recomputed from the configs, "17B active" holds for both models, and Maverick's 400B is almost entirely routed experts.

24 Ghost Attention: keeping a system instruction alive

Early Llama 2-Chat models "tended to forget the initial instruction after a few turns". Ghost Attention (GAtt) is a data trick, not an architectural change: it synthesises dialogues in which the instruction was present everywhere, then trains as if it had appeared only at the start.

GAtt, as a data recipe1. take a dialogue [u₁, a₁, …, uₙ, aₙ] and an instruction inst ("act as …", "speak in …") 2. sample assistant replies from the latest RLHF model with inst prepended to every uᵢ 3. keep inst only in the first user turn 4. fine-tune with loss = 0 on every token from earlier turns, including assistant turns
  • Step 4 prevents a mismatch: the intermediate assistant turns were generated with the instruction visible, so the model is not trained to produce them from a context where it has been removed.
  • Instructions were synthetic combinations of hobbies, languages and public figures (the lists themselves generated by Llama 2-Chat), and half the time shortened ("Always act as Napoleon from now" → "Figure: Napoleon").
  • Applied after RLHF V3; the paper reports the instruction held for "20+ turns, until the maximum context length is reached", and shows attention to the system message staying high across the dialogue.
Takeaway: multi-turn consistency was fixed with training data and a loss mask. The same "system message" idea became part of the chat templates of every later Llama.

29 Pitfalls

Mistakes that recur when using, fine-tuning or reasoning about Llama models.

Prompting and tokens

  • Wrong or missing chat template. Llama 2-Chat uses [INST] / <<SYS>>; Llama 3 uses header tokens and <|eot_id|>; Llama 4 uses a different set again. A base-model prompt sent to an instruct model (or the reverse) still produces text, usually worse. Use apply_chat_template.
  • Double begin-of-text. The template already emits <s> or <|begin_of_text|>; tokenising its output again with special tokens added gives two. Nothing errors.
  • Not stopping at end-of-turn. Llama 3 instruct turns end with <|eot_id|> (128009), not the base model's <|end_of_text|> (128001). If only the latter is a stop token, the model keeps writing past its turn.
  • No pad token. The original models use pad_id = −1. For batching, add a pad token and resize embeddings (the documented route), or reuse an existing token; pad on the left for generation, and make sure padding is masked out of the loss.

Positions and context

  • Dropping rope_scaling. Loading a Llama 3.1 checkpoint with code or configs that ignore the llama3 scaling reproduces Llama 3 positions; quality degrades beyond 8K with no error.
  • Assuming the advertised window is usable. 128K or 10M is what the positions support; retrieval and reasoning quality at that length must be measured.
  • Porting raw weights without the Q/K permutation between adjacent-pair and half-split RoPE layouts (§09).

Numerics and arithmetic

  • fp16 for training. Use bf16; fp16 fine-tuning of these models can overflow to NaN.
  • Quoting labels as sizes. "70B" is 69.0B (Llama 2) or 70.6B (Llama 3); "1B" is 1.24B. Use the config.
  • Comparing token counts across tokenizers. 2T Llama 2 tokens and 15T Llama 3 tokens are different units (§05).
  • Reading MoE totals as compute. Llama 4 Maverick is 400B to store but about 17B per token to run; memory planning uses the first number, FLOP planning the second.
Takeaway: most Llama failures are silent format errors — template, special tokens, padding, RoPE scaling — rather than exceptions. Check the rendered prompt and the config before debugging the model.

32 What to remember

Ten statements that summarise the family, each backed by a section above.

  • Same objective as GPT, modernised block: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases, untied head.
  • Trained for inference. LLaMA's small models saw 77–148 tokens per parameter, Llama 3 8B about 1,900; the 405B was sized near compute-optimal.
  • Public data first, then less disclosure. LLaMA 1 published its per-source mix; later generations published less, and Llama 4 includes Meta product data.
  • The FFN width is a rounding rule, not 8d/3: floor, optional multiplier, round up.
  • RoPE makes scores relative; the base sets reach. 10,000 → 500,000, plus the code-only llama3 rescaling for 128K.
  • GQA with 8 KV heads arrived at Llama 2 70B and became universal in Llama 3 — an 8× smaller cache at 70B.
  • The tokenizer changed once in the dense era (32K → 128K), improving English compression by about 24% and making token counts incomparable across it.
  • Alignment moved from rejection sampling + PPO with two reward models (Llama 2) to rejection sampling + DPO in rounds (Llama 3) to SFT → online RL → DPO (Llama 4).
  • Llama 4 is a different architecture: top-1 MoE with a shared expert, NoPE every fourth layer, early fusion; 17B active of 109B or 400B.
  • Open weights, custom licence. Downloadable and fine-tunable, but not open source and not reproducible without the data.
Takeaway: Llama's technical contribution was a clean, reusable decoder recipe trained on many more tokens than was fashionable; its larger effect was making that recipe's weights available to everyone else.
