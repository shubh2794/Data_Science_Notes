18 Llama 2-Chat: SFT and two reward models

Llama 2-Chat follows the InstructGPT pipeline summarised on the GPT page — supervised fine-tuning, a learned reward, then reinforcement learning — with three departures: far fewer but better SFT examples, two separate reward models, and iterative data collection over five RLHF versions.

Supervised fine-tuning

  • Started from public instruction data, then replaced it: "SFT annotations in the order of tens of thousands was enough", and collection stopped at 27,540 vendor-written examples, with no Meta user data.
  • Settings: learning rate 2e-5 with cosine decay, weight decay 0.1, batch 64, sequence length 4,096, 2 epochs; prompts and answers concatenated to fill sequences, loss only on answer tokens.

Preference data and the two reward models

  • Annotators compared two responses and rated the preferred one as significantly better, better, slightly better or negligibly better/unsure. Meta collected 1,418,091 such comparisons in weekly batches, alongside existing open datasets.
  • Two reward models, Helpfulness and Safety, because the two objectives trade off and a single model struggled to do both. Both are initialised from pretrained chat-model checkpoints, with the next-token head replaced by a scalar regression head, so that the reward model "knows" what the chat model knows.
  • The margin term helped most on clearly separable pairs: average helpfulness accuracy 62.5% without a margin, 63.0% with the small margin (Table 28). Llama 3 later dropped the margin, observing "diminishing improvements after data scaling".
Takeaway: quality over quantity for SFT, and two reward models rather than one. Preference data is collected against the latest chat model each week so the reward models stay in distribution.

19 Rejection sampling, then PPO

Llama 2-Chat was optimised with two algorithms in sequence. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning draws K samples per prompt, keeps the one the reward model likes best, and fine-tunes on those; PPO then optimises the reward directly with a KL penalty. Up to RLHF-V4 only rejection sampling was used; for V5, PPO was applied on top of the rejection-sampling checkpoint.

The reward model here is a fixed random toy — prompt difficulty plus a temperature-dependent mean and spread — chosen only to reproduce the shape of the paper's Figures 7 and 8, not their values. Every curve is computed by sorting the simulated samples.

How the two methods differ (as the paper frames it)

  • Breadth: rejection sampling explores K samples per prompt; PPO generates one.
  • Depth: PPO samples from the policy as it is being updated; rejection sampling samples everything from the starting policy, then fine-tunes like SFT. Iterating the rounds blurs the difference.
  • Rejection sampling was run only on the 70B; smaller chat models were fine-tuned on the 70B's selected samples — a form of distillation.
  • The best temperature for K between 10 and 100 was 1.2–1.3, and it shifts as RLHF versions progress, so it was re-tuned each round.
  • Result: in human evaluation on about 4,000 prompts, Llama 2-Chat 70B had a 36% win rate and 31.5% tie rate against ChatGPT.
Takeaway: Llama 2-Chat's RLHF is mostly best-of-K filtering fed back into supervised fine-tuning, with PPO added last. Llama 3 kept the rejection sampling and replaced PPO with DPO (§21); the PPO and DPO mechanics are on Policy & Preference Optimization.

20 8K → 128K: RoPE base 500,000 and the llama3 rescaling

Llama 3 pretrained at 8,192 tokens; Llama 3.1 extends to 128K. Two mechanisms are involved: a larger RoPE base chosen before training, and — in the released code and configs, though not described in the report — a frequency-dependent rescaling of the RoPE frequencies. Other extension methods (position interpolation, NTK, YaRN) are on Context-Length Extension.

What the report does say

  • Base 500,000 "to better support longer contexts", citing work showing the value effective up to 32,768 tokens.
  • Long-context pretraining "in six stages, starting from the original 8K context window and ending in the final 128K", using about 800B tokens; each stage continued until short-context performance fully recovered and needle-in-a-haystack retrieval was perfect at that length.
  • A document mask that prevents attention across documents packed into one sequence: "limited impact" in standard pretraining, "important in continued pre-training on very long sequences".
  • An annealing stage over the final 40M tokens, still at 128K context. The attention compute at 128K was handled with context parallelism (16-way at 131,072 tokens, Table 4).
Takeaway: with base 500,000 and a 128-dimensional head, 29 of the 64 frequency pairs already have wavelengths above 8,192; the llama3 rule divides exactly those by the factor, blends six, and leaves the 29 fast pairs alone — so local word order keeps its trained resolution while the slow pairs cover the longer window.

21 Llama 3 and 3.1: data, scale and a simpler post-training loop

The Llama 3 report is explicit that its gains come "primarily" from "improvements in data quality and diversity as well as by increased training scale" — the architecture deviates little from Llama 2. It also explains choosing a dense model over MoE "to maximize training stability".

Pretraining (§3 of the report)

  • Architecture deltas from Llama 2: GQA with 8 KV heads at every size, the document mask, the 128K vocabulary, base 500,000.
  • Scaling laws were fitted on small runs to pick the flagship size: extrapolated to 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs they suggested about 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens; the final 405B on 15.6T is "approximately compute-optimal". The 8B and 70B were trained "much longer than is compute-optimal".
  • The data mix was adjusted during training: more non-English data, upsampled maths, more recent web data late in training.
  • Annealing on high-quality code and maths improved the 8B's GSM8k and MATH validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4%, but had negligible effect on the 405B.

Post-training (§4)

  • Each of six rounds: train a reward model (Llama 2's loss without the margin; some samples have a third, human-edited response ranked above the chosen one), rejection-sample responses, SFT on them (lr 1e-5, 8.5K–9K steps for the largest models), then DPO (lr 1e-5, β = 0.1).
  • Two DPO modifications: mask special formatting tokens out of the loss (they otherwise caused tail repetition or early termination tokens), and add an NLL term with weight 0.2 on the chosen response.
  • PPO was tried and dropped: DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better", especially on instruction-following benchmarks.
  • Models from different data or hyperparameter variants are averaged at each stage.
Takeaway: Llama 3's recipe is "same block, better and much more data, one big dense model, then SFT + rejection sampling + DPO in rounds". The change from PPO to DPO between Llama 2 and Llama 3 is the main alignment-method change in the family.

22 Llama 3.2 and 3.3: small models, vision adapters, a new 70B

Llama 3.2 (September 2024) added two kinds of model on either side of 3.1: small text models for on-device use, and vision models that bolt an image encoder onto the frozen 3.1 text models. Llama 3.3 (December 2024) is one 70B text model.

1B and 3B: pruned and distilled

  • Initialised by "structured pruning in a single shot manner from the Llama 3.1 8B", then trained with "logits from the Llama 3.1 8B and 70B models" as token-level targets — knowledge distillation during pretraining.
  • Configs: 1B is 16 layers × 2048 with 32 query heads of dimension 64; 3B is 28 layers × 3072 with 24 heads of 128; both have 8 KV heads, an 8192-wide FFN, tied embeddings, and llama3 rope scaling with factor 32 for a 128K window.

11B and 90B vision

  • An image encoder feeds a series of cross-attention layers inserted into Llama 3.1 8B and 70B; the adapter and encoder are trained on image–text pairs while the team "intentionally did not update the language-model parameters", so the models are drop-in replacements for 3.1 on text. This is the approach described in the Llama 3 report's vision section; the broader design space is on Vision-Language Models.
  • This is late fusion through cross-attention — the opposite of Llama 4's early fusion (§23).

3.3 70B

  • Same architecture as 3.1 70B (identical dimensions and RoPE settings in the config), 128K context, "15T+" pretraining tokens and a December 2023 knowledge cutoff per its model card; it differs in post-training. Its model card lists 7.0M H100 GPU-hours, the same figure as 3.1 70B.
Takeaway: 3.2's small models are distilled from bigger Llamas and tie their embeddings; its vision models leave the text weights frozen and add cross-attention. 3.3 is a post-training refresh of the 70B, not a new architecture.

23 Llama 4: mixture of experts, interleaved NoPE, early fusion

Llama 4 was released in April 2025 with a blog post and model cards rather than a technical paper. Everything below is either stated in that material or read from the released configs, and the two are distinguished. General MoE mechanics: Mixture of Experts.

What Meta published

  • Scout: 17B active, 16 experts, 109B total; context 10M tokens; "pre-trained and post-trained with a 256K context length"; fits one H100 with int4 quantisation. Maverick: 17B active, 400B total, 128 routed experts plus a shared expert; context 1M. Behemoth (288B active, 16 experts, nearly 2T total) was previewed, still in training, as the teacher used to distil the other two; it was not released.
  • Maverick uses "alternating dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) layers"; "each token is sent to the shared expert and also to one of the 128 routed experts".
  • Early fusion: text and vision tokens are integrated "into a unified model backbone"; the vision encoder is "based on MetaCLIP" and trained with a frozen Llama.
  • iRoPE: "interleaved attention layers without positional embeddings", plus "inference time temperature scaling" of attention, aimed at length generalisation.
  • Training: FP8 (390 TFLOP/s per GPU reported for Behemoth on 32K GPUs), a hyperparameter-transfer technique called MetaP, more than 30T tokens and 200 languages overall; post-training is lightweight SFT, then online RL, then lightweight DPO.
The 48-layer stack of Scout and Maverick, from the configs switch model · slide context · parameters and KV cache recomputed from the layer pattern

What the configs add

  • no_rope_layers marks every fourth layer (indices 3, 7, …, 47) as NoPE: 12 of 48 layers have no positional signal at all; the other 36 use RoPE with base 500,000.
  • attention_chunk_size = 8192: in the Transformers implementation the RoPE layers attend within 8,192-token chunks and the NoPE layers attend globally, which is what makes a 10M window's cache tractable.
  • Scout: MoE in all 48 layers, use_qk_norm = true, llama3 rope scaling with factor 16. Maverick: MoE in the 24 odd-indexed layers and a 16,384-wide dense SwiGLU in the others, no QK norm, no rope scaling.
  • Top-1 routing (num_experts_per_tok = 1), an 8,192-wide SwiGLU per expert and a shared expert of the same shape; a 202,048-row vocabulary.
Takeaway: Llama 4 changes three things at once — dense to MoE with a shared expert, RoPE everywhere to RoPE plus global NoPE layers, and adapter-based vision to early fusion. With both total and active counts recomputed from the configs, "17B active" holds for both models, and Maverick's 400B is almost entirely routed experts.

24 Ghost Attention: keeping a system instruction alive

Early Llama 2-Chat models "tended to forget the initial instruction after a few turns". Ghost Attention (GAtt) is a data trick, not an architectural change: it synthesises dialogues in which the instruction was present everywhere, then trains as if it had appeared only at the start.

GAtt, as a data recipe1. take a dialogue [u₁, a₁, …, uₙ, aₙ] and an instruction inst ("act as …", "speak in …") 2. sample assistant replies from the latest RLHF model with inst prepended to every uᵢ 3. keep inst only in the first user turn 4. fine-tune with loss = 0 on every token from earlier turns, including assistant turns
  • Step 4 prevents a mismatch: the intermediate assistant turns were generated with the instruction visible, so the model is not trained to produce them from a context where it has been removed.
  • Instructions were synthetic combinations of hobbies, languages and public figures (the lists themselves generated by Llama 2-Chat), and half the time shortened ("Always act as Napoleon from now" → "Figure: Napoleon").
  • Applied after RLHF V3; the paper reports the instruction held for "20+ turns, until the maximum context length is reached", and shows attention to the system message staying high across the dialogue.
Takeaway: multi-turn consistency was fixed with training data and a loss mask. The same "system message" idea became part of the chat templates of every later Llama.
