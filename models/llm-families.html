LLM Families

A large-language-model family is a series of decoder-only Transformers released by one lab under one name — GPT, Llama, Mistral/Mixtral, Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek — whose members share a block design, a tokenizer and a training recipe and differ mainly in size, data and context length. This hub is the note on the category itself: the handful of design axes along which the families actually differ, how the recipe converged between 2019 and 2025, the published configurations side by side with their parameter and KV-cache arithmetic worked out from the configs, how to choose between them, and where each family's own page lives.

01 What LLM families are

An LLM family is a lineage of causally masked Transformer decoders trained on next-token prediction and published as a series of checkpoints that share an architecture template. Every family on this page optimises the same objective; what distinguishes them is a small set of engineering choices — how attention stores keys and values, how positions are encoded, whether the feed-forward layer is dense or a mixture of experts, how large the vocabulary is, and how many tokens the model sees.

the objective every family pretrains onℒ(θ) = − (1/n) ∑ᵢ log P_θ(xᵢ | x₁, …, xᵢ₋₁) the block every family stacks L times (pre-norm form)h ← h + Attn(Norm(h)) h ← h + FFN(Norm(h)) dense parameter count, to first orderN ≈ L · (attention + FFN) + embeddings FFN(SwiGLU) = 3 · d · f

What counts as a member

  • Decoder-only. All six families use one stack of masked self-attention blocks; there is no encoder and no cross-attention. Encoder-only models (BERT) and encoder–decoder models (T5, BART) live under NLP Models.
  • Released as a series. A family is recognisable because successive releases reuse a template: Llama 2 is Llama 1 plus grouped-query attention on its larger sizes and a longer context; Llama 3 keeps that block and swaps the tokenizer; Qwen3 keeps the Qwen2.5 block and replaces QKV bias with QK-Norm.
  • Documented enough to compare. The open-weight families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek) publish configs, so their parameter counts can be recomputed from the configs (up to a few negligible terms). GPT is included as the ancestor: GPT-2 and GPT-3 configs are public, the GPT-3.5/GPT-4-era reports are not, and OpenAI's later open-weight gpt-oss models (August 2025) are outside this page's tables.

Why a hub is useful here

  • Read one family page and the architecture looks bespoke. Read six side by side and it becomes clear that by 2024 they had largely converged on one recipe — pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU or GeGLU, RoPE, grouped-query attention, no linear biases — and that the live differences are in attention memory, sparsity, vocabulary and data scale.
  • Those differences are largely the ones that decide cost in deployment: KV-cache bytes per token set how many concurrent users fit in memory, active parameters set FLOPs per token, total parameters set the weight memory.
Takeaway: an LLM family is a named series of decoder-only Transformers that share one block template. Comparing families means comparing a short list of axes — attention variant, positional scheme, dense vs MoE, vocabulary, context, training tokens — which this hub lays out and computes from published configs.

02 Lineage and timeline

The families arrived in two waves. OpenAI's GPT-2 and GPT-3 (2019–2020) established the decoder-only recipe at scale; from 2023 the open-weight families — Llama, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen, DeepSeek — published configurations and weights, and the design space moved quickly from dense models towards sparse mixture-of-experts.

The two waves

  • 2019–2020, closed lineage. GPT-2 (1.5B, context 1024) and GPT-3 (175B, context 2048, 300B training tokens) fixed the template: learned absolute positions, LayerNorm moved before each sub-layer, GELU MLP with a 4× expansion, full multi-head attention. The GPT-3 paper also tabulated eight sizes, which later families imitated with their own size ladders.
  • 2023, the open recipe. LLaMA (February 2023) took the GPT-3 skeleton and swapped in three changes borrowed from other work: RMSNorm pre-normalisation, SwiGLU activations and rotary position embeddings. It trained much longer than GPT-3 for its size (1.4T tokens for 65B). Llama 2 (July 2023) added grouped-query attention to the 34B and 70B models and doubled the context to 4096. Mistral 7B (October 2023) combined GQA with sliding-window attention.
  • 2024, sparsity and scale of data. Mixtral (January 2024) put eight SwiGLU experts in each layer with top-2 routing. DeepSeek-V2 (May 2024) introduced multi-head latent attention (MLA) and scaled up the fine-grained routed plus shared experts first proposed in DeepSeekMoE (January 2024). Gemma (March 2024) and Gemma 2 (July 2024) used very large 256k vocabularies and, in Gemma 2, alternating local/global attention and logit soft-capping. Llama 3 (report July 2024) moved to a 128k vocabulary and 15.6T tokens for its dense 405B model.
  • Late 2024–2025, MoE at the frontier of open weights. DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) scaled MLA + DeepSeekMoE to 671B total / 37B active on 14.8T tokens. Qwen2.5 (December 2024) raised the data to 18T tokens; Qwen3 (May 2025) to about 36T, with dense and MoE members. Gemma 3 (March 2025) raised the local-to-global ratio to 5:1 with a 1024-token window.
Takeaway: read the timeline as a shift of where the ingenuity went — first scale (GPT-3), then a cleaner block (LLaMA), then attention memory (GQA, sliding window, MLA), then sparsity (Mixtral, DeepSeek, Qwen3-MoE) and data volume (15–36T tokens).

03 The design axes that separate families

Strip away the names and a modern decoder is described by roughly ten choices. Some have effectively converged (normalisation, activation, positional scheme); others are where the families still disagree (attention memory, dense vs sparse, vocabulary size, context, training tokens).

Figure. Rows are representative members; columns are the axes discussed in this and the next four sections. Within a column, cells sharing a colour made the same choice, so a column of one colour is a converged axis and a multicoloured column is a live one. The readout counts, for the highlighted axis, how many of the listed models chose each option — computed from the same spec table that draws the cells.

Axes that converged

  • Normalisation placement and type. Every family after GPT-2 normalises the input of each sub-layer (pre-norm) and, from LLaMA on, uses RMSNorm, which drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and bias: RMSNorm(x) = x / √(mean(x²) + ε) · g. Gemma 2 and 3 add a second RMSNorm on each sub-layer's output as well (pre- and post-norm).
  • Gated activations. The FFN became a gated linear unit: SwiGLU in Llama, Mistral, Qwen and DeepSeek, an approximate-GELU gate (GeGLU) in Gemma. A GLU has three matrices instead of two, so its hidden width f is usually set near (8/3)·d rather than 4·d to keep parameters comparable; in practice widths are rounded and frequently larger (Llama 3 8B uses 3.5·d, Gemma 7B 8·d).
  • Rotary position embeddings. After GPT-3, every family on this page uses RoPE: queries and keys are rotated by position-dependent angles so their dot product depends on relative offset. What differs is the base frequency θ and the extension method (see Positions and context).
  • No biases in linear layers, with a notable exception: Qwen2 and Qwen2.5 keep a bias on the Q, K, V projections, which Qwen3 removed in favour of QK-Norm.

Axes still in play

  • Attention memory: MHA (GPT), MQA (Gemma 2B), GQA (Llama 2 70B onward, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma 2/3), MLA (DeepSeek-V2/V3), with or without sliding-window layers.
  • Feed-forward: dense (Llama, Gemma, Qwen dense) vs mixture of experts (Mixtral, DeepSeek, Qwen-MoE), and within MoE, few large experts vs many small ones, with or without shared experts.
  • Vocabulary and tying: 32k (Llama 1/2, Mistral 7B), ~100–152k (DeepSeek, Llama 3, Qwen), 256k–262k (Gemma), and whether the output matrix reuses the input embedding.
  • Context and data: native training context from 2k to 32k, extended to 128k by fine-tuning with rescaled RoPE; training tokens from 0.3T (GPT-3) to about 36T (Qwen3).
Takeaway: by 2024 the "architecture" of an open LLM family is mostly a vector of a few choices on attention memory, sparsity, vocabulary and data. The block itself — pre-norm RMSNorm, gated FFN, RoPE — is shared and rarely the reason one family beats another.

04 Attention variants: MHA, MQA, GQA, MLA and sliding windows

During generation every past token's keys and values are cached, so the size of that cache — not the attention FLOPs — usually limits batch size and context length. The attention variants the families adopted are, first of all, ways of shrinking the KV cache while losing as little quality as possible.

KV-cache elements per token, standard attention2 · L · n_kv · d_h (keys and values, every layer) multi-head (MHA), multi-query (MQA), grouped-query (GQA)MHA: n_kv = n_h MQA: n_kv = 1 GQA: 1 < n_kv < n_h multi-head latent attention (MLA), per tokenL · (d_c + d_rope) DeepSeek-V2/V3: d_c = 512, d_rope = 64 sliding window W: a local layer caches at mostmin(T, W) tokens instead of T

From MHA to GQA

  • MHA gives every query head its own key and value head. GPT-3 175B caches 2 · 96 · 96 · 128 = 2,359,296 elements per token — about 4.5 MiB in bf16, so a 2048-token context costs 9 GiB per sequence.
  • MQA ("Fast Transformer Decoding: One Write-Head is All You Need", arXiv 1911.02150) shares one K/V head across all query heads, cutting the cache by n_h×. Gemma 2B uses it; the reported cost is some quality loss and training instability at larger scale.
  • GQA ("GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models from Multi-Head Checkpoints", arXiv 2305.13245) groups query heads to share K/V heads. With 64 query heads and 8 KV heads (Llama 2 70B, Qwen2 72B) the cache is 8× smaller than MHA. Llama 2 applied it to 34B and 70B only; Llama 3 used 8 KV heads at every size.

MLA: caching a latent instead of heads

  • DeepSeek-V2's multi-head latent attention down-projects each token's hidden state to a latent c_KV of width d_c = 512, caches that, and reconstructs per-head keys and values with up-projections. Because the up-projection can be absorbed into the query and output matrices at inference, the cache is just the latent plus a small decoupled RoPE key of width 64.
  • Per token and layer that is 576 elements, against 2 · 128 · 128 = 32,768 for MHA with the same 128 heads of width 128 — about 57× smaller. The DeepSeek-V2 report states a 93.3% KV-cache reduction relative to its dense DeepSeek 67B predecessor, and reports that MLA matched or exceeded MHA quality in its own ablations — an empirical result from that report, not a theorem, and not an established general property.

Sliding-window and local/global layers

  • Mistral 7B restricts every layer to the previous W = 4096 tokens and stores keys and values in a rolling buffer; stacked layers still propagate information further (the paper's theoretical span is about W · L ≈ 131k tokens). The paper reports an 8× cache saving at 32k sequence length.
  • Gemma 2 alternates local (window 4096) and global layers 1:1; Gemma 3 uses five local layers (window 1024) per global layer. Only the global layers pay full-context cache, which is why Gemma 3 27B's curve grows at roughly a sixth of the slope of a same-shape all-global model.
Takeaway: MHA → GQA cuts the cache by the grouping factor (typically 4–8×); MLA replaces heads with a 576-wide latent per layer; sliding windows cap the cache on local layers. For long-context serving these choices can matter more than parameter count.

05 Dense vs mixture of experts

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer replaces the single FFN with several FFNs ("experts") and a router that sends each token to a few of them. Total parameters grow with the number of experts; the feed-forward compute per token grows only with the number chosen. That decoupling is why the largest open-weight models since 2024 are mostly sparse.

MoE feed-forward for one token x (top-k routing over E experts)y = ∑_(i ∈ TopK(g(x))) gᵢ(x) · FFNᵢ(x) + ∑_(s ∈ shared) FFNₛ(x) parameters per MoE layer, gated experts of width f_etotal = E · 3 · d · f_e + d · E active = k · 3 · d · f_e + d · E Mixtral 8x7B, for instanceE = 8, k = 2, f_e = 14336 → total ≈ 46.7B, active ≈ 12.9B

Three MoE designs in the families

  • Few large experts (Mixtral). Mixtral 8x7B keeps Mistral 7B's attention and replaces each FFN with 8 copies of the same shape, routing each token to the top 2 with a softmax over the selected logits. The paper's round numbers are 47B total and 13B active; the exact config gives 46.7B and 12.9B. The name "8x7B" is not 56B because attention and embeddings are shared, not duplicated.
  • Fine-grained plus shared experts (DeepSeekMoE). DeepSeek-V2 uses 160 routed experts of width 1536 plus 2 always-on shared experts, routing to 6; DeepSeek-V3 uses 256 routed experts of width 2048 plus 1 shared, routing to 8, and keeps the first three layers dense. The DeepSeekMoE rationale is that many small experts give the router more combinations and that shared experts hold common knowledge so routed experts can specialise. V3 also balances load mainly with a per-expert bias adjusted during training ("auxiliary-loss-free" balancing), keeping only a complementary sequence-wise balance loss with a very small weight, and limits each token to at most 4 nodes.
  • Fine-grained without shared experts (Qwen3-MoE). Qwen3-235B-A22B and Qwen3-30B-A3B have 128 experts, 8 active, and — unlike Qwen2.5-MoE — no shared expert; the report adopts a global-batch load-balancing loss "to encourage expert specialization".

What sparsity buys and costs

  • Buys: training and per-token inference FLOPs scale with active parameters (≈ 2 · N_active FLOPs per token forward), so DeepSeek-V3's 37B active parameters cost roughly what a 37B dense model costs per token while storing 671B parameters of capacity.
  • Costs: all experts must sit in memory, so weight memory scales with total parameters; routing needs load balancing and expert-parallel communication; small batches may touch most experts anyway, eroding the savings at low load. Whether an MoE of total size N matches a dense model of size N is not settled in general; the comparisons these reports publish typically place an MoE model above a dense model of its active size and below one of its total size, but that is an observation from specific benchmarks, not a rule.
Takeaway: MoE decouples capacity (total parameters, memory) from compute (active parameters, FLOPs). Mixtral's 8 big experts, DeepSeek's many small experts plus a shared one, and Qwen3's many small experts without one are the three patterns to recognise. The mechanism itself is covered in Mixture of Experts.

06 Positions and context length

Every family after GPT-3 encodes position with rotary embeddings (RoPE): each pair of query/key dimensions is rotated by an angle proportional to the token's position, with frequencies set by a base θ. Context length is then a training-time choice extended afterwards by changing how those frequencies are scaled.

RoPE frequency for dimension pair j of a head of width d_hωⱼ = θ^(−2j / d_h) angle at position m: m · ωⱼ relative-position property of the rotated dot product⟨R_m q, R_n k⟩ = ⟨q, R_(n−m) k⟩ longest wavelength grows with the baseλ_max ≈ 2π · θ^((d_h − 2)/d_h)

Base frequency and native context

  • θ = 10,000 (the original RoPE value) in Llama 1/2, Mistral 7B, Gemma 1/2 and the DeepSeek-V2/V3 configs. Llama 1 trained at 2048 tokens, Llama 2 at 4096, Mistral 7B at 8192 with a 4096 window, Gemma 1/2 at 8192.
  • θ = 500,000 in Llama 3, chosen explicitly "to better support longer contexts"; Llama 3 pretrained at 8192 and Llama 3.1 was extended to 128K during a final long-context stage.
  • θ = 1,000,000 in Mixtral (trained at 32k), Qwen2/2.5/3 (raised from 10k with "adjusted base frequency", ABF) and on Gemma 3's global layers (its local layers keep 10k).

Extending past the training length

  • Frequency rescaling. Llama 3.1 ships a llama3 rope-scaling rule (factor 8 on an original 8192) that stretches low frequencies and leaves high ones; DeepSeek-V2/V3 use YaRN (factor 40 on an original 4096), with V3 extended in two stages to 32K and then 128K; Qwen2 and Qwen3 combine YaRN with Dual Chunk Attention at inference; Gemma 3 uses positional interpolation (linear factor 8) on its global layers.
  • Status of the claims. A "128K context" means the model was trained or fine-tuned so that retrieval at that length works on the lab's tests (often needle-in-a-haystack). How well a model uses long context for reasoning is a separate, weaker property; see Context-Length Extension for the methods and their evaluations.
Takeaway: RoPE is shared by every post-GPT-3 family here; families differ in θ (10k, 500k, 1M), native training length (2k–32k) and the rescaling rule used to reach 128K. Long-context memory cost is then set by the attention variant of §04.

07 Tokenizers, vocabularies and tied embeddings

The tokenizer fixes the vocabulary size V, which sets the embedding and output matrices (V · d each, or once if tied), and it fixes how many tokens a given text costs — which changes training-token counts, context lengths in characters, and inference price.

embedding parameters, untied vs tieduntied: 2 · V · d tied: V · d Gemma 7B, from its reportV · d = 256,128 · 3,072 = 786,825,216 (≈ 9% of 8.5B)

The vocabulary sizes, family by family

  • 50,257 (GPT-2, GPT-3) — byte-level BPE, the reference point.
  • 32,000 (Llama 1/2, Mistral 7B, Mixtral) — SentencePiece BPE with byte fallback.
  • ~100k–129k (DeepSeek, Llama 3) — DeepSeek-V2 uses 102,400 entries in its config, DeepSeek-V3 a 128K byte-level BPE (129,280 rows in its config); Llama 3's 128K vocabulary combines 100K tiktoken tokens with 28K extra for non-English, and the report says it improved compression from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token on English.
  • ~152k (Qwen) — byte-level BPE with 151,643 regular tokens plus control tokens; the configs pad the embedding to 152,064 (the larger Qwen2/2.5 models, 7B and up) or 151,936 (the small Qwen2/2.5 models and all of Qwen3) rows.
  • 256k–262k (Gemma) — inherited from Gemini's SentencePiece tokenizer: 256,128 entries in the Gemma 1/2 reports (the released Hugging Face configs list 256,000), and a 262k vocabulary for Gemma 3 (the report's text says 262k, its parameter-table caption says 256k; the released config has 262,208 rows, which include the image soft token at id 262,144).

Tying, and why vocabulary size matters more for small models

  • Gemma ties the output projection to the input embedding at every size; Qwen ties only its smallest models (up to 3B in Qwen2.5, up to 4B in Qwen3); Llama (apart from the later Llama 3.2 1B and 3B, which tie), Mistral and DeepSeek do not tie. Tying saves V · d parameters, which is a large fraction for a small model and negligible for a large one.
  • For Gemma 2B the embeddings are 524.6M of 2.5B parameters (about 21%); for Llama 3.1 405B the two untied matrices are 4.2B of 405.9B (about 1%). A big vocabulary is therefore a real design cost at small scale and nearly free at large scale — one reason multilingual families accept it.
  • Token counts are only comparable within a tokenizer. "15.6T tokens" of Llama 3 and "14.8T tokens" of DeepSeek-V3 are not the same amount of text; treat cross-family token comparisons as approximate.
Takeaway: vocabularies grew from 32k to 128k–262k to cover more languages and compress text better. The cost is V · d embedding parameters, significant mainly for small models, and the side-effect is that training-token counts are not directly comparable across families. Tokenizer algorithms themselves are covered in Tokenization.

08 Training tokens and the move past "compute-optimal"

The largest difference between GPT-3 and a 2025 open model is often not the block but the data: GPT-3 175B saw 300B tokens, Qwen3 about 36T. Families deliberately train far beyond the compute-optimal ratio, because inference cost depends on model size, not on how long the model was trained.

From undertrained to overtrained

  • GPT-3 used about 1.7 tokens per parameter. "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (arXiv 2203.15556) argued that for a fixed compute budget parameters and tokens should grow together, at roughly 20 tokens per parameter — a rule of thumb, since the fitted ratio depends on the data and the fitting method.
  • LLaMA reframed the target: for models that will serve many queries, the right target is the smallest model that reaches a quality level, even if training it is not compute-optimal. Its 7B model trained on 1.0T tokens (≈ 150 per parameter) and the paper reported the 7B's loss was still falling.
  • By 2024–2025 ratios of several hundred became normal for small and mid-sized dense models: Gemma 7B about 700, Qwen2.5 72B about 250. Llama 3's report states its 405B was sized to be roughly compute-optimal for its 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOP budget, while its 8B and 70B were trained "much longer than is compute-optimal".

What else changed in the data

  • Multi-stage schedules. Qwen3 describes a general stage (about 30T tokens at 4,096 length), a reasoning stage (about 5T higher-quality tokens), then a long-context stage at 32,768 tokens. Llama 3 anneals on high-quality data at the end. DeepSeek-V3 adds a multi-token-prediction objective and trains in FP8.
  • Distillation as pretraining. Gemma 2's 2B and 9B were trained with knowledge distillation from a larger teacher instead of plain next-token targets; Gemma 3 extended distillation to all sizes.
  • Disclosure varies. Mistral 7B and Mixtral reports give no token counts; others give totals but not mixtures. When a table on this page says "undisclosed", it means the primary source is silent.
Takeaway: modern families trade training compute for cheaper inference by training small models far beyond 20 tokens per parameter. Token count, not block design, is often the largest single difference between two families' same-size models.

Notes on the table

  • FFN width is the hidden width of one gated FFN (the size of each of its three matrices' inner dimension). The Gemma reports list twice this number (49,152 for 7B, 73,728 for 27B) because they count the gate and up projections together.
  • Vocabulary is the number of embedding rows in the released config, which is often padded beyond the tokenizer: Qwen2 reports 151,646 tokens (config 152,064); Llama 3's report says 128K while the config has 128,256 including special tokens. The one exception is Gemma 1/2, where the table follows the reports' 256,128 (the Hugging Face configs list 256,000) because the reports' printed embedding counts use 256,128; the difference is 128 · d, under 0.01% of either model.
  • Context for Qwen3: its report's architecture tables say 128K for the 32B and 235B models, while the released configs set 40,960 positions and the model card describes 32,768 native tokens extended to 131,072 with YaRN. The table reports both.
  • DeepSeek-V3's released checkpoint is 685B parameters because it includes a 14B multi-token-prediction module used in training; the 671B main-model figure excludes it.

How the counts are computed

  • GQA attention per layer: d · n_h · d_h (Q) + 2 · d · n_kv · d_h (K, V) + n_h · d_h · d (O), plus Q/K/V biases for Qwen2/2.5.
  • MLA attention per layer: query down-projection d · d_cq and up-projection d_cq · n_h · (d_nope + d_rope); KV down-projection d · (d_c + d_rope) and up-projection d_c · n_h · (d_nope + d_v); output n_h · d_v · d; plus the two latent RMSNorms.
  • Gated FFN: 3 · d · f; MoE layer: E · 3 · d · f_e + shared experts + router d · E. Norms: 2d per layer plus a final d. GPT uses the exact GPT formula with learned positions and biases.
  • Minor terms are ignored (Qwen3's and Gemma 3's per-head QK-Norm weights, Gemma 2/3's extra post-norms, DeepSeek-V3's expert-bias vectors), which moves the totals by far less than 0.01%.
Takeaway: the published round numbers can be reproduced from the configs to within about 4% — the largest gaps, GPT-2 "1.5B" = 1.56B and Mistral "7B" = 7.24B, are the rounding of the label itself — and exactly for the embedding counts the Gemma 1 and Gemma 2 reports print (Gemma 3's table rounds its embedding count to 1,416M, against 1,409.6M from the config). The same arithmetic shows why Llama 2 "70B" is 69.0B, why Gemma "7B" is 8.5B, and why Mixtral "8x7B" is 46.7B, not 56B.

10 How to choose between families

For most projects the choice is set by constraints before it is set by benchmarks: the memory you can serve from, the context you need, the languages you must handle, and the licence. Benchmarks move with every release; the constraints below change more slowly.

Start from memory and throughput

  • Weights must fit. In bf16 a model needs about 2 bytes per total parameter (≈ 16 GB for 8B, ≈ 140 GB for 70B, ≈ 1.3 TB for DeepSeek-V3). An MoE is priced by total parameters in memory and by active parameters in compute — attractive when you have memory and want throughput, unattractive on a single small GPU.
  • KV cache must fit too. At long context and high concurrency the cache can exceed the weights. Families with GQA at a small KV-head count (Qwen3-MoE's 4), MLA (DeepSeek) or local-attention layers (Gemma 2/3, Mistral 7B) are cheaper per token of context; see the figure in §04.
  • Quantisation (8-bit, 4-bit) changes the arithmetic by 2–4× and is supported for all these families by common inference stacks, with quality losses that depend on the method and the model.

Then context, languages and licence

  • Context. Llama 3.1, Qwen2.5/3, Gemma 3 (4B and up) and DeepSeek-V2/V3 advertise 128K; Llama 2 and Gemma 1/2 stop at 4K–8K. Advertised context is an upper bound on usable context, not a guarantee.
  • Languages and scripts. Larger multilingual vocabularies (Gemma's 256k+, Qwen's 152k, Llama 3's 128k) tokenise non-English text more efficiently than 32k vocabularies; Qwen3 reports 119 languages and dialects in pretraining.
  • Licence. Licences differ by family and even by size (Qwen2.5 released most sizes under Apache 2.0 but its 3B and 72B under separate Qwen licences; Llama and Gemma use their own licences with use policies). Read the licence of the exact checkpoint.
  • Fine-tuning path. Dense models are simpler to fine-tune with LoRA-style methods; MoE fine-tuning needs expert-parallel infrastructure and care with router stability. See Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning.
Takeaway: pick by constraint first — weight memory (total parameters), per-token compute (active parameters), KV cache (attention variant), context, languages, licence — then compare quality among the families that survive, on your own evaluation rather than headline benchmarks.

11 Family fingerprints

Each family has one or two signature choices that recur across its releases. Knowing them makes a config file readable at a glance and tells you which family page to open for the details.

GPT (2018–2020 configs public)

Learned absolute positions, LayerNorm (post-norm in GPT-1, pre-norm from GPT-2), GELU MLP at 4·d, full MHA, byte-level BPE with 50,257 tokens, tied input/output embeddings, and GPT-3's alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention layers. The GPT-3.5/GPT-4-era reports disclose capabilities, not architectures, so the family's dense configuration history stops at GPT-3; the exception is gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b (August 2025), open-weight mixture-of-experts models whose configs are public but which are not tabulated here.

Llama (2023–2024)

The template most later families copied: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases, untied embeddings. Its generations are cleanly separable by one change each — GQA and 4k context (Llama 2), a 128k tiktoken-based vocabulary, θ = 500,000 and 15T+ tokens (Llama 3), 128K context and a dense 405B (Llama 3.1). Llama 4 (April 2025) moved to mixture-of-experts models and is not tabulated here.

Mistral and Mixtral (2023–2024)

Mistral 7B is a Llama-style block with GQA (8 KV heads) and sliding-window attention (W = 4096) served from a rolling buffer cache. Mixtral 8x7B keeps the attention, drops the window in favour of a 32k dense context, and replaces each FFN with 8 experts routed top-2; Mixtral 8x22B scales the same pattern (56 layers, d = 6144, 141B total by the config's arithmetic).

Qwen (2024–2025)

A Llama-style block with a ~152k byte-level BPE vocabulary, Q/K/V biases through Qwen2.5 replaced by QK-Norm in Qwen3, θ = 1,000,000, and the most aggressive data scaling (7T → 18T → ~36T tokens). Qwen3 pairs dense models (0.6B–32B) with fine-grained MoE models (30B-A3B, 235B-A22B) that have 128 experts, 8 active, and no shared expert, plus switchable "thinking" and "non-thinking" modes after post-training.

Gemma (2024–2025)

Very large tied vocabularies (256k–262k), GeGLU, head dimension 256 in Gemma 1, RMSNorm both before and after each sub-layer from Gemma 2, alternating local/global attention (1:1 at window 4096 in Gemma 2, 5:1 at window 1024 in Gemma 3), logit soft-capping in Gemma 2 replaced by QK-Norm in Gemma 3, and distillation from a larger teacher for most sizes. Gemma 3 adds a ~400M SigLIP vision encoder to its 4B–27B models.

DeepSeek (2024–2025)

Multi-head latent attention for a tiny KV cache and DeepSeekMoE (many fine-grained routed experts plus shared experts), YaRN-extended 128K context, and in V3 auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, a multi-token-prediction objective and FP8 mixed-precision training. The V3 report states 2.788M H800 GPU hours for the full training run — a figure that excludes prior research and ablations, as the report itself notes.

Takeaway: GPT — learned positions and MHA; Llama — the clean RMSNorm/SwiGLU/RoPE/GQA template; Mistral — sliding windows and top-2 MoE; Qwen — biggest data and dense + MoE ladders; Gemma — huge tied vocabularies and local/global layers; DeepSeek — MLA and fine-grained MoE.

14 Takeaways

The short version of the hub, as a checklist to carry into any new model card or config file you meet.

The checklist

  • One objective, one block. Every family trains a causal decoder on next-token cross-entropy; since LLaMA the block has largely converged on pre-norm RMSNorm, a gated FFN (SwiGLU/GeGLU), RoPE and bias-free linears.
  • Attention memory is the live axis. MHA → GQA (4–8× smaller cache), MLA (a 576-element latent per layer), and sliding-window or local/global layers (Mistral, Gemma 2/3) are the main levers for long-context serving cost.
  • Sparsity separates capacity from compute. Mixtral (8 experts, top-2), DeepSeek (many fine-grained experts plus shared ones) and Qwen3-MoE (128 experts, top-8, no shared) cost memory by total parameters and compute by active parameters.
  • RoPE base and rescaling set context. θ moved from 10k to 500k–1M, and 128K contexts come from a late long-context stage plus YaRN, llama3 or linear rescaling — advertised context is not the same as usable context.
  • Vocabularies grew. 32k → 128k–262k, at a real parameter cost mainly for small models; token counts are tokenizer-specific.
  • Data grew fastest. 0.3T (GPT-3) → 1.4T (LLaMA) → 15.6T (Llama 3.1) → ~36T (Qwen3); training far past ≈ 20 tokens per parameter is now deliberate.
  • Check the arithmetic. Configs reproduce the reported sizes: Llama 2 "70B" is 69.0B, Gemma "7B" is 8.5B, Mixtral 8x7B is 46.7B total and 12.9B active, DeepSeek-V3 is 671B / 37B.
  • Choose by constraint. Weight memory, compute per token, KV cache, context, languages and licence narrow the field before benchmarks do.
Takeaway: to read a new family's config, ask six questions — attention variant and KV heads, dense or MoE (total/active), positional base and native context, vocabulary and tying, training tokens, and norm/activation — and the rest is detail for its own page.
