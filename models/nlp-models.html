NLP Models

NLP models, in the sense this hub uses, are pretrained Transformer language models built to read text and to transform it: encoder-only models such as BERT, RoBERTa, ELECTRA, DeBERTa and ModernBERT that turn a sequence into contextual vectors for classification, tagging, retrieval and extraction, and encoder–decoder models such as T5 and BART that read an input sequence and write a different output sequence for summarisation, translation and other conditional generation. This page is the note on the category itself: the design axes that separate its members, the lineage from 2018 to 2024, a table of published configurations with parameter counts you can recompute, how the pretraining objectives differ, and how to choose between an encoder, an encoder–decoder and a decoder-only model. The individual model pages hang off it.

01 What NLP models are

An NLP model here is a Transformer pretrained on unlabelled text with a self-supervised objective and then adapted to language tasks — either an encoder-only network that maps a sequence to one contextual vector per token, or an encoder–decoder network that maps an input sequence to an output sequence. Both are distinguished from the decoder-only large language models of GPT and its successors by how much of the input each position may see and by what the model is trained to reconstruct.

encoder-only: every output vector conditions on the whole inputh₁, …, hₙ = Enc(x₁, …, xₙ) task head: y = g(h) encoder–decoder: a full read of the source, then left-to-right generationP(y | x) = ∏ₜ P(yₜ | y₍<ₜ₎, Enc(x)) decoder-only, for contrast: one stream, causal throughoutP(x) = ∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | x₍<ᵢ₎)

What belongs in the category

  • Encoder-only models (BERT 2018, RoBERTa 2019, ALBERT 2019, ELECTRA 2020, DeBERTa 2020–2021, ModernBERT 2024). They do not generate text left to right on their own. A small head on top of the final hidden states produces a label per sequence, a tag per token, a start/end pair for extractive question answering, or a pooled embedding for search.
  • Encoder–decoder models (T5 2019, BART 2019, mT5 2020, Flan-T5 2022). The encoder reads the source bidirectionally; the decoder generates the target token by token, attending to the encoder output through cross-attention. In T5's framing every task becomes "text in, text out"; BART is also fine-tuned with classification heads.
  • Decoder-only models are not members of this hub — they live under LLM Families — but they are the constant point of comparison, because since roughly 2022 they have absorbed many workloads that used to go to encoders and seq2seq models.

Why the category still matters

  • Cost. A 110M–400M-parameter encoder classifies, tags or embeds a document in one forward pass, with no autoregressive loop. For high-volume classification, retrieval and extraction it is, by the forward-FLOP estimate in §10, often one to three orders of magnitude cheaper than prompting a multi-billion-parameter decoder for the same answer.
  • Bidirectional context. A token's representation sees both sides, which is what span extraction, token tagging and cross-encoder ranking want.
  • Conditional generation with a clear source. When the output is a transformation of a given input (summary, translation, structured record), an encoder–decoder separates "read" from "write" and remains a strong, efficient choice at small and medium scale.
Takeaway: the members of this hub differ from each other along a handful of axes — wiring, pretraining objective, positional scheme, tokenizer, length, data — and from decoder-only LLMs mainly in visibility (bidirectional input) and in what they are trained to reconstruct. The rest of the page makes each axis concrete.

02 The design axes that separate the members

Almost every model in this family is a stack of standard Transformer blocks. What separates them is a short list of choices, and many of the published gains between 2018 and 2024 came from changing one or two of them while holding most of the rest fixed.

Wiring

  • Encoder-only: one stack, full (bidirectional) self-attention. Output is n vectors; no built-in way to generate.
  • Encoder–decoder: two stacks. The decoder adds causal self-attention and a cross-attention sublayer over the encoder output, so each decoder block has three sublayers instead of two — about 1.33× the weights of an encoder block of the same width when the FFN is 4d (counted below).
  • Prefix LM: one stack whose mask is bidirectional over a prefix and causal after it. It has the same visibility pattern as encoder–decoder with shared weights — see the mask figure.

Pretraining objective

  • Masked LM (BERT, RoBERTa): predict ~15% of tokens from both sides. Next-sentence prediction was part of BERT's recipe and was dropped by RoBERTa, whose ablation found removing it matched or slightly improved downstream results.
  • Replaced-token detection (ELECTRA, DeBERTaV3): classify every token as original or replaced by a small generator.
  • Span corruption (T5): drop contiguous spans, replace each by a sentinel, and have the decoder emit only the dropped spans.
  • Denoising autoencoding (BART): corrupt the document (text infilling with Poisson-length spans, sentence permutation) and have the decoder regenerate all of it.

Positions, tokenizer, length, data

  • Positional scheme: learned absolute tables (BERT, RoBERTa, ELECTRA, BART), learned relative-position biases per head (T5), disentangled relative-position embeddings (DeBERTa), rotary embeddings (ModernBERT).
  • Tokenizer and vocabulary: WordPiece 30,522 (BERT, ELECTRA), byte-level BPE 50,265 (RoBERTa, BART), SentencePiece 32,000 (+100 sentinel ids = 32,100 tokens, embedding padded to 32,128 rows in the checkpoints; T5), SentencePiece 128,100 (DeBERTaV3), 250,112 (mT5), BPE 50,368 (ModernBERT).
  • Maximum length: 512 for the classic encoders, 1,024 for BART, 512 during T5 pretraining (relative biases let it run longer at a cost), 8,192 for ModernBERT.
  • Data: 3.3B words for BERT, 160GB of text for RoBERTa, BART and DeBERTaV3, C4 (~750GB) for T5, 2T tokens of text and code for ModernBERT.
Takeaway: read any new model in this family by asking five questions — which wiring, which objective, which positions, which tokenizer, how much data. The comparison table in §06 answers them for each member.

Three phases

  • 2018 — pretrained contextual models arrive. ELMo (February) showed deep contextual word vectors from a bidirectional LSTM language model help almost every task. GPT-1 (June) fine-tuned a whole pretrained Transformer decoder. BERT (October) combined the whole-network transfer of GPT-1 with bidirectional context through masking, and took the top of GLUE and SQuAD.
  • 2019–2020 — the recipe space is explored. RoBERTa showed BERT was undertrained; ALBERT cut parameters by sharing layers; T5 ran a systematic study of objectives, architectures and data and scaled to 11B; BART framed pretraining as denoising; ELECTRA made every position a training signal; DeBERTa changed how position enters attention.
  • 2021–2024 — consolidation. DeBERTaV3 combined DeBERTa's architecture with ELECTRA's objective; mT5 (posted in late 2020) and Flan-T5 (2022) extended T5 to 101 languages and to instruction tuning; ModernBERT imported decoder-era engineering (RoPE, GeGLU, local/global attention, long context, 2T tokens) into an encoder.
Takeaway: most of the family was designed in two years. The later steps are recipes and engineering on top of the same block, not new wirings.

04 Pretraining objectives compared

The objective decides three things at once: what the model sees as input, what it must produce, and how many positions of each training sequence contribute a gradient. Those three quantities account for much of the difference in pretraining efficiency reported across the family.

Green cells receive a loss; orange cells were corrupted; purple cells were selected for masking but left unchanged. The expected count at length N uses the objective's formula: r·N for masked LM, N for replaced-token detection, denoising and causal LM, and r·N + r·N/μ + 1 for span corruption with mean span length μ (sentinels plus the dropped tokens plus a final sentinel).

What each objective trades

  • Masked LM buys bidirectional context without letting a token see itself, at the price of a gradient on only ~15% of positions and a [MASK] token that never appears at fine-tuning time (BERT's 80/10/10 replacement rule softens the mismatch).
  • Replaced-token detection puts a loss on every position. ELECTRA's paper reports ELECTRA-Small at 79.9 GLUE dev against 75.1 for a BERT-Small trained with the same compute, and ELECTRA-Large matching RoBERTa and XLNet with less than a quarter of their pretraining compute. Its best generators were 1/4 to 1/2 the discriminator's size. Its ablation "ELECTRA 15%", which scores only the masked subset, performed much worse — which the paper reads as evidence that the all-position loss, not just the absence of [MASK], drives the gain.
  • Span corruption keeps the target short: with 15% corruption and mean span length 3, the target is about 20% of the input length, which the T5 study found both slightly better and cheaper than corrupting independent tokens.
  • Text infilling + sentence permutation makes the decoder reproduce the full document, so it is a genuine generation objective. The BART paper found text infilling the strongest single noise; sentence permutation added significant gains only on CNN/DailyMail summarisation. BART-large masked 30% of tokens and permuted all sentences.
Takeaway: "how many positions get a gradient" and "does the model practise generating" are the two numbers to read off any objective. The first explains ELECTRA's efficiency; the second explains why BART and T5 can be fine-tuned directly for abstractive summarisation, while BERT needs a separate decoder (or an extractive setup) to do it.

05 Three wirings, four masks

Encoder-only, decoder-only, prefix LM and encoder–decoder are best compared by which query may attend to which key. Two of the four turn out to have the same visibility pattern and differ only in whether the source and the target share weights.

Rows are queries, columns keys; a filled cell means the query may attend to that key. For encoder–decoder, the matrix concatenates the encoder's self-attention (top-left), the decoder's cross-attention (bottom-left) and its causal self-attention (bottom-right); source positions never see target positions. The pair counts are computed from the drawn grid.

Reading the four patterns

  • Encoder-only: all n² pairs. Best for representations; generation needs an extra decoder or an iterative masking trick.
  • Decoder-only: the lower triangle, n(n+1)/2 pairs. The same mask that makes training parallel makes every input token blind to what follows it — including the rest of a prompt.
  • Prefix LM (as in UniLM and in T5's architecture study): bidirectional over the first s positions, causal after. One set of weights serves both roles.
  • Encoder–decoder: the same s² + t·s + t(t+1)/2 pairs, but the source is processed by one stack and the target by another, joined by cross-attention. With L layers in each stack it has roughly twice the parameters of a prefix LM of the same depth at about the same compute per token, because each token passes through only one of the two stacks.

What the architecture studies found

  • The T5 study compared encoder–decoder, prefix LM and decoder-only under matched compute; encoder–decoder with a denoising objective performed best on its fine-tuning benchmarks, and sharing parameters between encoder and decoder cost surprisingly little.
  • A 2022 study at the 5B-parameter / 170B-token scale ("What Language Model Architecture and Pretraining Objective Work Best for Zero-Shot Generalization?") found causal decoder-only models strongest for zero-shot use after purely unsupervised pretraining, while non-causal models with masked-LM pretraining followed by multitask fine-tuning were best overall. Which wiring "wins" therefore depends on how the model will be used after pretraining.
Takeaway: the mask is the architecture's contract. Bidirectional input helps understanding; causal output enables generation; encoder–decoder and prefix LM are two parameterisations of the same "read fully, then write" contract.

Doing so exposes rounded labels, shows where the parameters sit, and makes one fact about this family vivid: for base-sized encoders with large vocabularies, the embedding table can rival or exceed all the Transformer blocks combined.

The count is the backbone as defined by each config: embeddings, blocks, pooler if present, relative-position tables and final norms, plus an untied output projection where the model has one (mT5, Flan-T5). Released checkpoints also store pretraining heads — BERT's MLM transform and NSP classifier, ModernBERT's prediction head — and T5 checkpoints carry one unused relative-bias table in the first cross-attention layer, which is why a few counts differ from the checkpoint by a few hundred to about a million parameters.

What the recount shows

  • BERT-base and RoBERTa-base have identical blocks (85.05M in twelve layers) and differ almost entirely in the vocabulary: 30,522 × 768 = 23.4M versus 50,265 × 768 = 38.6M. That is essentially the whole gap between "110M" and "125M": the vocabulary difference is 15,162,624 of the 15,163,392 parameters, and RoBERTa's two extra position rows minus its one fewer token-type row add the last 768.
  • DeBERTa-v3-base is mostly embedding. 128,100 × 768 = 98.4M of its roughly 184M parameters sit in one lookup table, which costs memory but almost no compute per token.
  • ALBERT's 12M is a sharing trick, not a small network. One block is reused twelve times, so it runs about the same FLOPs as BERT-base (plus a small 128 → 768 embedding projection); the savings are in storage and communication, not speed.
  • mT5-XXL spends about 2B parameters on vocabulary — an input table and an untied output table of 250,112 × 4,096 each. Multilingual coverage is paid for mostly in embeddings.
  • A decoder block is 4/3 of an encoder block at FFN width 4d (BART, T5 v1.0): the extra cross-attention adds 4d² to the 12d² of self-attention plus FFN. Scaling T5-3B and T5-11B by widening the FFN and head count instead of d is visible in the per-block numbers.
Takeaway: quote non-embedding parameters when comparing compute, total parameters when comparing memory. Across this family the two can differ by a factor of two.

08 Positions, tokenizers and length

Three quieter axes decide what inputs a model can handle at all: how it knows where a token is, how text is cut into tokens, and how long a sequence it was trained on. They matter more for choosing a checkpoint than most architectural details.

Positional schemes

  • Learned absolute (BERT, RoBERTa, ELECTRA, BART): a table with one row per position, added to token embeddings. Simple and effective, but there is no row 513 — sequences longer than the table cannot be encoded without extension tricks. RoBERTa's 514 rows are 512 positions plus an offset of two for the padding convention.
  • Relative position bias (T5): a learned scalar per head added to each attention logit, indexed by a bucketed distance — 32 buckets, exact for short distances, logarithmic up to 128, and shared across layers. Nothing hard-codes a maximum length, though quality beyond the training length is not guaranteed.
  • Disentangled attention (DeBERTa): content and relative-position vectors interact through separate content-to-position and position-to-content terms, with 256 buckets in DeBERTaV3. Absolute position enters only near the output ("enhanced mask decoder").
  • Rotary embeddings (ModernBERT): positions rotate queries and keys so dot products depend on relative offset. ModernBERT uses a larger rotary base for global layers (160,000) than for local ones (10,000), and alternates one global-attention layer with two 128-token sliding-window layers.

Tokenizers

  • WordPiece (BERT, ELECTRA) with a roughly 30k vocabulary (30,522 for the uncased BERT checkpoints, 28,996 for the cased ones), byte-level BPE (RoBERTa, BART, ModernBERT) that never produces an unknown token, and SentencePiece (ALBERT, T5, mT5, DeBERTaV3) that treats whitespace as a symbol and works without pre-tokenisation. The tokenizer is fixed at pretraining time; changing it means re-pretraining or careful embedding surgery.
  • Vocabulary size trades sequence length against parameters: a larger vocabulary shortens sequences (cheaper attention) and covers more languages but inflates the embedding table, as §07 shows.

Length

  • Most classic checkpoints were trained at 512 tokens, and attention cost grows with n². Long documents are usually chunked with a stride and the predictions merged.
  • ModernBERT was pretrained at 1,024 tokens and then extended to 8,192, with local attention and unpadding keeping the cost manageable. BART accepts 1,024; T5 was pretrained on 512-token inputs.
Takeaway: before comparing benchmark scores, check that the checkpoint's tokenizer covers your text and that its trained length covers your inputs. A model that must truncate your documents is solving a different problem.

09 Adapting them to tasks

A pretrained encoder or encoder–decoder is a starting point, not a product. The family has accumulated five standard ways to turn one into a task model, and they differ in how many weights change, how much labelled data they need, and what the output looks like.

encoder fine-tuning: a small head on the final states, everything trainedP(y | x) = softmax( W · h_[CLS] + b ) ℒ = − log P(y* | x) text-to-text fine-tuning: the label is written out as tokensℒ = − ∑ₜ log P(y*ₜ | y*₍<ₜ₎, Enc("sst2 sentence: " + x))

The five routes

  • Full fine-tuning with a task head (encoders). Add a linear layer on the [CLS] vector, on every token, or two vectors for span start and end; train everything for a few epochs at a small learning rate. The BERT paper's recipe, and still the default when a few thousand labelled examples exist.
  • Text-to-text fine-tuning (T5, BART). The task is named in a prefix — T5 used prefixes such as "translate English to German:" and "summarize:" — and the answer, even a class label, is generated as text. One model, one loss, one decoding procedure for every task.
  • Instruction tuning (Flan-T5). Fine-tune on a large mixture of tasks phrased as natural-language instructions — about 1.8k tasks for Flan-T5 — so the model follows unseen instructions zero-shot. This moved encoder–decoders toward the prompting interface of decoder LLMs.
  • Embedding models. Fine-tune an encoder contrastively on pairs (query–passage, paraphrase) so that pooled vectors are comparable by cosine similarity; the result powers dense retrieval and semantic search.
  • Compression and parameter-efficient training. Distillation (DistilBERT kept six of BERT-base's twelve layers and reported being 40% smaller and 60% faster while retaining about 97% of its language-understanding performance), and adapters or low-rank updates that train a small fraction of the weights.

10 How to choose: encoder, encoder–decoder or decoder

The choice is mostly about the shape of the output and the volume of traffic, then about length and language coverage. The rules below are practitioner guidance, not theorems; benchmark the two leading candidates on your own data before committing.

What one answer costs

FLOPs ≈ 2 × (non-embedding weights a token passes through) × tokens, plus the output projection for each generated token. The encoder produces its label in one pass, so output length does not affect it; the encoder–decoder runs the encoder once and the decoder once per output token; the decoder-only model runs every prompt and output token through the whole stack. Sizes come from the configuration spec used in §07 and, for GPT, from 12·L·d² with the published layer counts and widths. The comparison ignores accuracy: it shows the cost side of the choice only.

Then the constraints

  • Volume and latency. Millions of calls a day favour a fine-tuned encoder; the answer is one forward pass rather than a decoding loop.
  • Labelled data. With thousands of labelled examples a fine-tuned encoder usually matches or beats prompting a far larger model on a closed label set; with a handful, instruction-tuned models such as Flan-T5 or a decoder LLM are easier starting points. This is a widely reported pattern, not a guarantee.
  • Length and language. Beyond 512 tokens pick a long-context model (ModernBERT for encoding, a long-context decoder for generation); for many languages pick a multilingual vocabulary (mT5, XLM-R) rather than an English one.
  • Start modern. Among encoders, the original BERT checkpoints are a historical baseline; RoBERTa, DeBERTaV3 and ModernBERT each report higher GLUE-style scores than BERT at comparable size.
Takeaway: classification, tagging, extraction and retrieval at scale → encoder; bounded text-to-text transformation → encoder–decoder; open-ended generation → decoder. Then let length, language and volume pick the specific checkpoint.

13 Takeaways

What to remember about the family as a whole, independent of any one checkpoint.

Architecture and objective

  • Two wirings, one block. Encoder-only and encoder–decoder models use the same Transformer block; the decoder adds a causal mask and a cross-attention sublayer, about a third more weights per block at FFN width 4d.
  • The objective is the main design decision. Masked LM gives bidirectional representations with a loss on ~15% of positions; replaced-token detection puts a loss on every position; span corruption keeps targets short; denoising reconstruction teaches generation.
  • Recipe beat architecture more than once. RoBERTa (more data, longer training, no NSP) and ELECTRA (denser objective) produced large gains with BERT's architecture unchanged. ModernBERT's authors attribute its gains to decoder-era engineering and 2T training tokens, without an ablation isolating each.

Numbers and choices

  • Recount sizes from configs. Labels are rounded inconsistently — BERT-large's 340M is 335M by the config, T5-Large's 770M is 738M — and large vocabularies can make embeddings half the model.
  • Prefix LM and encoder–decoder share a visibility pattern; they differ in parameter sharing. Architecture studies disagree about which wiring is "best" because the answer depends on whether the model is used zero-shot, fine-tuned, or multitask-tuned.
  • Choose by output shape and volume. Labels, tags, spans and embeddings at scale → encoder; bounded transformations → encoder–decoder; open-ended generation → decoder. Check tokenizer coverage and trained length before benchmark scores.
Takeaway: decoder-only LLMs took over open-ended generation, but encoders and encoder–decoders remain the efficient default whenever the output is bounded and the traffic is high. Knowing the axes on which they differ is what lets you pick the right one quickly.
