T5: Transfer Study, Recipe & Results

The controlled comparison behind T5: a fixed baseline varied one factor at a time across architectures and mask patterns, denoising objectives and span lengths, pretraining corpora and repetition, fine-tuning and multi-task mixing strategies, and uses of extra compute, then the Adafactor schedule, the final recipe and the five sizes' test-set results.

14 How the systematic study was run

The study fixes a baseline — T5-Base-sized encoder–decoder, i.i.d. denoising, C4, 2¹⁹ pretraining steps, 2¹⁸ fine-tuning steps on each task — and changes one factor at a time ("coordinate ascent"). Every experiment is scored on the validation sets of GLUE, CNN/Daily Mail, SQuAD, SuperGLUE and three WMT pairs, and the paper's tables bold any score within two standard deviations of the best in that experiment.

15 Architectures: encoder–decoder, decoder-only LM and prefix LM

The study compares three attention-mask patterns — fully visible, causal, and causal with a fully visible prefix — as five models of matched parameters or compute, each pretrained with the denoising objective and with a language-modelling objective. The encoder–decoder with denoising was best on every task.

  • Bidirectional context over the input matters most. The causal LM, which sees its input only left to right, trails the prefix LM by 7.1 GLUE points with denoising; the prefix LM, which differs only in making the input fully visible, recovers most of the gap.
  • Sharing encoder and decoder weights costs little (−0.47 GLUE, −0.63 SuperGLUE) while halving parameters; halving depth instead (6 + 6) costs 2.4 GLUE points.
  • Denoising beats language modelling on GLUE for every architecture, e.g. encoder–decoder 83.28 against 79.56 (on SuperGLUE the causal LM is the exception, 56.51 with the LM objective against 55.02 with denoising).
  • Matched in what? The winning model has twice the parameters of the decoder-only one at the same compute. But even with parameters and compute both matched — the shared encoder–decoder (P, M) — it beats the prefix LM on every task, so the advantage is not only the extra parameters; halving depth (6 + 6) to match parameters at half the compute loses to the prefix LM on GLUE and SQuAD.

16 Objectives: from families down to span lengths

The objectives study narrows in four steps: pick a family (prefix LM, BERT-style denoising, deshuffling), simplify the BERT-style target, choose a corruption rate, choose a span length. The one large effect is the first — denoising beats language modelling and deshuffling — and the rest are within a point.

  • "Drop corrupted tokens" has the best GLUE (84.44) almost entirely through CoLA (60.04 against the baseline's 53.84) — plausibly because noticing missing words is close to judging grammaticality — but is worse on SuperGLUE, so the paper kept sentinels.
  • Corruption rate barely matters between 10% and 25%; 50% hurts GLUE and SQuAD and makes targets longer. The paper kept 15% partly on BERT's precedent.
  • Span length 3 is not the best in every column — it wins CNNDM and SuperGLUE, span 2 wins GLUE and SQuAD — but it and 2 both beat i.i.d. on most non-translation tasks, and longer spans mean fewer sentinels and shorter sequences.
  • The paper's conclusion: choose among denoising objectives mainly by computational cost; further tinkering with similar objectives "may not lead to significant gains".
Takeaway: the objective search found a plateau, not a peak. Span corruption at 15% with mean length 3 is a cheap point on that plateau — which is also why UL2 later looked for gains by mixing very different denoisers rather than tuning this one.

17 Pretraining data: which corpus, and how much repetition

Two experiments. The first swaps C4 for other corpora of 16 GB to 6.1 TB. The second truncates C4 so that the same 2³⁵ pretraining tokens repeat the data 64 to 4,096 times. In-domain data helps the matching tasks; heavy repetition hurts everything.

The repeat counts are simply 2³⁵ / N: 2³⁵ / 2²⁹ = 64, … , 2³⁵ / 2²³ = 4,096. Up to 64 repeats the effect is small; the training loss falls as data shrinks, consistent with memorisation. The paper cautions that larger models may overfit a small corpus sooner, and recommends large pretraining sets whenever possible — the reason the 1T-token final models stayed on C4 rather than the better-scoring but small domain corpora, which would have repeated hundreds of times.

Takeaway: clean beats raw, in-domain beats generic on the matching task, and repetition is tolerable up to tens of epochs but not thousands. These are the empirical grounds for "large, filtered, diverse" as the default pretraining corpus.

18 Transfer strategies: partial fine-tuning, multi-task mixing, and both

Three questions. Can one update fewer parameters than all of them? (Adapters and gradual unfreezing: not without some loss.) Can one train on all tasks at once instead of pretrain-then-fine-tune? (Not quite as well, whatever the mixing rates.) Can multi-task pretraining followed by fine-tuning match the baseline? (Yes — which is what the released models do.)

An apparent copy error in Table 10

In the main text's Table 10 the row "Adapter layers, d = 2048" (81.51, 16.62, 79.47, 63.03, 19.83, 27.50, 22.63) is identical, digit for digit, to the d = 128 row. The appendix's full results (Table 16) give different values for d = 2048 — GLUE 82.62, CNNDM ROUGE-2 18.30, SQuAD 79.40, SuperGLUE 68.61, EnDe 25.64, EnFr 36.92, EnRo 26.93 — which also fit the paper's own reading that high-resource tasks need large adapters (translation climbs steadily with d). This page and its figure use the appendix values.

  • Adapters (dense–ReLU–dense blocks after each feed-forward layer; only adapters and norms train): small d suffices for small tasks (SQuAD), large d is needed for large ones (WMT); none matches full fine-tuning here. Gradual unfreezing — one more layer every 2¹⁸/12 steps, from the top — costs a little everywhere.
  • Mixing rates have a sweet spot in K for most tasks (except WMT En–Fr, which always wants more), and T = 2 is the best temperature; equal mixing is worst.
  • Leave-one-out is only slightly worse than full multi-task pretraining, suggesting little task interference; supervised-only pretraining is clearly worse except on translation.

19 Scaling: what to do with 4× the compute

Given four times the baseline's compute, the study compares training longer, using bigger batches, a 2× model for 2× steps, a 4× model, and ensembles. Bigger models trained somewhat longer did best; ensembling is an orthogonal gain.

  • More steps versus bigger batches: no clear winner; both help. Size helps beyond steps: 2× size for 2× steps beats 4× steps on every task, and matches 4× size.
  • Ensembling four independently pretrained models wins CNN/DM, EnDe and EnRo outright; four fine-tunes of one pretrained model still help, more cheaply. Neither helps SuperGLUE.
  • Costs differ after training: a larger model costs more at every inference; a smaller model pretrained longer amortises its pretraining across tasks.
Takeaway: within the study's 4× budget, parameters and steps were complementary and parameters edged ahead — the basis for scaling T5 to 11B with 1T tokens. It is a snapshot at small scale; later compute-optimal scaling studies of decoder-only models revisited the trade-off with far larger sweeps.

20 The schedule: steps, tokens, the inverse-square-root rate and Adafactor

Pretraining in the study runs 2¹⁹ steps of 128 sequences of 512 tokens, packed so each batch holds about 2¹⁶ tokens — 2³⁵ ≈ 34B tokens in all, a small fraction of C4, so nothing repeats. The learning rate is 1/√max(n, k) with k = 10⁴ warm-up steps; the optimiser is Adafactor; decoding during the study is greedy.

tokens2¹⁹ steps × 2⁷ sequences × 2⁹ tokens = 2³⁵ = 34,359,738,368 ≈ 34B tokens, final models10⁶ steps × 2¹¹ sequences × 2⁹ tokens ≈ 1.07 × 10¹² ≈ 1T pretraining learning rate at step nlr(n) = 1 / √max(n, k), k = 10⁴ valueslr = 0.01 for n ≤ 10⁴, lr(2¹⁹) = 1/√524,288 = 0.00138, lr(10⁶) = 0.001 fine-tuning2¹⁸ = 262,144 steps, batch 128 × 512, constant lr = 0.001 checkpoint every 5,000 steps, best validation checkpoint per task Adafactor's factored second moment for an n × m weight matrix (row sums R, column sums C of the squared gradients)V̂ = R Cᵀ / (1ᵀ R), state: n + m numbers instead of n · m

Notes on the schedule

  • "Exponentially decays". The paper describes the post-warm-up phase as exponential decay; 1/√n is a power law — from step 10⁴ to 2¹⁹ the rate falls by √52.4 = 7.24×, not by an exponential factor. The formula, not the adjective, is what the code implements.
  • Why not a triangular schedule? It scored slightly better but needs the total step count in advance; the study varied step counts, so it used the schedule that does not.
  • BERT comparison. 34B tokens is about 1/4 of BERT's roughly 137B and 1/64 of RoBERTa's 2.2T; the final models' 1T is about 32× the baseline.
  • Adafactor (Shazeer and Stern 2018) keeps, for each weight matrix, row and column statistics of the squared gradients instead of a full second-moment matrix, and in its memory-saving configuration no momentum. The paper names only the optimiser and does not say which configuration it used; the memory saving matters most at 11B parameters (see the figure).
Learning rate over pretraining and fine-tuning, and optimiser memory change the warm-up length · pick a model for the memory comparison

The schedule is the paper's formula over 2¹⁹ pretraining steps, followed by 2¹⁸ fine-tuning steps at 0.001. Optimiser state counts are computed from the chosen config's weight shapes: Adam keeps two numbers per parameter; factored Adafactor without momentum keeps rows + columns per matrix and a full vector for each norm gain.

Takeaway: T5's recipe is simple and scale-free — a warm-up constant, then 1/√n, no knowledge of the total length needed — with an optimiser whose state for T5-11B is millions of numbers rather than tens of billions.

21 Putting it together: the recipe behind the released models

For the released T5 models the authors changed the baseline in six ways: span corruption (mean length 3) instead of i.i.d. noise; 1 million steps at 2¹¹ sequences of 512 (about 1T tokens); five sizes; multi-task pretraining; fine-tuning on individual GLUE and SuperGLUE tasks with small batches; and beam search for long outputs.

  • Multi-task pretraining. The unsupervised task was mixed with the supervised ones by examples-proportional mixing, with artificial sizes for the unlabeled data of 710,000 (Small), 2,620,000 (Base), 8,660,000 (Large), 33,500,000 (3B) and 133,000,000 (11B), and WMT En–Fr and En–De capped at 1M examples. This is why the released v1.0 checkpoints respond to translate English to German: before any fine-tuning.
  • Per-task GLUE / SuperGLUE fine-tuning with a batch of 8 sequences instead of 2¹¹ (so small tasks are not repeated within a batch) and checkpoints every 1,000 steps.
  • Decoding. Beam width 4 with length penalty 0.6 for WMT and CNN/DM; greedy elsewhere.
  • Test sets through the GLUE and SuperGLUE servers, standard CNN/DM test, newstest2014 / 2015 / 2016 for En–De / En–Fr / En–Ro; SQuAD on the validation set because the server could not run the largest models.

22 Results: the five sizes on the test sets (Table 14)

T5-11B set the state of the art on 18 of the 24 tasks the paper reports, including GLUE average 90.3, SuperGLUE 88.9 (human 89.8), SQuAD exact match 91.26 and CNN/DM ROUGE-2 21.55. It did not reach the state of the art on any WMT pair, which the authors attribute to English-only pretraining and the absence of back-translation.
