Vision Models

A vision model is a learned function from pixels to something useful — a class label, a set of boxes, a mask, or a grid of features another model can read. This hub is the note on the category: the handful of design axes that separate one vision architecture from another (how pixels are mixed, where the supervision comes from, what head sits on top, how cost grows with resolution), the lineage from AlexNet to promptable segmenters, a comparison of the landmark models with their published numbers, and guidance on which one to start from. The individual models — EfficientNet today, with DETR, Segment Anything and DINOv2 planned — each get their own page underneath it.

01 What vision models are

A vision model is a neural network that maps an image tensor of shape H × W × 3 to a task output, usually by way of a backbone that turns pixels into a feature map and a head that turns features into the answer. The landmark models in this category differ mainly in which of those two parts they changed, and in where the training signal came from.

the decomposition the models on this page followŷ = head( backbone( x ) ) x : an H × W × 3 array of pixel values backbone output — a grid of feature vectors, or a token sequenceF : an (H/s) × (W/s) × C grid s = output stride (8, 16 or 32 typically)

Backbone, neck, head

  • Backbone. The expensive, reusable part. A convolutional backbone (ResNet, EfficientNet, ConvNeXt) produces a pyramid of feature maps at strides 4 to 32; a plain Vision Transformer (ViT, DeiT, DINOv2) produces one sequence of patch tokens at a single stride equal to its patch size.
  • Neck. Optional glue that reshapes backbone features for the head — a feature pyramid network for detection, a 1×1 projection to 256 channels in Segment Anything, a flatten-plus-positional-encoding step in DETR.
  • Head. The small task-specific part: global pooling plus a linear classifier; a set of learned object queries that decode boxes; a prompt-conditioned mask decoder; or nothing at all, when the frozen features themselves are the product (DINOv2).

Why the category is organised this way

  • Between 2012 and roughly 2020 most progress came from backbones trained with ImageNet labels, and the head was a linear layer. After 2020 the backbone design converged (a Transformer or a modernised ConvNet reach similar accuracy at similar cost, as ConvNeXt showed) and the frontier moved to supervision (self-supervised and web-scale data) and to heads that make one backbone serve many tasks.
  • So each model page answers three questions: what does the backbone do to pixels, where did its labels come from, and what does its head output. The design axes section makes those questions concrete.
Takeaway: read a vision model as backbone + supervision + head. EfficientNet is a backbone-and-scaling paper, DETR a head paper, DINOv2 a supervision paper, and Segment Anything a head-plus-data-engine paper; Vision Transformers changed the backbone's basic operation.

02 The design axes that distinguish vision models

Five choices account for most of the difference between the models in this category. Each is a trade between built-in assumptions (inductive bias) and the amount of data or compute needed to learn those assumptions instead.

Inductive bias, stated carefully

  • A convolution applies the same small kernel at every position, so a shifted input gives a shifted feature map (translation equivariance, exact up to stride and border effects) and each output depends only on a local neighbourhood. A ViT has neither property built in beyond the patch embedding; it must learn locality from data.
  • The ViT paper reported that on ImageNet-1k alone its models trailed comparable ResNets, and overtook them only when pretrained on ImageNet-21k or JFT-300M. DeiT then closed most of the ImageNet-1k gap with augmentation, regularisation and distillation, so "Transformers need huge data" is better read as "Transformers need more help from data or recipe than ConvNets do" — a statement about the regime tested, not a law.
Takeaway: when two vision models are compared, first ask which axis differs. Many headline gains are really a change of supervision or training recipe, not of architecture — ConvNeXt's own ablation modernised a ResNet step by step, starting with the training recipe alone, which lifted ResNet-50 from 76.1% to 78.8%.

03 Convolution versus attention, counted in FLOPs

Convolutions cost linearly in the number of pixels. Global self-attention costs linearly in pixels for its projections and MLP, but quadratically for the attention matrix itself. That single fact explains patch sizes, windowed attention, and why Segment Anything's encoder dominates its runtime.

patch tokens for an H × W image with patch size PN = floor(H/P) · floor(W/P) (+1 if a class token is used) multiply-adds per Transformer layer, width d, MLP ratio 4projections 4·N·d² + attention 2·N²·d + MLP 8·N·d² windowed attention with w × w windows over a G × G gridattention 2·N·w²·d (linear in N for fixed w) a k × k convolution, C_in → C_out, on an H′ × W′ mapMACs = H′·W′·k²·C_in·C_out (linear in pixels)

Figure: every number is computed from the formulas above with a class token included and the patch-embedding projection added. At ViT-B, P = 16, 224² the total comes to about 17.6 G multiply-adds, which is the "17.6 GFLOPs" printed for DeiT-B and ViT-B/16 in the DeiT and ConvNeXt tables — those tables count multiply-adds as FLOPs.

What the arithmetic explains

  • Why patches are 14–16 pixels. Per-pixel tokens at 224² would give N = 50,176 and an attention matrix of 2.5 billion entries per head per layer. A 16-pixel patch cuts N by 256 and the attention term by 65,536.
  • Why the attention term stays small at 224². With N = 197 and d = 768, 2·N²·d is about 60 M multiply-adds per layer against about 1.4 G for projections and MLP — roughly 4%. Doubling resolution quadruples N and multiplies the attention term by 16, so at 1024² with global attention it becomes a large share.
  • Why Swin and SAM use windows. Restricting attention to fixed w × w windows makes its cost linear in N; Swin adds shifted windows between layers so information crosses window borders, SAM adds four global layers spaced through the encoder.
  • Why ConvNets remain competitive at high resolution. A ConvNet's cost grows as r² with no quadratic term, which is part of why EfficientNet could use 600² inputs for B7 at 37 B FLOPs.
Takeaway: projections and MLP dominate a ViT at ImageNet resolution; attention dominates mainly when tokens become numerous. Windowing and hierarchical downsampling are engineering responses to the N² term, not changes to what attention computes.

04 Where the training signal comes from

The same ViT-L architecture can be a mediocre ImageNet classifier or a state-of-the-art general-purpose feature extractor, depending entirely on what it was trained on and with which objective. Supervision is the axis along which vision models changed most after 2020.

Five regimes, in rough historical order

What each regime buys

  • Class labels teach the classes. An ImageNet-supervised backbone learns what separates 1,000 categories; features irrelevant to those labels (exact geometry, texture of unlabelled parts) can be discarded. Fine-tuning usually recovers what a new task needs, but a frozen linear probe exposes the gap.
  • Self-supervision keeps more of the image. DINOv2's frozen ViT-g/14 features reach 86.5% ImageNet-1k linear-probe accuracy and 49.0 mIoU on ADE20k with a linear head, without any label during pretraining. The claim is that its features are general, and the evidence is that one frozen backbone works across classification, segmentation and depth.
  • Data engines turn a model into an annotator. SAM's three stages collected 4.3 M masks from 120 k images (assisted-manual), added 5.9 M masks from 180 k images (semi-automatic), then generated 1.1 B masks fully automatically; 99.1% of SA-1B's masks came from that last stage.
Takeaway: when two models share an architecture, their supervision is usually the bigger difference. A ViT-L trained on ImageNet-1k, on ImageNet-21k and self-supervised on LVD-142M are three different tools.

05 Task heads: what sits on top of the backbone

The head decides what question the model answers. Four head designs cover the models in this hub, and they differ in how many outputs they produce and how those outputs are matched to ground truth during training.

classification — pool, then one linear layerŷ = softmax( W · pool(F) + b ) W : K × C set prediction (DETR) — best one-to-one assignment σ̂ over N = 100 queriesσ̂ = argmin over permutations σ of ∑ᵢ ℒ_match( yᵢ , ŷ_σ(i) ) promptable mask decoding (SAM) — image embedding reused across promptsmasks, IoU scores = decoder( E_img , encode(prompt) ) E_img : 64 × 64 × 256

The four heads

  • Linear classifier. Global average pooling (ConvNets) or the class token (ViT, DeiT) followed by one fully connected layer. It is so small that the backbone is effectively the whole model; EfficientNet-B0's classifier is 1,280 × 1,000 weights plus biases, about 1.28 M of its 5.3 M parameters.
  • Set prediction with object queries. DETR replaces anchors and non-maximum suppression with a Transformer decoder that turns 100 learned query vectors into 100 (class, box) predictions, most of them "no object". Training finds the minimum-cost one-to-one matching between predictions and ground-truth boxes with the Hungarian algorithm, then applies classification and box losses to the matched pairs only.
  • Prompt-conditioned mask decoder. SAM's decoder is two Transformer layers that cross-attend between prompt tokens (points, boxes, a mask) and the 64 × 64 image embedding, then upsample and output three candidate masks with predicted IoU scores, so an ambiguous click ("the shirt or the person?") returns several valid answers. Given a precomputed embedding it runs in about 50 ms in a web browser on CPU.
  • No head: frozen dense features. DINOv2 is released as a backbone. Users attach a linear layer, a k-NN classifier, or a small decoder, and the backbone stays frozen; the paper's evaluations are largely of this form.

Why the head matters for cost

  • DETR's set prediction removed hand-designed components but converged slowly: its reported COCO models trained for 500 epochs. Deformable DETR reported better accuracy, especially on small objects, with 10× fewer training epochs by attending to a small set of sampled points instead of the whole feature map.
  • SAM's split puts nearly all computation in the image encoder, run once per image, and almost none in the decoder, run once per prompt — the design that makes interactive use feasible.
Takeaway: heads range from one matrix (classification) to a small Transformer (DETR, SAM). The trend is toward heads that let one expensive backbone answer many questions — many objects, many prompts, many downstream tasks.

Training recipes differ (EfficientNet used AutoAugment; DeiT, Swin and ConvNeXt a heavier 300-epoch recipe), and FLOPs are an imperfect proxy for latency — depthwise convolutions have low arithmetic intensity and tend to run slower per FLOP on accelerators than dense ones, a point the follow-up EfficientNetV2 paper (2104.00298) makes explicitly about early depthwise stages.

Number conflicts worth knowing

  • ResNet-50 appears as 76.0% (EfficientNet), 76.1% (ConvNeXt) and 76.2% (DeiT); FLOPs as 3.8 × 10⁹ (ResNet paper) and 4.1 G (later tables). These are different weights and counting conventions, not errors.
  • DeiT-B is 86 M parameters in the DeiT paper and 87 M in the ConvNeXt table; ConvNeXt-T is 29 M in its own table and often quoted as 28 M.
  • DETR's AP is on COCO val with 500-epoch training; the paper's ablations use a shorter 300-epoch schedule and score lower, so check the schedule before comparing.
Takeaway: at equal ImageNet-1k training, the 2021–2022 generation (Swin, ConvNeXt) sits on nearly the same frontier; the large jumps in the classification column come from extra data (ViT-H, Swin-L, ConvNeXt-XL) or from a different protocol (DINOv2's frozen probe).

08 How the members scale

Each family has a rule for making a bigger model, and the rules are different: EfficientNet scales three dimensions together, ViT variants scale width and depth at a fixed patch size, DINOv2 trains one giant model and distils it downward, and SAM scales the encoder while keeping the decoder fixed.

EfficientNet compound scaling, one knob φdepth d = αᵠ width w = βᵠ resolution r = γᵠ the constraint, so FLOPs roughly double per unit of φα · β² · γ² ≈ 2 α = 1.2, β = 1.1, γ = 1.15 Transformer parameters, ignoring embeddings and biasesparams ≈ 12 · L · d² ViT-B: 12·12·768² ≈ 85 M

Four scaling rules

  • Compound scaling (EfficientNet). B0 → B7 grows parameters from 5.3 M to 66 M and FLOPs from 0.39 B to 37 B, while input resolution grows from 224 to 600. The derivation, the grid search that chose α, β and γ, and an exact recount of B0 are on the EfficientNet page.
  • Width and depth at fixed patch size (ViT, DeiT). Base (L = 12, d = 768), Large (24, 1024) and Huge (32, 1280) give 86 M, 307 M and 632 M parameters. Changing the patch size changes cost, not the Transformer's parameters, which is why ViT-L/16 and ViT-L/32 differ in size only through the patch projection and the position-embedding table.
  • Train big, distil down (DINOv2). Only ViT-g/14 (1.1 B parameters, 40 layers, d = 1536, 24 heads) is trained from scratch; S, B and L are distilled from it with the same loop. ViT-g/14's training used 22,016 GPU-hours by the paper's estimate.
  • Scale the encoder, freeze the interface (SAM). The released checkpoints use ViT-B, ViT-L and ViT-H encoders with the same prompt encoder and decoder. The paper reports ViT-H improving substantially over ViT-B but only marginally over ViT-L.

Resolution as a scaling axis

  • Fine-tuning at a higher resolution than pretraining is a common, cheap accuracy gain: DeiT-B goes from 81.8% at 224² to 83.1% at 384²; Swin-B from 83.5% to 84.5%; ConvNeXt-B from 83.8% to 85.1%.
  • For a ViT, raising resolution at fixed patch size adds tokens; learned position embeddings are interpolated to the new grid. The cost calculator in §03 shows the price.
Takeaway: "bigger" means different things per family. When a paper reports a gain from scale, check which of depth, width, resolution, data or teacher it scaled.

10 How to choose between them

Start from the task's output, then the compute budget, then how much labelled data exists. The architecture question usually comes last, because at matched budgets the modern ConvNet and Transformer families perform similarly.

Rules of thumb, with their status

  • "Fine-tune a pretrained backbone rather than train from scratch." Well supported across the transfer-learning literature when the target set is small; the gain shrinks as target data grows.
  • "Transformers beat ConvNets." Folklore. ConvNeXt's equal-FLOP comparison with Swin shows the gap can close with a modernised design and the same recipe; which wins depends on data scale, resolution and hardware.
  • "Frozen self-supervised features are as good as fine-tuning." Partly supported: DINOv2's linear probes are strong, but full fine-tuning typically still gains on the target task when labels are plentiful.
  • "Resolution is free accuracy." Fine-tuning at a higher resolution usually helps (+1.0 to +1.3 points in the §08 examples) but costs r² for ConvNets and more for global attention.
Takeaway: choose by output first (label, boxes, masks, features), then by budget and label supply. The backbone family is a secondary decision with a smaller effect than recipe and pretraining data.

Why this order

  • EfficientNet is the ConvNet reference point and the cleanest example of scaling arithmetic. Vision Transformers then introduce patch tokens, which DINOv2, DETR's encoder and SAM's encoder all build on.

What this hub does own

  • The comparison across vision models: which design axis each one moved, their published sizes, costs and results side by side, and how the numbers conflict between papers.
  • The cost arithmetic that decides between convolution, global attention and windowed attention at a given resolution.
  • Guidance on choosing a starting model by task output, compute budget and label supply.

Boundary cases

  • CLIP has a vision encoder but is trained jointly with a text encoder, so it lives under Multimodal Models; its image tower is often used as a vision backbone, and that use is noted there.
  • Document models such as Donut use vision encoders (Swin) on page images; they live under Document AI Models.
  • Diffusion image generators are generative models rather than recognition models and live on Diffusion Models.

13 Takeaways

The category in a dozen lines: what varies, what the numbers say, and where the frontier moved.

What varies

  • Backbone + supervision + head. Each model here is best read as a choice on each of the three; most landmark papers changed one of them.
  • Convolution versus attention is a cost-and-bias trade. Convolutions build in locality and scale as r²; global attention learns its mixing and adds an N² term that dominates mainly at high token counts.

What the numbers say

  • The arithmetic is checkable. ViT-B/16 at 224² comes to about 17.6 G multiply-adds from 4·N·d² + 2·N²·d + 8·N·d² per layer, matching the published tables.
  • At equal data and recipe, the modern families are close. Swin-T 81.3% and ConvNeXt-T 82.1% at 4.5 G FLOPs; the large jumps come from extra pretraining data.
  • Supervision moved the frontier after 2020. JFT-300M lifted ViT-H/14 to 88.55%; DINOv2 reached 86.5% with a frozen probe and no labels; SAM's data engine produced 1.1 B masks.
  • Heads made backbones general. DETR's queries answer "how many objects", SAM's decoder answers "which object for this prompt", DINOv2 leaves the head to the user.

How to use them

  • Match the protocol first. Single-crop, multi-crop, extra-data fine-tuning and frozen probes are different measurements; COCO AP averages ten IoU thresholds.
  • Numbers conflict across papers for the same model (ResNet-50 at 76.0–76.2%, DeiT-B at 86–87 M); quote the source and its protocol.
  • Choose by output, then budget, then labels; the backbone family is usually the smallest of the three effects.
Takeaway: vision models went from task-specific classifiers to general visual front ends. Knowing which axis a model moved — and checking its numbers against the arithmetic and the protocol — is more durable than memorising the leaderboard.
