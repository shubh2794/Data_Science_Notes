Whisper — speech recognition from 680,000 hours of weak supervision

Whisper is OpenAI's family of encoder–decoder Transformers for speech: a log-mel spectrogram of 30 seconds of 16 kHz audio goes into a Transformer encoder, and a text decoder, steered by a handful of special tokens, writes a transcript, an English translation, a language tag or timestamps. Its distinguishing choice is not the architecture — the paper deliberately uses an off-the-shelf one — but the training signal: 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts found on the internet, filtered by heuristics and used without any fine-tuning on the benchmarks it is evaluated on. This note covers it in detail: the problem weak supervision addresses, the data and its filtering, the audio front end and its frame arithmetic, the encoder and decoder of every released size with parameter counts recomputed from the released configurations, the multitask token format and timestamp tokens, training, evaluation and the text normaliser, the zero-shot and robustness results, long-form decoding with temperature fallback, large-v2, large-v3 and turbo, Distil-Whisper, hallucination and other limitations, use through Hugging Face Transformers, and the places where the paper and the released code disagree.

01 What Whisper is

Whisper ("Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision", arXiv 2212.04356, December 2022) is a sequence-to-sequence Transformer that maps a 30-second audio window x to a token sequence y. The encoder turns a log-mel spectrogram into 1,500 vectors; the decoder is an audio-conditioned language model that generates y one token at a time, reading the encoder output through cross-attention. Transcription, translation into English, language identification, voice-activity detection and timestamp prediction are all the same loss — next-token cross-entropy — with the task chosen by special tokens at the start of y.

encoder: a 30-s window becomes 1,500 vectors of width dh = Enc(LogMel(x)), LogMel(x) ∈ ℝ^(80 × 3000) (128 × 3000 for large-v3), h ∈ ℝ^(1500 × d) training objective: cross-entropy on every target token except the previous-text contextL(θ) = − Σ over t ∉ prev of log p_θ(yₜ | y₁ … yₜ₋₁, h) inference: fix the task prefix, then decode until the end tokeny = <|startoftranscript|> <|lang|> <|task|> [<|notimestamps|>] → ŷₜ = argmax p_θ(v | prefix, ŷ<ₜ, h) … <|endoftext|>

What goes in and what comes out

  • Input: mono audio resampled to 16 kHz, padded or trimmed to exactly 30 s (480,000 samples), turned into an 80-bin (large-v3: 128-bin) log-mel spectrogram of 3,000 frames.
  • Output: text tokens from a byte-level BPE vocabulary, interleaved with timestamp tokens on a 20 ms grid when timestamps are requested; the decoder's context is 448 tokens.
  • Training: 680,000 hours of (audio, transcript) pairs collected from the internet — 64% English transcription, 18% translation into English, 17% transcription in other languages (the paper's figure labels them 65 / 18 / 17%) — for 2²⁰ updates of 256 segments.
  • Evaluation: zero-shot — no model is fine-tuned on any benchmark's training split — with a text normaliser applied to both reference and output before word error rate is computed.

30 Pitfalls

Most Whisper problems produce fluent, plausible text rather than errors, which is what makes them expensive.

Input

  • Wrong sample rate. Audio at 44.1 or 48 kHz passed as 16 kHz shifts every frequency; the output degrades without any warning. Resample first.
  • Silent truncation at 30 s. The short-form path cuts longer audio. Use the long-form path or the pipeline for anything longer.
  • Mismatched feature extractor. 80-bin features into large-v3 or turbo (128 bins), or the reverse, fail or mislead; load the processor from the same checkpoint.
  • Long silences and music invite hallucination; run voice-activity detection or keep the no-speech rule on.

Decoding

  • Assuming the defaults are the paper's. The paper's long-form numbers use beam 5, fallback, the no-speech rule, previous-text conditioning and the initial timestamp constraint; library defaults differ.
  • Reusing thresholds across implementations. 2.4 is for text compression in the original code; Transformers' ratio is on token bytes (1.35 suggested).
  • Previous-text conditioning as a hazard. It helps on average, but a hallucinated or looping window becomes the prompt for the next; if loops cascade, turn it off.
  • Translation with turbo returns the source language; use large-v2/v3 or medium.
  • Hard-coded special-token ids are off by one between large-v2 and large-v3.

Evaluation

  • Comparing WERs with different normalisers. Normalisation alone can move WER by up to half on some datasets.
  • Reading "55.2%" as a mean-WER reduction. It is the mean of per-dataset relative reductions against one LibriSpeech-only baseline.
  • Trusting timestamps as alignments. Segment times are predicted on a 20 ms grid by a language model; for precise alignment use a forced aligner.
  • Averages hide catastrophes. Report the rate of hallucinated or looping windows, not only corpus WER.
Takeaway: write down the exact pipeline — resampling, feature extractor, decoding options, normaliser — and keep it identical between evaluation and production.

32 Takeaways

Whisper showed that scaling ordinary supervised training on noisy web transcripts could produce a speech recogniser that works zero-shot across domains, languages and tasks, and that the decoding algorithm around a model matters as much as the model for long audio.

What to remember

  • Data: 680k hours of weakly supervised pairs, filtered for machine-generated text and language mismatch; English-heavy with a steep multilingual tail; accuracy per language follows hours per language.
  • Model: an off-the-shelf encoder–decoder with a fixed 30-s log-mel input; half the weights of a large model are decoder.
  • Interface: tasks, languages, silence and timestamps are tokens; the decoder's prefix selects the job.
  • Evaluation: zero-shot, normalised WER; the gain is effective robustness, not in-distribution accuracy.
  • Long audio: sequential windows moved by timestamps, protected by beam search, temperature fallback, a compression-ratio check and a no-speech rule — workarounds the paper itself calls for replacing.
  • Descendants: large-v3 (128 mels, pseudo-labels), turbo (4-layer decoder), Distil-Whisper (2-layer distilled decoder, speculative decoding).
  • Risks: fluent hallucination on non-speech, repetition, uneven accuracy across languages and accents.
Takeaway: Whisper is the reference design for weakly supervised speech recognition. Read the Audio Models hub for how it compares with self-supervised encoders and CTC, Speech Processing for the signal background, and GPT for the decoder recipe it borrows.
