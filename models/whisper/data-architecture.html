02 The problem: robust recognition without fine-tuning

In 2022 the strongest speech recognisers were self-supervised encoders such as wav2vec 2.0, pretrained on unlabelled audio and then fine-tuned on a labelled set such as LibriSpeech. The Whisper paper argues that this recipe has a structural weakness: the encoder is good, but there is no equally good pretrained decoder, so every deployment needs a supervised fine-tuning stage — and fine-tuning on one dataset teaches the model that dataset's quirks.

The argument, as the paper makes it

  • Fine-tuning finds dataset-specific patterns. The paper cites a vision result — fine-tuning a model on ImageNet raised ImageNet accuracy by 9.2% without improving average accuracy on seven other datasets of the same objects — and argues that speech models "superhuman" on LibriSpeech are similarly brittle elsewhere.
  • Supervised data was scarce. SpeechStew mixed seven existing datasets for 5,140 hours; weakly supervised efforts had reached 10,000 hours (GigaSpeech) and 30,000 hours (The People's Speech). Self-supervised pretraining had used about 1,000,000 hours of unlabelled audio.
  • The bet: relax label quality to reach label quantity. Use transcripts that already accompany audio on the internet, filter the worst of them, and train on 680,000 hours — enough that a model can be evaluated zero-shot on every benchmark.

What "weak supervision" means here

The labels are real transcripts written for the audio — captions and subtitles, some by professionals, some by amateurs, some by other recognisers — with no guarantee of accuracy, completeness, alignment or style. They are "weak" in quality but "supervised" in kind: every training example is an (audio, text) pair, so the model learns the whole mapping to formatted text, including punctuation and capitalisation, rather than only an acoustic representation. The paper also trains to predict raw transcript text "without any significant standardization", which removes the need for a separate inverse text normalisation step.

Takeaway: Whisper trades the clean-but-small supervised set for a noisy-but-huge one and moves the burden of quality from annotation to filtering. The measurement that matters is then not accuracy on one test set but robustness across many sets the model never trained on.

03 The 680,000 hours

The dataset has three components: English audio with English transcripts, non-English audio with transcripts in the same language, and non-English audio with English transcripts, which becomes speech-translation data. Appendix E of the paper gives the hours per language; the sum is 681,070 hours, which the paper rounds to 680,000.

What the training set is made of composition and scale, or hours per language

Hours are from the paper's Appendix E (English transcription 438,218; translation 125,739; non-English transcription 117,113) and from its introduction (SpeechStew 5,140; weakly supervised sets of 10,000 and 30,000 hours). Percentages, ratios and medians are computed from these. The per-language view lists the first 40 transcription languages and first 30 translation languages of the appendix figure; Welsh is marked because the paper found most of its "translation" data was English audio misidentified as Welsh.

What the numbers say

  • English-heavy. Nearly two thirds is English transcription — 64.3% by the listed hours, labelled 65% in the paper's figure and the model card. The paper attributes this to data collection that "sourced primarily from English-centric parts of the internet" and notes that most languages have under 1,000 hours of transcription data.
  • A steep long tail. Chinese has 23,446 hours of transcription data; about 25 languages in the appendix figure have fewer than 10. Performance per language tracks these hours closely (multilingual results).
  • Translation data is a by-product of a filter. It exists because English transcripts of non-English audio were kept rather than discarded (filtering), which is also why it inherits the language detector's errors: Welsh appears with 8,263 hours of translation data in the appendix figure (the text says "9,000") but only 73 hours of transcription, and inspection showed most of it was English audio.
  • How many languages. The tokenizer has 99 language tokens; the paper says the transcription data covers "96 other languages" besides English, and elsewhere that there is speech-recognition training data "in 75 languages"; the released model card says the non-English data covers 98 languages. The counts differ by what is counted (any data at all, enough data to evaluate, transcription versus translation) and are not reconciled in the sources.
Takeaway: "680,000 hours" is really 438,000 hours of English plus a multilingual long tail. Whisper's multilingual ability is real but unevenly distributed, and the distribution is set by the data, not the architecture.

04 Filtering the web: the data pipeline

The paper describes a "minimalist" approach to preprocessing — no transcript standardisation — but several automated filters on transcript quality, plus one manual pass guided by a first model. Diversity in audio quality is welcome; diversity in transcript quality is not.

The steps, in the order the paper gives them

  1. Remove machine-generated transcripts. Training on another recogniser's output would teach its errors ("transcript-ese"). Heuristics look for the fingerprints of ASR output: missing complex punctuation (commas, exclamation and question marks), missing formatting whitespace, all-upper-case or all-lower-case text. The paper notes that simple rule-based inverse text normalisation is still detectable, for instance by never containing commas.
  2. Check the spoken language. An audio language detector — a prototype Whisper model trained on a prototype of the dataset and fine-tuned on VoxLingua107 — must agree with the transcript's language according to CLD2. Mismatched pairs are dropped as transcription data, except when the transcript is English: those become X → English translation examples.
  3. De-duplicate. Fuzzy de-duplication of transcript texts reduces duplicated and automatically generated content.
  4. Segment. Audio files are cut into 30-second segments, each paired with the part of the transcript that falls inside it. Segments without speech are kept, sub-sampled (by a factor of 10 according to the hyperparameter table), and become voice-activity-detection training data.
  5. Model-guided audit. After training an initial model, error rates were aggregated per data source and sources were inspected manually, sorted by a combination of high error rate and size. This found partially transcribed, poorly aligned and misaligned transcripts and machine-generated captions the heuristics had missed.
  6. De-contaminate. Transcript-level de-duplication against the evaluation sets thought most at risk of overlap, namely TED-LIUM 3.

A late fix: speaker names

Early models tended to "transcribe" plausible but almost always wrong guesses of the speaker's name, because many transcripts include the name of the person speaking, which is rarely inferable from 30 seconds of audio. The fix was to fine-tune the models briefly on the subset of transcripts without speaker annotations.

Takeaway: the filters target the label, not the audio: remove text another model wrote, text in the wrong language, duplicates and misalignments. What remains is still noisy — which is why the decoding heuristics later on exist — but noisy in ways that average out rather than systematically teach the wrong thing.

05 Architecture at a glance

The paper chose "an off-the-shelf architecture to avoid confounding our findings with model improvements": a standard encoder–decoder Transformer. Encoder and decoder have the same width and the same number of blocks in every original size; only later derivatives (turbo, Distil-Whisper) cut the decoder.

encoderX₀ = GELU(Conv₂(GELU(Conv₁(M)))) + PE_sin, Xₗ = Block(Xₗ₋₁), H = LN(X_Le) M ∈ ℝ^(mels × 3000), H ∈ ℝ^(1500 × d) each pre-LN block (encoder has no cross-attention)x ← x + SelfAttn(LN(x)); [decoder: x ← x + CrossAttn(LN(x), H)]; x ← x + MLP(LN(x)), MLP = Linear(4d → d) ∘ GELU ∘ Linear(d → 4d) decoder output, tied to the token embedding Ep(yₜ | y<ₜ, H) = softmax( LN(zₜ) · Eᵀ ), E ∈ ℝ^(V × d)

Layer counts, widths, heads, mel bins and vocabulary sizes are those of each checkpoint's config.json on the Hugging Face Hub; the parameter total and the cache sizes are computed from them (cross-attention keeps a key and a value of width d for each of the 1,500 encoder positions in every decoder layer).

Takeaway: nothing in the network is specific to speech except the first two convolutions and the spectrogram in front of them. Everything Whisper knows about recognition, language, punctuation and translation lives in ordinary Transformer weights trained on its data.

06 The audio front end: from samples to a log-mel spectrogram

All audio is resampled to 16,000 Hz and converted to a log-mel spectrogram on 25-millisecond windows with a 10-millisecond stride. The paper describes it in two sentences; the released log_mel_spectrogram function pins down every detail, including a dynamic-range floor the paper does not mention.

short-time Fourier transform: Hann window of N = 400 samples (25 ms), hop 160 (10 ms), centred frames, last frame droppedS[f, t] = | Σₙ w[n] · x[160t + n − 200] · e^(−2πi f n / 400) |², f = 0 … 200 (bins every 40 Hz) mel projection (Slaney-scale triangular filters, area-normalised) and logL[m, t] = log₁₀( max( Σ_f W[m, f] · S[f, t], 10⁻¹⁰ ) ), m = 1 … 80 (large-v3: 128) dynamic-range floor (80 dB below the loudest cell of this input), then fixed rescaleL ← max(L, max(L) − 8), input = (L + 4) / 4

What each step does

  • Power, not magnitude. The released code squares the STFT magnitude before the mel projection, so the spectrogram is a power spectrogram (the paper's text says "log-magnitude"). On a log scale the two differ by a factor of 2, which the fixed rescale then absorbs; it matters only if you reimplement the front end from the paper.
  • The mel filterbank is stored in the repository (mel_filters.npz) and was generated with librosa.filters.mel(sr=16000, n_fft=400, n_mels=80 or 128): Slaney's scale (linear below 1 kHz, logarithmic above) with filters normalised to equal area.
  • The floor max(L, max(L) − 8) clips everything more than 8 decades (80 dB of power) below the loudest cell of the current input, so digital silence and very quiet noise map to the same value. The subsequent (L + 4) / 4 is a fixed affine map; together they implement the paper's "globally scale the input to be between −1 and 1 with approximately zero mean across the pre-training dataset" for typical inputs, but the floor is per-input, not global.
  • 128 mel bins in large-v3. With a 400-point FFT the frequency bins are 40 Hz apart, while 128 mel bands put their centres about 23 Hz apart below 1 kHz, so many low filters see only one or two FFT bins — dozens of them, as the figure's readout counts, against a handful with 80 bands.
The log-mel spectrogram, computed here pick a test signal · switch 80 / 128 bins · toggle the floor

The signals are synthesised at 16 kHz (the vowels from harmonic series shaped by textbook formant frequencies). Every step follows log_mel_spectrogram in the released code: reflect-padded centred frames, a periodic Hann window, a 400-point DFT, squared magnitude, the last frame dropped, Slaney mel filters built with the same formulas librosa uses, log10 with a 10⁻¹⁰ floor, the max − 8 floor and (x + 4)/4. Turn the floor off on the "silence, then a tone" signal to see why it exists: the silent half otherwise sits many decades below the tone and dominates the input range.

Takeaway: Whisper's front end is fixed signal processing, not learned — 100 frames per second of 80 (or 128) log-energies. Feed audio at any other sample rate without resampling and every filter lands on the wrong frequencies; the model has no way to notice.

07 Thirty-second windows and the convolution stem

Whisper's encoder has a fixed input length. Every input is padded with zeros or truncated to 30 seconds — 480,000 samples, 3,000 mel frames — and the stem's second convolution halves that to 1,500 positions, one per 20 milliseconds. The encoder's sinusoidal position table has exactly 1,500 rows, and the released encoder asserts that its input has exactly that shape.

frame and position counts for one windowframes = 480,000 / 160 = 3,000 conv₁ (k 3, stride 1, pad 1): 3,000 conv₂ (k 3, stride 2, pad 1): ⌊(3,000 + 2 − 3)/2⌋ + 1 = 1,500 time per position and positions per second320 samples = 20 ms per encoder position → 50 positions per second

Counts are computed from the constants in whisper/audio.py (16 kHz, n_fft 400, hop 160, 30-s chunks) and the convolution length formula ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1. The receptive field of one encoder position through the stem is computed from the two kernels and strides and converted to samples with the STFT window. The long-form count assumes each window advances a full 30 s; the real sequential decoder usually advances less (long-form).

Consequences

  • Short clips pay for 30 seconds. A 3-second command costs the same encoder pass as 30 seconds of speech: 90% of the positions are padding. The model was trained on padded segments, so this is correct, just wasteful; alternatives that trim the encoder input exist in third-party ports but change what the model sees.
  • Long clips are silently truncated by the default short-form path: the Hugging Face feature extractor truncates to 30 s unless told not to (truncation=False), and the low-level pad_or_trim in the original code does the same.
  • 20 ms is the native time resolution, which is why timestamp tokens are quantised to 20 ms (timestamps).
Takeaway: the 30-second window is a hard architectural fact, not a default. Everything about long audio — the sequential algorithm, chunking, seeking by timestamps — follows from it.

08 The encoder: stem, sinusoidal positions, pre-LN blocks

The encoder is a plain bidirectional Transformer over 1,500 positions. Two details distinguish it from a textbook one: a convolutional stem that sees a few frames of local context before attention, and fixed sinusoidal position embeddings whose layout differs from the original Transformer's.

Whisper's sinusoidal table (released code): first half sines, second half cosines, geometric timescales from 1 to 10,000PE[p, j] = sin(p · s_j), PE[p, d/2 + j] = cos(p · s_j), s_j = exp(−j · ln(10,000) / (d/2 − 1)), j = 0 … d/2 − 1 per-block cost on n = 1,500 positions (multiply–accumulates)projections 4nd² + attention scores and mixing 2n²d + MLP 8nd²
  • Stem. Conv1d(mels → d, k=3, pad=1), GELU, Conv1d(d → d, k=3, stride=2, pad=1), GELU. It mixes adjacent frames and halves the sequence, so attention runs on 1,500 rather than 3,000 positions — a four-fold saving in the n² term.
  • Positions. The sinusoid table is concatenated (sin block, then cos block) rather than interleaved, and its frequencies are spaced over d/2 − 1 steps. It is a fixed buffer in the original code; Hugging Face stores it as a frozen embedding weight, which is why the Hub parameter totals include 1,500 × d for it.
  • Blocks. Pre-activation residual blocks (LayerNorm before each sub-layer) with multi-head self-attention and a 4d MLP with GELU; in the released attention module the key projection has no bias. A final LayerNorm is applied to the encoder output.
  • No masking, no streaming. Every position attends to every other position in the window, including padding: the encoder takes no attention mask, and the model learned during training what padded silence looks like.
Takeaway: the encoder is deliberately ordinary. Because it always sees exactly 1,500 positions, its cost per window is fixed and its attention matrix is always 1,500 × 1,500, whatever the speech content.

09 The decoder: an audio-conditioned language model

The decoder is a GPT-2-style causal Transformer with one addition per block — cross-attention to the encoder output. It uses learned position embeddings for up to 448 tokens and ties its output layer to its input token embedding. The paper's limitations section suspects that much of Whisper's robustness comes from this "strong decoder", while noting that it is "currently unclear" how much comes from the encoder, the decoder or both.

one decoder block for the prefix y₁ … yₜz ← z + SelfAttn_causal(LN(z)); z ← z + CrossAttn(q = LN(z), k = v = H); z ← z + MLP(LN(z)) caches during generation (per decoder layer)cross-attention: K, V ∈ ℝ^(1500 × d) computed once per window; self-attention: K, V ∈ ℝ^(t × d), growing by one row per token
  • Context of 448 tokens, split in practice: the released decoding code allows up to 223 tokens of previous text (n_ctx / 2 − 1) and samples at most 224 new tokens (n_ctx / 2) per window.
  • Tied embeddings. The logits are the final hidden state times the token embedding matrix, so the V × d embedding — 66.4M parameters in large-v3 — is counted once.
  • A language model inside. Because it is trained on text continuations conditioned on audio, the decoder learns spelling, punctuation, casing and plausible word sequences. That is what lets it produce readable transcripts without a separate language model, and also what lets it produce fluent text that was never spoken (hallucination).
  • Cross-attention heads as an aligner. The released code identifies, per checkpoint, a set of "alignment heads" whose cross-attention weights track the audio position of the token being generated; word-level timestamps are obtained by dynamic time warping over those weights (word_timestamps=True).
Takeaway: the decoder is the part that makes Whisper a multitask system and the part that makes it expensive: each output token is a full pass through all decoder layers, one after another.
