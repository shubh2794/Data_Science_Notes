18 Measuring it: word error rate and the text normaliser

Word error rate is the word-level edit distance between hypothesis and reference divided by the reference length. A zero-shot model is heavily penalised for style it never saw — "you're" versus "you are", "$68 million" versus "sixty eight million dollars" — so the paper normalises both texts before scoring, and released the normaliser.

word error rate after alignment (S substitutions, D deletions, I insertions, N reference words)WER = (S + D + I) / N (can exceed 100% when the hypothesis has many insertions) relative error reduction used throughout the paperRER = 1 − WER_B / WER_A

The English normaliser (paper Appendix C), in order

  1. Remove phrases between brackets [ ] and between parentheses ( ); remove the fillers hmm, mm, mhm, mmm, uh, um.
  2. Remove whitespace before an apostrophe; expand standard and informal contractions.
  3. Remove commas between digits and periods not followed by numbers; remove symbols and diacritics (Unicode categories M, S, P) except those needed for numbers.
  4. Convert spelled-out numbers and currencies to Arabic numerals ("Ten thousand dollars" → "$10000"); convert British spellings to American; collapse whitespace.

For other languages a basic normaliser removes bracketed text, replaces marks, symbols and punctuation with spaces, lower-cases, and collapses whitespace; for Chinese, Japanese, Thai, Lao and Burmese a space is put between every character, so their "WER" is effectively a character error rate.

The "English normaliser" option implements a subset of the Appendix C steps — brackets, fillers, a list of contractions, digits, a small number-word parser, "percent" and "dollars", and a few British spellings — enough for these examples; the released EnglishTextNormalizer handles far more. Is the normaliser fair?

The paper reports WER drops "of up to 50 percent" on some datasets from normalisation alone, often from quirks such as references splitting contractions with a space. Because the normaliser was developed by inspecting Whisper's errors, it risks being tuned to Whisper's style. The authors checked it against the independently developed FairSpeech normaliser (Figure 10): on most datasets the two reduce WER similarly for Whisper and for other models, but on WSJ, CallHome and Switchboard theirs reduces Whisper's WER noticeably more — traced to contractions (CallHome, Switchboard) and numeric expressions (WSJ).

Takeaway: a WER is only comparable to another WER computed with the same normaliser. Before comparing a Whisper number with anything, check which normaliser was used — the difference can exceed the gap between models.

19 English results and effective robustness

Whisper's LibriSpeech score is unremarkable — 2.5% WER on test-clean for large-v2 with beam search, "roughly the performance of modern supervised baseline or the mid-2019 state of the art". What changes is how that score transfers: across other English datasets, Whisper makes far fewer errors than supervised models with the same LibriSpeech accuracy.

effective robustness (Taori et al. 2020): out-of-distribution performance beyond what in-distribution performance predictsρ(model) = WER_ood(model) − f(WER_ref(model)), f = trend of the reference-trained models; ideal robustness: WER_ood = WER_ref (the line y = x)

Table 2, reproduced

The paper pairs Whisper large-v2 with wav2vec 2.0 Large (960 h, no language model), both at 2.7% on LibriSpeech test-clean with greedy decoding. On the 13 other sets the mean WER falls from 29.3 to 12.8. The paper's headline "55.2% fewer errors" is the average of the thirteen per-dataset relative reductions (from 74.7% on Artie down to 16.1% on LibriSpeech other); the reduction of the mean WER itself is larger, about 56%, as the figure's readout computes.

  • Even tiny transfers. The paper notes that the smallest model, 39M parameters and 6.7% on LibriSpeech test-clean (with beam search), is "roughly competitive with the best supervised LibriSpeech model when evaluated on other datasets".
  • Human comparison. On 25 recordings from Kincaid46, the computer-assisted professional service had an aggregate WER 1.15 points better than Whisper, and the four pure-human services were "only a fraction of a percentage point" better.
  • Caveat on the baselines. The comparison models are all LibriSpeech-trained; a supervised model trained on many datasets (like the NVIDIA ones, in teal) is far more robust than one trained on LibriSpeech alone. The effective-robustness gap is largest against the narrowest baselines.
Takeaway: Whisper's contribution is on the vertical axis, not the horizontal one. Judge a speech recogniser by its accuracy on audio unlike its training set, and read "55.2%" as the mean of per-dataset reductions against a LibriSpeech-only model.

20 Multilingual recognition and scaling with data

On Multilingual LibriSpeech the zero-shot model beats prior fine-tuned systems (7.3 WER against 10.9 for XLS-R 1B and 9.7 for mSLAM-CTC 2B — with the caveat, which the paper states, that its simple text standardiser prevents a direct comparison); on VoxPopuli it trails them badly (13.6 against 10.6, 9.1 and Maestro's 8.1). Across the 102 Fleurs languages, the best predictor of a language's WER is simply how many hours of it were in training.

the paper's log–log fit on Fleurs (Figure 3)log WER ≈ a + b · log hours, r² = 0.83, b ≈ −¼ ⇒ WER halves for every 2^(1/¼) = 16× more data

What limits a language

  • Hours first. Most languages have under 1,000 hours of transcription data, and many under 10, where WER is often above 50%.
  • Then script and family. The largest negative outliers — Hebrew, Telugu, Chinese, Korean — have unique scripts and are distant from the Indo-European majority; the paper suggests linguistic distance, a poor BPE fit, or data quality as causes.
  • Dataset scaling (Table 6). English WER falls quickly from 3,405 to 13,621 hours and then flattens: the last 12.5× more data buys about one point. Multilingual WER follows a power law to 54,000 hours and then gains 7 more points; translation is near zero below about 7,000 hours. The paper reads this as either under-training of the largest models or the approach nearing its limit, and says further analysis is needed.
  • Model scaling (Figure 8). Multilingual recognition, translation and language ID keep improving with size up to large; English recognition shows diminishing returns, which the paper attributes partly to approaching human-level accuracy.
Takeaway: for a given language, Whisper is roughly as good as the amount of that language in its training set. The cure the paper suggests is targeted data collection for low-resource languages, not a bigger model.

21 Speech translation and language identification

Translation into English is a trained task, so Whisper can translate zero-shot. Language identification is a by-product of the format (the language tag is predicted). The first is a strength, the second is not.

The gain is largest in low-resource languages (+6.7 BLEU over mSLAM); on high-resource languages Whisper does not beat Maestro or mSLAM. On Fleurs, BLEU correlates only weakly with translation hours (r² = 0.24), which the paper blames partly on language-ID errors in the training data — the Welsh case.
  • Language identification. 64.5% accuracy on Fleurs against 71.4% (w2v-bert-51, 0.6B) and 77.7% (mSLAM-CTC, 2B). Twenty of Fleurs' 102 languages have no Whisper training data, capping accuracy at 80.4%; on the 82 overlapping languages the best model reaches 80.3%.
  • Direction. Only X → English is trained. The model can be prompted with other combinations, but the paper evaluates none.
  • large-v3-turbo does not translate reliably: it was fine-tuned on transcription data only (releases).
Takeaway: use Whisper's translation for low- and mid-resource languages into English, where it was state of the art in 2022; use its language ID as a hint, not as a classifier, especially for short clips.

22 Noise robustness and long-form results

Two further robustness tests: additive noise on LibriSpeech, and full-length recordings — talks, podcasts, earnings calls, interviews — transcribed end to end.

  • Additive noise (Figure 5). White noise and "pub noise" (ambient chatter from the Audio Degradation Toolbox) are added at controlled signal-to-noise ratios. Many LibriSpeech-trained models beat Whisper at 40 dB SNR, but all of them degrade faster, and below 10 dB SNR of pub noise Whisper is better than every one of the 14 compared models.
  • Long-form (Figure 6, Table 16). On seven long-form sets (TED-LIUM3 full talks, Meanwhile, Kincaid46, Rev16, Earnings-21, Earnings-22, CORAAL) Whisper beats the best open-source model compared (NVIDIA's Conformer-CTC Large with buffered inference) on every set, and in most cases also beats the four commercial services queried with their default settings as of 1 September 2022. The paper warns that the commercial systems may have trained on some of these public datasets.
