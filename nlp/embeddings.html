Embeddings

Turning tokens — and images, audio, anything — into dense vectors where meaning becomes geometry.

NLP Representations Retrieval

01 What an embedding is

An embedding maps a discrete thing (a word, sentence, image) to a point in a continuous space, learned so that similar things land close together.

  • Formally a learned function fθ : X → ℝd — a lookup from a token to a vector, typically 256–4096 dimensions.
  • Training pulls items used in similar contexts together (the distributional hypothesis): "a word is known by the company it keeps."
  • Once everything is a vector, semantics turns into arithmetic and distance: similarity is measured with cosine, and relationships become consistent directions.
  • The same recipe generalizes far beyond text — images, audio, code, and multimodal pairs all embed into shared spaces, which is what powers search, clustering, recommendation, and RAG.
fθ : X → ℝd sim(a, b) = cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)

02 From sparse counts to dense meaning

Before learned vectors, text was counted, not understood. Knowing why counting fails is the best motivation for embeddings.

One-hot & Bag-of-Words

  • Give every vocabulary word its own axis. A document is the vector of word counts — huge and sparse (one slot per word in the language).
  • Fatal flaw: every pair of distinct words is equidistant. "cat" is exactly as far from "kitten" as from "helicopter" — there is no notion of similarity at all.

TF-IDF & BM25

  • TF-IDF down-weights common words and up-weights rare, distinctive ones — better ranking, still no semantics.
  • BM25 refines this with term-frequency saturation and document-length normalization (tunable k₁, b); it is still the strong lexical baseline in search.
TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) · log( N / DF(t) )

Sparse methods do lexical matching (exact terms); dense embeddings do semantic matching (meaning). They fail in opposite cases — which is why production search often hybridises both.

03 Word2Vec: meaning from prediction

The breakthrough: stop counting, start predicting. Learn a vector for each word by training it to predict the words around it.

Two architectures

  • CBOW (Continuous Bag-of-Words): use the surrounding context to predict the centre word. Fast, good on frequent words.
  • Skip-gram: use the centre word to predict each surrounding word. Slower, but stronger on rare words and small corpora.
maximise Σt Σ−c ≤ j ≤ c, j≠0 log P( wt+j | wt ) P( o | i ) = exp( uo · vi ) / Σw exp( uw · vi )

Why it scales: the softmax shortcut

  • The exact softmax sums over the whole vocabulary per step — far too expensive.
  • Negative sampling: reframe as binary classification — push the true (centre, context) pair together, push a handful of random pairs apart. (An early form of contrastive learning.)
  • Hierarchical softmax: arrange the vocabulary as a binary tree so each prediction costs log|V| instead of |V|.
For free: linear structure emerges — king − man + woman ≈ queen. Consistent attributes (gender, tense, plurality) become consistent offset directions.

04 GloVe & fastText

Two refinements of static word vectors — one adds global statistics, the other adds sub-word structure.

GloVe — Global Vectors

  • Word2Vec only sees local windows. GloVe factorises the global word-word co-occurrence matrix directly, fitting vectors so that dot products match log co-occurrence counts.
  • Result: count-based and predict-based views unified, with similar analogy behaviour.

fastText — sub-word n-grams

  • Represent a word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors (e.g. where → <wh, whe, her, ere, re>).
  • This gives sensible vectors for morphological variants and out-of-vocabulary words never seen in training — a big win for rich-morphology languages.

All three are static: one fixed vector per word type, regardless of sentence. That single assumption is what the next section breaks.

05 Similarity & vector arithmetic

Once words are vectors, comparing meaning is just geometry. The metric you choose matters.

cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖) dot(a, b) = a · b euclid(a, b) = ‖a − b‖
  • Cosine measures the angle only, ignoring magnitude — the default for embeddings, because direction carries the meaning.
  • Dot product rewards magnitude too; if you L2-normalise first, dot product equals cosine (which is why ANN indexes often store unit vectors).
  • Analogies are offset vectors: a − b is a direction ("more royal", "feminine"), and adding it transports you across the space.

06 Static → contextual embeddings

One vector per word can't survive polysemy — words with several meanings. The fix made modern NLP possible.

  • A static model gives one vector for "bank" — an average of the river sense and the money sense, true to neither.
  • Contextual models (ELMo, then BERT) read the whole sentence and emit a different vector for each occurrence of a token, so "bank" near river and "bank" near loan land in different places.
  • Mechanism: bidirectional self-attention mixes each token with its neighbours, so the representation is computed on the fly rather than looked up.

07 Modern embeddings: sentence, contrastive, multimodal

The frontier moved from single words to general-purpose representation engines for whole passages and every modality.

Sentence & document embeddings

  • Retrieval needs one vector per passage, not per token. Pool token vectors or use models like Sentence-BERT tuned so paraphrases sit close.

Contrastive learning — the modern recipe

  • Train on pairs: pull positives (a query and its relevant passage) together, push negatives — especially hard negatives (plausible-but-wrong) — apart.
  • This single objective produces today's strongest text and image embeddings (and is exactly what CLIP does across modalities).

LLM-derived & multimodal

  • LLM-derived: convert a decoder LLM into an encoder (enable bidirectional attention + a contrastive fine-tune, e.g. LLM2Vec), or distil a large embedder into a small fast one.
  • Multimodal / omnimodal: align text, images, and audio in one shared space (CLIP-style), enabling cross-modal retrieval — search images with text, and vice-versa.

08 Efficiency & scaling

A real system holds millions–billions of vectors. Then storage, memory, and latency become the whole game.

Dimensionality & storage

  • Cost is linear in d: N vectors × d dims × 4 bytes (fp32). At 768-d, 10M vectors ≈ ~30 GB just to store.

Matryoshka representation learning

  • Train so that every prefix of the vector is itself usable — the first 64 dims of a 768-d vector already carry most of the signal.
  • Lets you truncate on demand (short vectors for a cheap first pass, full vectors to re-rank) with no retraining.

Quantization & ANN search

  • Quantization: store int8 or binary instead of fp32 → 4×–32× smaller, with small quality loss.
  • Exact nearest-neighbour over millions of vectors is too slow; approximate indexes (HNSW, IVF) give sub-linear search — the job of a vector database.
