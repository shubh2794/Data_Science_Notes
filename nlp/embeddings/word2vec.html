Word2Vec: CBOW, Skip-gram and the Cost of a Word Vector

The embeddings hub gives the one-paragraph version: predict context, get vectors, king − man + woman ≈ queen. This page follows the original paper's actual argument, which is about cost: count the parameters a model touches per training word, notice that the non-linear hidden layer and the vocabulary-sized output dominate, delete the first and shrink the second to a Huffman-tree walk, and spend the savings on far more data. Then it reads the paper's analogy test set and result tables closely — including where the method's headline examples go wrong.

01 The goal: vectors from billions of words, not hundreds of millions

The paper's stated goal is to learn high-quality word vectors from data sets with billions of words and vocabularies of millions. At the time, no proposed neural architecture had, as far as the paper knew, been trained successfully on more than a few hundred million words, and typical vectors had only 50–100 dimensions.

  • Words as atomic indices hit a ceiling. Treating words as vocabulary indices — the N-gram view — is simple and robust, and N-gram models can be trained on essentially all available text (trillions of words). But in domains with little in-domain data (transcribed speech: often just millions of words; many machine-translation corpora: a few billion words or less) more of the same model does not help.
  • Distributed representations work, but are expensive. Neural language models already beat N-grams and learn word vectors as a by-product. The problem the paper attacks is that they are too slow to train on the data sizes where vectors would get really good.
  • The quality target is not just "similar words are close". The paper wants words to have multiple degrees of similarity — the regularity that a simple vector offset like vector("King") − vector("Man") + vector("Woman") lands nearest to Queen. It designs both its architectures and its test set around preserving such linear regularities.
  • Why not LSA or LDA? Neural distributed representations had been shown to preserve linear regularities significantly better than latent semantic analysis, and LDA becomes very expensive on large data. (Those methods are covered on the topic modeling page.)
The bet in one line. A simpler model "might not be able to represent the data as precisely as neural networks", but if it is cheap enough to train on far more data, the vectors come out better. Everything below is an accounting of what was removed to make it cheap, and evidence that the trade paid off.

02 The cost model: parameters touched per training word

To compare architectures fairly, the paper defines computational complexity as the number of parameters that must be accessed to fully train the model, and then tries to maximise accuracy while minimising it.

training complexity, for every model in the paperO = E × T × Q E number of training epochs common choice: 3 – 50 T number of words in the training set up to one billion Q cost per training example — defined separately for each architecture

All models are trained with stochastic gradient descent and backpropagation. E and T are properties of the training run; Q is the property of the architecture, so the rest of the argument is about making Q small. The symbols used for every Q below:

symbolmeaning
Vvocabulary size (words are 1-of-V coded at the input)

Hhidden-layer size
Cskip-gram's maximum context distance

03 Where the baselines spend it: NNLM and RNNLM

A feedforward NNLM pays for a dense projection-to-hidden layer and a vocabulary-sized output; a recurrent one pays for its recurrent matrix and the same output. Fix the output and the hidden layer is what remains.

Feedforward neural net language model

Input, projection, hidden and output layers. The N previous words are 1-of-V coded and projected through a shared matrix to a projection layer of size N×D — cheap, because only N inputs are active. The cost is after that: the projection is dense, so every projection unit connects to every hidden unit, and the hidden layer feeds a softmax over all V words.

feedforward nnlm, per training exampleQ = N × D + N × D × H + H × V projection projection→hidden hidden→output typical sizes given in the paper: N = 10, N×D ≈ 500 – 2000, H ≈ 500 – 1000
  • With a full softmax the dominating term is H × V. Hierarchical softmax (§04), or models that are not normalised during training, cut the output cost to about H × log₂(V) — and then most of the complexity is the N × D × H term.
  • Worked example (not from the paper): N = 10, D = 50, H = 500, V = 10⁶ gives 500 + 250 000 + 500 000 000 with a full softmax, and 500 + 250 000 + ≈ 9 966 once the output is a binary-tree walk. Removing the output bottleneck leaves the hidden layer as about 96% of the cost.

Recurrent neural net language model

No projection layer — input, hidden, output — plus a recurrent matrix connecting the hidden layer to itself with a time delay, which gives a short-term memory and removes the need to fix a context length N. The word representations have the same dimensionality as the hidden layer (D = H). Background on recurrent networks is on the RNNs & LSTMs page.

recurrent lm, per training exampleQ = H × H + H × V → H × H + H × log₂(V) with hierarchical softmax most of the remaining cost: H × H
The diagnosis. In both models, once the output is handled, "most of the complexity is caused by the non-linear hidden layer". That layer is what makes neural networks attractive; the paper's move is to delete it and see what the vectors lose.

04 The output layer: hierarchical softmax over a Huffman tree

Arrange the vocabulary as the leaves of a binary tree and a word's probability becomes a product of binary decisions along its root-to-leaf path — about log₂(V) of them for a balanced tree. The paper uses a Huffman tree, which gives frequent words short paths and brings the average down to about log₂(unigram perplexity).

output units evaluated per predictionfull softmax V balanced binary tree ≈ log₂(V) Huffman binary tree ≈ log₂( unigram_perplexity(V) ) reported example: V = 1 million words → about two times faster evaluation (Huffman vs balanced)

Why Huffman? Word frequency had already been found to work well for building output classes in neural LMs, and a Huffman code assigns short binary codes to frequent words. Because text is dominated by a few very frequent words, the average path length per training token drops. The connection to perplexity is a standard information-theory fact rather than something the paper derives: a Huffman code's expected length L̄ satisfies H ≤ L̄ < H + 1 bits, where H is the entropy of the unigram distribution, and log₂(perplexity) = H. The construction and that bound are proven on the Huffman coding page.

beyond this paper — the hierarchical softmax probabilityP(w | h) = ∏ⱼ₌₁…L(w)−1 σ( ⟦ next node on w's path is the left child ⟧ · v′ₙ₍w,ⱼ₎ · h ) h the input-side vector (the projected context, or the input word's vector) n(w, j) the j-th inner node on the path from the root to leaf w; L(w) = path length in nodes v′ₙ a learned vector for each INNER node (there are V − 1 of them, no per-word output vector) ⟦x⟧ +1 if x is true, −1 otherwise; σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ) the two children's probabilities at every node sum to 1, so ∑_w P(w | h) = 1 with no normalising sum over V

This paper states only the cost (log₂ output units) and the Huffman choice; the explicit product form above comes from the earlier hierarchical-softmax work it cites and is written out in its follow-up paper.

Huffman hierarchical softmax: path length tracks frequency pick a frequent word, then a rare one, and count the binary decisions each needs
  • What to notice. With s = 0 (uniform frequencies) the Huffman tree is essentially balanced and the average path is ≈ log₂ V: there is nothing to exploit. As s grows the average path falls below log₂ V and stays within one bit of the entropy — the toy version of the paper's "about log₂(unigram perplexity)".
  • The rare words pay. A rare word's path can be longer than log₂ V. That is fine for the cost, which is averaged over training tokens, and those tokens are mostly frequent words.
  • Why it matters more here than for an NNLM. For an NNLM the 2× output speedup is "not crucial", because N × D × H dominates. The new architectures have no hidden layer, so the output walk is the cost — they "depend heavily on the efficiency of the softmax normalization".

05 Continuous bag-of-words (CBOW): predict the middle word from its neighbours

Take the feedforward NNLM, remove the non-linear hidden layer, and share the projection layer itself (not just the projection matrix) across all context positions — so every context word is projected to the same place, i.e. their vectors are averaged. The averaged vector feeds a log-linear classifier for the current word.

cbow, per training exampleQ = N × D + D × log₂(V) N × D look up and average the N context vectors D × log₂(V) one hierarchical-softmax walk for the middle word best reported setting: 4 history words + 4 future words → N = 8
  • "Bag of words" because word order in the context does not affect the projection; "continuous" because, unlike a standard bag of words, the context is a dense distributed representation.
  • It uses the future too. A language model may only look back; CBOW is not trying to be one, so it uses words on both sides. The best performance on the paper's test set came from four words on each side.
  • The input-to-projection weight matrix is shared across all word positions, as in the NNLM. The learned word vectors are the rows of that matrix.
worked example, not from the paperN = 8, D = 300, V = 10⁶ (log₂ V ≈ 19.93, balanced tree) Q_CBOW = 8·300 + 300·19.93 ≈ 2 400 + 5 979 ≈ 8 379 parameters per training word the same D with a feedforward NNLM's N × D × H (N = 8, H = 500): 1 200 000 — before the output layer

06 Continuous skip-gram: predict the neighbours from the word

Invert CBOW. Use the current word as input to a log-linear classifier with a continuous projection layer, and predict words within some range before and after it. A larger range gives better vectors but costs more, and distant words are usually less related, so the paper samples distant words less often.

skip-gram, per training exampleQ = C × (D + D × log₂(V)) C the maximum distance of context words (the paper uses C = 10) for each training word: draw R uniformly from {1, …, C} use the R words before and the R words after as correct labels → R × 2 classifications, each with the current word as input

The sampling of R is how "less weight to distant words" is implemented — there is no explicit weight in the loss. The paper states the procedure; the consequences below are straightforward arithmetic on it.

worked arithmetic on the sampling rule, not from the papera context word at distance d (1 ≤ d ≤ C) is used ⇔ R ≥ d P(used) = (C − d + 1) / C C = 10: d = 1 → 1.0 d = 2 → 0.9 d = 5 → 0.6 d = 10 → 0.1 expected classifications per training word = 2 · E[R] = 2 · (C + 1)/2 = C + 1 = 11
  • So the C in the paper's Q is a proportionality, not an exact count: on average a training word costs C + 1 predictions (11 at C = 10), each D + D × log₂(V).
  • Skip-gram does C-ish times the work of CBOW per word at the same D: CBOW averages the context into one prediction; skip-gram makes one prediction per context word. This is why the reported single-machine skip-gram runs take roughly three times as long as CBOW (§11).
  • The hub's skip-gram window figure shows which (centre → context) pairs one fixed window produces; the random R above shrinks that window per word.

07 The four architectures side by side

Put the four Qs next to each other and the design is visible in the terms that are missing: no N × D × H, no H × H, and no × V anywhere.

  • The Huffman option uses an idealised Zipf(1) unigram law to estimate the average path (entropy in bits). That is a modelling assumption of this figure, not a number from the paper; for V = 10⁶ it gives about 13.4 bits against log₂ V ≈ 19.9 — a 1.5× saving. The paper reports about 2× for its real one-million-word vocabulary; real word frequencies are more skewed at the head than a pure Zipf(1) law, and a Zipf exponent near 1.1 already gives 1.8×.
  • Data and dimension trade one-for-one. In CBOW's Q both terms are linear in D, and O is linear in T, so — as the paper notes from its Eq. 4 — doubling the training data costs about the same as doubling the vector size. §09 shows that you have to grow both.

09 Answering a question with one subtraction and one addition

"What word is similar to small in the same sense that biggest is similar to big?" Compute the offset, add it, and take the nearest word by cosine — discarding the question's own words from the search.

the vector offset methodX = vector("biggest") − vector("big") + vector("small") answer = argmax over words w ∉ {biggest, big, small} of cos(X, vector(w)) cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖) correct ⇔ answer = "smallest" exactly

The toy below is a hand-built 2-D space, not trained vectors: each relation is given a consistent offset, and every word is then jittered by noise. In two dimensions cosine is just angle, so the answer is whichever word lies closest to the ray from the origin through X — length is ignored. Similarity metrics in general are on the hub's similarity section.

A toy analogy space: offset, ray, nearest by cosine add noise until questions start failing; then average the offset over the other pairs and watch toy-set accuracy rise
  • Excluding the inputs is not cosmetic. X is built from the third word plus a small offset, so that word is usually very close to X. Without the exclusion, it often wins; the paper's procedure explicitly discards the question words.
  • Averaging the offset is in the paper. Forming the relationship vector from ten example pairs instead of one, by averaging, improved the best models' accuracy by about 10 percentage points absolute on the test set (§14). The toy shows the mechanism: per-pair noise in the offset cancels, while the noise on the third word and on the answer does not, so averaging helps but cannot restore 100%. (In the toy, at noise 0.2–0.4, averaging raised toy-set accuracy in 17–19 of 20 random noise draws, by about 7–10 points on average — a property of this hand-built toy, not a reproduction of the paper's figure.)

12 Large-scale distributed training on 6B words

On a distributed parameter-server framework — many replicas of the model, each synchronising gradients through a central server holding all parameters, trained with mini-batch asynchronous gradient descent and Adagrad — the paper trains 1000-dimensional CBOW and skip-gram models on the full 6B-word corpus.

15 Limitations, stated and implied

stated · morphology
No access to word structure
Each word is an atomic input, so easiest cannot be inferred from easy + "-est". The paper expects further progress from incorporating word structure, especially on syntactic questions. Subword models address exactly this (fastText on the hub, and tokenization).
stated · metric
Exact match understates quality
Synonyms and near-misses score zero; 100% is likely unreachable. The test set also excludes multi-word entities.
stated · design
Less expressive by construction
Removing the hidden layer trades the ability to model the data precisely for throughput. The vectors are good for linear regularities; the model is not a usable language model.
implied · one vector per type
No word sense
Each word type gets one vector regardless of context, so senses are blended. The hub's static → contextual section covers the fix.
implied · evaluation
Single runs, one family of test
No variance across seeds is reported (§10 has a non-monotone cell), and the main benchmark is the paper's own analogy set; the sentence-completion result shows standalone performance on another task can be weak.

16 Beyond this paper: what "word2vec" usually means today

Everything above is this paper. Much of what is now called "word2vec" — and what the hub page summarises — comes from the follow-up paper on distributed representations of words and phrases, published later the same year.

ideawhat it issource
negative samplingreplace the tree walk with binary logistic regression: push up the true (word, context) pair, push down k sampled noise words — an early relative of the objective on the contrastive learning pagefollow-up paper, not this one

The full softmax formula on the hub (P(o | i) = exp(u_o · v_i) / ∑_w exp(u_w · v_i)) is the objective that both hierarchical softmax (this paper) and negative sampling (the follow-up) avoid computing. For where static vectors sit in the larger story — GloVe, fastText, then contextual encoders and LLM embeddings — go back to the embeddings hub.

17 Common misconceptions

"The original word2vec paper introduced negative sampling." No — this paper uses hierarchical softmax over a Huffman tree. Negative sampling and frequent-word subsampling are from the follow-up paper (§16).
"Word2vec is a (shallow) neural language model." It is a pair of log-linear classifiers with no hidden layer. CBOW uses future words, skip-gram predicts each neighbour independently from one word; neither gives a usable next-word distribution. Deleting the hidden layer is the whole point (§03).
"Skip-gram is better than CBOW." Depends on the question type. In the controlled comparison skip-gram leads semantics 55 vs 24, but CBOW leads syntax 64 vs 59 — and CBOW is roughly 3× cheaper on one machine (§11).
"Hierarchical softmax costs log₂ V per word." For a balanced tree. The paper's Huffman tree costs about log₂(unigram perplexity) on average — roughly half as many output units at a 1M vocabulary — while individual rare words can cost more than log₂ V (§04).
"king − man + woman lands exactly on queen." It lands nearest to queen once the three input words are excluded — and nearest by cosine, not equal. Under exact match even the paper's showcase examples score about 60% (§14).
"More dimensions always give better vectors." Only with enough data. At 24M words, 600 dims scores 24.0 against 13.4 at 50; at 783M words the same change is 23.2 → 50.4 (§10).

18 Takeaways

  • Training cost is O = E × T × Q, with Q the parameters accessed per training word; the paper makes Q small so T can be huge.
  • NNLM: N·D + N·D·H + H·V; RNNLM: H·H + H·V. A tree-structured output turns × V into × log₂ V, leaving the hidden layer dominant — so the hidden layer goes.
  • Hierarchical softmax over a Huffman tree walks about log₂(unigram perplexity) binary decisions per word, about 2× fewer than a balanced tree at 1M words.
  • CBOW: average 4 + 4 context vectors, predict the middle word, Q = N·D + D·log₂ V. Skip-gram: predict up to R ≤ C words each side, Q = C·(D + D·log₂ V), with C = 10 and random R down-weighting distant words.
  • The test set: 8 869 semantic + 10 675 syntactic analogy questions, answered by b − a + c and cosine nearest neighbour excluding the inputs, scored by exact match.
  • Results: skip-gram wins semantics, CBOW wins syntax; one epoch on twice the data beats three epochs; 1000-dim models on 6B words reach 63.7% (CBOW) and 65.6% (skip-gram) at a fraction of an NNLM's CPU-days; skip-gram + RNNLMs reach 58.9% on sentence completion.
  • Limits: no morphology, one vector per word type, an exact-match metric that understates quality, and much of today's "word2vec" (negative sampling, subsampling) is from the follow-up paper.
