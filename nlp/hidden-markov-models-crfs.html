Hidden Markov Models & CRFs

Before neural networks, sequence labeling — POS tagging, NER, chunking — ran on probabilistic models that score an entire label sequence at once. HMMs, MEMMs, and CRFs are that lineage: a story about where you put the normalizer, and why that one choice decides what the model can learn. CRF layers still sit on top of modern taggers.

NLP Sequence labeling Probabilistic Structured prediction

01 Labeling sequences

Sequence labeling asks for one tag per token, but the tags are not independent draws — they form a structured object with its own grammar. Modelling that structure is the whole point.

  • The task. Given tokens x = (x₁ … x_T), produce tags y = (y₁ … y_T) from a small tag set — Det/Noun/Verb for part of speech, B-PER/I-PER/O for entities. One label per position, so the output space has size |S|T: exponential in sentence length.
  • Why per-token classification fails. A classifier that sees each token alone will happily emit O I-PER I-PER — a continuation tag with nothing to continue. Its loss never penalised the combination. Tag sequences obey hard constraints (I-X must follow B-X or I-X) and soft ones (a determiner is rarely followed by a verb).
  • Structured prediction. Score whole sequences with a term for each adjacent label pair, then search for the best y. Because the score factorises over pairs, that search is a dynamic program rather than an exponential enumeration.
  • Two knobs, three models. Everything below is a choice on two axes: generative vs discriminative (model P(x, y) or P(y | x)?) and local vs global normalization (does probability sum to one per step or per sequence?). HMM = generative + local; MEMM = discriminative + local; CRF = discriminative + global.
the structured-prediction objectiveŷ = argmaxy ∈ ST score(x, y) score(x, y) = ∑t ψ(yt−1, yt, x, t) |ST| candidates — but pairwise factorisation ⇒ O(T · |S|²) search
One idea: a tagger that decides each token alone is guessing one jigsaw piece at a time. Sequence models score the assembled picture, and the pairwise factorisation is what makes scoring every possible picture affordable.

02 Hidden Markov Models

A generative story: an unobserved chain of states (the tags) walks from position to position, emitting an observation (the word) at each stop. We see only the words and must infer the walk.

Parameters and assumptions

  • Three tables specify the model: an initial distribution π(s), a transition matrix A[s,s′] = P(yt=s′ | yt−1=s), and an emission matrix B[s,w] = P(xt=w | yt=s).
  • Two independence assumptions do all the work. The Markov assumption: the next state depends only on the current one. Output independence: the word depends only on the state that emitted it. Together they turn a joint over 2T variables into a product of small factors — which is why every inference question below has a linear-time algorithm.
  • With labelled data, maximum likelihood is pure counting: Â[s,s′] = c(s→s′)/c(s), B̂[s,w] = c(s,w)/c(s). Sparsity bites at once — unseen (tag, word) pairs get probability zero, so emissions must be smoothed or backed off to word shape and suffix.
  • Because it models P(x, y), the HMM spends capacity explaining the input — wasted effort if you only want tags, and the motivation for everything after §05.
joint likelihood of words and tagsP(x, y) = π(y₁) · ∏t=1…T P(yt | yt−1) · P(xt | yt) └─ transition A ─┘ └─ emission B ─┘
Takeaway: an HMM is a naive-Bayes classifier unrolled in time — see Naive Bayes for the non-sequential cousin. Its independence assumptions buy exact tractable inference and cost you overlapping features of the input.

03 The three problems

Every use of an HMM reduces to one of three questions — how likely, which path, what parameters — and all three run on the same trellis.

Likelihood — the forward algorithm

  • What is P(x), summed over all tag sequences? Naively |S|T terms. The forward algorithm keeps αt(s), the total probability of all paths generating x₁…xt and ending in s. Paths reaching s share the same future, so they are summed once and reused — the classic collapse to O(T · |S|²).
  • A mirror backward recursion gives βt(s), the probability of the remaining observations. Together they yield per-position posteriors P(yt=s | x) ∝ αt(s) · βt(s) — the basis for token-level confidence.
forward recursionα₁(s) = π(s) · B[s, x₁] αt(s) = B[s, xt] · ∑s′ αt−1(s′) · A[s′, s] P(x) = ∑s αT(s)

Learning — Baum-Welch (EM)

  • With only unlabelled sequences, Baum-Welch is expectation-maximisation on the HMM. The E-step runs forward-backward under current parameters to get soft counts — the expected number of times each transition and emission was used. The M-step re-normalises those expected counts exactly as supervised counting would. Repeat.
  • Each iteration cannot decrease data likelihood, but it converges only to a local optimum and is very sensitive to initialisation. Unsupervised induction recovers internally coherent clusters that rarely match linguistic categories, so in practice it is used semi-supervised: seed from a small labelled set, refine on raw text.

04 Viterbi decoding

Find the single most likely tag sequence through the trellis of all possibilities — by dynamic programming, in time linear in sentence length.

  • Let δt(s) be the score of the best path covering x₁…xt and ending in s. Any best path ending at s has a best path as its prefix, so only |S| numbers per column survive; a back-pointer bpt(s) records which predecessor won.
  • Work in log space: products of many small probabilities underflow within a few dozen tokens, sums of logs do not. Sum-vs-max is the only difference between forward and Viterbi — the trellis is identical.
viterbi, in log spaceδ₁(s) = log π(s) + log B[s, x₁] δt(s) = log B[s, xt] + maxs′ [ δt−1(s′) + log A[s′, s] ] bpt(s) = argmaxs′ [ δt−1(s′) + log A[s′, s] ] ŷT = argmaxs δT(s) , ŷt−1 = bpt(ŷt) ⟵ backtrace
Viterbi: best path through the trellis Columns are words, rows are tags; the highlighted path is the most likely tagging

Viterbi reuses sub-path scores, so it finds the best of exponentially many sequences in linear time. Note what it returns: the single best joint sequence, which is not the same as taking the most probable tag at each position independently. Per-position marginals can produce a sequence Viterbi would never emit — one that may not even be legal under the tag scheme. Use marginals for calibrated token confidence; use Viterbi when you need one coherent output.

Takeaway: Viterbi is the algorithm that outlives the model. Its recurrence is unchanged whether the per-position scores come from a count-based emission table, a CRF feature vector, or a transformer logit head — only the source of ψ differs.

05 MEMMs and the label-bias problem

The obvious upgrade to an HMM is to stop generating the input and start conditioning on it. That buys arbitrary features — and introduces a subtle, fatal pathology.

Maximum-entropy Markov models

  • A MEMM replaces transition × emission with one discriminative log-linear classifier per position, modelling P(yt | yt−1, x). Conditioning on x instead of generating it means features may look at anything: suffixes, capitalisation, gazetteer membership, neighbouring words, the whole sentence.
  • That is a real advance — overlapping, redundant features are fine under a discriminative model, whereas an HMM would need them conditionally independent given the state. Training is convex; decoding is still Viterbi.
memm — one softmax per stepP(y | x) = ∏t P(yt | yt−1, x) P(yt | yt−1, x) = exp( ∑k λk fk(yt−1, yt, x, t) ) ⁄ Z(yt−1, x) ⟵ local normalizer

Label bias

  • The damage is done by that per-state normalizer. Probability mass arriving at a state must be passed on in full, however badly the observation matches. A state with a single outgoing transition sends 100% of its mass down that edge, so the observation at that step is literally ignored — it cannot reshape a distribution over one option.
  • The result: paths through low-entropy states (few successors) beat paths through high-entropy ones regardless of evidence, and a later observation cannot undo an early wrong turn. A CRF removes this by deleting the per-step normalizer and normalising once over all sequences, so a poor match contributes a low potential rather than a forced probability of one.

Toggle the normalizer below on a two-branch example. The upper branch passes through a state with one successor, the lower through a state with three. Under local normalization the upper branch wins on structure alone; under global normalization the observation decides.

Local vs global normalization the same potentials, two normalizers — one exhibits label bias
Takeaway: label bias is not a feature bug — it is a conservation law. Normalising per step forces every state to hand on all of its mass, so structure (how many successors a state has) outvotes evidence.

06 Conditional Random Fields

Keep the rich features of a MEMM, throw away the per-step normalizer, and normalise once over the whole sequence. That is a linear-chain conditional random field.

Model and feature functions

  • A CRF defines P(y | x) as a single globally normalised log-linear model. The score of a sequence sums feature functions fk over adjacent label pairs, weighted by learned λk; the partition function Z(x) sums the exponentiated score over every label sequence. Linear chain means factors span adjacent pairs only — which is exactly what makes that |S|T-term sum computable by the forward recursion of §03.
  • A feature function is any (usually binary) function of (yt−1, yt, x, t), free to inspect the entire input. Classic NER features: capitalisation, suffix, word shape (Xxxxx, ddd-dddd), gazetteer hit, the tokens at t−1 and t+1.
  • Pure transition features — functions of the label pair alone — learn the tag grammar: illegal pairs such as O → I-PER acquire large negative weights and effectively never appear in a decoded path.
linear-chain crfP(y | x) = (1 ⁄ Z(x)) · exp( ∑t ∑k λk fk(yt−1, yt, x, t) ) Z(x) = ∑y′ ∈ ST exp( ∑t ∑k λk fk(y′t−1, y′t, x, t) ) ⟵ one global normalizer e.g. f₁₇(yt−1, yt, x, t) = 1 if yt−1=B-PER ∧ yt=I-PER ∧ isCap(xt)

Training and decoding

  • Train by maximising conditional log-likelihood. The objective is convex, so gradient methods reach the global optimum; L2 (or L1 for feature selection) regularisation is standard.
  • The gradient is strikingly interpretable: per feature, the observed count in the gold data minus its expected count under the model. Training pushes weights until model expectations match what was seen. Those expectations are the pairwise marginals from forward-backward — one such pass per sentence per gradient step.
  • Decoding is unchanged: Viterbi over the same pairwise potentials. Since Z(x) does not depend on y, it is needed only for the loss and can be dropped at decode time.
gradient of the conditional log-likelihood∂ℒ ⁄ ∂λk = ∑t fk(yt−1, yt, x, t) − ∑t 𝔼y′ ∼ P(·|x)[ fk(y′t−1, y′t, x, t) ] └──── observed count ────┘ └──── model-expected count ────┘
Takeaway: a CRF is a MEMM with the normalizer moved outward. That move costs a forward-backward pass per gradient step and buys freedom from label bias.

08 CRFs in the neural era

Neural encoders killed feature engineering, not the CRF. The layer survived because a softmax head still has no idea what a legal tag sequence looks like.

BiLSTM-CRF and transformer+CRF

  • The recipe that defined mid-2010s tagging: a bidirectional LSTM emits per-token emission scores (one real number per tag), and a CRF layer adds a learned |S| × |S| transition matrix and decodes the best path. Division of labour: the encoder handles everything depending on the input, the CRF everything depending on the output structure — a few hundred extra parameters.
  • Training uses the CRF negative log-likelihood, so the forward algorithm runs inside the network's forward pass and its gradient flows back into the encoder. Swap the BiLSTM for a pretrained transformer (see BERT) and the same head applies unchanged.
neural crf score of a tag sequencescore(x, y) = ∑t [ Et[yt] + T[yt−1, yt] ] E = encoder emissions (T × |S|) , T = learned transitions (|S| × |S|) ℒ = −score(x, y*) + log ∑y′ exp score(x, y′) ⟵ forward algorithm gives the second term

When to skip the CRF

  • With a strong pretrained encoder and a plain BIO scheme, a per-token softmax often matches a CRF head on entity-level F1 — the encoder has already absorbed the tag grammar from context. The CRF earns its place when you need a guarantee of well-formed output rather than a tendency toward it.
  • The cheapest version of that guarantee is constrained decoding: run Viterbi with hard −∞ scores on illegal transitions, no training required. Cost matters too — a CRF layer adds O(T · |S|²) per forward pass, negligible for a few entity types, a bottleneck for fine-grained schemes with hundreds of tags.
Takeaway: the old idea survives as a decoding constraint. Modern taggers changed where per-token scores come from; explicit structure over labels still pays whenever malformed output is unacceptable.

09 Pitfalls & what to remember

Where these models go wrong in practice, and the lines worth carrying away.

Pitfalls

  • Underflow. Always run forward, backward, and Viterbi in log space with a stable logsumexp; raw probability products underflow within a few dozen tokens.
  • Unseen words. An unsmoothed HMM assigns probability zero to any sentence containing an OOV word, collapsing the whole trellis. Discriminative models sidestep this by never having to explain the input.
  • Marginals ≠ best path. The most probable tag at each position, taken independently, need not form the most probable — or even a legal — sequence. Pick the decoder that matches your question.
  • A CRF does not fix bad features. Global normalization removes label bias; it adds no information. With a weak feature set you get a well-normalised weak model.
  • The Markov horizon. A first-order chain sees exactly one label back. Document-level consistency (the same string tagged the same way everywhere) is outside the model and needs higher-order factors — raising decoding to O(T · |S|n+1) — or a post-processing pass.

What to remember

  • Sequence labeling needs label dependencies — score the whole tag sequence, not each token alone.
  • HMM = generative, transition × emission; forward, Viterbi, and Baum-Welch answer likelihood, decoding, and learning on one trellis.
  • MEMM is discriminative and feature-rich but locally normalised, and so suffers label bias.
  • CRF keeps the features and normalises globally; convex training, gradient = observed − expected counts, decoding still Viterbi.
  • CRF layers live on atop BiLSTM and transformer encoders — the encoder supplies emissions, the CRF supplies transitions.
Threads to pull: these models power Named Entity Recognition; the neural encoder side is RNNs & LSTMs and BERT; the generative non-sequential cousin is Naive Bayes; and label-to-token alignment starts at Tokenization.
