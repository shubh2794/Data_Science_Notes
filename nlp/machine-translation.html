Machine Translation

Rewriting a sentence in another language so that the meaning survives the crossing. MT is the task that forced sequence models to learn alignment and reordering — it gave us attention, it is still the cleanest testbed for encoder-decoder generation, and it is where evaluation metrics get argued about hardest.

NLP Seq2seq Generation

01 The task, and why it resists word-for-word

Given a source sentence f, produce a target sentence e that means the same thing and reads naturally — adequacy and fluency at once.

Four axes of difficulty

  • Alignment isn't one-to-one. One source word may become three target words or none; articles, classifiers and auxiliaries appear and vanish.
  • Reordering. Word order belongs to the grammar, not the meaning, so the target's third word may correspond to the source's last.
  • Information the source never stated. Gender, number, definiteness and politeness must be inferred from context — one English "you" forces a formal/informal choice elsewhere.
  • Non-compositionality and low resource. Idioms mean something other than the sum of their parts, and most language pairs have almost no parallel text.

The statistical era, in a paragraph

Statistical MT framed translation as a noisy channel: pretend the target was corrupted into the source, then invert it. That factors into a translation model P(f | e) — phrase alignments estimated over a parallel corpus and stored as a giant phrase table — and a monolingual language model P(e) for fluency, plus a reordering model, combined log-linearly and tuned on a dev set. Effective, but a pipeline of loosely coupled parts whose fluency was capped by an n-gram model's short memory.

noisy channel ê = argmaxe P(e | f) = argmaxe P(f | e) · P(e) log-linear score(e, f) = ∑k λk · hk(e, f) (hk = phrase, reordering, LM, length)
One idea: translation is structured prediction over a reordering, not a lookup. Every method below is a different way of learning which source content feeds which target position.

02 Seq2seq, the bottleneck, and attention

Neural MT replaced the pipeline with one network: an encoder reads the source, a decoder writes the target. The first version had a fatal flaw, and fixing it produced attention.

The fixed-vector bottleneck

  • Early encoder-decoder models squeezed the whole source into a single fixed-length vector — the encoder RNN/LSTM's last hidden state — and generated from that alone. Quality collapsed as sentences grew: one vector cannot hold forty words, and the decoder had no way to look back at a specific source word.

Soft alignment as a learned distribution

  • Attention lets the decoder read all encoder states each step and take a weighted average, recomputed per target position.
  • Those weights are a differentiable version of SMT's separately-estimated alignment tables — learned end-to-end from the translation loss alone.
Source ↔ target attention alignment Brighter = the target word (row) attends more to that source word (column)

The alignment is not monotonic — "noir/black" and "chat/cat" swap order — and the bright cells drift off the diagonal exactly where the two grammars disagree. Formally, each target position i builds a context vector as a weighted sum of encoder states hj, with weights αij a softmax over alignment scores eij. The heatmap is that matrix.

context contexti = ∑j αij · hj weights αij = softmaxj( eij ) = exp(eij) / ∑k exp(eik) score eij = a( si−1, hj ) (∑j αij = 1)
Attention was invented for this task. Treat the heatmap as diagnostic rather than proof: the weights are an artefact of the model, not a guaranteed account of what it used.

03 The Transformer encoder-decoder

Drop the recurrence, keep the attention. The Transformer was introduced as an MT architecture, and its encoder-decoder form is still the standard shape for dedicated systems.

Three attention blocks, three jobs

  • Encoder self-attention is bidirectional: every source token sees every other, in parallel rather than left-to-right.
  • Decoder masked self-attention lets each target position see only earlier ones, preserving the autoregressive factorisation.
  • Cross-attention is §02's alignment generalised — decoder queries over encoder keys and values, many heads, every layer.

Objective and sub-words

  • Train with teacher forcing on token-level cross-entropy; label smoothing is near-universal because it stops the model over-committing to one of several acceptable renderings.
  • MT needs an open vocabulary in two languages, so sub-word tokenization is load-bearing: a joint vocabulary over both sides lets related languages share pieces and lets names and numbers copy through unchanged.
  • Sub-word choice silently sets sequence length, and therefore cost — unequally across scripts the tokenizer saw little of.
factorisation P(y | x) = ∏t=1T P( yt | y<t, x ) loss L = − ∑t ln P( ytgold | y<tgold, x )
Encoder-decoder is the right bias when there is a fixed source to condition on: bidirectional reading in, autoregressive writing out, cross-attention as the bridge.

04 Decoding: distribution → sentence

The model defines P(y | x) over all outputs; finding the best one exactly is intractable. Every system uses a search heuristic, and the heuristic visibly changes the output.

Greedy vs beam search

  • Greedy takes the argmax each step — fast, but one early wrong word commits the sentence with no way back.
  • Beam search keeps the k best partial hypotheses (typically 4–8) and re-prunes each step, recovering many greedy mistakes for roughly k× the cost.
  • Scores sum log-probabilities, which are negative, so longer hypotheses score worse by construction. Length normalisation divides by a smoothed length term (exponent α ≈ 0.6); a coverage penalty additionally discourages source words no target position attended to.
  • Counter-intuitively, bigger beams can hurt — push k into the hundreds and quality falls, because the mode of a length-biased model is often degenerate.
greedy ŷt = argmaxy ∈ V P( y | ŷ<t, x ) beam score s(y) = ∑t ln P( yt | y<t, x ) normalised s*(y) = s(y) / lp(|y|) lp(n) = ( (5 + n) / 6 )α

Sampling, and when it earns its place

  • Temperature τ rescales logits before the softmax; top-k and top-p truncate the tail. Translation has one intended meaning, so diversity is mostly a liability.
  • Sampling earns its place in synthetic data generation and reranking — including minimum Bayes risk decoding, which picks the candidate most similar to all the others rather than the most probable one.
temperature Pτ(y) = softmax( z / τ ) nucleus sample from smallest S with ∑y ∈ S P(y) ≥ p
Decoding is not a detail: beam width, length penalty and stopping rule move BLEU by several points on an unchanged model. Report them with results.

05 Data, back-translation, multilinguality

Supervision here is sentence pairs, and there are never enough for the pair you care about. Most practical gains come from manufacturing or borrowing data.

Parallel corpora — and filtering them

  • Pairs come from multilingual proceedings, subtitles, localised documentation and web crawls aligned by document then by sentence.
  • Web-mined data is noisy: misalignments, untranslated copies, boilerplate, and machine-translated text that teaches another system's errors. Filtering by length ratio, language ID and bilingual similarity beats simply adding more — and benchmark sentences leaking into crawls is a real contamination hazard.

Back-translation

  • Train a reverse target→source model, run it over abundant monolingual target text, and pair the synthetic source with the real target as extra training data.
  • It works because the noise lands on the input side, where the model is robust, while the side the loss trains to produce stays human-quality. Sampled back-translations usually beat beam-search ones — more varied inputs, stronger signal.

Multilingual models and zero-shot

  • One many-to-many model over dozens of pairs, with a target-language tag telling the decoder what to write. Related languages share representation, so a low-resource pair rides on a high-resource relative — the strongest known lever for the long tail.
  • Zero-shot translation of unseen pairs becomes possible, but fails characteristically as off-target output — fluent text in the wrong language. Fixed capacity also brings the "curse of multilinguality": each added language eventually costs accuracy on the rest.
Reliable order of operations for a weak pair: clean the parallel data, then back-translate, then borrow from related languages. Architecture changes come last.

06 Evaluation

There is no single correct translation, so automatic metrics compare a candidate to references — each encoding a different theory of "close enough". See also Evaluation Metrics.

BLEU — clipped n-gram precision with a length brake

BLEU multiplies a brevity penalty by the geometric mean of modified n-gram precisions pn. "Modified" means each candidate n-gram is clipped to its reference count, so repeating a correct word can't inflate the score; BP punishes candidates shorter than the reference (lengths c, r). It is a corpus-level statistic, so per-sentence BLEU is noisy and best avoided.

precision pn = ( ∑ clipped n-gram matches ) / ( ∑ candidate n-grams ) brevity BP = 1 if c > r BP = exp( 1 − r / c ) if c ≤ r BLEU BLEU = BP · exp( ∑n=1N wn · ln pn ) (wn = 1/N)

Worked example — BLEU on one sentence

Reference “the cat sat on the mat” (r = 6), candidate “the cat sat on a mat” (c = 6) — one wrong word out of six. Counting clipped matches at each order:

One substituted token in six drops the score to roughly 54, because that token breaks four n-grams at once — three of them at higher orders where there is less to match. This steepness is why small BLEU differences are hard to interpret, and why the 4-gram order dominates short sentences.

chrF and the learned metrics

  • chrF scores character n-gram overlap as an F-score with β weighting recall (β = 2 by default) — working below the word level, it degrades gracefully on rich morphology and needs no tokenizer agreement.
  • Learned metrics (COMET, BLEURT and kin) fine-tune a multilingual encoder to regress human quality scores from hypothesis, reference and often the source. They correlate far better with human ranking, and reference-free variants double as quality estimation at inference time — but they are models, with domain dependence and version numbers, so report the exact variant.

Because BLEU takes a geometric mean, a single pn = 0 zeroes the score — hence smoothing for short texts. Use overlap metrics for iteration, learned metrics for ranking, humans before claiming a real win.

07 BLEU, computed live

Pick a candidate against the fixed reference. Precisions, brevity penalty and BLEU are computed in-browser from the tokens — real n-gram clipping, no hard-coded numbers.

Modified n-gram precision → BLEU Each bar is a clipped precision pn; BLEU combines them with the brevity penalty

Four behaviours are worth provoking. The repeated-word candidate shows clipping: padding with "the the the" caps credit at the reference count, so precision collapses. The too short one can hit perfect unigram precision and still be punished by exp(1 − r/c). The reordered one keeps every word but loses higher-order n-grams — that is how BLEU encodes word order without modelling it. And the paraphrase, which a human would accept, scores badly because a synonym shares no n-grams with the reference.

That last case is the whole argument for learned metrics: BLEU cannot tell a different wording from a wrong wording, so adequate translations are penalised for not being the reference.

08 LLM translation, and what's still hard

General LLMs now translate high-resource pairs at or above dedicated systems without task-specific training — and fail in ways those systems did not.

What they bring

  • Context beyond the sentence: a long window carries pronoun antecedents, terminology and register across a document — coherence that sentence-level NMT structurally lacked.
  • Steerability: formality, dialect, glossary terms and "keep the markup" become prompt arguments, and a few example pairs adapt style at inference time.

Characteristic failure modes

  • Hallucinated additions and silent omissions — fluent content with no source, or a dropped clause, worst on noisy or low-probability input. See Hallucination & Factuality.
  • Instruction drift: answering or explaining the source instead of translating it, especially when the source is itself a question.
  • Off-target language on low-resource pairs and non-Latin scripts, plus terminology inconsistency across a long document unless terms are pinned.
  • Cost, latency and non-determinism against a small dedicated model — and §03's sub-word asymmetry makes low-resource languages the most expensive to serve.

Some problems survive every architecture: genuinely low-resource languages, ambiguity the source never resolves, and evaluation itself once systems are good enough that reference overlap stops discriminating between them.

09 Pitfalls & what to remember

Pitfalls

  • Comparing BLEU across tools. Tokenization, casing and smoothing all move the number; under a point of difference usually means nothing.
  • Forgetting the length penalty. Un-normalised beam search favours short output, and the symptom — dropped clauses — looks like a modelling bug.
  • Reading attention maps as explanations. They are an alignment diagnostic, not evidence of what the model relied on.
  • Evaluating sentence-by-sentence when the product is a document — terminology and pronoun consistency are invisible to sentence-level metrics.

What to remember

  • Reordering plus rewriting, not lookup — alignment, morphology, idiom and missing grammatical information are the recurring four.
  • Attention was born here, and cross-attention in a Transformer encoder-decoder is its mature form.
  • Decoding is part of the system: beam search with length normalisation by default; sampling for data and reranking.
  • Data beats architecture on the long tail — filter, back-translate, then transfer multilingually.
  • Metrics disagree with humans predictably: overlap punishes paraphrase, learned metrics carry their own biases.
