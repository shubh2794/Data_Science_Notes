Machine Translation · the source paper

Learning to Align & Translate (2014)

The paper that introduced attention for translation. Instead of squeezing the source into one vector, the encoder keeps one annotation per source word, and at every output step the decoder computes a fresh weighted average of them, the weights coming from a small learned alignment model. The general mechanism and the score-function comparison are on Alignment, Scores & Scaling; this page reads the paper as a paper. It covers the hypothesis it set out to test, the exact encoder, alignment model and decoder, the training setup, the BLEU table against the fixed-vector baseline and a phrase-based system, the sentence-length result, and the alignments it learned without supervision.

01 The hypothesis: the fixed-length vector is the bottleneck

The paper starts from a conjecture, stated in its abstract: "the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck in improving the performance of this basic encoder–decoder architecture". An encoder that must compress every sentence into one vector of the same size will find this hardest on long sentences, especially ones longer than those in the training corpus.

  • The proposal. Let the model "(soft-)search" the source for the parts relevant to the next target word, without any hard segmentation. The source becomes a sequence of vectors, and the decoder picks a different mixture of them for each word it writes. In the paper's words this relieves the encoder "from the burden of having to encode all information in the source sentence into a fixed-length vector".
  • The experiment is built to test the hypothesis. Two models are trained on the same data with the same sizes: RNNencdec, the fixed-vector encoder–decoder, and RNNsearch, the proposed model. Each is trained twice, once on sentences of up to 30 words and once on sentences of up to 50 (the -30 and -50 suffixes). If the bottleneck matters, the gap between them should grow with sentence length (§07).
  • Context. The fixed-vector design being argued against is the one on Seq2seq: Deep LSTM Translation. The paper's own baseline is a smaller gated-unit version of it, not the deep LSTM system. The general version of the bottleneck argument, as a rank bound, is on the fixed-context bottleneck section.

02 The encoder: one annotation per word, from both directions

A left-to-right RNN's state at word j summarises only the words up to j. The paper wants each annotation to summarise the words around j, both before and after it. So it uses a bidirectional RNN and concatenates the two states at each position.

the annotations, as the paper defines themforward →hⱼ = f( →hⱼ₋₁ , xⱼ ) j = 1 → T_x backward ←hⱼ = f( ←hⱼ₊₁ , xⱼ ) j = T_x → 1 annotation hⱼ = [ →hⱼ ; ←hⱼ ] concatenation: 1000 + 1000 = 2000 numbers per word
  • Why the annotation is local. The paper relies on "the tendency of RNNs to better represent recent inputs". So hⱼ concentrates on the words around xⱼ while still carrying some of the whole sentence. That is what makes it usable as the representation of word j.
  • Sizes. Each direction has 1000 hidden units, so the annotations are 2000-dimensional (our arithmetic from the two 1000-unit directions). Word embeddings are 620-dimensional.
  • The memory now grows with the sentence. A 50-word source is represented by 50 × 2000 = 100 000 numbers instead of one fixed vector (our arithmetic). That is the whole point. When bidirectional encoders are legitimate, and when they leak the future, is covered on the bidirectional-RNN section.

03 The alignment model: score, normalise, blend, once per output word

At output step i the decoder has its previous state sᵢ₋₁. A small feed-forward network scores how well each annotation hⱼ matches it. A softmax turns the scores into weights, and the weighted sum of annotations becomes the context vector cᵢ for this word only. The alignment model is trained jointly with everything else, from the translation loss alone.

  • Two readings of the weights. The paper reads αᵢⱼ as the probability that target word yᵢ is "aligned to, or translated from" source word xⱼ. It reads cᵢ as the expected annotation under that distribution. It also calls this a mechanism of attention: the decoder decides which parts of the source to pay attention to.
  • Not a latent variable. Classical alignment models treat alignment as a hidden variable to be marginalised. Here it is computed directly and is differentiable, so the gradient of the translation loss trains the alignment model by plain backpropagation. Why this "soft" choice is the trainable one is on What "soft" alignment means.
  • Why additive, and what came after. The score is a one-hidden-layer network, now called additive attention. How it compares with dot-product scoring, the odd-function property of the bias-free form, and why the dot product later won on speed are all covered on Score functions and not repeated here.

04 The decoder: gated units, a context in every gate, and a maxout readout

The decoder is a recurrent network built from gated hidden units, with an update gate and a reset gate (the unit is on LSTM & GRU Cells). The context cᵢ enters the candidate state and both gates, and it enters the output layer again.

  • The baseline is the same model with attention switched off. The appendix notes that fixing cᵢ to the forward encoder's last state →h_T_x turns this into the fixed-vector encoder–decoder. That is why the RNNencdec vs RNNsearch comparison isolates attention so cleanly.
  • Maxout takes the larger of each pair of values, a learned piecewise-linear activation. The readout sees the previous word, the decoder state and the current context, so it can draw on the source directly when choosing the word.
  • Sizes as given in the paper: n = 1000 hidden units for encoder directions, decoder and alignment model; embeddings m = 620; maxout l = 500. The paper does not print a total parameter count.

05 The training setup

Data

  • WMT'14 English→French parallel corpora: Europarl (61M words), news commentary (5.5M), UN (421M) and two crawled corpora (90M and 272.5M), 850M words in total. A data-selection method reduced this to 348M words. No monolingual data was used.
  • Development set: news-test-2012 and news-test-2013 concatenated. Test set: news-test-2014, 3003 sentences, not seen in training.
  • Vocabulary: a shortlist of the 30 000 most frequent words in each language. Every other word is mapped to [UNK]. Tokenised with a standard script; no lowercasing or stemming.

Optimisation

  • Attention wins at both training lengths: +7.57 BLEU at 30 words and +8.93 at 50 on all sentences (our differences). In the paper's words, "the RNNsearch-30 even outperforms RNNencdec-50".
  • Against the phrase-based system. On all sentences the longest-trained model is still 4.85 BLEU behind (28.45 vs 33.30). On sentences with no unknown words it is 0.52 ahead (36.15 vs 35.63). The paper's claim is that RNNsearch is "as high as" the conventional system "when only the sentences consisting of known words are considered", and it calls this "a striking result".
  • The comparison favours the statistical system. It also used a separate 418M-word monolingual corpus, which the neural models did not.
  • The gap between the columns is the UNK problem. A shortlist of 30 000 words means many test sentences contain at least one [UNK]. The neural models lose far more BLEU on those sentences than the phrase-based system, whose two scores differ by only 2.3 (§10).

07 BLEU against sentence length: the test of the hypothesis (Figure 2)

Figure 2 plots each model's test BLEU against sentence length. Its shape is the paper's main evidence for the hypothesis in §01. Both fixed-vector models fall sharply as sentences get longer. RNNsearch-30 falls much less. RNNsearch-50, in the paper's words, "shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more".

  • What the figure does not give. The paper prints no per-length numbers, so this page does not attach any. The claim to take away is qualitative. The fixed-vector models degrade with length; the attention model trained on 50-word sentences does not.
  • The examples behind it. The appendix shows long test sentences translated by both models. In one, the fixed-vector model translates correctly up to a certain phrase and then drifts away from the meaning of the source. In another, it starts to drift after about 30 words. RNNsearch-50 keeps the whole meaning in both.
  • Training length is part of the result. The -30 models never saw a sentence longer than 30 words. So the right-hand end of the figure measures generalisation to lengths the model was not trained on, as well as capacity.
Reconciling this with the deep-LSTM result. Seq2seq: Deep LSTM Translation reported little degradation with length from a fixed vector. Its model, though, had four 1000-cell layers, an 8000-number state, a reversed source and a five-model ensemble. The baselines here are much smaller. Read together (our interpretation), the bottleneck is real and grows with sentence length, a large enough model can hide much of it, and attention removes the constraint rather than working around it.

08 What the learned alignments look like (Figure 3)

Figure 3 plots the weights αᵢⱼ from RNNsearch-50 as grey-scale matrices. One is an arbitrary sentence. The other three were sampled at random from test sentences of 10 to 20 words with no unknown words. No alignment supervision was given at any point.

  • Mostly monotonic. The strongest weights lie along the diagonal, as expected between two languages with largely the same word order.
  • Non-trivial reorderings where the languages differ. French and English usually order adjectives and nouns differently. The paper's example: the model translates "European Economic Area" as "zone économique européenne". It aligns "zone" with "Area" first, then jumps back two words to finish the phrase, which is the reversed block in the figure in §03.
  • Diagnostic, not explanation. These are four chosen matrices, illustrations rather than measurements. How much attention maps can tell us about what a model relied on was debated later (beyond this paper; see Attention Cost & Limits).

09 Why soft alignment, in the paper's own argument

The paper argues for soft alignment on linguistic grounds as well as for its trainability. Its example is "the man" translated as "l' homme".

hard alignment
[the] → [l'], [man] → [homme]
Any hard alignment pairs the words one-to-one. That does not help translation, because the article depends on the word after it: "the" can become le, la, les or l', and which one is right depends on the noun.
soft alignment
[l'] attends to both [the] and [man]
The decoder can look at both source words when writing the article, and so can pick the elided form that "homme" requires.
a second benefit
Phrases of different lengths
Source and target phrases need not have the same length, and nothing has to be mapped "to or from nowhere", the NULL token that classical word-alignment models need.

The mathematical side of the same choice, that an argmax has no useful gradient so a hard version would need sampling-based estimators, is worked through on Alignment, Scores & Scaling.

10 Limitations the paper states, and what followed

  • Unknown and rare words. The conclusion names this as the main challenge left: "One of challenges left for the future is to better handle unknown, or rare words." Table 1 shows its cost: on all sentences the best model trails the phrase-based system by 4.85 BLEU, and on known-word sentences it leads by 0.52.
  • Quadratic scoring cost. Every target word scores every source word. The paper accepts this because "most of input and output sentences are only 15–40 words" in translation, and notes it could limit use on other tasks.
  • A comparison under unequal conditions. The phrase-based system had extra monolingual data. The neural models were trained on a fixed 30 000-word shortlist for about 110 hours each (252 for the longest run).
  • Later developments (beyond this paper). Sub-word vocabularies largely solved the [UNK] problem (Tokenization). Dot-product and multi-head attention replaced the additive scorer, and self-attention replaced the recurrent encoder and decoder (The Original Transformer). The decoder-to-encoder attention introduced here survives as cross-attention.

11 Common misconceptions

"Attention beat phrase-based translation." It matched it on sentences without unknown words. On all sentences the best model was 4.85 BLEU behind, and the phrase-based system also used extra monolingual data.
"The context vector is computed from the current decoder state." From the previous one, sᵢ₋₁. The context then feeds the gates, the candidate state and the readout for step i. Variants that score against sᵢ came later (beyond this paper).
"Each annotation is the encoder state at word j." It is two states concatenated, forward and backward, so each annotation summarises both sides of the word.
"The alignments were supervised." They were learned only from the translation loss. That the matrices look like word alignments is a finding, not a training target.
"The fixed-vector baseline was the deep LSTM system." It was a same-size gated-unit encoder–decoder, which is what makes the RNNencdec vs RNNsearch comparison a controlled one.

12 Takeaways

  • Hypothesis: a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck, worst on long sentences. The test is RNNencdec vs RNNsearch, trained on sentences of up to 30 and up to 50 words.
  • Encoder: a bidirectional RNN with 1000 units per direction. Each annotation is hⱼ = [→hⱼ ; ←hⱼ].
  • Alignment: eᵢⱼ = vₐᵀ tanh(Wₐ sᵢ₋₁ + Uₐ hⱼ), softmax to αᵢⱼ, cᵢ = ∑ⱼ αᵢⱼ hⱼ, all learned jointly.
  • Decoder: gated units with cᵢ in every gate, and a maxout readout (l = 500) over a 30 000-word shortlist.
  • Setup: 348M words after data selection, minibatch 80, Adadelta (ε = 10⁻⁶, ρ = 0.95), clipping at 1, about 5 days per model, beam search.
  • Results: RNNsearch-50* scores 28.45 / 36.15 BLEU (all / no UNK) against 33.30 / 35.63 for the phrase-based system. RNNsearch shows no deterioration with length where RNNencdec collapses.
