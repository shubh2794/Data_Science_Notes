Machine Translation · the source paper

Seq2seq: Deep LSTM Translation (2014)

The paper that showed a plain neural network could translate a large benchmark better than a phrase-based statistical system: one deep LSTM reads the sentence into a fixed-dimensional vector, a second deep LSTM writes the translation from it. The general encoder–decoder idea, teacher forcing and beam search are taken apart on Bidirectional, Stacked & Seq2seq RNNs; this page reads the paper as a paper. It covers the exact model and its 384M parameters, the source-reversal trick and the paper's explanation for it, the training recipe, the BLEU tables with and without a statistical system, and what the analysis of long sentences, rare words and sentence vectors did and did not show.

01 The framing: map a sequence to a vector, and a vector to a sequence

The paper's problem statement is about shapes. Feed-forward networks need inputs and outputs of fixed dimension, and translation has neither. Its answer is to use one LSTM to read the input one token at a time into a fixed-dimensional vector representation, and a second LSTM to generate the output from that vector. Nothing else connects source and target.

what is modelled, as the paper writes itp( y₁, …, y_T′ | x₁, …, x_T ) = ∏ₜ₌₁…T′ p( yₜ | v, y₁, …, yₜ₋₁ ) v = the last hidden state of the encoder LSTM after reading x₁ … x_T (a fixed-dimensional vector, whatever T is) each p( yₜ | … ) is a softmax over the whole target vocabulary; every sentence ends in an end-of-sentence symbol, which lets the model define a distribution over sequences of ALL lengths.
  • Training maximises the average log-probability of the correct translation T given the source S, (1/|𝒮|) ∑ log p(T | S) over the training set 𝒮. Decoding looks for T̂ = argmax_T p(T | S), approximately (§05). The general mechanics, including why maximum likelihood means teacher forcing, are on the RNN architectures page.
  • The headline claim. On WMT'14 English→French the LSTM system alone reached 34.8 BLEU, against 33.3 for a phrase-based statistical baseline on the same data. The paper calls this the first time a pure neural translation system beat a phrase-based baseline by a sizeable margin on a large task. Used to rerank the baseline's 1000 best hypotheses, it reached 36.5 (§06).
  • The bottleneck is a design choice, not an oversight. The whole source has to fit into one vector. The paper expected this to hurt on long sentences and found far less damage than expected (§07). The next paper on this topic, Learning to Align & Translate, made removing that bottleneck its central argument.

02 The model: two different deep LSTMs, and where 384M parameters come from

Three choices separate this model from the generic seq2seq diagram. The encoder and decoder are two different LSTMs with separate weights. Both are deep, with four layers. And the source is fed reversed (§03). The paper's reason for separate networks: they add parameters at negligible computational cost, and they make it natural to train on several language pairs at once.

The totals only add up if the paper's "recurrent connections" counts each layer's input weights as well as its hidden-to-hidden weights, and if the 8000 numbers are the hidden state plus the memory cell of all four layers. That is our reading, not a statement in the paper. Either way, the point stands: five-sixths of the parameters are word embeddings and the softmax. The part that actually models sequences is 64M.

  • Depth mattered. The paper found deep LSTMs "significantly" outperformed shallow ones, with each additional layer cutting perplexity by nearly 10%. No per-depth table is given. (How stacking changes path lengths and time constants is on the stacked-RNN section.)
  • Why an LSTM. The paper's stated reason is the long-range temporal dependencies between a source sentence and its translation, which plain RNNs learn poorly. The cell itself is on LSTM & GRU Cells.
  • The softmax was the expensive part. An 80 000-way softmax at every output position is why it got four of the eight GPUs to itself (§04). Later systems shrank the output vocabulary with sub-word units (beyond this paper; see Tokenization).

03 Reversing the source, and the "minimal time lag" explanation

The single most effective trick in the paper costs nothing: feed the source sentence backwards and leave the target alone. Instead of mapping a, b, c to α, β, γ, the network maps c, b, a to α, β, γ. Nothing else changes.

The text's 25.9 and Table 1's 26.17 for the forward model do not match. The paper does not say why; they are probably different runs or decoding settings, but that is a guess. Both versions show a gain of 4.4 to 4.7 BLEU from reversal.

The explanation the paper offers, and how far it goes

The paper says it has no complete explanation. Its proposed one is the minimal time lag. With the source in order, every source word is far from its translation: the whole rest of the sentence sits between them. Reversal leaves the average distance between corresponding words unchanged, but it puts the first few source words right next to the first few target words. The "minimal time lag" falls to almost nothing, so backpropagation has an easy time "establishing communication" between source and target, and the paper suggests this improves performance overall.

the claim in numbers, for an n-word pair aligned word-for-word (our arithmetic)time step of source word xⱼ: j (in order) n − j + 1 (reversed) time step of target word yᵢ: n + i (the decoder writes after the encoder has read all n) in order dist( xᵢ → yᵢ ) = (n + i) − i = n for every i reversed dist( xᵢ → yᵢ ) = (n + i) − (n − i + 1) = 2i − 1 1, 3, 5, …, 2n − 1 average in order n reversed (1/n) · ∑ᵢ (2i − 1) = n ← unchanged minimum in order n reversed 1 ← the "minimal time lag" maximum in order n reversed 2n − 1 ← the last word pays

The average stays at exactly n for any one-to-one alignment, not only a monotone one. Each source and each target position is used once, so the mean of i + j − 1 is always n (our arithmetic). So reversal does not make dependencies shorter on average. It makes them unequal, short at the start and long at the end. The bet is that getting the first words right gives the decoder a good start it can build on. That ties in with the paper's separate observation that reversed models did "much better on long sentences".

Source reversal: distance from each source word to its translation drag the sentence length · switch on local reordering (adjacent pairs swapped, as with adjective–noun order) · our arithmetic, not paper data

Choose fully inverted order to see the trick backfire. If the target already reverses the source, reading the source backwards turns a constant lag into the worst possible pattern. Reversal is a bet on the language pair (English and French are mostly monotone), not a general law. Why long time lags hurt gradient-based training is on Vanishing & Exploding Gradients.

04 The data and the training recipe

Data

  • WMT'14 English→French, trained on a cleaned, "selected" subset of 12M sentences: 348M French words and 304M English words. The test set is the WMT'14 test set ("ntst14").
  • Fixed vocabularies: the 160 000 most frequent English words and the 80 000 most frequent French words. Every other word becomes a single UNK token (§09).

Optimisation

as publishedinitialisation every parameter ~ Uniform( −0.08, +0.08 ) optimiser plain SGD, no momentum, learning rate 0.7 schedule after 5 epochs, halve the learning rate every half epoch; 7.5 epochs in total batch 128 sequences gradient clipping g = gradient / 128; s = ‖g‖₂; if s > 5: g ← 5g / s batching sentences in a batch have roughly equal length → 2× speed-up what those numbers imply (our arithmetic)updates per epoch 12 M / 128 ≈ 94 000 → about 700 000 updates in 7.5 epochs learning rate 0.7 until epoch 5, then 0.35, 0.175, 0.0875, … ≈ 0.02 by the end (if the halvings fall at epochs 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5 and 7.0 — our reading)
  • Why clip. The paper notes that LSTMs do not suffer from vanishing gradients the way plain RNNs do, but can have exploding ones, so it imposes a hard limit on the gradient norm. Clipping in general is on Optimizers.
  • Why bucket. Most sentences are short and a few are long. A random batch of 128 wastes most of its computation on padding, because the batch is only finished when its longest sentence is. Grouping by length halved training time.

Hardware: model parallelism across eight GPUs

  • A single-GPU C++ implementation managed about 1 700 words per second, which the paper found too slow.
  • The final setup used one machine with 8 GPUs. Each of the four LSTM layers ran on its own GPU and passed activations to the next as soon as they were computed. The other four GPUs split the softmax, each multiplying by a 1000 × 20 000 slice (4 × 20 000 = 80 000, the target vocabulary).
  • Throughput was 6 300 words per second (English and French combined) at batch 128, and training took about ten days.

05 Decoding: a small left-to-right beam, and ensembles

The decoder is the most basic one possible: left-to-right beam search. It keeps the B most probable prefixes, extends each by every word in the vocabulary, and keeps the best B again. A hypothesis that emits the end-of-sentence symbol leaves the beam and joins the set of complete hypotheses. Beam mechanics and length normalisation are on Machine Translation. What this paper adds is how little search it needed.

  • Beam 1 already "performs well", and beam 2 gives most of the benefit of beam search, in the paper's words. The table below shows the size of that benefit for a 5-model ensemble: 33.00 at beam 1, 34.50 at beam 2, 34.81 at beam 12.
  • Ensembles average the predictions of several LSTMs that differ only in random initialisation and the random order of minibatches. The paper points out that its best cheap setting, 5 reversed LSTMs with beam 2, costs less to decode than a single LSTM with beam 12 and is far more accurate.
  • Scoring. BLEU is cased, computed with the standard multi-bleu script on tokenised output. That script reproduces the baseline's 33.3. It gives the best WMT'14 system 37.0 rather than the 35.8 published on the evaluation campaign's site, so compare the numbers here only with each other. (How BLEU is computed: Evaluation Metrics.)

The 28.45 for the attention-based model is the same figure that paper reports for its longest-trained model, RNNsearch-50* (next page). The two systems differ in size, vocabulary and decoding, so the gap between them is not a clean architecture comparison.

06 Rescoring a statistical system's 1000-best list

The second way to use the network leaves the statistical system in charge of search. The baseline produces its 1000 best hypotheses per sentence. The LSTM scores each one, and the final score is an even average of the baseline's score and the LSTM's log-probability. The best hypothesis under the averaged score is the output.

  • Within 0.5 BLEU of the best published system on this task. In this setting the gain from reversal shrinks to 0.24 BLEU (35.61 → 35.85). The statistical system's hypotheses already have sensible word order, so the LSTM is ranking them rather than building them.
  • The oracle row is a ceiling. If a perfect scorer picked the best of the 1000 hypotheses for each sentence, BLEU would be about 45. Rescoring at 36.5 recovers less than a third of the gap between 33.3 and that ceiling (our arithmetic).

07 Long sentences and rare words

The fixed vector should fail on long sentences. Not much space, a lot of content. The paper's Figure 3 says it did not, at least not badly: "There is no degradation on sentences with less than 35 words, there is only a minor degradation on the longest sentences." The paper says it was "surprised" by how well the LSTM translated very long sentences, and puts it down partly to reversal.

  • How it was measured. The left panel of Figure 3 plots BLEU for the 5-LSTM ensemble and the baseline against test sentences sorted by length. The paper quotes no per-bucket numbers, so none are given here.
  • Rare words. The right panel sorts sentences by their "average word frequency rank", so sentences full of rarer words sit further right, and plots both systems. Again no numbers are quoted, so read it only as a shape. Rare words are where a fixed vocabulary is expected to bite, because many of them are UNK (§09).
  • Qualitative evidence. Table 3 shows a few long test sentences with the LSTM's translations next to the references. These are examples, not measurements.
A tension with the next paper, and why it is not a contradiction. Learning to Align & Translate reports fixed-vector baselines whose BLEU collapses as sentences get longer. That paper's baseline was a much smaller encoder–decoder: 1000 hidden units in the encoder and in the decoder, a 30 000-word vocabulary, the source read in order, no ensemble. The model here had four layers of 1000 cells, 8000 numbers of state, a reversed source and a 5-model ensemble. Reading the two together suggests (our interpretation) that the bottleneck is real but its size depends on model capacity and training tricks. A big enough model hides it for sentence-length inputs, and attention removes it.

09 Limitations the paper states, and the ones that followed

  • Out-of-vocabulary words. Any word outside the 160 000 / 80 000 lists becomes UNK. A reference containing a real word where the system wrote UNK loses n-gram matches, so the headline 34.8 is, as the abstract says, "penalized on out-of-vocabulary words". The paper stresses that it beat the baseline "despite its inability to handle out-of-vocabulary words".
  • A limited vocabulary is how the conclusion describes the model, and it is also why the softmax was so expensive (§02, §04).
  • No search over alignments. The decoder sees the source only through one vector, and the only help with word order is the fixed reversal heuristic (§03).
  • Later developments (beyond this paper). Sub-word vocabularies removed most UNKs and shrank the softmax (Tokenization). Attention removed the single-vector channel (next page). The Transformer then removed recurrence (The Original Transformer). The decoder-as-conditional-language-model framing, trained by maximum likelihood and decoded by beam search, survived all three.

10 Common misconceptions

"Reversal shortens the dependencies." It shortens the first ones and lengthens the last. The average distance between corresponding words is unchanged, as the paper itself says. What drops is the minimal lag (§03).
"A single LSTM beat phrase-based translation." An ensemble of five did. The best single reversed LSTM scored 30.59 against the baseline's 33.30. Ensembles took it to 34.81.
"The fixed vector made long sentences fail." Not in this paper. It reports no degradation below 35 words and only minor degradation beyond. The collapse often quoted comes from smaller baselines in the next paper (§07).
"Most of the 384M parameters are the LSTMs." 64M are. The other 320M are the two embedding tables and the output softmax (§02).
"Big beams were essential." Beam 2 gave most of the gain. An ensemble at beam 2 was cheaper than a single model at beam 12, and better.

11 Takeaways

  • Architecture: two separate 4-layer LSTMs, 1000 cells and 1000-d embeddings, 160k/80k vocabularies, an 80k-way softmax, 384M parameters (64M recurrent), an 8000-number sentence state.
  • Reversal: perplexity 5.8 → 4.7, BLEU 25.9 → 30.6. The explanation is a lower minimal time lag with an unchanged average.
  • Recipe: Uniform(±0.08) initialisation, SGD at 0.7 halved every half epoch after epoch 5, 7.5 epochs, batch 128, clipping at norm 5, length bucketing, 8 GPUs, about ten days on 12M sentence pairs.
  • Results: 34.81 BLEU from 5 reversed LSTMs with beam 12 against a 33.30 phrase-based baseline. Rescoring that baseline's 1000-best list gives 36.5, within 0.5 of the best WMT'14 result.
  • Analysis: little degradation with sentence length, more trouble with rare words, and sentence vectors that track word order more than voice.
  • Limits: UNK for every out-of-vocabulary word, and a single vector as the only bridge between the two languages.
