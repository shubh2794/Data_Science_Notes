Retrieval-Augmented Generation · deep dive

RAG-Sequence & RAG-Token: the original RAG model

The 2020 study that named retrieval-augmented generation did not paste search results into a prompt. It built one probabilistic model: a dense retriever over a Wikipedia index supplies latent documents, a pretrained seq2seq generator conditions on each, and the documents are marginalised out — either once for the whole output (RAG-Sequence) or afresh at every token (RAG-Token). Both halves are fine-tuned end to end from input–output pairs alone. This page covers the two formulations, how they are trained and decoded, what the reported results do and do not show, and how today's "RAG" drifted away from it.

01 Two kinds of memory, and the gap the study aimed at

A pretrained language model stores knowledge in its weights — parametric memory. It cannot easily expand or revise that memory, cannot show where an answer came from, and may hallucinate. A non-parametric memory — a retrievable collection of text — can be revised, expanded, inspected and interpreted directly. The study's goal was a hybrid of the two for generation.

  • Knowledge-intensive tasks are defined in the study as tasks a human could not reasonably be expected to do without access to an external knowledge source: open-domain question answering, fact verification, factual question generation.
  • What existed. Two earlier hybrids (REALM and ORQA) combined a masked language model with a differentiable retriever, but had only been explored for open-domain extractive QA — the answer is a span copied out of a retrieved passage.
  • What was missing. The same hybrid for sequence-to-sequence models, which the study calls the "workhorse of NLP": a general-purpose fine-tuning recipe that works on any input-to-output-text task, where both the parametric memory (a pretrained seq2seq model) and the non-parametric memory (a dense Wikipedia index with a pretrained retriever) come pre-trained and pre-loaded, so knowledge access works before any task training.

02 The components: a dense retriever, a 21M-passage index, a seq2seq generator

Two parameterised pieces: a retriever p_η(z | x) that returns a top-k truncated distribution over passages z for an input x, and a generator p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) that produces the next output token given the input, one passage and the tokens so far.

the retriever: a bi-encoder scored by inner productp_η(z | x) ∝ exp( d(z)ᵀ q(x) ) d(z) = BERT_d(z) document encoder (BERT-base) q(x) = BERT_q(x) query encoder (BERT-base) top-k( p_η(· | x) ) = the k passages with the largest d(z)ᵀ q(x) = a maximum inner product search (MIPS), solvable approximately in sub-linear time the generator: a pretrained encoder–decoderp_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) BART-large (≈400M parameters) input = x and z simply concatenated
  • Retriever initialisation. Both encoders and the document index come from a pre-trained dense passage retriever (DPR) bi-encoder, which had been trained to retrieve passages containing answers to TriviaQA and Natural Questions questions. Remember this when reading "no retrieval supervision" later (§04, §13). The bi-encoder idea itself — encode query and document independently so all document vectors can be precomputed — is on the hub's embeddings & similarity section; BERT is on its model page.
  • Generator. BART-large is a seq2seq transformer pre-trained with a denoising objective (its objective is compared with others on the NLP models page). Any encoder–decoder could fill this slot; the study chose BART for its state-of-the-art results on a range of generation tasks, where it outperforms comparably sized T5 models (T5). The study calls the BART parameters θ the parametric memory and the document index the non-parametric memory.
  • Normalisation. The distribution over passages is top-k truncated: only the k retrieved passages receive probability, so in practice the softmax runs over the k retrieved scores.

03 The two formulations: one document per answer, or one per token

The retrieved passage is a latent variable: nobody says which passage should produce the answer, so the model sums over the top-k. The two variants differ only in where the sum sits relative to the product over output tokens.

RAG-Sequence — the same document generates the whole outputp_RAG-Sequence(y | x) ≈ ∑_(z ∈ top-k(p(·|x))) p_η(z | x) · p_θ(y | x, z) = ∑_(z ∈ top-k) p_η(z | x) · ∏ᵢ₌₁ᴺ p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) mix over documents OF whole-sequence products RAG-Token — a different document may generate each tokenp_RAG-Token(y | x) ≈ ∏ᵢ₌₁ᴺ ∑_(z ∈ top-k(p(·|x))) p_η(z | x) · p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) product over tokens OF per-token mixtures classification: a target of length oneN = 1 ⇒ ∑_z p_η(z|x) p_θ(y₁|x,z) in both ⇒ RAG-Sequence ≡ RAG-Token
  • RAG-Sequence is a mixture model over complete answers: pick a document from the retriever's distribution, then generate the entire answer from it. An answer scores well if some single high-prior passage supports all of it.
  • RAG-Token re-chooses the document at every step, so it can stitch content from several passages into one output — useful when an answer has two separate facts that live in two passages. Note that the mixing weights are the retriever prior p_η(z|x) at every step; they are not updated by the tokens already generated.
  • Neither is larger in general. Both are proper distributions over outputs, so if one gives a particular y more probability, it gives some other output less. Which one prefers a given answer depends on how the support is spread across documents — the explorer below makes this concrete.

Which document generated which token: the posterior

document posteriors implied by each model (our derivation from the definitions)RAG-Token, at step i: p(z | x, y₁:ᵢ) = p_η(z|x) · p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) / ∑_z′ p_η(z′|x) · p_θ(yᵢ | x, z′, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) RAG-Sequence, after the prefix y₁:ᵢ: p(z | x, y₁:ᵢ) ∝ p_η(z|x) · ∏ⱼ₌₁ⁱ p_θ(yⱼ | x, z, y₁:ⱼ₋₁) evidence accumulates; one document tends to win

The RAG-Token posterior is what the study plots for its Jeopardy example (§07): it can jump from one document to another between tokens. The RAG-Sequence posterior can only sharpen as the prefix grows, because every token's likelihood multiplies into the same per-document product.

Marginalisation explorer: the same retrieved documents, scored by both formulations illustrative toy values, not reported numbers — try k = 1, then the "two passages" preset, then "one passage has it all"
  • When they agree. Exactly at k = 1 (one term in every sum). Also when every document gives the same token probabilities (then both reduce to ∏ᵢ p(yᵢ) — the "no passage helps" preset with zero noise), and approximately when the retriever puts almost all its mass on one document (high sharpness).
  • When RAG-Token is higher. When different tokens are supported by different documents: each document alone makes the other's fact unlikely, so every per-document product is small, while the per-token mixture can take each fact from wherever it lives.
  • When RAG-Sequence is higher. When one document supports the whole answer: the mixture of products keeps that document's large product weighted by its prior once, while the product of mixtures pays the prior's dilution at every content token. (Formally, a mean of products versus a product of means; with perfectly correlated support Jensen's inequality favours RAG-Sequence.)

04 Training: maximum marginal likelihood, a frozen index, no labels on retrieval

Given input–output pairs (xⱼ, yⱼ), the study minimises the negative marginal log-likelihood of each target with Adam. Retriever and generator are trained jointly, and nothing tells the model which passage it should have retrieved — the retrieved document is a latent variable.

the objectiveminimise ∑ⱼ − log p(yⱼ | xⱼ) with p = p_RAG-Sequence or p_RAG-Token what reaches the retriever (our derivation, scores s_z = d(z)ᵀq(x) over the top-k)RAG-Sequence: ∂ log p(y|x) / ∂ s_z = p(z | x, y) − p_η(z | x) RAG-Token: ∂ log p(y|x) / ∂ s_z = ∑ᵢ [ p(z | x, y₁:ᵢ) − p_η(z | x) ] posterior above prior → raise this passage's score posterior below prior → lower it

So the retriever is taught by the generator: passages that made the target answer more likely than the retriever expected get pulled toward the query; passages that did not, get pushed away. The gradient flows into q(x) through the scores of the k passages that were actually retrieved; the top-k selection itself is a discrete search and is not differentiated (general knowledge about this setup).

Why the document encoder and the index stay frozen

  • Cost. Updating BERT_d would change every one of the 21M passage vectors, so the index would have to be periodically re-encoded and rebuilt during training — which REALM does during its pre-training.
  • The study's position. "We do not find this step necessary for strong performance": the document encoder and index are kept fixed, and only the query encoder BERT_q and the BART generator are fine-tuned.
  • What the ablation actually tests. The reported retrieval ablation (§09) freezes the whole retriever, including the query encoder, and shows learning the query encoder helps on almost every task. There is no reported ablation that also trains the document encoder; the claim that doing so is unnecessary rests on the study's statement, not on a table.

Training set-up (as reported)

  • k ∈ {5, 10} retrieved documents per training query; test-time k chosen on dev data. Training with 5 or 10 made no significant difference (§09).
  • Mixed-precision training on 8 GPUs with 32 GB each, though the study notes training and inference can run on one GPU.
  • For Natural Questions and WebQuestions, each of a question's multiple reference answers is used as a separate training pair, which gave a small accuracy increase. TriviaQA answer candidates are dropped if they do not occur in the top 1000 retrieved documents.
  • The small sets — CuratedTrec and WebQuestions — are initialised from the Natural Questions RAG model.

05 Decoding: one beam search, or one per document

At test time each variant needs its own approximation to argmax_y p(y | x). RAG-Token factorises over tokens, so ordinary beam search works. RAG-Sequence does not, so it needs a beam per document and a way to score hypotheses that some beams never produced.

RAG-Token: a standard autoregressive decoder with a mixed transitionp′_θ(yᵢ | x, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) = ∑_(z ∈ top-k) p_η(z | x) · p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) plug p′_θ into any standard beam decoder RAG-Sequence: thorough decodingfor each retrieved z: beam search scored by p_θ(yᵢ | x, z, y₁:ᵢ₋₁) → hypotheses Y = union of all beams' hypotheses for each y ∈ Y and each z whose beam did NOT contain y: run an extra forward pass to get p_θ(y | x, z) p(y | x) = ∑_z p_η(z | x) · p_θ(y | x, z) → return the best y RAG-Sequence: fast decodingassume p_θ(y | x, z) ≈ 0 whenever y was not produced by z's beam ⇒ no extra forward passes once Y is built

The cost of thorough decoding grows with |Y|, which is large for long outputs: in the worst case it is k beam searches plus up to k · |Y| additional scoring passes (our arithmetic). Fast decoding drops those passes and in exchange under-counts hypotheses that only some documents found.

06 Open-domain QA: generating answers beats extracting them

Questions and answers are treated as plain text pairs and the model is trained directly on the answers' negative log-likelihood. RAG is compared with closed-book generators (T5, no retrieval) and with open-book retrieve-and-extract systems (REALM, DPR). Metric: exact match.

TriviaQA has two conventional test sets: the open-domain one used by DPR (the public web development split) and the official Wiki test set used by the T5 closed-book work. The study reports both, and notes its scores are much higher on the Wiki set, which it attributes to those questions being simpler to answer from Wikipedia. The state of the art claim on TriviaQA holds only on the T5-comparable (Wiki) split — DPR's 57.9 on the open test is above both RAG models.

  • No re-ranker, no extractive reader. The DPR system re-ranks passages with a BERT cross-encoder and then extracts a span; RAG does neither and still scores higher on three of the four datasets. And unlike REALM and T5 + SSM, it needed no salient-span-masking pre-training.
  • Generation can use clues. A passage that hints at the answer without containing it verbatim can still raise the probability of the right answer — impossible for a span extractor. On Natural Questions, when the correct answer is in none of the retrieved documents, RAG still answers 11.8% correctly; an extractive model would score 0% by construction.
  • Parameters. The appendix compares with the T5 model nearest in size: T5-large (770M parameters) scores 28.9 exact match on Natural Questions, against 44.5 for RAG-Sequence with 626M reported trainable parameters — and T5-11B, the best closed-book model, has 11B. That is about 18× fewer trainable parameters than T5-11B for about 10 points more (our arithmetic).
  • The variants are close. RAG-Sequence is ahead on four of the five columns, RAG-Token on WebQuestions; the largest gap is 2.2 points, on CuratedTrec, the smallest dataset.

07 Knowledge-intensive generation: abstractive QA and Jeopardy questions

Two generation tasks against a BART-large baseline with no retrieval, trained by the study for comparison. RAG is more accurate on the automatic metrics, judged more factual and more specific by people, and produces more diverse text without any diversity-promoting decoding.

Abstractive QA: MS-MARCO NLG v2.1, without the gold passages

The task supplies ten search-engine passages per question and a full-sentence reference answer. RAG uses only the questions and answers and retrieves from Wikipedia instead, turning it into open-domain abstractive QA. That handicaps it: some questions cannot match the reference without the gold passages ("What is the weather in Volcano, CA?"), and some are not answerable from Wikipedia at all — there RAG falls back on parametric knowledge.

Why RAG-Token wins here: the "Hemingway" posterior

Jeopardy clues often combine two separate pieces of information, which suits per-token marginalisation. The study plots the RAG-Token document posterior for each generated token with input "Hemingway" and 5 retrieved documents, for a clue naming two of his novels. The posterior is high for document 2, which mentions "The Sun Also Rises", when generating "Sun", and document 1 dominates when "A Farewell to Arms" is generated. Then, after the first token of each title, the posterior flattens: the rest of the title does not depend on any particular document.

Parametric and non-parametric memory working together. The study's reading is that the generator completes the titles from its own weights. As a check, the BART-only baseline given the partial clue ending in "The Sun" completes "The Sun Also Rises", and given a partial clue ending in "A" completes "A Farewell to Arms" — the titles are stored in BART's parameters. Retrieval steers which knowledge comes out; the weights supply the rest. The toy explorer in §03 has the same structure: the second and fourth tokens are predicted by every document.

08 Fact verification without evidence labels: FEVER

FEVER asks whether a claim is supported or refuted by Wikipedia, or whether there is not enough info — retrieval plus an entailment-style judgement (see textual entailment). Each label is mapped to a single output token, so RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token are the same model here (§03). Crucially, the study trains on claim–label pairs only, with no supervision on which evidence to retrieve.

test label accuracyFEVER 3-wayFEVER 2-way (supports / refutes)
previous best76.8 — a pipeline trained with retrieval supervision92.2 * — RoBERTa given the gold evidence sentence
BART (no retrieval)64.081.1

  • Within 4.3 points of the 3-way state of the art, which used complex domain-specific pipelines, substantial engineering and intermediate retrieval supervision; within 2.7 points of the 2-way model that was handed the gold evidence, while RAG gets only the claim and retrieves its own. Against BART alone, retrieval adds 8.5 and 8.4 points (our arithmetic).
  • Does it retrieve the right evidence anyway? Comparing article titles of RAG's retrieved documents with the gold evidence annotations: the top retrieved document comes from a gold article in 71% of cases, and a gold article is in the top 10 in 90%.
  • How classification is done. Following BART's practice, the model re-generates the claim and classifies from the final hidden state, then marginalises across documents to get class probabilities. FEVER's second sub-task, extracting evidence sentences, was not attempted because FEVER uses a different Wikipedia dump.

In the dev-set ablation (§09) FEVER is the one task where a plain BM25 retriever does best — the study suggests because FEVER claims are heavily entity-centric and so suit word-overlap retrieval.

How many documents: k at training and at test

  • Training: models trained with 5 or 10 retrieved documents showed no significant difference.
  • Test, open-domain QA: retrieving more documents improves Natural Questions results monotonically for RAG-Sequence, while RAG-Token peaks at 10 documents. (This is why the QA test numbers use 50 documents for RAG-Sequence but 15 for RAG-Token, §05.)
  • Test, MS-MARCO: more documents give RAG-Token higher ROUGE-L at the expense of BLEU-1; the effect is less pronounced for RAG-Sequence.

10 Index hot-swapping: updating knowledge without retraining

Because knowledge lives partly in a text index, it can be changed at test time. A parametric-only model like T5 or BART would need further training to learn that the world has changed; RAG only needs a different index.

The experiment: build a second index from an older Wikipedia dump (December 2016), list 82 world leaders who changed between 2016 and 2018, and ask the Natural Questions RAG model "Who is {position}?" (for example, "Who is the President of Peru?") with each index.

accuracy2016 leaders (the right answer in 2016)2018 leaders (the right answer in 2018)
2016 index70%4%
2018 index12%68%
  • The diagonal is the point. Same model, same weights — swap the index and the answer follows the index. Matched index and question year: 70% and 68%; mismatched: 12% and 4%.
  • It is not a guarantee. Even with the matched index, about three in ten answers are wrong. Hot-swapping updates what the model can retrieve; the answer is still only as good as retrieval and generation on that question.
  • The study frames the index as both human-readable (a form of interpretability) and human-writable (the memory can be edited directly), because it holds raw text rather than learned vectors of facts.

11 Limitations and broader impact the original study states

training
Retrieval can collapse
In preliminary experiments on tasks such as story generation, the retriever learned to return the same documents regardless of input; the generator then learned to ignore them and the model behaved like BART. Suggested causes: less explicit need for factual knowledge, or longer targets giving less informative gradients to the retriever.
supervision
Not retrieval-label-free from scratch
Fine-tuning uses no retrieval labels, but the retriever starts from a bi-encoder trained with retrieval supervision on Natural Questions and TriviaQA — the study notes this when comparing with DPR.
knowledge source
The index has its own errors and biases
Wikipedia, or any external knowledge source, will probably never be entirely factual and free of bias.
misuse
The concerns of any language model
Arguably to a lesser extent than for unconstrained language models: abuse, faked or misleading news and social media content, impersonation, and automated spam or phishing; and job automation in the longer term.
null retrieval
No explicit "nothing useful" option
A REALM-style null document (learned embedding, static bias, or predicted logit) did not improve performance and was dropped. On MS-MARCO the model instead learned to retrieve a fixed set of documents for questions unlikely to benefit from retrieval.
future work
Joint pre-training
Whether retriever and generator can be pre-trained jointly from scratch, with a BART-like denoising or other objective, is left open — as is how best to combine the two memories.

On the positive side the study argues that grounding in real factual knowledge makes generations hallucinate less, and gives more control and interpretability — with the example of a medical index for open-domain medical questions.

12 In practice: how "RAG" diverged from this model

Everything above is the original model and its reported evidence. This section is later practice and general knowledge — fast-moving and not part of the original study's claims.

  • What survived. The architecture split — a dense bi-encoder over a precomputed ANN index feeding a generator — and the two arguments for it: knowledge can be updated by changing the index (§10), and retrieved text gives provenance.
  • What mostly did not. Joint fine-tuning and latent-document marginalisation. With a frozen general-purpose LLM there is no gradient path back to the retriever, so retrievers are tuned separately, if at all.
  • A well-known successor, one line: fusion-in-decoder (FiD) encodes each retrieved passage together with the question separately and lets the decoder attend over all of them jointly, instead of marginalising over per-passage outputs — closer in spirit to how passages are combined in today's long-context prompts.

13 Common misconceptions

"RAG means pasting retrieved passages into an LLM's prompt." Not originally. The model that introduced the name fine-tunes the generator and query encoder jointly and treats the passage as a latent variable that is summed out (§03–§04). Prompt-stuffing a frozen LLM is a later, simpler pattern (§12).
"RAG-Token is the better variant because it can use several documents." Neither dominates. RAG-Token wins on Jeopardy question generation; RAG-Sequence wins on most QA columns, on MS-MARCO at test and on diversity (§06–§07). The explorer in §03 shows why: which is higher depends on how the evidence is spread across passages.
"RAG learns to retrieve with no retrieval supervision at all." Only during fine-tuning. The retriever is initialised from a dense passage retriever trained with retrieval supervision on Natural Questions and TriviaQA (§02, §11).
"The whole retriever is trained end to end." The document encoder and the index are frozen. Only the query encoder and the generator are updated; re-encoding 21M passages during training was judged unnecessary (§04).
"A generator with retrieval can only repeat what the passages say." It also draws on its weights. On Natural Questions RAG answers 11.8% correctly when the answer is in none of the retrieved documents (§06), and the "Hemingway" posterior shows titles being completed from parametric memory (§07).
"Swapping the index keeps the model fully up to date." It changes what can be retrieved, not how well. Matched index and question year gave 70% and 68% — far above the 12% and 4% mismatched, but far from 100% (§10).
"Marginalising over more documents always helps." Not for RAG-Token. Its open-domain QA performance peaks at 10 retrieved documents, while RAG-Sequence keeps improving (§09).

14 Takeaways

  • Hybrid memory: a pretrained seq2seq generator (parametric) plus a dense index of 21M 100-word Wikipedia passages searched by inner product (non-parametric), both pre-trained before any task training.
  • Retrieved passages are latent: RAG-Sequence is ∑_z p_η(z|x) ∏ᵢ p_θ(yᵢ|…) — one document per answer; RAG-Token is ∏ᵢ ∑_z p_η(z|x) p_θ(yᵢ|…) — one per token. Identical for one-token targets and for k = 1.
  • Training maximises the marginal likelihood of the target; the retriever learns from the gap between each passage's posterior and its prior. The document encoder and index stay frozen.
  • Decoding: RAG-Token is a normal beam search on the mixed transition; RAG-Sequence needs a beam per document, with thorough or fast scoring of the union.
  • Results: state of the art on three open-domain QA sets plus the T5-comparable TriviaQA split without re-ranker or extractive reader; more factual, specific and diverse generation than BART; FEVER within 4.3 points of supervised pipelines with no evidence labels.
  • The index is the update mechanism: swapping a 2016 for a 2018 Wikipedia index moved world-leader answers with it, no retraining.
  • Modern "RAG" kept the retriever-plus-index architecture and dropped the joint training and marginalisation.
