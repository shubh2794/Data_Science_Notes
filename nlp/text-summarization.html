01 The task: extractive vs abstractive

Summarization shortens text while keeping what matters. The field splits on a single question: do you reuse the source's own sentences, or write new ones?

  • Extractive: pick a subset of the original sentences and stitch them together. Always faithful to the source's wording — it can only copy — but choppy and unable to merge ideas.
  • Abstractive: generate a fresh summary that may paraphrase, fuse, and compress. Fluent and concise, but can hallucinate facts never in the source.
  • Single- vs multi-document: summarize one article, or fuse many (news clusters, reviews) where redundancy and contradiction must be resolved.

One trade-off: extractive buys faithfulness by giving up fluency; abstractive buys fluency at the risk of faithfulness. The whole field lives on this axis.

02 Extractive: scoring & graph centrality

Extractive summarization is a ranking problem: score every sentence, take the top-k. The interesting question is what makes a sentence "important".

Sentence scoring & centroid

  • Feature scoring: position (early sentences matter), length, presence of title words, TF-IDF weight of the words it contains.
  • Centroid: represent the document by the average (centroid) of its sentence vectors (from Embeddings or TF-IDF), then rank sentences by similarity to that centroid — the most "central" sentences win.

TextRank — PageRank over sentences

  • Build a graph: each sentence is a node; connect two with an edge weighted by their similarity (word overlap or cosine).
  • Run the PageRank recurrence: a sentence is important if it is similar to other important sentences. Iterate to convergence, then take the highest-scoring nodes.
  • Unsupervised — no labels needed — which is why it remains a strong, fast baseline.
S(vi) = (1 − d) + d · Σvj ∈ In(vi) ( wji / Σvk ∈ Out(vj) wjk ) · S(vj) d = damping factor (≈ 0.85) wij = similarity(sentence i, sentence j)

03 Abstractive: seq2seq & LLMs

Abstractive summarization generates the summary token by token, conditioned on the source — the same encoder-decoder machinery as Machine Translation.

  • Encoder-decoder: the encoder reads the document into hidden states; the decoder attends over them and emits the summary autoregressively — see Encoder vs Decoder Models.
  • Copy / pointer mechanism: lets the decoder point back and copy a source token (names, numbers) instead of generating from the vocabulary — crucial for rare words and factual fidelity.
  • Modern LLMs: an instruction-tuned decoder summarizes zero-shot from a prompt; long-context models can ingest whole reports at once.
P(y | x) = Πt P(yt | y<t, Encoder(x)) copy gate: p(w) = pgen · Pvocab(w) + (1 − pgen) · Σi: xi=w αi
Pointer intuition: a generation gate pgen mixes "speak from the vocabulary" with "copy from the source via attention weights α". Names and figures get copied; connective glue gets generated.

04 Faithfulness & hallucination

A fluent summary can still be wrong. Faithfulness — staying true to the source — is the hardest open problem in abstractive summarization, and ties directly to Hallucination & Factuality.

  • Intrinsic hallucination: the summary contradicts the source (says "rose" when the source said "fell").
  • Extrinsic hallucination: the summary adds facts not in the source at all — possibly true in the world, but unsupported by the document.
  • Why it happens: the decoder is a language model first; it prefers fluent, plausible continuations over source-faithful ones, especially when the source is sparse.
  • Mitigations: copy mechanisms, faithfulness-aware training, entailment-based checks (does the source entail each summary sentence?), and grounding against a Knowledge Graph of source facts.

05 Evaluation: ROUGE & its limits

The standard summarization metric is ROUGE — recall-oriented n-gram overlap against reference summaries. It is cheap and correlated-enough to dominate, but blind to meaning.

ROUGE-N = ( Σ overlapping n-grams between candidate & reference ) / ( Σ n-grams in reference ) ROUGE-L = LCS(candidate, reference) / length(reference) (longest common subsequence)
  • ROUGE-N: n-gram recall (ROUGE-1 = unigrams, ROUGE-2 = bigrams). Rewards covering the reference's words.
  • ROUGE-L: longest common subsequence — credits in-order overlap without requiring contiguity.
  • Recall-oriented by design: it asks "how much of the reference did you cover?", which suits summarization (don't miss key content).
  • Limits: pure surface overlap — a perfect paraphrase using different words scores low. This is exactly why the field is moving to embedding-based and LLM-judge metrics, covered in NLP Evaluation Metrics.
