Topic Modeling

Given a pile of documents and no labels, find the themes running through them. Topic models treat each document as a mixture of topics and each topic as a distribution over words. This page builds that idea up from a matrix factorisation (LSA, NMF), to a probabilistic model (pLSA), to a full Bayesian generative model (LDA) with its Dirichlet priors and inference algorithms. It then covers how to evaluate topics, how to choose their number, and the embedding-and-clustering pipeline that is now the usual starting point.

01 The task: thematic structure without labels

A topic model compresses a corpus into a few interpretable themes and says, for every document, how much of each theme it contains. Nobody supplies the themes; they are inferred from which words co-occur across documents.

  • Two objects come out. A set of K topics, each a probability distribution over the vocabulary (topic 3 puts high mass on goal, team, coach, match), and for each document a topic mixture, a probability vector over the K topics (this article is 70% sport, 25% finance, 5% everything else).
  • The signal is co-occurrence. If flour, oven and bake keep showing up in the same documents, a model that explains documents as mixtures of a few word distributions is pushed to put them in the same topic. Nothing about word meaning is supplied, which is why the output needs a human to name it.
  • Mixed membership, not clustering. Clustering gives each document one label. A topic model lets a single document belong partly to several themes, which suits real text: a news story about a footballer's tax case is both sport and law.
  • Uses. Exploring a corpus too big to read (archives, support tickets, survey answers, papers), tracking how themes rise and fall over time, producing compact document features for a downstream model, and organising search results by theme.
the decomposition every topic model aims atP(w | d) = ∑k=1..K P(w | topic k) · P(topic k | d) in wordsword distribution of a document = its topic mixture × the topics' word distributions
One idea: the term-document matrix is approximately a product of two thin matrices, words × topics and topics × documents. LSA, NMF, pLSA and LDA differ mainly in what they demand of those two factors (orthogonal, non-negative, probabilities, probabilities with priors).

02 Bag-of-words and the term-document matrix

Every classical topic model starts by throwing away word order. A document becomes a vector of word counts, and the corpus becomes one large, very sparse matrix.

  • Bag-of-words. Document d is the count vector xd ∈ ℕV, where V is the vocabulary size and xw,d is how often word w occurs in d. "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are the same bag. Building the vocabulary (tokenising, lower-casing, removing stop words, lemmatising) is covered in Text Preprocessing.
  • The term-document matrix. Stack the count vectors as columns: X is V × D. A corpus of 100,000 documents with a 50,000-word vocabulary is a 5-billion-cell matrix, typically more than 99.9% zeros, so it is always stored sparse.
  • Weighting. Raw counts let frequent words dominate. LSA and NMF usually factorise a TF-IDF or log-scaled matrix instead. pLSA and LDA are models of counts and must be given raw integer counts: their likelihood is a product over word tokens, and a TF-IDF weight is not a number of tokens.
  • The exchangeability assumption. Treating word positions within a document as order-free (and documents as order-free) is the formal version of bag-of-words. It is what makes the models tractable and also their main limitation: no syntax, no negation, no phrases unless you add n-grams to the vocabulary.
term-document matrixX ∈ ℝV×D, Xw,d = count (or weight) of word w in document d document lengthNd = ∑w Xw,d total tokens N = ∑d Nd
The term-document view is the bridge between linear algebra and probability on this page: LSA factorises X as a matrix; pLSA and LDA explain each column of X as draws from a mixture of distributions.

03 LSA / LSI: topics as singular vectors

Latent semantic analysis (also called latent semantic indexing in retrieval) takes the truncated singular value decomposition of the term-document matrix. The top k singular directions are the "concepts" that explain the most variation in word usage.

truncated svd, rank kX ≈ Xk = Uk Σk Vkᵀ Uk : V × k, Σk : k × k diagonal, Vk : D × k eckart–youngXk = argminrank(B) ≤ k ‖X − B‖F error² = ∑i>k σi² fold in a new document qq̂ = Σk⁻¹ Ukᵀ q (its coordinates in concept space, comparable to rows of Vk)
  • What each factor means. Column j of Uk is a direction in word space: a weighted list of words that tend to vary together, the closest thing LSA has to a topic. The singular value σj is how much of the matrix that direction accounts for. Row d of Vk gives document d's coordinates on the k concepts; the rows of UkΣk do the same for words.
  • Why it helps retrieval. In the raw matrix "car" and "automobile" never match because they rarely appear together in the same document. They do appear with the same other words, so they load on the same singular directions, and their projections are close. Truncation keeps the shared structure and discards the idiosyncratic noise; Eckart–Young guarantees it is the best rank-k approximation in squared error. The derivation and the low-rank figure are in The Singular Value Decomposition.
  • Relation to PCA. LSA is PCA without the centring: the SVD of the uncentred (and usually TF-IDF-weighted) matrix. Centring a sparse matrix would make it dense, so it is skipped. See SVD and PCA.
  • The interpretability problem. Singular vectors are orthogonal and have entries of both signs. A "topic" reads as "+0.31 goal, +0.28 team, −0.22 stock, −0.19 rate": a contrast between two themes, not a theme. A document coordinate of −0.4 has no meaning as an amount of anything. Orthogonality also forces later directions to be whatever is left over after earlier ones, so only the first few look sensible.
  • Cost and choice of k. A truncated sparse SVD (Lanczos or randomised) is fast and deterministic: no sampling, no local optima, the same answer every run. Typical k for retrieval is 100–300; the singular value spectrum gives no sharp cut-off.

NMF: the non-negative alternative

  • Non-negative matrix factorisation drops orthogonality and instead requires both factors to be non-negative: X ≈ WH with W ≥ 0 (V × k) and H ≥ 0 (k × D). Since nothing can be subtracted, every document has to be built up additively from parts, and the parts come out as recognisable themes: a column of W is a weighted bag of words, a column of H is how much of each theme a document uses.
  • It is fitted by alternating updates (multiplicative updates, or alternating non-negative least squares) to a local optimum, so it depends on initialisation; an SVD-based start (NNDSVD) makes it nearly deterministic.
  • With the generalised KL divergence as the loss, NMF optimises the same objective as pLSA (next section) once the columns are normalised to sum to one. NMF with squared loss on TF-IDF is a strong, cheap baseline, often competitive with LDA on long documents.
nmf, squared lossminW ≥ 0, H ≥ 0 ‖X − WH‖F² nmf, kl lossminW ≥ 0, H ≥ 0 ∑w,d [ Xw,d log( Xw,d / (WH)w,d ) − Xw,d + (WH)w,d ] multiplicative update (squared loss)H ← H ⊙ (WᵀX) ⊘ (WᵀWH) W ← W ⊙ (XHᵀ) ⊘ (WHHᵀ)

04 pLSA: a probabilistic factorisation

Probabilistic LSA replaces the SVD with a latent-variable model of counts. Every word token is explained by a hidden topic z, and the two factors become genuine probability distributions.

modelP(d, w) = P(d) · ∑z P(w | z) P(z | d) ⇔ P(w | d) = ∑z P(w | z) P(z | d) log-likelihoodℒ = ∑d ∑w n(d, w) · log ∑z P(w | z) P(z | d) (+ a constant from P(d)) e-stepP(z | d, w) = P(w | z) P(z | d) / ∑z′ P(w | z′) P(z′ | d) m-stepP(w | z) ∝ ∑d n(d, w) P(z | d, w) P(z | d) = ∑w n(d, w) P(z | d, w) / Nd
  • Why EM. The log of a sum does not separate, but if we knew each token's topic it would. EM alternates between computing the posterior responsibility of each topic for each (document, word) pair, and re-estimating both distributions from those soft counts. Each iteration cannot decrease ℒ; it converges to a local maximum, so restarts matter. The same pattern is EM for Gaussian mixtures, with multinomials in place of Gaussians.
  • What it fixed. Everything is non-negative and sums to one, so topics are readable as word distributions and mixtures as proportions. The likelihood is the right loss for counts, which squared error on X is not.
  • Overfitting. There is a separate parameter vector P(z | d) for every training document, so the parameter count is K·V + K·D and grows linearly with the corpus. With nothing tying the document mixtures together, the model memorises; the original work needed "tempered" EM (flattening the E-step posteriors with an exponent below one) and early stopping on held-out data to control it.
  • No generative model for new documents. P(z | d) is defined only for d in the training set. A new document has no mixture until you run extra EM steps on it with P(w | z) held fixed ("folding in"), which is a heuristic, not inference in the model. This gap is exactly what LDA closes by placing a prior on the mixtures.
pLSA → LDA in one line: make the per-document mixture P(z | d) a random variable θd drawn from a shared Dirichlet prior, instead of a free parameter. The parameter count then stops growing with D, and any document, seen or unseen, has a well-defined posterior over its mixture.

05 LDA: the generative story

Latent Dirichlet allocation describes, step by step, how a corpus could have been written: pick topics, pick each document's mix of topics, then for each word pick a topic from the mix and a word from that topic. Fitting the model means running this story backwards.

for each topic k = 1..Kφk ~ DirichletV(β) (a distribution over the V words) for each document d = 1..Dθd ~ DirichletK(α) (its topic proportions) for each position n = 1..Ndzd,n ~ Categorical(θd) wd,n ~ Categorical(φzd,n)
  • Three levels. φ is shared by the whole corpus, θd is per document, zd,n is per token. Only the words w are observed. A token's topic is chosen independently each time, so one document can use many topics, and the same word type can be assigned to different topics in different places (the polysemy handle: "bank" in a river document versus a loan document).
  • Hyperparameters. α (length K, often symmetric) shapes the document mixtures, β (length V, almost always symmetric) shapes the topics. The original formulation treated the topic-word matrix as a fixed parameter; putting the Dirichlet(β) prior on it ("smoothed LDA") is the version used everywhere now and the one the Gibbs sampler below assumes.
  • The inference problem. We want the posterior p(θ, φ, z | w, α, β). Its normaliser p(w | α, β) requires summing over KN joint topic assignments (coupled through θ and φ) and is intractable, so LDA is always fitted approximately, either by sampling (§07) or by variational optimisation (§08).
joint distributionp(w, z, θ, φ | α, β) = ∏k p(φk | β) · ∏d [ p(θd | α) · ∏n θd, zd,n · φzd,n, wd,n ] marginal for one document, topics fixedp(wd | α, φ) = ∫ p(θ | α) ∏n ( ∑k θk φk, wd,n ) dθ
Read the inner sum: ∑k θk φk,w is exactly pLSA's ∑z P(z | d) P(w | z). LDA keeps the same mixture per token and integrates over the mixture weights instead of fitting them.

06 What α and β do: Dirichlet sparsity

The Dirichlet is a distribution over probability vectors, so it lives on the simplex. Its concentration parameter decides whether typical draws are spread evenly or piled onto a few coordinates. That single knob is how LDA encodes "a document is about a few topics" and "a topic uses a few words".

dirichlet density on the simplexp(θ | α) = Γ(α₀) / ∏k Γ(αk) · ∏k θkαk − 1 α₀ = ∑k αk mean and varianceE[θk] = αk / α₀ Var[θk] = αk(α₀ − αk) / ( α₀²(α₀ + 1) ) conjugacyθ ~ Dir(α), counts n | θ ~ Multinomial(θ) ⇒ θ | n ~ Dir(α + n)
  • Below one, sparse. With a symmetric α < 1 the density blows up at the edges and corners of the simplex, so most draws put nearly all their mass on one or two coordinates. With α = 1 the density is flat: every mixture equally likely. With α > 1 the density peaks at the centre and draws are near-uniform mixtures. The mean is the same in all three cases; only the spread changes.
  • For documents (α). Small α says each document uses a handful of topics. Raising it smears every document across all topics and makes topics harder to tell apart. Common defaults are α = 1/K, or α = 50/K from early Gibbs-sampling work (which is above one whenever K < 50, so not sparse), or an α learned from the data.
  • For topics (β). β lives on a V-dimensional simplex with tens of thousands of coordinates, so values like 0.01–0.1 are typical. Small β gives peaked topics with few high-probability words; larger β smooths topics and tends to merge them into fewer, broader ones.
  • Pseudo-counts. Conjugacy means α and β act as imaginary counts added to every cell before normalising. You can see this directly in the Gibbs update below: nd,k + α and nk,w + β. Learning an asymmetric α (some topics a priori more common) is a well-documented improvement; an asymmetric β is rarely worth it.
  • The full development of the Dirichlet (density, normalised-gamma construction, conjugacy to the multinomial and a simplex figure with independent sliders) is in Mixtures, hierarchies and the Dirichlet.

Each dot below is one draw θ ~ Dir(α, α, α) on the 3-topic simplex: a corner is a document about one topic only, the centre is an even three-way mix. Drag α from 0.05 to 50 and watch the cloud move from the corners to the centre; the bars show five of the draws as document mixtures.

07 Collapsed Gibbs sampling

Integrate θ and φ out analytically (conjugacy makes this exact), leaving only the discrete topic assignments z. Then resample one token's topic at a time from its conditional given all the others. The result is a short loop over counts.

collapsed conditional for token i (word w, document d)P(zi = k | z−i, w) ∝ ( nd,k−i + α ) · ( nk,w−i + β ) / ( nk−i + Vβ ) countsnd,k = tokens in d assigned to k nk,w = tokens of word w assigned to k nk = ∑w nk,w (−i : with token i removed) point estimates from one sampleφ̂k,w = ( nk,w + β ) / ( nk + Vβ ) θ̂d,k = ( nd,k + α ) / ( Nd + Kα )
  • Reading the update. The first factor is "how much does this document already use topic k"; the second is "how much does topic k already like this word". A token goes where both are high. The rich-get-richer dynamics are what pull co-occurring words into the same topic. The left factor has no denominator because Nd − 1 + Kα is the same for every k and cancels in the normalisation.
  • The algorithm. Assign every token a random topic and fill the three count tables. Then sweep: for each token, decrement its counts, compute the K unnormalised probabilities, sample a new topic, increment. Cost is O(K) per token per sweep; samplers that exploit sparsity in the counts bring this close to O(1) amortised on large K.
  • Using the chain. Discard a burn-in (hundreds of sweeps on real corpora), then read φ̂ and θ̂ from the counts. Averaging over several samples is only valid within one mode, because topic labels are exchangeable: two runs may find the same topics in a different order (label switching), so never average topic 3 of run A with topic 3 of run B.
  • Monitoring. The collapsed joint log p(w, z) has a closed form in log-gamma functions of the counts; it rises and then fluctuates around a plateau. A plateau is evidence of mixing within a mode, not of having found the best one.
collapsed joint (monitored in the figure)log p(w | z) = K·[ log Γ(Vβ) − V log Γ(β) ] + ∑k [ ∑w log Γ(nk,w + β) − log Γ(nk + Vβ) ] andlog p(z) = D·[ log Γ(Kα) − K log Γ(α) ] + ∑d [ ∑k log Γ(nd,k + α) − log Γ(Nd + Kα) ]

08 Variational inference, at intuition level

Instead of sampling from the posterior, pick a simple family of distributions and find the member closest to it. Inference becomes optimisation, which is deterministic for a given initialisation, easy to parallelise, and can be run on mini-batches of a stream.

evidence lower boundlog p(w) = ELBO(q) + KL( q ‖ p(θ, z | w) ) ELBO(q) = Eq[ log p(w, θ, z) ] − Eq[ log q(θ, z) ] mean-field family, per documentq(θ, z) = q(θ | γ) · ∏n q(zn | rn) q(θ | γ) = Dir(γ), q(zn) = Cat(rn) coordinate updatesrn,k ∝ φk, wn · exp( Ψ(γk) − Ψ(∑j γj) ) γk = αk + ∑n rn,k
  • Why a bound. log p(w) is fixed by the data, and KL is never negative, so pushing the ELBO up is the same as pushing q toward the true posterior. The ELBO is computable because it only involves expectations under q, which we chose to be tractable.
  • Mean-field. Pretend the latent variables are independent given the variational parameters. γ plays the role of "pseudo-counts for this document's topics" and rn is a soft topic assignment for token n; the updates have the same shape as the Gibbs conditional, with soft expected counts in place of hard sampled ones (Ψ is the digamma function, which turns the expected log of a Dirichlet coordinate into a closed form).
  • Variational EM. E-step: iterate the two updates per document to convergence. M-step: update the topics from the soft counts ∑d,n rd,n,k·[wd,n = w], plus β in the smoothed model (where φ also gets its own Dirichlet factor). Online variational inference applies the M-step as a stochastic-gradient-style step on each mini-batch, which is what lets LDA run over corpora that do not fit in memory.
  • Sampling or optimisation? Mean-field ignores posterior correlations and tends to under-estimate uncertainty; it can also get stuck. Collapsed Gibbs is simpler to implement and asymptotically exact but slow to mix and hard to diagnose. In practice both produce topics of similar quality when tuned; libraries pick whichever scales better for their use case.
The same ELBO and mean-field machinery reappears in variational autoencoders; neural topic models put an encoder network in place of the per-document γ optimisation ("amortised" inference).

09 Evaluating topics: perplexity versus coherence

There is no ground truth, so evaluation splits into two questions: does the model predict held-out text well (perplexity), and do its topics make sense to people (coherence and human judgments)? The two often disagree.

held-out perplexityperplexity(Dtest) = exp( − ∑d log p(wd) / ∑d Nd ) lower is better umass coherence, top words w₁..wM ordered by probabilityCUMass = ∑m=2..M ∑l=1..m−1 log( ( D(wm, wl) + 1 ) / D(wl) ) npmi of a word pairNPMI(wi, wj) = log( P(wi, wj) / ( P(wi) P(wj) ) ) / ( − log P(wi, wj) ) ∈ [−1, 1] npmi coherence of a topicCNPMI = ( 2 / (M(M − 1)) ) · ∑i<j NPMI(wi, wj)
  • Perplexity is the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood per token: a perplexity of 900 means the model is as uncertain as a uniform choice among 900 words. Computing p(wd) for a held-out document requires integrating over its unknown θd, so it is itself an estimate; "document completion" (infer θ from half the words, score the other half) avoids leaking the scored words into θ.
  • Why perplexity misleads. In large human studies where people had to spot an intruder word inserted into a topic's top words, or an intruder topic in a document's list, models with better held-out likelihood frequently produced topics people found less interpretable. Likelihood rewards spreading probability to cover every word well, including through many fine-grained, overlapping or junk topics; humans reward a short, distinctive list of top words. Optimising perplexity therefore tends to push K up past the point where topics stay readable.
  • UMass coherence uses document co-occurrence counts from the training corpus: D(w) is the number of documents containing w, D(w, w′) the number containing both. It asks whether lower-ranked top words appear in documents that contain the higher-ranked ones. Values are typically negative; closer to zero is better. Being intrinsic, it can be fooled by words that co-occur only because of the training corpus' quirks.
  • NPMI coherence estimates probabilities from co-occurrence in sliding windows (often 10 words) over a large external reference corpus such as an encyclopaedia dump. Normalising PMI by −log P(wi, wj) bounds it in [−1, 1]: 1 for words that only ever occur together, 0 for independence, −1 for words never seen together (a small smoothing constant avoids log 0). Of the automatic scores, NPMI over a reference corpus has tended to track human ratings best.
  • Diversity. A model can score high coherence by repeating one good topic. Report topic diversity as well: the fraction of unique words among all topics' top-25 words.
Practical rule: use perplexity to check that inference converged and to compare inference settings at fixed K; use coherence (NPMI with a reference corpus when possible) plus diversity plus reading the topics to compare models and choose K. Coherence scores are noisy proxies too: always look at the top words of every topic before trusting a number.

10 Choosing the number of topics

K is a resolution knob, not a fact about the corpus. A news archive genuinely has 10 broad themes and 200 narrow ones. The right K is the one whose topics are coherent, stable, and useful for what you are doing.

  • Coherence curves. Fit models over a grid of K, several seeds each, and plot mean coherence. Look for a peak or the start of a plateau, not the single maximum, since adjacent values usually differ by less than the seed-to-seed spread.
  • Stability. Refit with different seeds (or on bootstrap samples) and match topics across runs by their top-word overlap (Jaccard). At a good K the same topics come back; past it, the extra topics are different every time. Stability is cheap to compute and surprisingly informative.
  • Task-driven. If topics feed a classifier, choose K by downstream validation accuracy. If they are for exploration, choose by how many themes a person can usefully inspect, and consider a hierarchy (fit a small K, then sub-model each topic).
  • Let the model choose. The hierarchical Dirichlet process replaces the fixed K with a nonparametric prior that adds topics when the data support them; it still has a concentration hyperparameter that effectively sets the granularity. The embedding pipeline of §11 makes the same move with a minimum cluster size.

The figure fits LDA (collapsed Gibbs, 80 sweeps, three seeds per point) to a synthetic corpus of 240 documents generated from a true LDA with a known number of topics, for every K from 2 to 10. Held-out perplexity falls steeply until the true K and then is nearly flat: its minimum does sit at the true K on this clean synthetic data, but K = 10 is only a few percent worse, so on real data, where the curve is noisier and keeps drifting down, it gives little guidance. Cross-seed stability peaks at the true K for every setting. UMass coherence is the noisiest of the three: it finds the true K when there are four topics but overshoots by one to three for the others, because splitting a block-structured topic in two still yields word lists that co-occur. Switch the true K to compare; this is why the advice above is to read several criteria together, never one maximum.

11 The embedding pipeline: embed, reduce, cluster, name

The common modern recipe (BERTopic-style) drops the generative model. Embed each document with a pretrained sentence encoder, reduce dimension, cluster densely packed regions, and only then go back to the words to describe each cluster.

  • 1 · Embed. Map each document to a dense vector with a sentence-embedding model (sentence and contrastive embeddings), typically 384–1024 dimensions. Documents with no shared words but the same meaning now land close together, which bag-of-words methods cannot do.
  • 2 · Reduce. Project to a handful of dimensions (5–10 is typical) with a neighbour-graph method such as UMAP. Density-based clustering degrades badly in hundreds of dimensions, where all distances look alike. UMAP preserves local neighbourhoods better than PCA on curved embedding manifolds; it is stochastic, so fix its seed.
  • 3 · Cluster. Run HDBSCAN, the hierarchical, variable-density descendant of DBSCAN. It needs no K, only a minimum cluster size, and it labels points in no dense region as noise, topic −1. On real corpora the outlier topic can hold a large share of the documents, a third or more is not unusual; it can be shrunk by reassigning outliers to the nearest topic, at the price of purity. k-means can be swapped in when every document must get a topic.
  • 4 · Name. Concatenate all documents of each cluster into one "class document" and score words with class-based TF-IDF. The highest-scoring words describe the topic; nearby topics can then be merged by the similarity of their c-TF-IDF vectors. An LLM is now often asked to turn the top words and a few representative documents into a short label (practice as of 2026, not part of the method).
class-based tf-idf, term t, cluster cc-TF-IDFt,c = tft,c · log( 1 + A / ft ) wheretft,c = frequency of t in cluster c ft = frequency of t across all clusters A = average number of words per cluster
  • Reading c-TF-IDF. It is ordinary TF-IDF with clusters in place of documents. A word frequent in this cluster scores high (tf), unless it is frequent everywhere (ft large makes the log term small). The 1 + keeps the weight positive.
  • What you give up. Each document gets a single cluster, not a mixture (a soft distribution can be approximated afterwards by comparing a document's embedding or its word windows to every topic). Topic words come from a separate step and are not what the clustering optimised, so a cluster can be tight in embedding space and still have a muddy word list. Results depend on the encoder, on UMAP's neighbour count and on HDBSCAN's minimum cluster size.
  • What you gain. Semantic grouping, no stop-word-sensitive likelihood, works on short texts and many languages (with a multilingual encoder), and no K to fix in advance.
Both families answer the same question with different objects: LDA explains tokens with a probabilistic model; the embedding pipeline groups documents geometrically and explains the groups afterwards. When each document really has one theme, the second is usually better; when documents are long and genuinely mixed, the first still earns its place.

12 Short texts: tweets, titles, queries

LDA learns topics from words that co-occur within a document. A ten-word post has almost no co-occurrence to learn from, so classical topic models on short text tend to produce noisy, incoherent topics.

  • Sparse co-occurrence. In a 10-token document, each word pair is seen together once or not at all, and the document's mixture θd is estimated from 10 samples. Both the topic and the mixture posteriors stay close to their priors.
  • One topic per document. A short text is usually about one thing. Models that assume exactly that (a Dirichlet–multinomial mixture, fitted by a collapsed Gibbs sampler of the same shape as §07 but resampling one z per document) pool all a document's words as evidence for one topic and do much better than LDA on short text.
  • Pool or model pairs. Aggregate short texts into pseudo-documents (by author, hashtag, thread or time window) before running LDA, or model the corpus-wide set of word pairs ("biterms") directly instead of documents.
  • Embeddings help most here. A pretrained encoder carries semantic knowledge from its training corpus, so two posts with no shared word can still be close. This is the setting where the embedding pipeline of §11 most clearly beats LDA.

Pitfalls and habits

  • Preprocessing dominates LDA and NMF. Remove stop words (and a corpus-specific list: "said", "also", boilerplate), drop very rare words (in fewer than ~5 documents) and very common ones (in more than ~50–80%), and add frequent bigrams like "new_york". Without this, the top topics are function words. See stop words and normalisation. The embedding pipeline needs far less: embed the raw text, and only strip stop words in the c-TF-IDF naming step.
  • Give count models counts. LDA and pLSA on TF-IDF weights are mis-specified; use raw counts and let β and vocabulary pruning do the down-weighting.
  • Seeds and label switching. Fix and report seeds, run several, and compare topics by content, not index. Topic 7 in one run is not topic 7 in another.
  • Long documents. A whole book has every topic; split long documents into paragraphs or pages before modeling, then aggregate mixtures back up.
  • Topics as features. θ̂d (or the LSA/NMF coordinates) is a compact, interpretable document representation for a downstream model. For pure accuracy, fine-tuned encoders usually beat it (see text classification), but topic features stay useful when labels are scarce or the model must be explained.
  • Validate with people. Read the top 10–20 words and a few highest-weighted documents per topic. A topic whose words look fine but whose documents are a grab-bag is not a topic.
  • Extensions worth knowing. Time-indexed topics (topic word distributions that drift across time slices), topics conditioned on metadata such as author or source, correlated topic proportions (a logistic-normal instead of a Dirichlet prior), and seeded or guided topics where a user supplies anchor words.
Default advice: for short or semantically varied text, start with the embedding pipeline. For long documents where mixed membership matters, or where you need a probabilistic model to extend, use LDA with careful preprocessing, several seeds, and coherence plus human reading to pick K. NMF on TF-IDF is the quick baseline for both.
