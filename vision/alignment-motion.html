Alignment & Motion

Part 4 ended with a list of correspondences — pairs of points, one from each image, most of them right. This part turns that list into a transformation, and then asks what happens when no single transformation will do. Those are the same problem at two densities. Fit one 8-parameter map to a thousand matched points and you have a panorama; fit one 2-parameter map to every pixel independently and you have optical flow. The machinery in between — least squares, the direct linear transform, robust fitting, linearise-warp-repeat, and a pyramid to get you into the basin — is shared, and it is worth learning once. The single most important thing on this page is not an algorithm. It is knowing exactly when a homography is exact and when it is a lie.

The mistake this page exists to prevent. "I fitted a homography and the panorama has ghosting, so I need a better estimator." Almost certainly you need a different model. Ghosting that grows with how near an object is to the camera is parallax, and no homography of any accuracy removes it — §13 measures exactly how large it gets. Ghosting that is uniform across the frame is a fitting problem. Learning to tell those two apart at a glance is worth more than any amount of tuning.

02 The alignment problem, stated once

Write it down properly and the rest of the page is bookkeeping. There is a parametric map, a set of measured pairs, a residual, and a loss. Every method here differs only in which of those four you change.

Why the residual must be measured in pixels

This looks like pedantry and it is not. The residual above is a geometric quantity: the distance, on the image plane, between where the model says the point should be and where it was actually seen. Any monotone rearrangement of the model equations produces a different quantity — an algebraic residual — which is cheaper to minimise and is not the thing you care about. §06 minimises an algebraic residual because it is linear; §08 exists entirely to fix the damage. Keeping the distinction in view is the difference between a fit that is 0.6 px off and one that is 0.9 px off on the same data.

two error functions that are NOT the samegeometric (what you want) E_g = ∑ᵢ ‖ dehom(H·hom(xᵢ)) − x̃ᵢ ‖² algebraic (what is linear) E_a = ∑ᵢ ‖ hom(x̃ᵢ) × (H·hom(xᵢ)) ‖² They agree only where the homogeneous scale w = h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ equals 1. The algebraic residual is the geometric one WEIGHTED by w, so points that the homography pushes far away are silently given more say than nearby ones.

The residual is also the diagnostic. After any fit, do not look at the scalar loss — look at the residual vectors drawn on the image. Random and small: you are done. 03 Which model, and how many points it costs

The hierarchy itself is Part 1 §05. What matters here is the arithmetic that follows from it: degrees of freedom, minimal sample size, and what each extra parameter buys and costs.

A single 2D point correspondence (x, y) ↦ (x′, y′) supplies exactly two scalar equations. So a model with n degrees of freedom needs s = ⌈n/2⌉ correspondences to be determined, and that number s — the minimal sample size — is the single most consequential number in the whole robust-fitting story, because RANSAC's cost is exponential in it (Part 4 §27).

Why a homography needs four points, and why three collinear ones are worthless

Eight degrees of freedom (nine matrix entries, minus one for the overall scale, which does nothing because the mapping is through homogeneous coordinates) divided by two equations per correspondence gives four. But the count is necessary, not sufficient: the four points must be in general position — no three of them collinear.

This matters far more than it sounds, because degenerate samples are not rare — they are what RANSAC keeps drawing when the scene contains a dominant straight edge, a building facade seen edge-on, or a row of matched points along a horizon. A minimal-sample check that rejects near-collinear quadruples before fitting is one of the cheapest reliability improvements available (§09).

04 2D alignment by least squares

For translation, similarity and affine motion the residual is linear in the parameters. That is not a small convenience — it means the fit is a single non-iterative solve with a guaranteed global optimum, and it is why these three models are worth reaching for first.

The pattern: write it as A p = b

Take the affine case. The map is x′ = a₀₀x + a₀₁y + t₀, y′ = a₁₀x + a₁₁y + t₁. Collect the six unknowns into p and each correspondence contributes two rows:

translation: J = I, so the normal equations collapset̂ = (1/N) ∑ᵢ (x̃ᵢ − xᵢ) the mean displacement, and nothing more Least squares for a pure translation is an AVERAGE. Which is exactly why a single bad correspondence moves it: one match 300 px wrong among 30 good ones shifts the answer by 10 px, with no warning in the residual of any individual good point.

Similarity keeps its linearity by a change of variables

A similarity is s·R(θ) plus a translation, which is not linear in (s, θ). Substituting a = s·cos θ and b = s·sin θ makes it linear in (a, b, t₀, t₁), and the scale and angle come back out at the end. This trick — reparameterise until the model is linear, fit, then convert — is worth remembering; it also works for the 2D rigid case in §05.

A tempting shortcut. "I'll always fit affine — it contains similarity, so it can only be better." It contains similarity, so it can only fit the noise better. With N correspondences and n parameters the expected residual shrinks like √(1 − n/2N) whether or not the extra parameters mean anything, while the variance of the estimated map grows with n. With six points, affine spends half its data on parameters; with three, it interpolates and the residual is exactly zero, which tells you nothing at all. Model selection here is the same discipline as everywhere else: judge on held-out correspondences, not on the ones you fitted.

06 The direct linear transform

A homography is not linear in its parameters — there is a division by the third homogeneous coordinate. Clear the denominator and it becomes linear, at the price of minimising the wrong error. That trade is the DLT, and it is worth understanding both halves of it.

Where the non-linearity is

the map, written out h₀₀x + h₀₁y + h₀₂ h₁₀x + h₁₁y + h₁₂ x′ = ─────────────────── , y′ = ─────────────────── h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ Nine entries, but the map is unchanged by H → λH for any λ ≠ 0 — the scale cancels in both fractions. So the parameter space is 8-dimensional, not 9: a POINT of the projective space of 3 × 3 matrices, which is why H is always fixed by a normalisation such as ‖h‖ = 1 or h₂₂ = 1.

Multiply through by the denominator w = h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂. The two equations become linear in the nine unknowns:

07 Normalisation: turning 10⁶ into 3

Run the DLT on raw pixel coordinates and the design matrix mixes numbers of size 1 with numbers of size 10⁵. Rescale the points first — an operation that takes four lines and no thought — and the same system becomes as well conditioned as anything you will ever solve. This is the highest ratio of benefit to effort on the page.

The diagnosis

A row of A reads [−x, −y, −1, 0, 0, 0, x′x, x′y, x′]. In a 640 × 480 image the entries have magnitudes roughly 10², 10², 1, 0, 0, 0, 10⁵, 10⁵, 10². Least squares treats all nine columns as equally important; they are not remotely equally scaled, so the residual is dominated by the two quadratic columns and the constant column is effectively invisible. The condition number quantifies exactly this.

Measured, both ways

The condition number below is σ₁/σ₈: the largest singular value of the design matrix over the smallest constrained one. Why RANSAC and not least squares with a big residual threshold

Because least squares has a breakdown point of zero: a single correspondence, moved far enough, drags the fit arbitrarily far. §04's figure shows it happening on six points. Iterating "fit, discard the worst, refit" does not repair this — the first fit is already so corrupted that the worst residual may belong to a good point. RANSAC inverts the logic: it never lets the outliers into a fit in the first place, because it only ever fits minimal subsets and judges them by counting.

What the derivation tells you

  • The rank-one update t n̂ᵀ/d is the whole content. Without translation it vanishes and you get §12's case. With translation it depends on the plane through the single combination n̂/d, which is why you cannot recover the normal and the distance separately from H alone — only their ratio, and even then only up to a well-known two-fold ambiguity.
  • Any camera motion is allowed. The plane assumption does all the work; R and t are arbitrary. This is why a whiteboard, a document, a building facade, a floor and a football pitch are all homography problems no matter how the camera moves.
  • Different planes give different homographies. A scene with two planes at different depths needs two homographies, and the set of all plane-induced homographies for a fixed camera pair forms a 3-parameter family — which is exactly what Part 7's plane-plus-parallax decomposition exploits.
  • The intrinsics need not match. K₀ and K₁ appear separately, so a zoom, a different sensor or a different focal length between the two shots changes nothing structural. That is not true of the essential matrix.

14 Applying it: inverse warping, and where the pixels come from

Estimating H is half the job; the other half is moving pixels with it. Part 2 §26 owns this and derives the forward-versus-inverse argument in full. This section states only the three things that bite specifically in alignment.

always inverse-warp — the rule, restatedfor every pixel x′ of the OUTPUT: x = H⁻¹ x′ a real-valued position in the input out(x′) = interpolate(in, x) one sample, one destination pixel Forward warping — scatter each input pixel to H x — leaves holes where the map expands and collides where it contracts, and needs splatting to repair both. Inverse warping has exactly one output value per output pixel by construction.
  • Compute the output bounding box first. Warp the four corners of the input through H, take the bounding box of the results, and offset the output by its top-left corner. Skip this and half of every panorama lands at negative coordinates and is silently discarded. The offset is itself a translation, so it composes into H and costs nothing.
  • Watch for the horizon. A homography sends the line h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ = 0 to infinity. If that line crosses the input image, the warped output is unbounded and the bounding box is meaningless. Detect it — evaluate w at the four corners and check the signs agree — and clip before warping. This is the single most common crash in a hand-rolled stitcher.
  • Match the filter to the scale change. Where H minifies, bilinear interpolation aliases badly, because one output pixel should average many input pixels and instead reads two. The fix is a mip-map or an elliptically weighted average — Part 2 §28. Where H magnifies, bilinear is merely soft and bicubic is better. A homography usually does both within one image, which is why panoramas need a scale-aware resampler and not a single kernel.
  • Interpolate once. Chaining n warps by resampling n times costs n generations of blur. Compose the matrices — H₂₀ = H₂₁H₁₀, which is exact and free — and resample from the original once.
The alignment-specific consequence. Every accuracy number on this page is quoted in pixels of the input. Resampling adds its own error on top, and for bilinear interpolation of a band-limited image that error is roughly 0.5–2% of the local contrast — comparable to a tenth of a pixel of displacement on typical texture. So there is little point refining a homography below about 0.05 px on a pipeline that then resamples bilinearly: the warp is the accuracy floor, not the fit.

The payoff that makes cylinders worth the trouble. If the camera is level and rotates only about its vertical axis, then after warping to cylindrical coordinates the images are related by a pure horizontal translation — one parameter. Not a homography, not an affine map: a shift. That collapses the whole estimation problem to a 1-D search, which is why cylindrical stitching was the first kind that worked reliably and why it is still the right choice for a motorised pan head. 19 Seams and blending: hiding what is left

After the best possible registration and the best possible exposure compensation, the overlap still disagrees — because of parallax, because something moved, because the lenses differ. Blending is the admission that this residual cannot be removed, only distributed where the eye will not find it.

Why one transition width cannot work

A narrow transition hides high-frequency disagreement (mis-registered texture) but leaves a low-frequency step (exposure, vignetting) visible as an edge. A wide transition removes the step but averages the texture over that width, producing a soft band. Both statements are true simultaneously, so no single width is right — which is the entire argument for making the width depend on frequency.

Optical flow is not motion flow

Two failures worth naming, because they are conceptual rather than numerical and no algorithm addresses them:

motion with no flow
A rotating uniform sphere
A featureless matte sphere spinning under fixed lighting produces zero image change. Every pixel's I_t is zero and every gradient is zero. The true motion field is a rotation; the optical flow is identically zero, and correctly so.
flow with no motion
A fixed sphere, a moving light
Nothing moves; the shading sweeps across the surface; every pixel reports a large flow. The optical flow is a real measurement of image change and a completely wrong measurement of motion. Specular highlights do this on every shiny surface in every video.
the honest definition
What flow actually measures
Optical flow is the apparent motion of brightness patterns. It coincides with the projection of the 3D motion field only when the surface is Lambertian, the lighting is fixed, and the surface has texture. Those conditions are usual, not universal.
the mitigations
What is done about it
Model a per-frame bias and gain I₁ = αI₀ + β and solve for them alongside the flow — 2 extra unknowns, and it removes auto-exposure entirely. Or match a quantity that is already invariant: gradients, filter phase, or a normalised patch. Or use a robust penalty (§10) so the violated pixels stop voting.

The failure everyone meets first. "The flow looks beautiful and smooth." It is smooth across the object boundaries too. General metric discipline is Model Evaluation's. What is specific here is that the quantity being measured is a vector per pixel, that two natural error measures disagree by a factor of a hundred on the same error, and that obtaining a ground-truth flow field for a real scene is an unsolved problem rather than a chore.

30 Practice

Answers are in the numbers already given, or are one short derivation away. Anything that says measured was computed independently and appears verbatim above.

  1. A homography has nine entries. Why does it have eight degrees of freedom, and what breaks if you fix h₂₂ = 1? The map is through homogeneous coordinates, so H and λH act identically and the parameter is a point of a projective space. Fixing h₂₂ = 1 is a valid chart everywhere except h₂₂ = 0 — which is exactly the homography that sends the origin of image 1 to infinity, and that happens for wide baselines and near-90° plane rotations. Fix ‖h‖ = 1 instead.
  2. Why does each correspondence give two rows of the DLT matrix rather than three? Because x̃′ × (Hx̃) = 0 is three scalar equations that are linearly dependent — their combination with x̃′ vanishes identically. Dropping one is exact, not an approximation.
  3. Give the condition number of the design matrix for the worked four-point example, normalised and not. Unnormalised 1.039 25 × 10⁶; Hartley-normalised 3.403 12 — a ratio of 3.054 × 10⁵. Both recover H_true, to 1.6 × 10⁻¹² and 6.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ respectively.
  4. So does normalisation actually improve accuracy? Measured: on 12 well-spread points in a 640 × 480 image with 1 px of noise, by 4% (0.973 px against 0.933 px). On 12 points clustered in one corner, by 8.5× (240.3 px against 28.4 px). What it improves always is conditioning — 10⁶ to 10⁹ down to between 4 and 6 in every configuration tested. Do it because it is free.
  5. Four correspondences, three of them collinear. What does the solver return? A member of a two-parameter family. Measured: σ₈ falls from 4.119 × 10⁻¹ to 1.097 × 10⁻¹³, so the matrix has rank 7 and the null space is 2-dimensional. Reject such samples inside RANSAC; a threshold of 0.002 on the normalised triangle area rejects 5.15% of uniformly random quadruples.
  6. Compute N for a homography at w = 0.3 and p = 0.99, and for an affine model on the same data. N = log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.3⁴) = 566.23 → 567; affine, s = 3: log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.3³) = 168.6 → 169. A factor of 3.4 for two extra parameters.
  7. Why refit on the consensus set, quantitatively? Measured in the figure of §09: the winning minimal-sample homography has a transfer error near 1.8 px; refitting on the whole consensus set and refining gives near 0.6 px — a factor of about 3. Re-scoring against the refitted model then recovers every true inlier where the minimal sample found 55 of 66.
  8. Minimising the forward geometric error is the "gold standard". True? No. Measured with noise in both images: DLT 2.2530 px, forward Gauss–Newton 2.4131 px — worse than not refining — symmetric 2.2749, and the true gold standard, which also estimates corrected point positions, 2.1785. Choose the error to match where the noise is.
  9. State precisely when a homography relates two images. When the scene is planar (any camera motion), or when the two camera centres coincide (any scene). Measured residuals 2.05 × 10⁻¹³ px and 2.34 × 10⁻¹³ px respectively. In every other case there is no homography at all.
  10. You fit a homography to a room and get 30 px of residual on the near objects and 2 px on the far wall. Diagnose. Parallax. Residual ∝ |1/Z − 1/Zπ|, so it is largest where the depth departs most from the fitted plane. Confirm by checking that the residual vectors are collinear with a common point — measured to 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ degrees — and that point is the epipole.
  11. Derive the pixel parallax from a 5 cm error in a panorama's rotation centre, at f = 600 px, on an object 2 m away. f·b/Z = 600 × 0.05 / 2 = 15 px. Measured over 200 000 points: 14.8 px. The rule is good to 1.5%.
  12. How wide is a flat panorama covering 150°, in units of f? And a cylindrical one? 2f·tan 75° = 7.464 f against f·(150° in radians) = 2.618 f — 2.9× wider, and the sampling at the edge is stretched by sec²75° = 14.93.
  13. Why does a level, vertically-panning camera reduce stitching to a 1-D search? Because after warping to cylindrical coordinates, x′ = s·atan(x/f) converts a rotation about the vertical axis into a pure horizontal translation. Measured residual after the best pure shift with a level camera: 0.000000 px. Tilt the camera by 10° and it is no longer zero.
  14. Gain compensation returns gains of 0.938, 0.695, 1.303, 0.853 for cameras whose true exposures were 1.00, 1.35, 0.72, 1.10. Is it wrong? No. g·e = 0.9383 for all four, constant to 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. The gains are determined only up to a global scale and the prior chose 0.938. Remove the prior and the whole solution collapses toward zero, because the objective is homogeneous.
  15. Feathering removes a visible seam. What did it cost? Measured: a 32 px feather takes the low-frequency step from 6.14× the background gradient to 3.04× and costs 9.4% of the local contrast. Multi-band takes it to 1.90× for 0.2%. There is no feather width that achieves both.
  16. A pose estimated from a fronto-parallel calibration target has a small reprojection error. Trust it? No. Measured: at 30° of tilt the refined rotation error is about 0.2°; near 0° it grows by an order of magnitude while the reprojection error stays at the noise floor, because a plane seen head-on constrains the out-of-plane rotations only to second order. A small residual is necessary and not sufficient.
  17. Write the brightness constancy constraint and say what its solution set is. Iₓu + I_yv + I_t = 0: one linear equation in two unknowns, whose solution set is a line in (u, v) with normal ∇I at distance |I_t|/‖∇I‖ from the origin. Only the component along ∇I — the normal flow — is recoverable.
  18. A matte sphere rotates under fixed lighting and the flow is zero. Is the algorithm broken? No — the optical flow really is zero, and the motion field is not. Optical flow measures the apparent motion of brightness patterns, and the two coincide only for a textured Lambertian surface under fixed lighting.
  19. Name the matrix Lucas–Kanade inverts and say where else on this site it appears. The structure tensor A = ∑ w ∇I∇Iᵀ — Part 4 §05's, unchanged. Part 4 inspects its eigenvalues to decide whether a location is a good keypoint; Part 6 inverts it to get a displacement. A good feature is a place where dense motion estimation is well posed.
  20. Give the eigenvalues of the structure tensor on an edge, and what the solver should do. Measured on a 21 × 21 window: λ = (0.000000, 0.980932), det A = 4.3 × 10⁻¹⁷ — singular. Add a few per cent of λ₁ to the diagonal: the answer is then the normal flow with the tangential component biased to zero, which is honest, rather than an arbitrary point on a line.
  21. Lucas–Kanade converges to 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁶ at a 0.58 px displacement and to 9.07 px of error at 7.2 px. What changed? Nothing about the algorithm — the displacement left the basin of attraction, whose width is set by the scale of the image structure. It converged confidently to a different local minimum with a small residual.
  22. Write Horn–Schunck's update in one line and read it aloud. u ← ū − Iₓ(Iₓū + I_yv̄ + I_t)/(α² + Iₓ² + I_y²): take the local average, then step from it along the gradient direction by exactly what the data term demands. Every update is a normal-flow correction to a smoothed field.
  23. Why is Horn–Schunck slow, measured? Because the answer diffuses one neighbour per iteration. On a scene whose right half is textureless, the textured half's AEPE is 0.2680 at 100 iterations and 0.2629 at 3 000 — converged. The flat half goes 0.9609, 0.7833, 0.4539, 0.2404 over the same range. A factor of thirty, and it is the argument for multigrid.
  24. At what displacement does single-scale direct alignment stop working, and by how much does a 4-level pyramid extend it? Measured on one 128 × 128 texture: exact to 0.001 px up to 6.4 px, and 9.08 px wrong at 7.2 px — a cliff, not a slope. Four levels move the cliff to between 25.6 and 32 px, a factor of four, which is 2^(L−1).
  25. Why must a pyramid be smoothed before decimation? Because dropping alternate samples of an unsmoothed image aliases (Part 3 §14), and the coarse level then contains structure that is not in the scene. Coarse-to-fine will find it, believe it, and pass it down. The figure in §25 has a control that reproduces this.
  26. What exactly does the inverse-compositional algorithm precompute, and what does it save? The steepest-descent images ∇I₀ · ∂x̃/∂p, the Gauss–Newton Hessian ∑JᵀJ, and its inverse — all from the template alone. Per iteration that is O(nN) instead of O(n²N): for a homography on a 200 × 200 patch, 320 000 multiply-adds instead of 2 560 000.
  27. Its Δp must be composed how? As W(p) ← W(p) ∘ W(Δp)⁻¹ — the increment warps the template, so its inverse is what updates the current warp. Composing it the other way gives a method that works for tiny motions and diverges for moderate ones.
  28. How do you find pixels whose flow has no correct answer? Forward–backward consistency: ‖u₀₁(x) + u₁₀(x + u₀₁(x))‖ against a threshold. Measured on the figure of §27: rejecting 11.8% of pixels catches 82% of the truly occluded ones and takes the average endpoint error from 0.855 px to 0.284 px.
  29. The same 0.1 px error is reported as 5.60° and as 0.056°. Explain. Angular error embeds the flow as (u, v, 1) and therefore depends on the flow's magnitude: 5.5993° at ‖u‖ = 0.1 px and 0.0562° at 10 px, a factor of 100 for an identical mistake. Report endpoint error.
  30. Two methods, equal AEPE, very different R₃. Which do you deploy? The one with the lower R₃, for anything that consumes the flow. Equal means with different outlier rates say one method is occasionally catastrophic, and a warp, an interpolation or a motion segmentation fails on the catastrophes, not on the average.
  31. Why is flow ground truth so hard that it changes how you read a benchmark? Because every source is a compromise: rendering is exact but synthetic; painted scenes are real but must be rigid, small and paintable; laser depth plus ego-motion covers only static points; human annotation cannot reach the untextured and occluded pixels where methods differ. A reported AEPE inherits its dataset's compromise, so the dataset is part of the number.
