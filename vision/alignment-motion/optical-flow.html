21 Dense motion: the brightness constancy equation

Everything from here on drops the features and works from the pixels. The whole subject rests on one assumption, one linearisation of it, and the honest admission that neither is quite true.

the assumptionI(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t) "The same physical point has the same brightness in both frames." Not a law — an assumption, and it is violated by shading changes, specular highlights, auto-exposure, vignetting, occlusion and anything transparent. the linearisation — a first-order Taylor expansion of the left sideI + Iₓu + I_yv + I_t + O(‖u‖²) = I ⇒ Iₓ·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0 the BRIGHTNESS CONSTANCY CONSTRAINT ⇒ ∇I · u + I_t = 0 (also called the optical flow constraint) what it is, geometricallyONE linear equation in TWO unknowns per pixel. Its solution set is a LINE in (u, v) space: · the line's NORMAL direction is ∇I / ‖∇I‖ · its perpendicular distance from the origin is |I_t| / ‖∇I‖ The component of motion ALONG ∇I is determined and is called the NORMAL FLOW: u_n = −(I_t / ‖∇I‖) · ∇I/‖∇I‖ The component perpendicular to ∇I is entirely unconstrained. That is the aperture problem (§22), and it is not a defect of the method — the information is absent from the data. how big a motion the linearisation toleratesThe expansion drops O(‖u‖²·I″). It is accurate while u is small compared with the scale over which ∇I itself changes — roughly the width of the image's autocorrelation peak. On real texture that is a few pixels, and §25 measures the breakdown on one image at between 6.4 and 7.2 px.

Optical flow is not motion flow

Two failures worth naming, because they are conceptual rather than numerical and no algorithm addresses them:

motion with no flow
A rotating uniform sphere
A featureless matte sphere spinning under fixed lighting produces zero image change. Every pixel's I_t is zero and every gradient is zero. The true motion field is a rotation; the optical flow is identically zero, and correctly so.
flow with no motion
A fixed sphere, a moving light
Nothing moves; the shading sweeps across the surface; every pixel reports a large flow. The optical flow is a real measurement of image change and a completely wrong measurement of motion. Specular highlights do this on every shiny surface in every video.
the honest definition
What flow actually measures
Optical flow is the apparent motion of brightness patterns. It coincides with the projection of the 3D motion field only when the surface is Lambertian, the lighting is fixed, and the surface has texture. Those conditions are usual, not universal.
the mitigations
What is done about it
Model a per-frame bias and gain I₁ = αI₀ + β and solve for them alongside the flow — 2 extra unknowns, and it removes auto-exposure entirely. Or match a quantity that is already invariant: gradients, filter phase, or a normalised patch. Or use a robust penalty (§10) so the violated pixels stop voting.
One pixel, one equation, one line drag the true motion — everything on the dashed line produces the same brightness change at this pixel

22 The aperture problem, now as underdetermination

Part 4 §03 introduced this as the reason detectors need corners. Here it becomes the reason dense flow is ill-posed — and every method from §23 to §26 is a different answer to the same question: what extra assumption makes it well-posed again?

the counting argument, which is the whole difficultyunknowns 2 per pixel (u, v) equations 1 per pixel Iₓu + I_yv + I_t = 0 The system is underdetermined by exactly a factor of two, EVERYWHERE, no matter how good the image is. This is not a small-data problem and more pixels do not help: every new pixel brings one new equation AND two new unknowns. the three ways out, and they exhaust the field1 · ASSUME the flow is CONSTANT over a window → Lucas–Kanade (§23) N equations, 2 unknowns, over-determined. Fails where the window straddles a motion boundary, because then the assumption is false. 2 · ASSUME the flow is SMOOTH over the image → Horn–Schunck (§24) Add a penalty on ‖∇u‖² and the whole field becomes determined. Fails at motion boundaries in the opposite way: it smooths across them. 3 · ASSUME a PARAMETRIC form for the whole field → §26, and §04–§13 2 to 8 parameters for the entire image, so it is wildly over-determined. Fails when the scene does not obey the model — which is §13, again. Every dense-flow method ever published is one of these three, a combination, or a learned prior that plays the same role with more parameters.

Two constraint lines are enough — if they are different enough

Stack the constraint over a window and it becomes a 2 × 2 system. Which is solvable exactly when the window contains gradients in two significantly different orientations. The matrix that decides it is one you have already met:

Part 4 INSPECTED its eigenvalues to decide whether a location was worth keeping as a keypoint. Part 6 INVERTS it to compute a displacement. The Harris corner score is a test of whether Lucas–Kanade will work, computed before running it — and that is not an analogy, it is the same matrix.
Every pixel's constraint line, all at once on an edge they pile up on top of each other; on a corner they cross
The link worth remembering. Part 4's Harris detector keeps a pixel when det A − κ·tr²A is large; Shi and Tomasi keep it when λ₀, the smaller eigenvalue, is large. Both are asking "is A well conditioned?", and the reason that is the right question for a detector is that A⁻¹ is the covariance of the displacement estimate you would get by running §23 there. A good feature is, by definition, a place where dense motion estimation is well posed. One matrix, two chapters, and the same eigenvalues doing the same job.

23 Lucas–Kanade: solve, warp, repeat

Assume the flow is constant over a window, solve the 2 × 2 system, apply the answer as a warp, and do it again on what is left. It is Gauss–Newton on the sum of squared differences, and §08's loop with a two-parameter Jacobian.

the objective, and its linearisation about the current estimate uE(u + Δu) = ∑ᵢ [ I₁(xᵢ + u + Δu) − I₀(xᵢ) ]² ≈ ∑ᵢ [ I₁(xᵢ + u) + ∇I₁(xᵢ + u)·Δu − I₀(xᵢ) ]² = ∑ᵢ [ ∇I₁(xᵢ + u)·Δu + eᵢ ]² eᵢ = I₁(xᵢ + u) − I₀(xᵢ) the normal equations — a 2 × 2 solve, per window, per iterationA Δu = b A = ∑ ∇I₁∇I₁ᵀ (the structure tensor, §22) b = −∑ eᵢ ∇I₁ᵀ u ← u + Δu , then RE-WARP I₁ by the new u and repeat. Stop when ‖Δu‖ < 0.01–0.1 px, or after a fixed small number of iterations. the solvability condition, and it is exactly §22'sΔu is determined ⟺ A is invertible ⟺ λ₀ > 0 and the estimate's covariance is Cov[û] ≈ σₙ²·A⁻¹, whose worst direction has standard deviation σₙ/√λ₀. The basin's size is set by the scale of the image structure — the texture's finest period is about 11 px, and it fails at 7.2 px of displacement. §25 fixes this, and nothing else does.
Lucas–Kanade, iteration by iteration, with the conditioning that decides it step it; then choose the edge and watch a perfectly reasonable-looking window produce nothing at all
The convenient shortcut, and its price. "Use ∇I₀ instead of ∇I₁(x + u) — near convergence they are the same, and then A can be computed once." Correct, and it is exactly what the inverse-compositional algorithm of §26 formalises — but only near convergence. Far from it the two gradient fields genuinely differ, and the resulting step is no longer a Gauss–Newton step for the problem you posed. It still converges, from a smaller basin, which is why the trick is paired with a pyramid rather than used alone.

24 Horn–Schunck: make the whole field smooth instead

The other way out of §22's counting problem. Rather than assuming the flow is constant on a window, assume it varies slowly everywhere, and solve for all two million unknowns at once. The result is a flow field that is defined even where the image says nothing — which is both the great advantage and the characteristic failure.

the energyE = ∬ (Iₓu + I_yv + I_t)² + α²( ‖∇u‖² + ‖∇v‖² ) dx dy └──── data term ────┘ └──── smoothness term ────┘ α is the ONLY parameter and it has units: it trades intensity error against flow gradient. Large α → a very smooth, very wrong field. Small α → the data term dominates, and where the data says nothing the answer is noise. the Euler–Lagrange equationsIₓ(Iₓu + I_yv + I_t) = α²∇²u I_y(Iₓu + I_yv + I_t) = α²∇²v Approximating ∇²u ≈ ū − u with ū a weighted local average gives the classic iteration, which is one line per component and needs no linear solver at all: u^(n+1) = ū^(n) − Iₓ · (Iₓū^(n) + I_yv̄^(n) + I_t) / (α² + Iₓ² + I_y²) v^(n+1) = v̄^(n) − I_y · (Iₓū^(n) + I_yv̄^(n) + I_t) / (α² + Iₓ² + I_y²) Read it: take the local average, then step from it along the GRADIENT direction by whatever the data term demands. Every update is a normal-flow correction to a smoothed field — the two ideas of §21 and §22 in one line.

The flat half takes THIRTY TIMES longer, because the information has to diffuse across 32 pixels one neighbour at a time, and Jacobi iteration on a Laplacian propagates like heat. At α = 0.3 and 3 000 iterations the whole-field AEPE reaches 0.1769. That is the argument for multigrid, for coarse-to-fine (§25), and for conjugate gradients — not elegance, a factor of thirty in wall time.
The failure everyone meets first. "The flow looks beautiful and smooth." It is smooth across the object boundaries too. The quadratic penalty ‖∇u‖² charges a large flow discontinuity enormously, so the optimiser prefers a gentle ramp between a moving car and a static background over the step that is actually there. The fixes are all the same idea: replace the quadratic with something that saturates. Total variation (‖∇u‖₁, still convex), a robust ρ from §10, or an anisotropic weight that reduces the penalty across strong image gradients. Every modern variational method does one of these, and the improvement is concentrated entirely at boundaries.

25 Coarse-to-fine: the only thing that handles large motion

Every method from §23 onward linearises, and a linearisation is local. The displacement it can handle is bounded by the scale of the image structure — a few pixels. A pyramid changes what "a pixel" means, and that is the entire trick.

the algorithmbuild pyramids P₀(I₀), P₀(I₁) … P_L(I₀), P_L(I₁) Gaussian, ÷2 per level u ← 0 for l = L down to 0: u ← LucasKanade( P_l(I₀), P_l(I₁), starting from u ) a few iterations if l > 0: u ← 2u rescale to the next level return u why it works, in one sentenceA displacement of 16 px at full resolution is 1 px at level 4. One pixel is inside ANY linearisation's basin. Solve it there, double it, and every finer level only ever has to find a fraction of a pixel of correction. the two things that must be right1 · SMOOTH BEFORE DECIMATING. Dropping every other pixel of an unsmoothed image aliases (Part 3 §14), and an aliased coarse level has structure that is not in the scene. Coarse-to-fine will find it, believe it, and hand a confidently wrong initialisation to every finer level. Use Part 2 §23's pyramid. 2 · WARP, DON'T ACCUMULATE. At each level, re-warp I₁ by the current u before computing the gradients and the error, so the remaining motion really is small. Adding up per-level estimates computed from unwarped images does not work and is the most common way to implement this incorrectly.

The transition is a CLIFF, not a slope, and it sits at about the correlation length of the texture. Four levels move the cliff from 7 px to somewhere between 25.6 and 32 px — a factor of four, which is 2^(L−1) with L = 4, exactly as the doubling argument predicts. More levels buy more range until the coarsest level has no usable structure left.
Note what failure looks like. At 12.8 px the single-scale estimate is not "noisy" or "large" — it is 16.97 px wrong while the residual it converged to is perfectly small, because it found a different local minimum of the same objective. There is no internal signal that anything went wrong. This is the reason a flow estimate can never be trusted on its own: it needs either a pyramid to make the basin big enough, or a forward–backward consistency check (§27) to catch the cases where it was not.

26 Parametric alignment: forward, compositional, inverse

Lucas–Kanade with two parameters is a tracker. With six or eight it is a direct alternative to everything in §04–§13 — the same homography, estimated from pixels instead of correspondences. Four algebraically equivalent formulations exist, and the differences between them are entirely about which quantities can be precomputed.

the parametric objectiveE(p + Δp) = ∑ᵢ [ I₁( x′(xᵢ; p + Δp) ) − I₀(xᵢ) ]² J = ∂I₁/∂p = ∇I₁ · ∂x′/∂p the image gradient TIMES the warp Jacobian a 1 × 2 row times a 2 × n block = 1 × n the warp Jacobian of a homography, evaluated at the identity (p = 0)∂x̃/∂p |_(p=0) = ⎡ x y 1 0 0 0 −x² −xy ⎤ ⎣ 0 0 0 x y 1 −xy −y² ⎦ Eight columns, two rows, no division — because at p = 0 the denominator is 1. This is exactly why the COMPOSITIONAL formulation is worth the trouble: it only ever needs the Jacobian at the identity, so it is a constant table. the four formulationsFORWARD ADDITIVE p ← p + Δp, Jacobian at the current p Recompute ∇I₁ warped, and ∂x′/∂p at p, every iteration. Simplest, slowest. FORWARD COMPOSITIONAL W(p) ← W(p) ∘ W(Δp), Jacobian at the IDENTITY Warp I₁ by the current estimate first, then estimate an incremental warp of the warped image. ∂x̃/∂p is now the constant table above. ∇I₁̃ still changes. INVERSE ADDITIVE gradients from the TEMPLATE, additive update INVERSE COMPOSITIONAL gradients from the TEMPLATE, compositional update E(Δp) = ∑ [ Ĩ₁(xᵢ) − I₀( x̃(xᵢ; Δp) ) ]² Now the derivative is ∂I₀/∂p, which involves only the TEMPLATE and the identity Jacobian. Both are constant across iterations, so the steepest- descent images ∇I₀·∂x̃/∂p AND the Hessian A = ∑ JᵀJ AND its inverse are all PRECOMPUTED ONCE. Each iteration is then: warp, subtract, n dot products, one n × n multiply by a stored matrix, compose the inverse of the result. what it costs, per iteration, for n parameters and N pixelsforward O(n²N) build A and invert it every iteration inverse O(nN) A⁻¹ and the steepest-descent images are already there For a homography (n = 8) on a 200 × 200 patch that is the difference between 2.6 million and 320 thousand multiply-adds per iteration — about 8×, and the gap grows with n. The two produce the same sequence of estimates to first order.
  • The inverse update must be inverted before composing. The Δp that comes out of the inverse formulation warps the template, so the current warp is updated by W(p) ← W(p) ∘ W(Δp)⁻¹. Composing it the other way gives a method that diverges, and the symptom — it works for tiny motions and fails for moderate ones — is easy to misread as a step-size problem.
  • It assumes the template's gradients stand in for the target's. True near convergence, less true far from it, and false if the two images differ photometrically. So the inverse-compositional method has a smaller basin than the forward one, and is essentially always run inside §25's pyramid.
  • The connection to §04–§13. Run this with the 8-parameter homography Jacobian and you have estimated a homography without extracting a single feature — a direct method. It is more accurate than the feature-based route on the same pair, because it uses every pixel; and it needs the feature-based route first, because its basin is a few pixels wide and the two images may be a third of a frame apart.
Forward against inverse compositional, on the same warp both reach the same answer — count the per-iteration work, not the iterations

27 Where flow has no answer: occlusion and the other failures

Some pixels have no correct flow vector, because the thing they show is not in the other image. Every method above will still return a number for them. Knowing which numbers to throw away is most of what separates a working system from a demo.

FailureWhat is actually happeningHow to detect it
OcclusionA pixel visible in frame 0 is hidden in frame 1. There is no correct (u, v) — the question has no answer. Occluded regions form on the leading side of a moving object and their width is the relative displacement.Forward–backward consistency. Compute u₀₁ and u₁₀; a pixel is consistent if ‖u₀₁(x) + u₁₀(x + u₀₁(x))‖ is small. A common threshold is 0.01(‖u₀₁‖² + ‖u₁₀‖²) + 0.5 px. Occluded pixels fail it by construction, because their forward vector points at something else's flow.
DisocclusionThe mirror image: a pixel in frame 1 that was hidden in frame 0. Symmetric in the two frames, so the same check finds it.The same consistency test run the other way. Together the two identify a band around every motion boundary whose width is the displacement discontinuity.
Motion boundariesThe flow is genuinely discontinuous. §23's window assumption is false there (the window straddles two motions), and §24's smoothness assumption is false there (it penalises the true step).Large residual after warping; a large local flow gradient; disagreement between window sizes. Robust penalties and boundary-aware smoothing reduce the damage but do not remove the fact that both models are wrong there.
Large homogeneous regionsNo gradient means no constraint (§21). Lucas–Kanade returns nothing; Horn–Schunck returns whatever diffused in from the boundary, which may be right and may be an interpolation of two unrelated motions.λ₀ of the structure tensor below a threshold. 28 Evaluation, and why flow ground truth barely exists

General metric discipline is Model Evaluation's. What is specific here is that the quantity being measured is a vector per pixel, that two natural error measures disagree by a factor of a hundred on the same error, and that obtaining a ground-truth flow field for a real scene is an unsolved problem rather than a chore.

endpoint error — the primary metric, and the one to reportEPE(x) = ‖ (û, v̂)(x) − (u, v)(x) ‖₂ in pixels AEPE = the mean over the evaluated pixels. Report it separately over ALL pixels · pixels near motion boundaries · pixels in untextured regions, because a method that is good on one can be bad on the others and the average hides it. angular error — historically first, and it is a trapAE = arccos( (û·u + v̂·v + 1) / ( √(û² + v̂² + 1) · √(u² + v² + 1) ) ) The "+1" embeds the 2D flow as a 3D space-time direction (u, v, 1) and measures the angle between those. That makes it dimensionless — and it makes it depend on the MAGNITUDE of the flow in a way nobody wants. measured — the SAME 0.1 px error at different flow magnitudesground truth estimate EPE angular error (0.10, 0) (0.20, 0) 0.1000 5.5993° (1.00, 0) (1.10, 0) 0.1000 2.7263° (5.00, 5.00) (5.10, 5.00) 0.1000 0.5673° (10.00, 0) (10.10, 0) 0.1000 0.0562° A hundredfold spread in the reported error for an identical mistake. And the reverse — a fixed 10% relative error: ‖u‖ = 0.5 → EPE 0.050, AE 2.2457° ‖u‖ = 2.0 → EPE 0.200, AE 2.1211° ‖u‖ = 8.0 → EPE 0.800, AE 0.6419° ‖u‖ = 20 → EPE 2.000, AE 0.2598° Neither metric is scale-free, but the endpoint error is at least in units anyone can act on. Report AEPE; report angular error only alongside it, and never alone. the outlier rates that actually correlate with usefulnessR_x = the % of pixels with EPE > x typically x = 0.5, 1.0, 3.0 px Fl = the % of pixels with EPE > 3 px AND EPE > 5% of ‖(u, v)‖ — the two-sided rule, so a 3 px error on a 100 px motion is not an outlier A method can win on AEPE and lose badly on R₃, which means it is accurate on the easy pixels and catastrophically wrong on a few hard ones. For anything that CONSUMES the flow — warping a frame, segmenting a motion — the outlier rate predicts the failure and the mean does not.

Why the ground truth is the hard part

Source of truthHow it is obtainedWhat it costs you

The consequence. Every public flow benchmark is a compromise on this list, and the ranking on one does not transfer cleanly to another. When a paper reports an AEPE, the first question is which dataset and therefore which of these compromises the number inherits — not whether the number is small.
The self-check that needs no ground truth at all. Warp frame 1 by the estimated flow and subtract frame 0. If the flow is right, the result is noise; if it is wrong, the result contains a ghost of the structure that failed to align. This interpolation error is measurable on any video ever shot, correlates well with endpoint error where both exist, and is the only evaluation available on real footage. It is also exactly the objective that was minimised, so it cannot detect a wrong answer that fits — which is why it complements the forward–backward check of §27 rather than replacing it.
