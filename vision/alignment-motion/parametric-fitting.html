03 Which model, and how many points it costs

The hierarchy itself is Part 1 §05. What matters here is the arithmetic that follows from it: degrees of freedom, minimal sample size, and what each extra parameter buys and costs.

A single 2D point correspondence (x, y) ↦ (x′, y′) supplies exactly two scalar equations. So a model with n degrees of freedom needs s = ⌈n/2⌉ correspondences to be determined, and that number s — the minimal sample size — is the single most consequential number in the whole robust-fitting story, because RANSAC's cost is exponential in it (Part 4 §27).

Why a homography needs four points, and why three collinear ones are worthless

Eight degrees of freedom (nine matrix entries, minus one for the overall scale, which does nothing because the mapping is through homogeneous coordinates) divided by two equations per correspondence gives four. But the count is necessary, not sufficient: the four points must be in general position — no three of them collinear.

measured: the design matrix of §06, on two 4-point setsfour points in general position σ₁ = 4.280e+05 σ₈ = 4.119e−01 rank 8 ✓ H determined three of the four collinear σ₁ = 2.565e+05 σ₈ = 1.097e−13 rank 7 ✗ H is a 2-parameter family Geometrically: three collinear points constrain only the image of ONE line, and a line has 2 degrees of freedom in the plane, not 4. The configuration is degenerate, not merely ill-conditioned, and the solver returns an arbitrary member of a pencil.

This matters far more than it sounds, because degenerate samples are not rare — they are what RANSAC keeps drawing when the scene contains a dominant straight edge, a building facade seen edge-on, or a row of matched points along a horizon. A minimal-sample check that rejects near-collinear quadruples before fitting is one of the cheapest reliability improvements available (§09).

04 2D alignment by least squares

For translation, similarity and affine motion the residual is linear in the parameters. That is not a small convenience — it means the fit is a single non-iterative solve with a guaranteed global optimum, and it is why these three models are worth reaching for first.

The pattern: write it as A p = b

Take the affine case. The map is x′ = a₀₀x + a₀₁y + t₀, y′ = a₁₀x + a₁₁y + t₁. Collect the six unknowns into p and each correspondence contributes two rows:

affine, one correspondence → two rows⎡ xᵢ yᵢ 1 0 0 0 ⎤ ⎡ a₀₀ ⎤ ⎡ x̃ᵢ ⎤ ⎣ 0 0 0 xᵢ yᵢ 1 ⎦ · ⎢ a₀₁ ⎥ = ⎣ ỹᵢ ⎦ ⎢ t₀ ⎥ Jᵢ (2 × 6) ⎢ a₁₀ ⎥ b ᵢ (2 × 1) ⎢ a₁₁ ⎥ ⎣ t₁ ⎦ stack all N and solve the normal equations( ∑ᵢ JᵢᵀJᵢ ) p = ∑ᵢ Jᵢᵀ bᵢ i.e. (AᵀA) p = Aᵀb A is 2N × 6. In practice do NOT form AᵀA — solve the 2N × 6 system by QR, which squares nothing and so costs you half the digits AᵀA would.

Translation has a one-line answer

translation: J = I, so the normal equations collapset̂ = (1/N) ∑ᵢ (x̃ᵢ − xᵢ) the mean displacement, and nothing more Least squares for a pure translation is an AVERAGE. Which is exactly why a single bad correspondence moves it: one match 300 px wrong among 30 good ones shifts the answer by 10 px, with no warning in the residual of any individual good point.

Similarity keeps its linearity by a change of variables

A similarity is s·R(θ) plus a translation, which is not linear in (s, θ). Substituting a = s·cos θ and b = s·sin θ makes it linear in (a, b, t₀, t₁), and the scale and angle come back out at the end. This trick — reparameterise until the model is linear, fit, then convert — is worth remembering; it also works for the 2D rigid case in §05.

similarity, one correspondence → two rows⎡ xᵢ −yᵢ 1 0 ⎤ ⎡ a ⎤ ⎡ x̃ᵢ ⎤ s = √(a² + b²) ⎣ yᵢ xᵢ 0 1 ⎦ · ⎢ b ⎥ = ⎣ ỹᵢ ⎦ θ = atan2(b, a) ⎢ t₀ ⎥ ⎣ t₁ ⎦ recovered AFTER the linear solve

The Euclidean and similarity fits share a rotation angle, exactly. Both report 15.1080°. That is not a coincidence: in the closed form of §05, the optimal rotation is computed from the cross-covariance of the centred point sets, and the optimal scale divides out of the expression for the angle. So you can fit the rotation once and decide about the scale afterwards. Verified: both routes agree to 1 × 10⁻¹⁵.
The model ladder: drag a correspondence and watch every fit move translation, Euclidean, similarity and affine fitted to the same points — add noise, add an outlier, and see which models notice
A tempting shortcut. "I'll always fit affine — it contains similarity, so it can only be better." It contains similarity, so it can only fit the noise better. With N correspondences and n parameters the expected residual shrinks like √(1 − n/2N) whether or not the extra parameters mean anything, while the variance of the estimated map grows with n. With six points, affine spends half its data on parameters; with three, it interpolates and the residual is exactly zero, which tells you nothing at all. Model selection here is the same discipline as everywhere else: judge on held-out correspondences, not on the ones you fitted.

05 Rigid alignment has a closed form

For the Euclidean and similarity models you do not need a linear solver at all. Centre both point sets, form one small cross-covariance matrix, and read the rotation off an SVD. It is exact, it never fails to converge, and the same three lines work in 3D — which is why Part 7 uses it too.

the problem (orthogonal Procrustes, with scale)minimise E(s, R, t) = ∑ᵢ ‖ sRxᵢ + t − x̃ᵢ ‖² subject to RᵀR = I, det R = +1 step 1 — the translation drops out∂E/∂t = 0 ⇒ t̂ = x̄̃ − sR x̄ with x̄ = (1/N)∑xᵢ, x̄̃ = (1/N)∑x̃ᵢ Substituting it back CENTRES both sets. Everything that follows is about the centred coordinates yᵢ = xᵢ − x̄ and ỹᵢ = x̃ᵢ − x̄̃ , and translation never appears again. This is the whole trick. step 2 — the rotation, from one SVDC = ∑ᵢ ỹᵢ yᵢᵀ (2 × 2, or 3 × 3 in 3D) C = U Σ Vᵀ R̂ = U · diag(1, …, 1, det(UVᵀ)) · Vᵀ The diag(…, det(UVᵀ)) factor is NOT decoration. Without it the maximiser of tr(RᵀC) may be a REFLECTION, which fits mirrored data beautifully and is not a rotation. It costs one determinant and it is the only subtle line here. step 3 — the scale, if you want itŝ = tr(Σ′) / ∑ᵢ ‖yᵢ‖² Σ′ = diag(σ₁, …, det(UVᵀ)·σ_d) Note ŝ depends on R̂ but R̂ does NOT depend on ŝ — the scale is a positive factor in tr(sRᵀC) and cannot change which R maximises it. Hence §04's observation that the Euclidean and similarity fits report the same angle.

06 The direct linear transform

A homography is not linear in its parameters — there is a division by the third homogeneous coordinate. Clear the denominator and it becomes linear, at the price of minimising the wrong error. That trade is the DLT, and it is worth understanding both halves of it.

Where the non-linearity is

the map, written out h₀₀x + h₀₁y + h₀₂ h₁₀x + h₁₁y + h₁₂ x′ = ─────────────────── , y′ = ─────────────────── h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ Nine entries, but the map is unchanged by H → λH for any λ ≠ 0 — the scale cancels in both fractions. So the parameter space is 8-dimensional, not 9: a POINT of the projective space of 3 × 3 matrices, which is why H is always fixed by a normalisation such as ‖h‖ = 1 or h₂₂ = 1.

Clearing the denominator

Multiply through by the denominator w = h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂. The two equations become linear in the nine unknowns:

one correspondence → two linear rows−h₀₀x − h₀₁y − h₀₂ + x′(h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂) = 0 −h₁₀x − h₁₁y − h₁₂ + y′(h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂) = 0 as a 2 × 9 block acting on the stacked entries h = (h₀₀ … h₂₂)ᵀ⎡ −x −y −1 0 0 0 x′x x′y x′ ⎤ ⎣ 0 0 0 −x −y −1 y′x y′y y′ ⎦ · h = 0 the same thing said projectively — where the two rows come fromx̃′ ~ Hx̃ means the two 3-vectors are PARALLEL, i.e. x̃′ × (Hx̃) = 0. A cross product of 3-vectors gives three scalar equations, but they are linearly dependent (their combination with x̃′ vanishes identically), so only TWO are independent. That is the honest reason a correspondence contributes two rows and not three, and it is also why dropping the third row is not an approximation.

The homogeneous least-squares solve

stack N correspondences → A is 2N × 9minimise ‖Ah‖² subject to ‖h‖ = 1 ← the constraint is essential: without it h = 0 wins. solution: h = the right singular vector of A belonging to its SMALLEST singular value; equivalently the eigenvector of AᵀA with the smallest eigenvalue. N = 4 (general position) → A is 8 × 9, rank 8, one-dimensional null space, EXACT. N > 4 → rank 9, no exact null space, and the smallest singular vector is the least-squares compromise. what is actually being minimised‖Ah‖² = ∑ᵢ ‖ x̃ᵢ′ × (Hx̃ᵢ) ‖² = ∑ᵢ wᵢ² · ‖geometric residualᵢ‖² + O(r³) The ALGEBRAIC error is the geometric error weighted by wᵢ², the homogeneous scale at that point. Points the homography sends far away carry more weight; points it brings close carry less. Nobody wants that weighting. It is the price of linearity, and §08 removes it.

The scale ambiguity, concretely. The solver returns h with ‖h‖ = 1, which for this example means every entry is about 10⁻² to 10⁻⁷ — not a matrix anyone can read. Dividing by h₂₂ puts it back in the familiar form with a 1 in the corner. That division is safe here and is not safe in general: h₂₂ = 0 for any homography that sends the origin of image 1 to infinity, which happens for wide baselines and near-90° plane rotations. Production code keeps ‖h‖ = 1 and never divides.

07 Normalisation: turning 10⁶ into 3

Run the DLT on raw pixel coordinates and the design matrix mixes numbers of size 1 with numbers of size 10⁵. Rescale the points first — an operation that takes four lines and no thought — and the same system becomes as well conditioned as anything you will ever solve. This is the highest ratio of benefit to effort on the page.

The diagnosis

A row of A reads [−x, −y, −1, 0, 0, 0, x′x, x′y, x′]. In a 640 × 480 image the entries have magnitudes roughly 10², 10², 1, 0, 0, 0, 10⁵, 10⁵, 10². Least squares treats all nine columns as equally important; they are not remotely equally scaled, so the residual is dominated by the two quadratic columns and the constant column is effectively invisible. The condition number quantifies exactly this.

the transform (isotropic, per image, independently)T = ⎡ s 0 −s·x̄ ⎤ x̄, ȳ = the centroid of the points ⎢ 0 s −s·ȳ ⎥ s = √2 / (mean distance from the centroid) ⎣ 0 0 1 ⎦ After T, the centroid is at the ORIGIN and the mean distance from it is √2 — i.e. the average point sits at about (1, 1), so all nine columns of A are O(1). the algorithm1 · T₁ from {xᵢ}, T₂ from {x̃ᵢ} (two different transforms) 2 · x̂ᵢ = T₁xᵢ , x̂̃ᵢ = T₂x̃ᵢ 3 · Ĥ = DLT( {x̂ᵢ}, {x̂̃ᵢ} ) the conditioned solve 4 · H = T₂⁻¹ Ĥ T₁ undo the conditioning Step 4 is exact — a homography conjugated by two similarities is a homography — so nothing is approximated. Only the ARITHMETIC changed.

Read that table honestly, because it is easy to oversell. On well-spread points in a modest image and in double precision, normalisation buys about 4% of accuracy — 0.973 px against 0.933 px. The famous factor-of-ten improvements come from the other rows: clustered points (8.5× better), large images (1.3×), coordinates far from the origin such as the tiles of a mosaic (1.3×, and the absolute errors are catastrophic in both). What normalisation reliably buys in every row is conditioning: cond goes from 10⁶–10⁹ to between 4 and 6, always. That is what makes the method safe in single precision, safe when the configuration degrades, and safe when it is nested inside an iteration that will amplify anything it is given. Do it because it costs four lines, not because the average case demands it.
What normalisation does to the design matrix move the point configuration and watch both condition numbers — they respond to completely different things
A claim that sounds right and is not. "Normalisation makes the DLT minimise the geometric error." It does not. The DLT still minimises an algebraic error; normalisation only makes the numerical problem of minimising it well posed. The weighting by wᵢ² from §06 is still there, merely applied to conditioned coordinates. Getting the geometric error requires the iterative refinement of §08, and on real data that refinement is worth more than the normalisation is — but it needs the normalised DLT as its starting point, so the two are not alternatives.

08 Refining to the right error

The DLT minimises an algebraic quantity because that is what is linear. Now that we have a good starting point, minimise the quantity we actually care about — with Gauss–Newton, from the normalised DLT, in about five iterations.

the objective, in pixelsE(h) = ∑ᵢ ‖ f(xᵢ; h) − x̃ᵢ ‖² f = the projective map, h = its 8 free entries the Jacobian, two rows per correspondence, with w = h₂₀x + h₂₁y + 1∂x′/∂h = [ x/w y/w 1/w 0 0 0 −x′x/w −x′y/w ] ∂y′/∂h = [ 0 0 0 x/w y/w 1/w −y′x/w −y′y/w ] Everything divides by w and the last two columns carry a factor of the OUTPUT coordinate — which is the same structure that made the algebraic error weight by w², now appearing where it belongs, inside a proper derivative. the iteration (Gauss–Newton, with a Levenberg damping term)( JᵀJ + λ·diag(JᵀJ) ) δ = −Jᵀr h ← h + δ λ = 0 is pure Gauss–Newton and converges quadratically near the optimum. λ large makes the step a small gradient-descent step, which always decreases E. The Levenberg–Marquardt rule — decrease λ after a successful step, increase it after a failed one — is the standard compromise. See Optimisation for the general treatment; nothing here is special to vision.

Which geometric error?

There are three, and choosing wrongly can make the fit worse than the DLT you started from. That is not a hypothetical — it is measured below.

forward transfer error — assumes image 1's coordinates are exactE_f = ∑ᵢ ‖ H xᵢ − x̃ᵢ ‖² symmetric transfer error — splits the blameE_s = ∑ᵢ ‖ H xᵢ − x̃ᵢ ‖² + ‖ H⁻¹x̃ᵢ − xᵢ ‖² gold standard / reprojection error — the maximum-likelihood oneE_g = ∑ᵢ ‖ x̂ᵢ − xᵢ ‖² + ‖ H x̂ᵢ − x̃ᵢ ‖² minimised over H AND over the corrected points {x̂ᵢ} It adds 2N nuisance parameters — a corrected position for every correspondence — and it is the correct estimator when BOTH images' coordinates are noisy, because that is an errors-in-variables problem and forcing all the error into one image is a misspecification, not a simplification.

The practical rule, from those three tables. Always normalise (§07); it is free. Refine only with an error consistent with where the noise is: symmetric if both images are measured, gold standard if you can afford the extra parameters, forward only if image 1's coordinates really are exact — which is true when they are the corners of a printed calibration pattern and false almost everywhere else. And on a near-affine homography, do not bother at all: 0.3%.
