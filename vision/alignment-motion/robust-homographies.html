09 Robust fitting: RANSAC, with a homography inside it

The estimator is Part 4 §27's and is not re-derived. What this section owns is what goes inside it for the models on this page: the minimal sample, the degeneracies that sample can hit, the threshold, and the refit.

the loop, with the homography-specific parts markedbest ← ∅ repeat N times: S ← 4 correspondences drawn uniformly at random ← s = 4 for a homography if S is degenerate (any 3 collinear): continue ← §03; CHEAP and essential H ← DLT(S), normalised ← §06–§07, exact from 4 I ← { i : ‖H xᵢ − x̃ᵢ‖ < t } ← the consensus set if |I| > |best|: best ← I; N ← adaptive update from |I|/n H* ← refit on `best`, all of it, by §07 then §08 ← NEVER return the minimal-sample H

The four decisions that actually matter

DecisionWhat to doWhat goes wrong if you do not
The threshold tSet it from the expected inlier noise, not by taste: for isotropic Gaussian error of standard deviation σ per coordinate, the squared distance is σ²χ²₂, so t = σ√5.99 ≈ 2.45σ admits 95% of true inliers. With σ = 1 px that is about 2.4 px.Too small and the consensus sets are tiny and noisy, so N explodes and the winner is chosen by luck. Too large and a wrong homography that happens to be close over part of the image collects enough support to win.
Degenerate samplesReject any minimal sample with three near-collinear points before fitting. Test all four triangle areas against the square of the sample's own largest pairwise distance; it costs four cross products. A threshold of 0.002 rejects 5.15% of uniformly random quadruples (measured over 20 000 draws) and essentially all the harmful ones.The DLT returns an arbitrary member of a family (§03, σ₈ ≈ 10⁻¹³). That arbitrary matrix occasionally scores a large consensus by accident and is then returned as the answer.
Adaptive NRecompute N from the best inlier ratio seen so far after every improvement. Start from a pessimistic w and let the data raise it.A fixed N chosen for w = 0.3 wastes 500 samples on data that is 80% inliers; one chosen for w = 0.7 silently fails on data that is 30%.
The final refitRefit on the whole consensus set, then re-classify inliers with the refitted model and repeat once or twice. The minimal-sample homography is fitted to exactly 4 points and has no noise averaging whatsoever.You keep an estimate whose accuracy is that of 4 noisy points instead of 200 — typically 3–5× worse, for no reason.

Why RANSAC and not least squares with a big residual threshold

Because least squares has a breakdown point of zero: a single correspondence, moved far enough, drags the fit arbitrarily far. §04's figure shows it happening on six points. Iterating "fit, discard the worst, refit" does not repair this — the first fit is already so corrupted that the worst residual may belong to a good point. RANSAC inverts the logic: it never lets the outliers into a fit in the first place, because it only ever fits minimal subsets and judges them by counting.

RANSAC fitting a homography, one sample at a time step through samples, or run to completion — the outlier slider is the thing to play with
A misreading of the iteration count. "I ran N = 72 samples, so I have a 99% chance of the right homography." You have a 99% chance of having drawn at least one all-inlier sample. That is a strictly weaker statement, as Part 4 §27 measures: even given a clean sample, four inliers that happen to be nearly collinear or clustered define a badly conditioned homography that will lose the consensus vote to a luckier sample. The formula bounds one failure mode out of three; the other two are degeneracy and bad conditioning within a clean sample, and only the degeneracy check and the final refit address them.

10 The other robust route: bounded influence

RANSAC decides which points to use. An M-estimator decides how much each point is allowed to say. They solve the same problem from opposite ends, and in practice a pipeline uses both — RANSAC first to get a starting point, an M-estimator afterwards to polish without a hard inlier/outlier cut.

replace the square with something that grows more slowlyE(p) = ∑ᵢ ρ(rᵢ) instead of E(p) = ∑ᵢ rᵢ² ∂E/∂p = ∑ᵢ ψ(rᵢ) · ∂rᵢ/∂p = 0 with ψ = ρ′ — the INFLUENCE function. ψ(r) is how hard a residual of size r pushes on the answer. the reweighting identity that makes it practicalwrite w(r) = ψ(r)/r , and the stationarity condition becomes ∑ᵢ w(rᵢ) · rᵢ · ∂rᵢ/∂p = 0 ⟺ the normal equations of a WEIGHTED least-squares problem. Iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS): compute residuals, compute weights, solve a weighted linear system, repeat. Three or four passes is usual. Scale. Every a above is in units of the inlier noise, which you do not know. The standard robust estimate is σ̂ = 1.4826 · median|rᵢ| — the median absolute deviation, scaled so that it is consistent for Gaussian noise. Re-estimate it at each IRLS pass, from the current residuals.

When to use which. If you can enumerate a minimal sample cheaply and the outlier fraction may exceed ~30%, use RANSAC — its breakdown point is essentially 1, limited only by patience. If the outliers are mild (mis-localisation rather than mismatch), the model is expensive to fit from a minimal sample, or you need a differentiable objective to embed in a larger optimisation such as §17's bundle adjustment, use an M-estimator. Dense motion (§21 onwards) has no minimal-sample structure at all — every pixel contributes one equation — so it uses M-estimators exclusively, which is why robust norms reappear in §24's smoothness term.

11 Case one: two views of a plane

This is the first of exactly two situations in which a homography is not an approximation but an identity. It is worth deriving in four lines, because the derivation shows precisely which assumption is doing the work.

setup — the camera model is Part 1 §16's, unchangedcamera 0: x̃₀ ~ K₀ X world frame = camera 0's frame camera 1: x̃₁ ~ K₁ (R X + t) the plane: n̂ᵀX = d n̂ a unit normal, d > 0 the distance to camera 0 the derivation1 · A pixel back-projects to a ray: X = Z · K₀⁻¹x̃₀ for some unknown depth Z. 2 · Impose the plane: n̂ᵀX = d ⇒ Z · (n̂ᵀK₀⁻¹x̃₀) = d ⇒ 1 = (n̂ᵀX)/d. That single scalar equation is what makes the depth recoverable, and it is the ONLY place the plane is used. 3 · Substitute the harmless-looking identity t = t · 1 = t · (n̂ᵀX)/d : x̃₁ ~ K₁(R X + t·(n̂ᵀX)/d) = K₁( R + t n̂ᵀ/d ) X 4 · And X ~ K₀⁻¹x̃₀ , so x̃₁ ~ K₁ ( R + t n̂ᵀ/d ) K₀⁻¹ x̃₀ = H₁₀ x̃₀ ∎ verified numericallyf = 600, R = 8° about y then 3° about z, t = (−0.60, 0, 0.18) m, plane at Z = 10 m, 300 points scattered ON the plane: max ‖ dehom(H x̃₀) − x̃₁ ‖ = 2.05 × 10⁻¹³ px Exact. Not "accurate to a fraction of a pixel" — exact, to double precision.

What the derivation tells you

  • The rank-one update t n̂ᵀ/d is the whole content. Without translation it vanishes and you get §12's case. With translation it depends on the plane through the single combination n̂/d, which is why you cannot recover the normal and the distance separately from H alone — only their ratio, and even then only up to a well-known two-fold ambiguity.
  • Any camera motion is allowed. The plane assumption does all the work; R and t are arbitrary. This is why a whiteboard, a document, a building facade, a floor and a football pitch are all homography problems no matter how the camera moves.
  • Different planes give different homographies. A scene with two planes at different depths needs two homographies, and the set of all plane-induced homographies for a fixed camera pair forms a 3-parameter family — which is exactly what Part 7's plane-plus-parallax decomposition exploits.
  • The intrinsics need not match. K₀ and K₁ appear separately, so a zoom, a different sensor or a different focal length between the two shots changes nothing structural. That is not true of the essential matrix.
The plane-induced homography, built from the geometry move the second camera and tilt the plane — the transfer error stays at machine zero for every point on the plane

12 Case two: a camera that rotates about its own centre

The second exact case, and the one panoramas are built on. Here the scene is arbitrary — any shape, any depth, any clutter — and the constraint is on the camera instead.

set t = 0 in §11's derivation and the plane disappearsx̃₁ ~ K₁ R X ~ K₁ R K₀⁻¹ x̃₀ = H₁₀ x̃₀ No plane. No depth. No d, no n̂. The depth Z cancelled in step 1 because it is a scalar multiplying a homogeneous vector, and with t = 0 nothing ever reintroduces it. THAT is why a rotating camera can photograph anything at all. verified numericallyf = 600, R = 8° about y then 3° about z, t = 0, 300 points at depths from 2 m to 80 m: max ‖ dehom(H x̃₀) − x̃₁ ‖ = 2.34 × 10⁻¹³ px A fortyfold range of depth, and it does not matter at all. the useful special form (principal point at the origin, square pixels)⎡ x₁ ⎤ ⎡ x₀ ⎤ K = diag(f, f, 1) ⎢ y₁ ⎥ ~ R₁₀⎢ y₀ ⎥ so a pixel is literally a 3D RAY (x, y, f), the ⎣ f₁ ⎦ ⎣ f₀ ⎦ rotation acts on rays, and the focal length is the z coordinate. Everything about panoramas follows.

Read them: ω_z is a rotation in the image plane (the (−y, x) field); ω_x and ω_y are pans and tilts, and the f₁ + x²/f₁ term is what makes a pan stretch the edges of a wide-angle frame more than the centre.
The nodal point: how exact is "pure" rotation? offset the rotation centre by a few centimetres and watch a supposedly exact model fail on the near objects first
Why panoramic tripod heads exist. Rotating a camera about anything other than its optical centre introduces a real translation, and a real translation introduces parallax. Measured at f = 600 px, a 25° pan, and a scene from 2 to 60 m, averaged over 200 000 points: an offset of 1 cm puts 2.95 px of error on an object 2 m away; 5 cm puts 14.8 px; 20 cm — roughly the distance from the sensor to the front of a long lens — puts 59.1 px. At 10 m the same offsets give 0.63, 3.13 and 12.5 px. The rule of thumb is error ≈ f·b/Z pixels, and it is remarkably tight: 3.00, 15.00 and 60.00 predicted against 2.95, 14.8 and 59.1 measured, an error of 1.5%. It is the single most useful formula there is for predicting whether a stitch will ghost.

13 And nowhere else: the plane-plus-parallax residual

This is the most misapplied fact in the subject, so here it is stated three ways, with the failure quantified rather than described.

true · a theorem
Any two views of a plane
For a planar scene, any camera motion, any two sets of intrinsics: H = K₁(R + t n̂ᵀ/d)K₀⁻¹, exact. Measured residual 2.0 × 10⁻¹³ px (§11).
true · a theorem
Any scene, one camera centre
For any scene, when the two camera centres coincide: H = K₁RK₀⁻¹, exact at every depth. Measured residual 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ px over a fortyfold depth range (§12). A zoom about the centre is included, because it only changes K.
false · and not approximately true
Any scene, any motion
For a non-planar scene seen from two different centres there is no homography at all — not a bad one, none. The residual after the best possible H is a well-defined non-zero field, and it is measured below.
the useful limiting case
A distant scene
As every depth Z → ∞ the parallax term vanishes, so a homography becomes arbitrarily good. This is the plane at infinity, and it is why a landscape stitches from a hand-held camera and a room does not. "Distant" means f·b/Z is below your tolerance — a number, not an adjective.

The exact decomposition

Take any homography H induced by a reference plane π. Every point in the scene, on the plane or not, satisfies:

One homography, a scene that is not a plane move the reference plane through the scene and watch the zero-residual surface move with it
The sentence to stop saying. "A homography relates two views of the same scene." A homography relates two views of the same plane, or two views from the same point. Both qualifications are essential and each is sufficient on its own. The version without a qualifier is false, and it is false in a way that no amount of data, no better estimator and no robust loss will fix — because there is no H that fits, not merely a hard-to-find one. RANSAC will happily return the homography of whichever surface happens to dominate the correspondences and declare everything else an outlier, which looks exactly like success right up until you composite the image.
How to tell, from the residuals alone, in one glance. Fit your homography, then draw the residual vectors on image 2. If they point along a common pencil of lines through a single point, you have parallax: the scene is not planar and the camera translated, the common point is the epipole, and the fix is a different model — Part 7's two-view geometry — not a better fit. If they are random in direction, you have noise and you are done. If they curve smoothly outward from the image centre, you have unmodelled radial distortion (Part 1 §17) and should undistort first.
