14 Applying it: inverse warping, and where the pixels come from

Estimating H is half the job; the other half is moving pixels with it. Part 2 §26 owns this and derives the forward-versus-inverse argument in full. This section states only the three things that bite specifically in alignment.

always inverse-warp — the rule, restatedfor every pixel x′ of the OUTPUT: x = H⁻¹ x′ a real-valued position in the input out(x′) = interpolate(in, x) one sample, one destination pixel Forward warping — scatter each input pixel to H x — leaves holes where the map expands and collides where it contracts, and needs splatting to repair both. Inverse warping has exactly one output value per output pixel by construction.
  • Compute the output bounding box first. Warp the four corners of the input through H, take the bounding box of the results, and offset the output by its top-left corner. Skip this and half of every panorama lands at negative coordinates and is silently discarded. The offset is itself a translation, so it composes into H and costs nothing.
  • Watch for the horizon. A homography sends the line h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ = 0 to infinity. If that line crosses the input image, the warped output is unbounded and the bounding box is meaningless. Detect it — evaluate w at the four corners and check the signs agree — and clip before warping. This is the single most common crash in a hand-rolled stitcher.
  • Match the filter to the scale change. Where H minifies, bilinear interpolation aliases badly, because one output pixel should average many input pixels and instead reads two. The fix is a mip-map or an elliptically weighted average — Part 2 §28. Where H magnifies, bilinear is merely soft and bicubic is better. A homography usually does both within one image, which is why panoramas need a scale-aware resampler and not a single kernel.
  • Interpolate once. Chaining n warps by resampling n times costs n generations of blur. Compose the matrices — H₂₀ = H₂₁H₁₀, which is exact and free — and resample from the original once.
The alignment-specific consequence. Every accuracy number on this page is quoted in pixels of the input. Resampling adds its own error on top, and for bilinear interpolation of a band-limited image that error is roughly 0.5–2% of the local contrast — comparable to a tenth of a pixel of displacement on typical texture. So there is little point refining a homography below about 0.05 px on a pipeline that then resamples bilinearly: the warp is the accuracy floor, not the fit.

15 Where to put the panorama: flat, cylindrical, spherical

A wide panorama cannot live on a plane. Choosing the surface it lives on is the one decision in stitching that no amount of good estimation can undo afterwards, and the trade is completely explicit: straight lines against uniform sampling.

The two standard surfaces

cylindrical — parameterise by an angle and a height(sin θ, h, cos θ) ∝ (x, y, f) forward x′ = s·θ = s · atan(x / f) y′ = s·h = s · y / √(x² + f²) inverse x = f · tan(x′/s) y = (y′/s) · √(x² + f²) = f · (y′/s) · sec(x′/s) s is the cylinder's radius in pixels; s = f keeps the centre of the image at unit magnification. Round-trip verified to 5.7 × 10⁻¹⁴ px. spherical — two angles(sin θ cos φ, sin φ, cos θ cos φ) ∝ (x, y, f) forward x′ = s · atan(x / f) y′ = s · atan( y / √(x² + f²) ) inverse x = f · tan(x′/s) y = tan(y′/s) · √(x² + f²) = f · tan(y′/s) · sec(x′/s) The x mapping is IDENTICAL to the cylinder's; only the vertical coordinate differs, replacing a linear height by a second angle. Round-trip verified to 5.7 × 10⁻¹⁴ px. Note the pole is at the y axis here, not the z axis, because images are viewed with y along gravity.
The payoff that makes cylinders worth the trouble. If the camera is level and rotates only about its vertical axis, then after warping to cylindrical coordinates the images are related by a pure horizontal translation — one parameter. Not a homography, not an affine map: a shift. That collapses the whole estimation problem to a 1-D search, which is why cylindrical stitching was the first kind that worked reliably and why it is still the right choice for a motorised pan head. The moment the camera tilts, the assumption fails and you are back to §12's rotations.

Where the labour actually is. Steps 1–6 are this page's subject and are essentially solved: given decent features, a modern pipeline registers a hundred images to a few tenths of a pixel without supervision. Steps 7–9 are where the visible quality lives, and they are not geometry problems — they are about a scene that moved, an exposure that changed, and a parallax you cannot remove. That inversion surprises people: the hard part of stitching is not the alignment.

17 Global alignment: drift, gap closing, bundle adjustment

Registering pairs and chaining them is a recipe for accumulating error. The fix is to stop treating the problem as a sequence of pairs and to solve for all the cameras at once. Part 7 owns bundle adjustment properly; this is the rotation-only special case, which is small enough to see whole.

the pairwise objective, summed over EVERY verified correspondenceE = ∑_(pairs jk) ∑_(i ∈ inliers jk) ‖ x̃ᵢʲ − P(Rⱼ, fⱼ ; Rₖ, f_k ; x̃ᵢᵏ) ‖² unknowns: R₀ … R_(n−1) (3 each) and f₀ … f_(n−1) (1 each) gauge: the whole panorama can be rotated freely, so fix R₀ = I — otherwise the Hessian is singular in exactly three directions. the better-behaved residual: compare RAYS, not pixelsr = R_j Kⱼ⁻¹x̃ᵢʲ − R_k K_k⁻¹x̃ᵢᵏ both are unit-ish 3-vectors in the world Working in ray space rather than in one image's pixels removes the arbitrary choice of which image to reproject into, keeps the cost symmetric between the two frames, and stays finite for correspondences near 90° apart — where a reprojection into a flat image would diverge. why it is cheap despite the sizeThe Hessian is BLOCK-SPARSE: an entry couples two cameras only if they share a correspondence, and a panorama's topology graph is nearly a chain. With n images the dense cost is O(n³) and the sparse cost is closer to O(n). A 200-image panorama with 4 parameters each is a 800-variable problem that solves in milliseconds — which is why bundle adjustment is never the bottleneck here.

Drift and the 360° gap, quantified

Chain n pairwise rotations around a full circle with a focal length that is wrong by a factor, and the panorama does not close. The error is not random — it is systematic and it tells you the focal length.

The patch that hides the problem. "There is a gap, so distribute it evenly over the frames." That fixes the symptom of a wrong focal length by corrupting every rotation. Gap closing as an estimator of f is legitimate and is what the formula above does; gap closing as a cosmetic redistribution leaves every image slightly mis-rotated, and it only works for a panorama that turns continuously in one direction. Bundle adjustment removes the need for either, because a loop closure is just one more constraint among thousands.

18 Exposure compensation: one gain per image

Automatic exposure means every frame of a panorama was taken with a different sensitivity. That is a photometric misalignment, and it is solved the same way as the geometric one: write a least-squares problem over all the overlaps at once.

the model and the objectiveimage k has an unknown gain g_k; the corrected image is g_k · I_k e = ½ ∑ⱼ ∑ₖ N_jk [ (g_j Ī_jk − g_k Ī_kj)² / σ_N² + (1 − g_j)² / σ_g² ] Ī_jk the mean intensity of image j over its overlap with image k N_jk the number of pixels in that overlap — the natural weight σ_N the tolerated intensity error; σ_g the tolerated deviation of a gain from 1 why the prior is not optionalWithout the (1 − g_j)² term the objective is homogeneous of degree 2 in g, so g = 0 is a global minimum with e = 0. The gains are determined only UP TO A GLOBAL SCALE, and the prior is what chooses it. Everyone rediscovers this by watching their panorama go black. the solvee is quadratic in g, so ∂e/∂g = 0 is a linear system with one row per image: [ 1/σ_g² + ∑ₖ N_jk Ī_jk²/σ_N² ] g_j − ∑ₖ ( N_jk Ī_jk Ī_kj/σ_N² ) g_k = 1/σ_g² Sparse, symmetric, positive definite, and the same size as the number of images. measured — four images in a chain, true exposures (1.00, 1.35, 0.72, 1.10)ideal gains 1/e_k (up to scale) 1.0000 0.7407 1.3889 0.9091 solved gains g_k 0.9383 0.6951 1.3032 0.8530 g_k · e_k 0.9383 0.9383 0.9383 0.9383 spread 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ The product g_k·e_k is CONSTANT to five decimal places — the gains are correct up to exactly one global factor, and the prior has set that factor to 0.938 rather than 1 because pulling every gain toward 1 is a compromise, not a constraint. Seam mismatches fall from 0.147, 0.347 and 0.118 to under 7 × 10⁻⁶.
  • Work in the log domain, or better, in radiance. A gain is multiplicative, so log g makes the problem additive and symmetric in the direction of the error. Better still, undo the camera response curve first and stitch radiances; then a gain is exactly an exposure ratio and the model is physically right rather than merely convenient.
  • One gain per image is not enough for vignetting. Lens fall-off is a smooth function of image position, so the correction has to be spatially varying — typically a low-order polynomial in radius, or a coarse spline of block-wise corrections re-estimated a few times against the blended composite. A single scalar cannot represent it, and forcing it to try produces a panorama with periodic dark bands at every frame boundary.
  • Use means, not medians, over the overlap — but robustly. The objective above assumes Ī_jk summarises the overlap. If a person walked through it, the mean is wrong. Compute it over the region that survived the geometric verification of §09, or use a robust mean (§10).

19 Seams and blending: hiding what is left

After the best possible registration and the best possible exposure compensation, the overlap still disagrees — because of parallax, because something moved, because the lenses differ. Blending is the admission that this residual cannot be removed, only distributed where the eye will not find it.

The compositing equation, and the three choices inside it

weighted compositingC(x) = ∑ₖ w_k(x)·Ĩ_k(x) ⁄ ∑ₖ w_k(x) Ĩ_k = image k, warped Everything is a choice of w_k: w_k = 1 inside image k → averaging. Ghosts and blurs. w_k = 1 on k's side of a chosen seam → hard seam. Sharp, but a visible step. w_k = distance to k's nearest border → feathering. A smooth ramp of width d. w_k = 1 for a moving object's owner → de-ghosting: pick ONE source per region so a person appears once.

Why one transition width cannot work

A narrow transition hides high-frequency disagreement (mis-registered texture) but leaves a low-frequency step (exposure, vignetting) visible as an edge. A wide transition removes the step but averages the texture over that width, producing a soft band. Both statements are true simultaneously, so no single width is right — which is the entire argument for making the width depend on frequency.

multi-band (Laplacian pyramid) blending1 · Build a band-pass pyramid of each warped image (Part 2 §23). 2 · Build a Gaussian pyramid of the blending mask. 3 · Blend each band with the mask AT THAT LEVEL — so the transition is a few pixels wide at the finest band and hundreds of pixels wide at the coarsest. 4 · Collapse. One line of intuition: high frequencies get a narrow transition, which does not blur them; low frequencies get a wide one, which hides the step. Each band gets the width that is right for it, and nothing else changes. measured — 128 × 128, a smooth photometric difference and a 1.5 px misalignmentmethod low-frequency step local contrast kept at the seam ÷ elsewhere in the seam band hard seam 6.14 1.000 ← the reference feather, width 2 px 6.14 1.000 feather, width 8 px 5.76 0.998 feather, width 32 px 3.04 0.906 multi-band, 5 levels 1.90 0.998 plain averaging 0.96 0.858 Both columns want to be small and 1 respectively. Choosing the seam, before blending across it

Blending works far better if the seam is put somewhere the two images already agree. Formulate it as a labelling problem — assign each output pixel a source image, penalising a change of label by how much the two sources differ there — and it is exactly the graph-cut energy of Part 5 §23, solved with the same machinery. The seam then runs around a moving person rather than through them, and along a wall rather than across a face.

gradient-domain compositing — the other routeCopy GRADIENTS instead of pixels, then integrate: min_C ∑_x ‖ ∇C(x) − ∇Ĩ_ℓ(x)(x) ‖² ℓ(x) = the source chosen at x which is a sparse linear least-squares problem (a Poisson equation) with one unknown per output pixel. Any constant offset between the sources dies in the gradient and never appears in C, so exposure differences vanish exactly rather than being faded out. The cost is a global solve, and the failure mode is that a large true gradient at a seam gets copied and becomes a permanent double edge.
