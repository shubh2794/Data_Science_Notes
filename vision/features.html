Features & Matching

Part 2 handed over an array whose gradients mean what they appear to mean. This part is the first one that throws most of the array away. Out of a million pixels it keeps a few thousand locations, attaches a short vector to each, and hands the result to Part 6 and Part 7 as a set of correspondences. Everything here is organised by one question: what survives? A location that moves when the camera moves is worthless; a location that reappears at the same physical point under rotation, scale change, blur, noise and a different exposure is the entire substrate of alignment, stitching, tracking and 3D reconstruction. The three tools are a detector (which locations), a descriptor (what to remember about them) and a robust fitter (which of the resulting guesses to believe).

01 Detect, describe, match, verify

Every sparse-correspondence system ever built has the same four stages, and they have survived three complete changes of technology. Only the contents of the boxes changed.

an image→detect→describe→match→verify→correspondences
stage 1 · where
Detection
Find locations that will be found again. Edges (§09–§14), corners (§03–§08), blobs (§16–§20). The output is a list of positions, usually with a scale and an orientation attached. The measure of success is repeatability, and it is measurable — §28.
stage 2 · what
Description
Convert the neighbourhood of each detection into a short vector that is the same for the same physical patch seen differently, and different for different patches. §21–§24. Every invariance is bought with discriminability; the design problem is choosing which to buy.
stage 3 · which
Matching
For each descriptor in one image, find its counterpart in the other. Nearest neighbour, the ratio test, cross-checking, approximate search. §25–§26. The output is a set of putative matches, most of which are right and some of which are catastrophically wrong.
stage 4 · believe
Verification
Fit a geometric model to the putative matches and keep only the ones that agree with it. RANSAC, §27. This stage exists because stage 3 cannot be made reliable — the right response to a 20% error rate is not a better descriptor, it is an estimator that tolerates 20% nonsense.

What a "feature" is, precisely

The word is used for three different things and conflating them causes real confusion. A feature point or keypoint is a location (x, y), usually with a scale σ and an orientation θ — a geometric object. A descriptor is a vector attached to that location — an appearance object. A feature in the machine-learning sense is a coordinate of an input vector, which is a third thing entirely. This page uses keypoint and descriptor wherever the distinction matters.

the object this page producesa keypoint k = (x, y, σ, θ, s) position, scale, orientation, strength a descriptor d ∈ ℝᴰ or {0,1}ᴰ D = 128 (float) or 256 (bits) are the usual sizes a putative match (k_a, k_b, dist) one from each image, plus how good it looked an inlier set ⊂ putative matches the subset that a geometric model explains An image of 1000 × 1000 pixels typically yields 2000–5000 keypoints. That is a compression of about 500:1 in count, and after descriptors, roughly 4:1 in bytes — but the point is not compression. The point is that the surviving 0.2% of locations are the ones whose identity is stable across viewpoint.

The pipeline is older than any of its parts

Corner detection by local autocorrelation dates to the mid-1980s; scale selection to the mid-1990s; gradient-histogram descriptors to 1999; robust fitting by random sampling to 1981. Every one of those components has been replaced at least once, and in most modern systems the detector and descriptor are both learned. The pipeline shape survived all of it. That is the argument for learning it in this order: the boxes are stable even when their contents are not.

03 The aperture problem, and why it forces corners

Look at a moving edge through a hole small enough that the edge's endpoints are hidden, and you cannot tell how it is moving. This is not an illusion or a limitation of the algorithm — the information is not in the data. Everything on the next five sections exists to find places where it is.

The statement, three ways

geometricallyThrough an aperture that shows only a straight edge, a displacement along the edge produces an IDENTICAL image. Every displacement of the form u + t·ê (ê the edge tangent, t any real number) explains the observation equally well. as an equation — the brightness-constancy constraint of one pixelI(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t) ⇒ Iₓ·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0 one linear equation, TWO unknowns The solution set is a LINE in (u, v) space — the "constraint line". Its normal is the image gradient ∇I. The component of motion ALONG ∇I is determined; the component perpendicular to it is completely free. This is why the recoverable part is called NORMAL FLOW: u_n = −I_t / ‖∇I‖ along ∇I/‖∇I‖, and nothing else. as a rank condition — the version that connects to §05Stack the constraint over a whole window and it becomes A·Δu = b with A = ∑ w·∇I∇Iᵀ. The displacement is determined ⟺ A is INVERTIBLE ⟺ both eigenvalues of A are non-zero ⟺ the window contains gradients in at least two significantly different orientations.

The three statements are the same fact at three levels of abstraction, and the third is the useful one, because it turns "is this patch localisable?" into a question about a 2 × 2 matrix that can be computed at every pixel with three convolutions.

Why this matters for detection. The uncertainty ellipse has semi-axes proportional to 1/√λ₀ and 1/√λ₁. The worst direction is governed by the smaller eigenvalue, so a patch is well localised exactly when λ₀ — the smaller one — is large. That single sentence is the entire justification for every corner detector in §06, and it is why the Shi–Tomasi score is λ₀ and nothing else.
The tempting shortcut. "The aperture problem is solved by using a bigger window." It is solved by using a window that contains a corner, and a bigger window merely makes that more likely. Enlarge the aperture around an infinitely long straight edge and the problem is exactly as bad as before. What breaks the ambiguity is two different gradient orientations, not more pixels — and the whole-image version of the same ambiguity is why Part 6's optical flow needs either a smoothness prior or a parametric motion model.

04 The autocorrelation surface

Before choosing a corner measure, formalise what "localisable" means: shift a patch against itself and see how fast the mismatch grows. The resulting surface — a function of the shift, not of position — is the object every corner detector approximates.

The surface near the origin is a paraboloid, and the whole of its local shape is carried by one 2 × 2 symmetric matrix. Everything after this point is a question about that matrix.

Two things are worth noticing about that derivation. First, the approximation is local in the shift, not in position: it is accurate for |Δu| small compared with the scale of image structure, and it is exactly why a detector built on A reports the shape of the bottom of the valley and knows nothing about far-away repeated structure. Second, it is the same expansion that Part 6 uses to derive Lucas–Kanade tracking, and A is the same matrix. A detector that finds points where A is well conditioned is, by construction, finding points where the tracker's linear system is well conditioned. Detection and tracking are the same criterion, read in two directions.

the level sets of the approximation — the picture to keepE_AC(Δu) = c ⟺ Δuᵀ A Δu = c ⟺ an ELLIPSE with axis directions = eigenvectors of A semi-axis lengths = √(c/λ₀) and √(c/λ₁) Big λ ⇒ short axis ⇒ the mismatch grows fast in that direction ⇒ well constrained. 05 The structure tensor and its eigenvalues

Three convolutions produce a 2 × 2 symmetric matrix at every pixel. Its two eigenvalues are the complete local answer to "in how many directions does this neighbourhood constrain a match, and how strongly?" — and they are the last place in the pipeline where anything is exact.

The matrix

the definition, and the fact that it is three convolutions and not a summation ⎡ Iₓ² IₓI_y ⎤ A = w ∗ ⎢ ⎥ w = a Gaussian of INTEGRATION scale σᵢ ⎣ IₓI_y I_y² ⎦ Iₓ, I_y at DIFFERENTIATION scale σ_d Read the outer product first and the smoothing second. ∇I∇Iᵀ at one pixel is a rank-1 matrix — the outer product of a single vector with itself. Summing many of them with positive weights is what can make the result rank 2. That is the entire mechanism. the algorithm — four passes over the image, all separable (part 2 §11)1. Iₓ = (∂G_σ_d/∂x) ∗ I and I_y = (∂G_σ_d/∂y) ∗ I 2. form the three products Iₓ², IₓI_y, I_y² (per pixel, no filtering) 3. smooth each of the three with G_σᵢ 4. at every pixel, read out A = [[A₁₁, A₁₂], [A₁₂, A₂₂]] Cost: 4 separable passes plus 3 multiplies per pixel. Nothing here is quadratic in the window size, which is why this works at video rates on a whole image.

Two scales, and why one of them is not optional

The two scales do different jobs and confusing them is the most common implementation error. σ_d sets how much the derivative is smoothed — it defines what counts as an edge rather than noise. σᵢ sets the size of the neighbourhood over which gradient orientations are pooled. The second one is what makes the matrix informative at all.

Why the window is a Gaussian and not a square. A square window makes the response depend on the image's orientation relative to the pixel grid — rotate the image by 45° and the same corner is pooled over a different set of pixels, so its score changes. A circularly symmetric window removes that dependence, and among separable circularly symmetric kernels the Gaussian is the only one (Part 3 § on why). It also removes the discontinuity as a feature crosses the window boundary, which is what makes sub-pixel maxima meaningful.

06 Corner response functions, and why nobody uses the eigenvalues

Everything needed is in (λ₀, λ₁). The remaining question is how to collapse two numbers into one score that can be thresholded — and the standard answer deliberately does not compute the eigenvalues at all.

The four candidates

all four are functions of the same two numbers, plotted as isocontours in the figureShi–Tomasi R = λ₀ the smaller eigenvalue, alone Harris R = det A − k·(tr A)² = λ₀λ₁ − k(λ₀ + λ₁)², k ∈ [0.04, 0.06] Noble R = det A / tr A = λ₀λ₁/(λ₀ + λ₁), half the harmonic mean Triggs R = λ₀ − α·λ₁ α ≈ 0.05 Shi–Tomasi is the one with a derivation: §03 showed the worst-case localisation uncertainty is governed by λ₀, so "maximise λ₀" is not a heuristic, it is the answer to the question. The other three are approximations to it that are cheaper, smoother, or both.

Why a determinant-and-trace combination, and not the eigenvalues

There are three separate reasons, and only the first is the one usually quoted.

reason 1 · cost
No square root
det and tr are three multiplies and two adds from A₁₁, A₁₂, A₂₂. The eigenvalues need a square root at every pixel. On a megapixel image at thirty frames per second that mattered enormously when this was designed, and still matters on embedded hardware.
reason 2 · smoothness
λ₀ is not differentiable
λ₀ = t − √(…) has a square-root singularity exactly where λ₀ = λ₁ — that is, at the most corner-like points. The Harris response is a polynomial in the entries of A, so it is smooth everywhere, which is what makes sub-pixel interpolation of its maxima (§07) well behaved.
reason 3 · completeness
There is nothing else to use
det and tr are a complete set of rotation invariants of a symmetric 2 × 2. Any rotation-invariant score is a function of them — including λ₀ itself. Using them is not an approximation to using eigenvalues; it is the same information in a cheaper coordinate system.

The claim you will see everywhere. "Harris is rotation invariant but not scale invariant." Half right, and the half that is right needs a caveat. Rotation invariance holds for the ideal operator with an isotropic window; a discrete implementation with a square window or an axis-aligned derivative stencil has a measurable orientation bias, which is why Part 2 §13's Scharr kernel exists. Scale invariance genuinely fails, and §16–§19 are the fix. What is not true is the further claim that Harris is invariant to affine intensity change: it is invariant to a brightness offset (gradients kill it) but the response scales as a⁴ under I → aI, because A is quadratic in the gradient and det is quadratic in A. A fixed threshold on R is therefore exposure dependent, which is why thresholds are set relative to the maximum response rather than absolutely.

07 The detector, end to end — including the parts nobody writes down

The response map is not the detector. Getting from a response map to a list of keypoints takes three more steps, and two of them are where the accuracy actually comes from.

Steps 5–7 are the detector. Papers describe 1–4 and implementations live or die on 5–7.

Step 5: threshold and non-maximum suppression, and why the order matters

A single corner produces a blob of high response several pixels wide, because A was pooled over a window of radius roughly 2σᵢ. Thresholding alone would return that whole blob as dozens of keypoints. Non-maximum suppression over a 3 × 3 neighbourhood keeps only the peak. Two details are easy to get wrong: the comparison must be strictly greater in at least one direction or a plateau of exactly equal values reports every pixel in it; and the threshold should be relative to the image's own maximum response, because §06 showed R scales as the fourth power of contrast and an absolute threshold is therefore an exposure setting in disguise.

08 Taking the strongest n is the wrong selection rule

A detector returns far more candidates than a matcher can afford. The obvious way to cut them down — keep the n strongest — concentrates every keypoint in the highest-contrast corner of the image and leaves the rest bare. Geometry fitted to a clustered point set is badly conditioned, and that is a real failure, not an aesthetic one.

09 Edges: what they are and what they are not

Corners are rare and precisely localisable. Edges are everywhere, badly localisable along their length, and carry most of the semantics — object outlines, occlusion boundaries, the structure of man-made scenes. Two entirely different jobs, from the same gradient field.

An edge is not a boundary. A brightness discontinuity has four physically distinct causes — a depth discontinuity, a surface-orientation crease, a reflectance change, and an illumination change such as a shadow edge (Part 1 §20). Only the first two are object boundaries. A local operator cannot tell them apart, because locally they look identical, and no amount of tuning will make it able to. Grouping edges into object boundaries needs region evidence and is Part 5's problem; combining brightness, colour and texture gradients is where a hand-designed detector stops and a learned one starts (§29).
A category error worth naming. "The edge detector missed the boundary between the two grey regions." If the two regions have the same mean intensity, there is no gradient, and the detector is behaving correctly. An iso-luminant colour boundary, a texture boundary and a boundary defined only by motion are all invisible to a luminance gradient operator by construction. The fix is a different feature channel, not a different threshold.

Three traps

trap 1
Comparing along the edge
Using the tangent (−n̂_y, n̂_x) instead of the normal deletes the entire edge, because along a straight edge the magnitude is roughly constant and nothing is a strict maximum. Symptom: an empty output. The comparison direction is the gradient, which is perpendicular to the contour.
trap 2
≥ versus >
On a flat crest two adjacent pixels can have exactly equal magnitude. With strict > on both sides, both are deleted and the edge acquires holes. With ≥ on both sides, both survive and the edge is two pixels wide in places. Neither is disastrous; a common compromise is > forward and ≥ backward, which breaks the tie consistently.
trap 3
Suppressing before thresholding
Order matters. Suppress first, then threshold: a weak-but-genuine crest survives suppression and can be recruited by hysteresis. Threshold first and the crest is gone before suppression ever sees it, so hysteresis has nothing to link and the fading arc in the figure vanishes at the same low threshold that would otherwise have saved it.

12 Hysteresis thresholding: why one threshold cannot work

A real contour's contrast varies along its length. A single threshold must be low enough to keep the faint part and high enough to reject noise, and on almost every real image those two constraints are incompatible. Hysteresis resolves it by making the decision contextual: a weak pixel is believed if it is connected to a strong one.

the ruleSTRONG = { p : ‖∇I‖(p) ≥ τ_hi } seeds EDGE = every 8-connected component of { p : ‖∇I‖(p) ≥ τ_lo } that contains at least one STRONG pixel Implemented as a flood fill from the strong set through the weak set — one pass, one stack, linear in the number of pixels. The name is from magnetism: the threshold to KEEP following an edge (τ_lo) is lower than the threshold to START one (τ_hi), so the decision depends on the path taken to reach the pixel.

14 From edge pixels to contours

Everything downstream — line fitting, shape matching, contour descriptors — wants a curve, meaning an ordered list of points. An edge map is an unordered set. The gap between them is a small, dull, essential algorithm.

Linking

the procedure1. pick any unvisited edge pixel 2. walk to its unvisited 8-connected edge neighbours, preferring the one whose gradient ORIENTATION is closest to the current one (this is what disambiguates a crossing) 3. when the walk stops, restart from the same seed in the OPPOSITE direction 4. the two halves, reversed and concatenated, are one chain 5. repeat until every edge pixel is assigned Junctions are the only hard part, and there is no local rule that resolves them correctly. The standard compromise — break every chain at a pixel with more than two edge neighbours — is wrong at X-crossings and right almost everywhere else.

Three representations of a chain, and what each is for

compact
Chain code
Store the start point and then 3 bits per step, one of the eight compass directions. Compresses well with run-length or predictive coding. Nearly useless for further processing: it is quantised to 45° and to the pixel lattice, so any derivative computed from it is garbage.
analytic
Arc-length parameterisation x(s)
Store the chain as a function of arc length. Now it can be resampled uniformly, smoothed, differentiated for curvature, or Fourier-transformed. Normalise s to [0, 1] and subtract the centroid and two closed contours of the same shape differ only by a cyclic shift in s and a rotation — which makes shape matching a correlation.
structural
Polyline / spline
Approximate the chain by a few vertices — §15. Turns thousands of pixels into a handful of parameters and makes "is this a straight line?" a question you can answer. The cost is a tolerance parameter that decides what counts as a corner.
The trap in smoothing a contour. Convolving x(s) with a Gaussian to remove pixel noise shrinks the curve: a circle of radius R smoothed at scale σ becomes a circle of radius approximately R(1 − σ²/2R²), and iterating the smoothing collapses any closed curve to a point. The standard fixes either compensate analytically for the shrinkage or alternate a smoothing step with an expanding step chosen so the two cancel at low frequencies. This is the same failure as §13's σ²/(2R) bias, arriving by a different route, and it has the same cause: a linear operator applied in a curved geometry.

15 Lines: voting, splitting, and sampling

Straight lines are worth a detector of their own: the man-made world is full of them, they survive occlusion in a way that points do not, and a set of lines sharing a vanishing point determines the camera's orientation. Three quite different algorithms find them, and they fail in three different ways.

Method 1 · successive approximation

The bin size is the whole problem. Too fine and one real line's votes scatter across several cells and no peak forms. Too coarse and two nearly parallel lines merge into one, and the fitted θ is quantised. There is no setting that is right for all images, because the correct bin size depends on the accuracy of the edgel positions and orientations, on the length of the lines, and on how many there are. The standard mitigation is to use the accumulator only to find candidates and then re-fit each line by total least squares to its own voting edgels — which recovers full precision and makes the bin size merely a grouping parameter rather than an estimator.

Method 3 · random sampling

Pick two edgels at random, form the line through them, count how many other edgels lie within τ of it, keep the best. Repeat. 16 Scale space, and the theorem that is not quite a theorem

Every detector so far has a scale parameter that had to be chosen. Scale space is the decision to stop choosing: build the image at all scales, treat scale as a third coordinate, and search in it. That reframing is what makes a detector scale invariant, and it is the single most important idea on this page.

the objectL(x, y; t) = G(·, ·; √t) ∗ I(x, y) t ≥ 0, L(x, y; 0) = I(x, y) A one-parameter FAMILY of images, not a stack of approximations to one image. The parameter t = σ² is the natural one because variances add (part 2 §10): smoothing by t₁ and then by t₂ is smoothing by t₁ + t₂, which makes the family a SEMIGROUP. and the equation it satisfies — which is what makes it more than a definition∂L/∂t = ½·∇²L the HEAT EQUATION, with the image as initial data Blurring an image IS diffusing heat through it. Every property of scale space that matters is a property of diffusion: information is destroyed monotonically, the process is irreversible, and structure can only be REMOVED as t grows, never created. §13's curvature displacement was this fact seen from the side.

The uniqueness result, stated with its conditions attached

The sentence you will read everywhere. "The Gaussian is the unique scale-space kernel." That is a theorem only once you say which axioms you are assuming, and every one of them can be dropped to obtain a different, perfectly good scale space. Stated without its conditions it is folklore, and it has misled people into believing that no other multi-scale representation is legitimate.

18 Blob detection, and the approximation everyone uses

A corner is a point where two edges meet. A blob is a region that differs from its surroundings. Corners are precise but rare and unstable under scale change; blobs are plentiful, and — because §17 gives them a size — naturally scale invariant. Almost every scale-invariant detector in use is a blob detector.

the two operatorsLoG σ²∇²G_σ ∗ I one filter, the exact object of §17 DoG (G_{kσ} − G_σ) ∗ I = L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ) free, if the stack exists why they are related at all — the heat equation again, differentiated in t∂L/∂σ = σ∇²L ⇒ σ∇²L ≈ (L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ)) / (kσ − σ) so L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ) ≈ (k − 1)·σ²·∇²L — a finite difference in SCALE. The DoG is the scale derivative of the stack, evaluated with a step of (k − 1)σ. The (k − 1) factor is CONSTANT across scale, so it does not disturb the location of the extremum in either space or scale, which is why nobody bothers to divide it out.
The folklore, restated from Part 2 §13 because it matters twice as much here. "A difference of Gaussians with k = 1.6 is the Laplacian of Gaussian." It is a 17.89% approximation to it, and the error grows with k, not shrinks. Measured as the maximum relative shape error against the true LoG after rescaling the two to agree at the centre: k = 1.05 → 2.21%, 1.1 → 4.25%, 1.2 → 7.87%, 1.3 → 10.95%, 1.6 → 17.89%, 2.0 → 23.64%, 2.5 → 27.86%. So why is a 17.9% approximation acceptable? Because the detector uses the response's argmax, not its value. A smooth 18% distortion of a unimodal function moves its peak a little and changes its height, but does not create or destroy peaks. What the DoG buys is that it is already computed: if you have built the scale-space stack of §16, every adjacent pair of levels is a DoG for free, whereas the true LoG needs a second filter bank. Free beats 18% accurate, when only the argmax is read.

With the stack built, detection is a local-maximum search in (x, y, σ) instead of in (x, y). The search is trivial. What follows it — three rejection tests and one interpolation — is where a usable detector is separated from a list of noise.

20 Affine adaptation, in outline

Scale invariance handles a camera moving toward or away from a plane. It does not handle a camera moving around it: a circle on a slanted surface projects to an ellipse, and no isotropic detector can normalise that. The fix is to let the smoothing window itself be an ellipse, and to solve for which one.

the transformation hierarchy this rests on (part 1 §5)a small enough patch of ANY smooth warp is well approximated by an AFFINE map: x′ = Ax + t A a general 2 × 2, six parameters in all Scale invariance handles A = sR (four parameters). Affine invariance handles all six, and for a locally planar surface that is the whole of perspective, to first order. the normalisation, which is the entire ideaCompute the second-moment matrix M (§05's structure tensor, with an ELLIPTICAL window) and transform the patch by M^(−1/2). Under x′ = Ax the second-moment matrix transforms as M′ = A⁻ᵀ M A⁻¹, and therefore M′^(−1/2) x′ = R · M^(−1/2) x for some rotation R Two views of the same patch, each normalised by its OWN M^(−1/2), differ by a pure ROTATION — which §22's orientation assignment already knows how to remove. Six parameters are reduced to one, and that one is already handled.

The iteration

M^(−1/2) is a fixed point, not a formula: normalising changes the M you measureU₀ ← I repeat: 1. warp the patch by U_k and measure M in the WARPED frame 2. U_{k+1} ← M^(−1/2)·U_k, renormalised so that det U = 1 and the larger eigenvalue is 1 (otherwise the iteration drifts in overall scale) 3. re-select the scale, since warping changed it until M's eigenvalue ratio is close enough to 1 — typically λ₁/λ₀ < 1.05, in 3 to 10 iterations. On the figure's clean synthetic patch the iteration converges for every setting the sliders allow: a 70° slant starts at a ratio of 4.20 and reaches 1.0037 in ten steps. On real data it does NOT always converge, and the usual guard is to abandon any point whose ratio exceeds about 6, because the normalisation would then have to stretch the patch further than the sampling can support. Detecting the failure is part of the algorithm, not an optional refinement.
What it costs and when it pays. Affine adaptation multiplies detection cost by the iteration count, roughly 5–10×, and it reduces repeatability on easy pairs because the extra parameters are estimated from noisy data. It pays only when the viewpoint change is large — wide-baseline stereo, matching a façade photographed from two sides of a street, recognising a planar object seen obliquely. For image stitching or video tracking it is a net loss. This is a general pattern worth naming: every invariance is bought with discriminability and with variance in the estimate, and buying one you do not need makes the system worse.
An entirely different route to the same place. Rather than fitting an ellipse to a point, one can detect regions that are stable under a changing threshold: sweep the intensity threshold from black to white, watch each connected component's area as a function of the threshold, and keep the components whose area changes least. Such regions are invariant to any monotonic intensity mapping — not merely affine, but any gamma curve, any tone curve from Part 2 §03 — and to any affine geometric transformation, because both properties follow from the construction rather than being fitted. The region view of this is Part 5's territory; the point here is that photometric invariance by construction is stronger than photometric invariance by normalisation, and §23 has to settle for the weaker kind.

21 The descriptor design problem

A detector answers where. A descriptor answers which. It has to produce the same vector for the same physical patch photographed differently, and different vectors for different patches — and those two requirements pull in opposite directions with complete symmetry.

22 Orientation assignment

Rotation invariance can be built into the descriptor's form — or bought by measuring the patch's own orientation and cancelling it. The second is what everybody does, because the first destroys discriminability, and this section is the measurement that shows why it is worth the trouble.

Two ways, and why the cheap one loses

option 1
A rotation-invariant descriptor
Use only quantities that do not change under rotation — the magnitude of a Fourier coefficient, a radial profile, a differential invariant. Genuinely invariant, with no estimate to get wrong. But it maps every rotation of a patch to one point, and therefore also maps genuinely different patches that happen to share those invariants to the same point. Discriminability collapses.
option 2
Estimate and cancel
Measure a dominant orientation θ₀, rotate the sampling frame by −θ₀, then use a fully discriminative descriptor in that frame. Invariance is now conditional on the estimate being right — but when it is right, nothing has been given up.

On real imagery the reported rate is around 15% of keypoints; the figure's synthetic patches, which by construction have one dominant edge, produce a secondary peak on only 1.7% of 600 random draws. That gap is itself informative — ambiguous orientation is a property of real scene structure, not of the algorithm, and a synthetic test bed will understate how often step 5 matters.

23 Gradient-histogram descriptors

The design that dominated hand-engineered vision for fifteen years, and that convolutional networks partly re-derived. It is a histogram of gradient orientations, computed separately in each cell of a small spatial grid, with every step of the construction defending against a specific failure.

the construction, one line per decision1. take a 16 × 16 sample grid around the keypoint, at the DETECTED scale, from the pyramid level where it was detected — scale invariance (§17) 2. rotate the sampling frame by −θ₀ — rotation invariance (§22) 3. compute ∇I at every sample; keep MAGNITUDE and ORIENTATION — brightness-offset invariance, for free 4. weight every magnitude by a Gaussian centred on the keypoint, of about half the support width — far samples are the ones a localisation error moves most, so trust them least 5. split the grid into 4 × 4 CELLS; in each, build an 8-bin orientation histogram 6. add every sample to its 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 surrounding bins by TRILINEAR interpolation in (x, y, θ) — so a sample crossing a cell or bin boundary moves its weight smoothly instead of jumping 7. concatenate: 4 × 4 × 8 = 128 non-negative numbers 8. normalise to unit length — gain invariance 9. cap every component at 0.2 and renormalise — the contentious step, examined below

Why the cells, and why so few of them

A single global orientation histogram over the whole patch would be far too coarse: it records what orientations are present and nothing about where. A per-pixel record would be maximally specific and would break under a half-pixel localisation error. The 4 × 4 grid is the compromise, and the trilinear interpolation of step 6 is what makes the compromise soft rather than brittle: without it, a sample sitting on a cell boundary jumps its entire weight from one cell to another when the keypoint moves by one pixel, and the descriptor changes discontinuously. Soft binning is a good idea anywhere a histogram is computed — it is the same argument as Part 2 §06's interpolated local equalisation and the same argument as §15's hybrid Hough voting.

24 Binary descriptors and Hamming matching

A 128-dimensional float vector costs 512 bytes and a Euclidean distance costs 128 multiplies. Replace the vector with 256 bits and the distance with a population count and both costs collapse by more than an order of magnitude — at a real but surprisingly small accuracy cost.

the constructionchoose a fixed set of D sample-point PAIRS (pᵢ, qᵢ) inside the patch, once, offline bit i = 1 if I_smoothed(pᵢ) < I_smoothed(qᵢ), else 0 The descriptor is a string of D pairwise intensity COMPARISONS. That is all it is. Smoothing before comparing is not optional: a raw pixel comparison is dominated by noise, and the usual choice is a Gaussian of about 2 px or a box filter evaluated with an integral image (part 2 §20), which makes each bit cost 6 additions. the distanced_H(a, b) = popcount(a XOR b) 256 bits = 4 machine words = 4 XORs + 4 popcounts Roughly 8 CPU instructions against 128 multiply-adds plus a square root — an arithmetic ratio of about 40 : 1, and reported end-to-end speed-ups in the matching stage are of that order. That is an instruction count, not a measurement made on this page. What matters more than the exact factor: matching — not detection, not description — is what dominates the cost once there are more than a few thousand features, so a constant-factor win there is a constant-factor win overall.

Why the sampling pattern is not random. A pattern drawn uniformly at random works, but a better one is learned: generate thousands of candidate pairs, rank them so that each bit is close to an even split of 0s and 1s over a training set of patches (maximum entropy per bit), and greedily select pairs whose responses are as uncorrelated as possible with the already-selected ones. Two bits that agree 95% of the time carry roughly one bit of information between them, so 256 correlated bits are worth far less than 256 independent ones. The same logic applies to any binary code: the capacity of a descriptor is its number of independent bits, not its number of bits.
An easy misreading of the speed-up. "Binary descriptors are 40× faster." Forty times faster at computing one distance, which is only part of the pipeline. Detection is unchanged, description is somewhat cheaper, and the approximate-search structures of §26 do not speed up by 40× because their cost is dominated by memory access rather than by arithmetic — although the 16× smaller descriptor does help that too. The honest claim is a large constant-factor win in the matching stage, on top of a substantial memory win, in exchange for a measurable accuracy loss.

25 Matching strategies, and why the ratio test wins

Given two sets of descriptors, decide which pairs correspond. The obvious rule — accept every pair closer than some threshold — fails for a reason worth understanding, and the standard fix is one division.

The four strategies

in order of increasing sophistication and increasing cost1. FIXED THRESHOLD accept (a, b) if ‖dₐ − d_b‖ < τ a feature can match many, or none 2. NEAREST NEIGHBOUR accept (a, NN(a)) if ‖dₐ − d_NN‖ < τ at most one match per feature in the first image 3. RATIO TEST accept (a, NN₁(a)) if d₁/d₂ < ρ where d₁ and d₂ are the FIRST and SECOND nearest distances 4. CROSS-CHECK accept (a, b) only if b is a's nearest AND a is b's nearest symmetric; composes with any of the above

Why a fixed threshold cannot work

The useful value of τ is not a property of the algorithm — it is a property of the neighbourhood in descriptor space. A feature on a distinctive, isolated structure sits alone: its correct match may be far away in absolute terms and still be unmistakable. A feature on a repetitive texture — one window of a façade, one leaf of a tree — sits in a dense cluster of near-identical descriptors: its correct match is close, and so are twenty wrong ones. One global threshold has to serve both, and no value does.

what the ratio measures, and why it is the right quantityρ = d₁ / d₂ nearest distance ÷ second-nearest distance, ρ ∈ [0, 1] d₂ is an ESTIMATE OF THE LOCAL DENSITY of descriptor space around this feature. Divide by it and the test becomes "is the best match distinctly better than the runner-up?" rather than "is the best match close?". That is a scale-free question, and it asks the thing you actually want to know. The second-nearest neighbour should be taken from a DIFFERENT structure — in object recognition, from a different database object — so that it estimates the background density rather than a second view of the same thing.

A subtle bug that produces mysteriously bad results. Applying the ratio test when the two nearest neighbours are two views of the same structure — for instance, matching against a database that contains the same object twice, or against an image with a genuine repeated element. Then d₂ is small for a perfectly good reason, the ratio is near 1, and the correct match is rejected. The defence is to require the second-nearest neighbour to come from a different object, a different image, or a location far from the first — which is why serious implementations do not simply take the second element of the sorted list.

26 Finding the nearest neighbour without looking at everything

Matching two images with 3000 features each means nine million distance computations. Matching one image against a database of a million images is not a bigger version of that problem, it is a different problem, and the answer is always to stop insisting on the exact nearest neighbour.

the cost, stated plainlybrute force, two images: n·m·D = 3000 × 3000 × 128 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁹ operations brute force, a database: n·M·D with M in the hundreds of millions — impossible And the exact nearest neighbour is not even what you want: §25 is going to accept the match only if it is distinctly better than the runner-up, so a search that returns the true nearest neighbour 95% of the time costs 5% of the recall and 2% of the time.

The curse of dimensionality, concretely. In 128 dimensions the distances from a query to all points in a random dataset concentrate: the nearest and the farthest differ by a small relative margin, so a bounding-box argument can rarely exclude a subtree and every exact structure degrades toward brute force. Real descriptors are not uniformly distributed — they lie near a much lower-dimensional manifold — which is exactly why tree methods work in practice while the worst-case theory says they cannot. When they stop working, the reason is usually that the data has become more uniform, not that the structure is broken.
The ordering mistake. Applying the ratio test to the results of an approximate search without checking what the search returned. If the approximate search misses the true nearest neighbour, then d₁ is a wrong match's distance and d₂ may be the true match's — so the ratio can be small for entirely the wrong reason, and the test endorses the error. The standard mitigation is to retrieve k ≈ 2–10 approximate neighbours rather than 2, and to apply the ratio test within that list, which makes the failure much less likely without costing another pass.

27 RANSAC: fitting a model when a third of the data is nonsense

§25 hands over a set of putative matches of which perhaps 60–80% are right. Least squares fitted to that set is garbage, because a squared-error objective lets one outlier at distance d outvote d² good points. The answer is not a better matcher. It is an estimator that reads the data as a hypothesis generator rather than as measurements.

the algorithm, completegiven data D, a model needing s points to determine it, a tolerance τ, and N: best ← ∅ repeat N times: S ← s points drawn uniformly at random from D, WITHOUT replacement M ← the model determined exactly by S I ← { p ∈ D : residual(p, M) < τ } the CONSENSUS SET if |I| > |best| : best ← I finally: re-fit the model to ALL of best, by least squares Note what is NOT here: no weights, no robust loss, no iteration to convergence. The model is determined EXACTLY from a MINIMAL sample, so the fit is either right or completely wrong, and the consensus count is the only thing that decides between them. That is what makes it tolerate an arbitrary fraction of outliers rather than a small one — the breakdown point is not 50%, it is however low the inlier rate can go before N becomes unaffordable.

28 Evaluation: repeatability, precision and recall

Detectors and descriptors fail independently and must therefore be measured independently. Mixing them produces a number that goes down when something is wrong and does not say what.

Detector evaluation: repeatability

the definition — it needs known ground-truth geometry, which is why it is measured on planar scenesgiven images I₀, I₁ related by a known map H, and detections K₀, K₁: repeatability = |{ k ∈ K₀ : ∃ k′ ∈ K₁ with ‖H(k) − k′‖ < ε }| / |K₀ ∩ overlap| with ε typically 1.5 px. Three details decide whether the number means anything: · the denominator must count only detections in the OVERLAPPING region, or a detector that finds points outside the shared area is unfairly punished · the two images must have COMPARABLE detection counts, or a detector that returns ten times as many points scores highly by carpeting the plane · for scale-covariant detectors, ε must scale with the detection's own σ, or large-scale detections are held to an impossible standard a scale-aware variant, which is what serious comparisons useoverlap error = 1 − |R₀ ∩ H⁻¹(R₁)| / |R₀ ∪ H⁻¹(R₁)| for elliptical regions R This is exactly the IoU of part 5 §26, applied to detection ellipses rather than to segments — the same metric, with the same properties, and it is not redefined here. A detection is "repeated" if the overlap error is below about 0.4.

Descriptor evaluation: precision and recall on the match set

The general theory of these metrics — the confusion matrix, the ROC curve, the area under it, why accuracy is the wrong summary under class imbalance — belongs to Model Evaluation and is not repeated. What is specific to matching is what a positive is, and that is where the confusion lives:

the four cells, with their matching-specific meaningsTP a proposed match whose two features are the same physical point FP a proposed match whose two features are not — the one §27 must survive FN a genuine correspondence the matcher did not propose TN a pair correctly not proposed — and there are O(n²) of them, which is why FALSE POSITIVE RATE is a useless axis here and PRECISION is the right one With n = 3000 features per image there are 9 000 000 possible pairs and perhaps 2000 true ones. The evaluation trap that has misled published comparisons. Reporting the number of correct matches without reporting the number proposed. A matcher that proposes everything has perfect recall; a matcher that proposes one thing has perfect precision. Only the pair is meaningful, and only a curve — swept over the threshold, as §25's figure does — compares two methods honestly, because a single operating point can be chosen to favour either. Measuring in that order localises the failure. If repeatability is 30%, nothing downstream can be fixed by a better descriptor. If repeatability is 70% and matching precision is 40%, the descriptor is the problem. If both are good and the inlier ratio after RANSAC is 10%, the failure is geometric — a degenerate configuration, or a scene that does not satisfy the model — and belongs to Part 6 or Part 7, not here.

Repeatability under a transformation, measured for three detectors sweep the magnitude and watch the curves fall — under rotation they barely move, which is itself the result

29 What learned features changed, and what survived

Between roughly 2012 and 2020 essentially every hand-designed component on this page was replaced by a learned one for most tasks. The interesting fact is which parts were replaced and which were not, because the pattern is not "everything".

ComponentStatusWhy
The detectorReplaced for most tasksA learned detector optimises repeatability on the data it will see, which a fixed operator cannot. Hand-designed detectors survive where there is no training data, where the compute budget is tiny, and where the geometry must be provably scale-covariant.
The descriptorReplaced, decisivelyThe clearest win of the three. A descriptor trained on matched patches beats a hand-designed one at every operating point, and the margin is largest exactly where hand-designed descriptors are weakest — large viewpoint change, illumination change, non-rigid deformation.
The pipeline shapeSurvivedDetect, describe, match, verify. Learned systems have the same four stages, sometimes trained end to end but still identifiably four stages, because each is solving a genuinely different problem.
The ratio testSurvivedIt is a statement about the geometry of descriptor space, not about how the descriptor was made. Learned descriptors have the same local density variation, and the same fix applies. Modern learned matchers do replace it — with an attention-based assignment that reasons about all matches jointly — but the plain ratio test remains the baseline that has to be beaten.
RANSACSurvived, essentially unchangedIt solves a problem that better descriptors do not remove. Even a perfect descriptor cannot match an occluded point or a repeated structure correctly, so the match set still contains outliers, and a geometric model still has to be fitted robustly. The variants that have appeared — better sampling, better local optimisation, better scoring — are refinements of the same algorithm.
Scale spacePartly absorbedA convolutional network's stride and pooling are a pyramid (Part 2 §23), and multi-scale feature hierarchies are the direct descendant. What was lost is the explicit, continuous scale coordinate: a network's scale response is quantised by its architecture rather than searched, which is why explicitly scale-covariant detectors still win on tasks with extreme scale change.
What the derivation was for, if the operator has been replaced. Three things. First, the invariance analysis is unchanged: a learned descriptor still has to be invariant to the same nuisances, and §21's table is still the specification it is trained against. Second, the failure modes are unchanged: a learned detector still cannot resolve the aperture problem, still has a repeatability that falls with viewpoint change, and still puts most of its points on texture. Third, the evaluation is unchanged, and §28's discipline — measure the detector separately, plot curves not points, never plot ROC for matching — is exactly how learned systems are compared. The operators changed; the problem did not.
Where the learned version lives. Convolutional networks · what the filters learn shows first-layer filters that are recognisably oriented derivative-of-Gaussian and centre-surround operators — the network re-derives Part 2 §13's kernels from data, which is the strongest available evidence that those kernels were the right ones. Why convolution gives the weight-sharing and locality argument that makes a learned filter bank tractable. This page does not teach either.

30 Practice

Every answer below is worked out, and every number has been recomputed independently — several of them caught errors in earlier drafts of this page. If a step feels like recall rather than derivation, the section it came from is the one to reread.

Drills

  1. A window contains 7 pixels with gradient (10, 0) and 7 with (0, 10). Give A, its eigenvalues, and the Harris response at k = 0.04. A = [[700, 0], [0, 700]], λ = (700, 700), det = 490 000, tr = 1400, R = 490 000 − 0.04 × 1400² = 411 600. Rotate the same corner by 45° and every entry of A changes while λ, det and tr do not.
  2. Same window, but all 14 gradients are (10, 0). What is R? A = [[1400, 0],[0, 0]], det = 0, R = −0.04 × 1400² = −78 400. Negative, not small — Harris distinguishes "edge" from "nothing", which a threshold on λ₀ alone does not.
  3. For what k is the Harris response positive somewhere? R > 0 ⟺ r > k(1 + r)² ⟺ kr² + (2k − 1)r + k < 0, whose discriminant is 1 − 4k. So 0 < k < ¼, and at k = ¼ the admissible band collapses to the single point r = 1.
  4. At k = 0.06, how anisotropic may a patch be and still score positive? r± = [(1 − 2k) ± √(1 − 4k)]/(2k) gives (0.068502, 14.598165). Exhaustive search over r confirms the largest admissible ratio is 14.59815.
  5. What Harris k is equivalent to the blob detector's tr²/det < (r + 1)²/r at r = 10? The threshold is 121/10 = 12.1, and R > 0 ⟺ tr²/det < 1/k, so k = 1/12.1 = 0.082645. The two tests are the same inequality on two different matrices.
  6. Set the integration scale to zero. How many corners are there? None, anywhere, in any image. A = ∇I∇Iᵀ is one outer product, hence rank 1, hence det A = 0 — measured at 2.7 × 10⁻²⁰ over 5776 pixels, with 0 positive Harris responses. A corner is a property of a neighbourhood.
  7. An integer-valued detector on a shifted image: what localisation error should you expect, and what does a quadratic fit buy? Measured over 7 sub-pixel shifts and 176 matched detections: 0.5369 px integer, 0.1153 px refined — a factor of 4.66. The half pixel is forced: the true corner is uniformly distributed inside its pixel.
  8. Does localisation accuracy degrade monotonically with σᵢ? No. Measured refined error against σᵢ: 0.345, 0.258, 0.259, 0.115, 0.125, 0.122, 0.139 at σᵢ = 0.8 … 4.0. U-shaped, with a minimum at the conventional σᵢ ≈ 2σ_d. Too small is worse than too large.
  9. Why does non-maximum suppression compare along ∇I and not along the edge? Along the edge the magnitude is roughly constant, so nothing is a strict maximum and the output is empty. Across the edge there is a genuine crest. Making this mistake produces a completely blank edge map, which is at least an unambiguous symptom.
  10. Non-maximum suppression thins a gradient image by what factor? On a clean image, 7.15× at σ = 1.2 (2196 → 307 pixels above 2% of the maximum). With noise 0.10 the factor falls to 3.59, because noise creates one-pixel ridges that are already thin. Suppression removes width, not noise.
  11. A contour's contrast fades from full to 6% along its length. Compare one high threshold, one low threshold, and hysteresis. Measured: high (0.40) gives precision 1.000 and arc recall 0.600 in 2 components; low (0.12) gives precision 0.614 and recall 0.950 in 56 components; hysteresis (0.12/0.40) gives precision 0.991 and recall 0.925 in 2. Almost the low threshold's recall at almost the high one's precision.
  12. A blurred disc of radius 10 at σ = 3.2: where is the mid-intensity level set, where does the gradient peak, and where does the LoG cross zero? 9.4591, 9.4594, 10.5402. The smoothing itself moved the edge inward by √(R² − σ²) − R = −0.5258 — curve-shortening flow — the gradient maximum found that moved edge to four decimal places, and the LoG zero sits σ²/(2R) = 0.512 outside the original radius.
  13. Why does the Laplacian's zero crossing move on a curved edge at all? Because ∇²I = I_ηη − κ‖∇I‖ exactly, so ∇²I = 0 means I_ηη = κ‖∇I‖ and not I_ηη = 0. Verified to 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ on a random smooth field.
  14. Original Hough voting against oriented voting, on 789 edgels with a 72 × 72 accumulator. Oriented: 789 votes, all four true lines recovered. Original: 56 808 votes — 72× more, one per θ bin per edgel — and only three of the four recovered, because the sinusoid background swamps the weakest line.
  15. Is the number of local extrema of a scale space non-increasing? In 1D yes; in 2D no. Measured on this page's scene: 28, 27, 36, 33, 26, 22, 17, 10, 7, 5, 5, 5. The rise survives a 10⁻³ strictness guard. Non-enhancement bounds an extremum's value, not the count.
  16. A bright disc of radius 8. At what σ does the scale-normalised LoG peak, and what is the peak worth? σ* = R/√2 = 5.65685 (measured 5.65684), and the peak value is 2/e = 0.735759 for every R. The un-normalised response peaks at σ = R/2 = 4 with a value that depends on R as (16/R²)e⁻², which is why raw responses cannot be compared across scale.
  17. You detect a blob at σ = 3.5. What circle do you draw? Radius √2 σ = 4.95, not 3.5. In N dimensions the factor is √N. Getting this wrong makes every drawn circle 41% too small.
  18. How good an approximation to the LoG is a DoG at k = 1.6? A 17.89% maximum relative shape error (17.8846% at high resolution, and independent of σ), worst at r = 1.23σ. The error grows with k: 2.21% at 1.05, 23.64% at 2.0. It is acceptable because the detector reads the argmax, not the value.
  19. Why does an implementation refuse to go below about σ = 0.8 per scale-space level? Because the sampled Gaussian stops satisfying the semigroup: the maximum per-tap error in h(σ₁) ∗ h(σ₂) against h(√(σ₁² + σ₂²)) is 6.3 × 10⁻³ at σ = 0.7 and 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ by σ = 1.5. The exact lattice analogue is the discrete Gaussian e⁻ᵗ I_n(t), whose second moment is exactly t.
  20. What does orientation assignment buy, quantitatively? Mean descriptor distance over 12 rotations: 0.0390 with it, 0.7985 without — 20.5×. Tracking error at most 2.94°, and exactly 0° at 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°, the only rotations the lattice maps to itself.
  21. Show that a gradient-histogram descriptor is exactly invariant to I → aI + b. b dies in the derivative; a multiplies every histogram entry and dies in the unit normalisation. Measured distance: 0.000000 for every (a, b) tested, against up to 5.72 for the un-normalised histogram.
  22. Does capping the descriptor at 0.2 improve robustness to non-linear illumination? Over 200 random patches, no: mean distance rises from 0.1753 to 0.1839 under gamma 1.6, and from 0.1500 to 0.1749 under saturation, winning on 19–86 of 200. It is a database-tuned heuristic targeting match precision, not a theorem about descriptor distance, and should be described as one.
  23. A binary descriptor under a gamma of 2.0, and under inversion. Gamma 2.0 is monotonically increasing, so every comparison is unchanged: Hamming distance 1 of 128, the single bit being a numerical tie. Inversion is monotonically decreasing: 128 of 128, every bit flipped. Polarity has to be recorded, not assumed.
  24. Why not a fixed distance threshold for matching? Because the useful threshold depends on the local density of descriptor space. Measured at matched recall on the same data: at recall 0.81 the ratio test admits 9 false matches and the distance threshold admits 43. Dividing by d₂ estimates that density and normalises it away.
  25. Compute N for a homography at w = 0.4, p = 0.99. Then for a fundamental matrix. Homography: s = 4, N = log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.4⁴) = 176.2 → 177. Fundamental matrix with the seven-point algorithm: s = 7, N = log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.4⁷) = 2806.9 → 2807. Sixteen times as many, from three extra points.
  26. Does N iterations give you a p chance of the right answer? No. It gives a p chance of drawing a clean minimal sample. Measured: at w = 0.7 the formula predicts 0.9910, the clean-sample rate is 0.9925, and the rate of actually recovering the right line is 0.9530. Even given a clean sample it is only 0.9592, because two nearby inliers define a badly conditioned model.
  27. Why plot precision–recall rather than ROC for matching? With 3000 features per image there are 9 000 000 candidate pairs and perhaps 2000 true ones. A false positive rate of 0.001 means 9000 false matches — the FPR axis is dominated by the true-negative count, which carries no information. Precision does not depend on it at all.
  28. Repeatability is reported as 82%. What three things do you need before believing it? The tolerance ε; whether the denominator was restricted to the overlapping region; and whether the detection counts were capped, since repeatability rises trivially with detection density.
