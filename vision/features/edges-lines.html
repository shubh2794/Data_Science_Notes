09 Edges: what they are and what they are not

Corners are rare and precisely localisable. Edges are everywhere, badly localisable along their length, and carry most of the semantics — object outlines, occlusion boundaries, the structure of man-made scenes. Two entirely different jobs, from the same gradient field.

The definition, and the two equivalent tests

an edge is a local maximum of gradient magnitude ALONG THE GRADIENT DIRECTIONJ(x) = ∇I(x) = (∂I/∂x, ∂I/∂y) magnitude = strength, direction ⟂ the contour A "ridge" in ‖J‖ is not enough — the ridge is several pixels wide. The edge is the CREST of the ridge, found by walking along ∇I and keeping only the top. §11. the second-derivative form of the same testmaximum of ‖J‖ along ∇I ⟺ ∂²I/∂n̂² = 0 with a sign change, n̂ = ∇I/‖∇I‖ A maximum of a first derivative is a ZERO CROSSING of the second. Take the second derivative in the gradient direction only and you get the exact criterion; replace it with the ISOTROPIC second derivative — the Laplacian — and you get an approximation that is cheaper, orientation-free, and wrong wherever the edge is curved. §13.

What the gradient is, and where it comes from

Nothing about the gradient is re-derived here. Part 2 §13 owns the derivative-of-Gaussian identity ∂(G ∗ f) = (∂G) ∗ f, the Sobel and Scharr stencils and their scale factors, the Laplacian and its r = √2σ zero crossing, and the moment conditions a derivative filter must satisfy. §14 owns steerability and oriented energy. This page starts one step later: given Iₓ and I_y, what is the decision rule?

An edge is not a boundary. A brightness discontinuity has four physically distinct causes — a depth discontinuity, a surface-orientation crease, a reflectance change, and an illumination change such as a shadow edge (Part 1 §20). Only the first two are object boundaries. A local operator cannot tell them apart, because locally they look identical, and no amount of tuning will make it able to. Grouping edges into object boundaries needs region evidence and is Part 5's problem; combining brightness, colour and texture gradients is where a hand-designed detector stops and a learned one starts (§29).
A category error worth naming. "The edge detector missed the boundary between the two grey regions." If the two regions have the same mean intensity, there is no gradient, and the detector is behaving correctly. An iso-luminant colour boundary, a texture boundary and a boundary defined only by motion are all invisible to a luminance gradient operator by construction. The fix is a different feature channel, not a different threshold.

10 The Canny detector: three criteria and four stages

Canny's contribution was not an operator. It was writing down what "good" means as three functionals, optimising them, and discovering that the answer is very nearly a derivative of a Gaussian — which is why the four-stage pipeline that came out of it is still the default forty years later.

The three criteria

CriterionThe functionalWhat it buysWhich stage delivers it
Good detectionMaximise the output signal-to-noise ratio Σ at a step edge in additive white noise.Few responses to noise, and few missed real edges.Gaussian smoothing before differentiation — stage 1.
Good localisationMaximise Λ, the reciprocal of the expected distance between the marked edge and the true one.The edge is marked in the right place.Non-maximum suppression — stage 3, which is the discrete form of "find the crest".
Single responseConstrain the mean spacing x_max between spurious maxima of the response to a noisy step to be large.One edge is reported once, not as a set of parallel ghosts.Non-maximum suppression again, plus hysteresis — stages 3 and 4.
the uncertainty principle that makes the first two criteria a real tradeScale the operator: f_w(x) = f(x/w)/w. Then, for a step edge in white noise, Σ(f_w) = √w · Σ(f) Λ(f_w) = Λ(f) / √w The PRODUCT ΣΛ is therefore INDEPENDENT of the operator's width. A wide filter detects better and localises worse, by exactly reciprocal factors, so no choice of σ beats any other on the product — σ selects a point on a curve, it does not move the curve. This is the same trade the frequency-domain uncertainty relation describes, in the time domain and for a specific pair of functionals. It is why edge detection has a scale parameter that cannot be optimised away, and why §16 makes scale a search dimension instead. and how close the practical filter is to the theoretical optimumoptimum numerically optimised antisymmetric filter, r = 0.576 practice the first derivative of a Gaussian, r = 0.51 The derivative of a Gaussian is worse than the optimum by about 20% on the detection × localisation product, and by about 10% on the multiple-response measure. Twenty percent, in exchange for separability, steerability and a closed form — which is why nobody has used the exact optimum since.

The four stages, and why each one is there

the pipeline1. SMOOTH convolve with G_σ — because differentiation amplifies noise without limit (part 2 §13) 2. GRADIENT Iₓ, I_y → ‖∇I‖, ∠∇I — one convolution if fused with stage 1 3. NON-MAX thin the ridge to its — because a ridge is 4–8 px wide and an SUPPRESSION crest, sub-pixel "edge" is one pixel wide 4. Stages 3 and 4 are the detector, and they are the two stages that have no equivalent in the corner pipeline of §07 — because a corner is a point and can be thresholded, while an edge is a CURVE and must be traced.
What the smoothing scale actually chooses. Not "how much noise to remove" — that framing has no answer. σ chooses which edges exist. At σ = 0.5 the texture of a brick wall is a thousand edges; at σ = 4 it is one rectangle. Both are correct answers to different questions, and the detector has no way to know which was asked. §16–§18 are the systematic response to that, and the practical response is to run the detector at two or three scales and keep the union.

11 Non-maximum suppression, in detail

The stage that turns a four-pixel-wide ridge into a one-pixel-wide curve. It is three lines of code, it is where all the localisation accuracy comes from, and it is the stage implementations most often get subtly wrong.

the rulekeep (x, y) ⟺ ‖∇I‖(x, y) ≥ ‖∇I‖(x + n̂) AND ‖∇I‖(x, y) ≥ ‖∇I‖(x − n̂) with n̂ = ∇I/‖∇I‖ the UNIT gradient direction, so the two comparison points are one pixel forward and one pixel back ALONG the gradient — i.e. ACROSS the edge, which is the direction the ridge actually has a crest in. the problem: x ± n̂ is not on the pixel gridtwo standard answers — (a) ROUND n̂ to one of 8 compass directions and compare against the two neighbours (b) BILINEARLY INTERPOLATE ‖∇I‖ at the two off-grid points ← use this one (a) is faster and quantises the edge normal to 45° steps, which biases the surviving crest by up to 22.5° of direction error. (b) costs four multiplies and is what makes sub-pixel edge localisation possible at all. how much the choice actually matters — measured, kept against suppressedsetting the two variants agree on σ = 1.4, no noise 97.90% of interior pixels σ = 1.2, no noise 97.24% σ = 2.0, noise 0.10 96.06% σ = 1.2, noise 0.10 (the figure's default) 94.85% A few percent, and the disagreements concentrate on diagonal edges, where the 45° quantisation error is largest — and on noise ridges, where neither answer is right. Note the direction of the effect: MORE noise and LESS smoothing make the two variants disagree more, because the gradient direction itself becomes unreliable.

Three traps

trap 1
Comparing along the edge
Using the tangent (−n̂_y, n̂_x) instead of the normal deletes the entire edge, because along a straight edge the magnitude is roughly constant and nothing is a strict maximum. Symptom: an empty output. The comparison direction is the gradient, which is perpendicular to the contour.
trap 2
≥ versus >
On a flat crest two adjacent pixels can have exactly equal magnitude. With strict > on both sides, both are deleted and the edge acquires holes. With ≥ on both sides, both survive and the edge is two pixels wide in places. Neither is disastrous; a common compromise is > forward and ≥ backward, which breaks the tie consistently.
trap 3
Suppressing before thresholding
Order matters. Suppress first, then threshold: a weak-but-genuine crest survives suppression and can be recruited by hysteresis. Threshold first and the crest is gone before suppression ever sees it, so hysteresis has nothing to link and the fading arc in the figure vanishes at the same low threshold that would otherwise have saved it.
what the thinning is worth — pixels with ‖∇I‖ above 2% of the maximum, before and aftersetting before after thinning factor σ = 1.2, no noise 2196 307 7.15 × σ = 1.4, no noise 2280 305 7.48 × σ = 2.0, noise 0.10 5252 1035 5.07 × σ = 1.2, noise 0.10 (the default) 5420 1509 3.59 × On a clean image the gradient magnitude has SEVEN TIMES more above-threshold pixels than the edge map: that factor is the difference between "a blurry picture of where the edges are" and "a list of edge locations". Add noise and the factor FALLS, to 3.59 — not because thinning got worse but because the noise creates thousands of tiny one-pixel ridges that are already thin and survive suppression untouched. Suppression removes width; it does not remove noise, and nothing before the two thresholds of §12 does either.

12 Hysteresis thresholding: why one threshold cannot work

A real contour's contrast varies along its length. A single threshold must be low enough to keep the faint part and high enough to reject noise, and on almost every real image those two constraints are incompatible. Hysteresis resolves it by making the decision contextual: a weak pixel is believed if it is connected to a strong one.

the ruleSTRONG = { p : ‖∇I‖(p) ≥ τ_hi } seeds EDGE = every 8-connected component of { p : ‖∇I‖(p) ≥ τ_lo } that contains at least one STRONG pixel Implemented as a flood fill from the strong set through the weak set — one pass, one stack, linear in the number of pixels. The name is from magnetism: the threshold to KEEP following an edge (τ_lo) is lower than the threshold to START one (τ_hi), so the decision depends on the path taken to reach the pixel.

The measurement, on a contour that fades

The figure's scene contains an arc whose contrast falls smoothly from full to about 6% along its length — the exact situation hysteresis was invented for. Ground truth is known from the scene's construction, so recall on that arc and precision over the whole image are both measurable. At σ = 1.2 with noise 0.10:

The high threshold is perfectly precise and loses 40% of the arc. The low threshold recovers the arc and spends 195 spurious pixels for it, dropping precision to 0.614 and SHATTERING the map into 56 pieces. Hysteresis recovers 92.5% of the arc at 0.991 precision in TWO components — it gets almost all of the low threshold's recall at almost all of the high one's precision, which is exactly the claim, and the claim is measurable.
Why the component count is the honest metric. Pixel counts undersell what hysteresis does. The single low threshold finds more arc pixels than hysteresis does — 0.950 against 0.925 recall — but delivers them inside 56 disconnected fragments, of which 54 are noise. Anything downstream that wants a contour rather than a set of pixels — edge linking (§14), the Hough transform (§15), a shape descriptor — has to solve a grouping problem that hysteresis already solved for free by refusing to start a chain at a weak pixel.
the usual advice, and what it is worthτ_hi / τ_lo ≈ 2 : 1 to 3 : 1 This is a heuristic, not a result. What it encodes is that the ratio should exceed the CONTRAST VARIATION ALONG a single contour, so that a contour which starts strong stays above τ_lo, but should stay below the ratio between the WEAKEST REAL EDGE and the NOISE FLOOR, so that noise cannot bridge. If those two intervals do not overlap — faint edges as weak as the noise — no pair of thresholds works, and the honest answer is that the image does not contain the information, not that the parameters are wrong.

13 The other edge detector: zero crossings of the Laplacian of Gaussian

A maximum of the first derivative is a zero of the second, so an edge can be found by looking for sign changes instead of for peaks. The result is cheaper, orientation-free, produces closed contours for free, and is systematically wrong wherever the edge is curved — and the size of that error is computable exactly.

the operator, from part 2 §13 — not re-derived here∇²G(x, y; σ) = ( (x² + y²)/σ⁴ − 2/σ² )·G(x, y; σ) zero crossing at r = √2·σ S(x) = (∇²G_σ) ∗ I an edgel wherever S changes sign sub-pixel: linearly interpolate the crossing between the two pixels that straddle itx_z = ( xᵢ·S(x_j) − x_j·S(xᵢ) ) / ( S(x_j) − S(xᵢ) ) the x-intercept of the segment Same arithmetic as §07's sub-pixel refinement and §17's scale interpolation: fit the simplest thing that has the right root and solve for it.

The exact error term, and why curved edges move

Decompose the Laplacian into the second derivative along the gradient and the second derivative along the contour. Write η̂ = ∇I/‖∇I‖ for the gradient direction and ξ̂ for the perpendicular:

an exact identity, not an approximation∇²I = I_ηη + I_ξξ with I_ηη = (Iₓ²Iₓₓ + 2IₓI_yIₓ_y + I_y²I_yy) / ‖∇I‖² I_ξξ = (I_y²Iₓₓ − 2IₓI_yIₓ_y + Iₓ²I_yy) / ‖∇I‖² = −κ·‖∇I‖ where κ is the ISOPHOTE CURVATURE — how sharply the level curve through the pixel bends. Verified numerically on a random smooth field: max |(I_ηη + I_ξξ) − ∇²I| = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ and max |I_ξξ + κ‖∇I‖| = 6.9 × 10⁻¹⁸. Exact to machine precision. therefore∇²I = 0 ⟺ I_ηη = κ·‖∇I‖ but the TRUE edge is at I_ηη = 0 The two coincide exactly when κ‖∇I‖ = 0 — that is, on STRAIGHT edges. Everywhere else the LoG zero crossing is displaced, and the displacement grows with curvature. Canny's non-maximum suppression differentiates in the gradient direction only, so it does not carry this term — but as the measurements below show, that does not make it unbiased, because the smoothing that precedes it has its own curvature effect.

Measured on a disc of known radius — and a surprise about Canny

The obvious experiment is a bright disc of exactly known radius R, blurred at scale σ. Three positions can be measured: where the smoothed image's mid-intensity level set actually is, where the gradient magnitude peaks, and where the Laplacian crosses zero. They are all different, and the differences are all predictable.

Non-maximum suppression is doing its job perfectly. It is the smoothing before it that moved the edge. The LoG zero, by contrast, is +0.1 to +0.7 px OUTSIDE the original radius, tracking σ²/(2R) with a consistent overshoot of 15–30% that comes from the discreteness of the filter, and it is therefore roughly σ²/R away from where Canny puts the same edge.
What that table refutes, including a claim this page nearly made. "Canny is unbiased on curved edges and the Laplacian is not." Both are displaced, by comparable amounts, in opposite directions. The correct statement is finer: non-maximum suppression is unbiased with respect to the smoothed image it is given — it lands within 0.07 px of the smoothed edge at every radius and scale measured — but the smoothing has already moved that edge inward by σ²/(2R), and no detector downstream can undo that. The Laplacian adds a second, independent displacement of the same size in the opposite direction. So: Canny's error is the smoothing's error, and the LoG's error is the smoothing's error plus its own. If you want the original edge back you must correct for curvature explicitly, whichever detector you used.

The claim to be careful with. "LoG zero crossings give closed contours, so they are better for segmentation." They give closed contours, and most of those contours are not object boundaries. Every extremum of the smoothed image is surrounded by a closed zero-crossing loop, including extrema created by noise. The closure is a topological guarantee about the operator, not evidence about the scene. It is genuinely useful — it saves the linking stage — but it does not save the thresholding stage.

14 From edge pixels to contours

Everything downstream — line fitting, shape matching, contour descriptors — wants a curve, meaning an ordered list of points. An edge map is an unordered set. The gap between them is a small, dull, essential algorithm.

Linking

the procedure1. pick any unvisited edge pixel 2. walk to its unvisited 8-connected edge neighbours, preferring the one whose gradient ORIENTATION is closest to the current one (this is what disambiguates a crossing) 3. when the walk stops, restart from the same seed in the OPPOSITE direction 4. the two halves, reversed and concatenated, are one chain 5. repeat until every edge pixel is assigned Junctions are the only hard part, and there is no local rule that resolves them correctly. The standard compromise — break every chain at a pixel with more than two edge neighbours — is wrong at X-crossings and right almost everywhere else.

Three representations of a chain, and what each is for

compact
Chain code
Store the start point and then 3 bits per step, one of the eight compass directions. Compresses well with run-length or predictive coding. Nearly useless for further processing: it is quantised to 45° and to the pixel lattice, so any derivative computed from it is garbage.
analytic
Arc-length parameterisation x(s)
Store the chain as a function of arc length. Now it can be resampled uniformly, smoothed, differentiated for curvature, or Fourier-transformed. Normalise s to [0, 1] and subtract the centroid and two closed contours of the same shape differ only by a cyclic shift in s and a rotation — which makes shape matching a correlation.
structural
Polyline / spline
Approximate the chain by a few vertices — §15. Turns thousands of pixels into a handful of parameters and makes "is this a straight line?" a question you can answer. The cost is a tolerance parameter that decides what counts as a corner.
The trap in smoothing a contour. Convolving x(s) with a Gaussian to remove pixel noise shrinks the curve: a circle of radius R smoothed at scale σ becomes a circle of radius approximately R(1 − σ²/2R²), and iterating the smoothing collapses any closed curve to a point. The standard fixes either compensate analytically for the shrinkage or alternate a smoothing step with an expanding step chosen so the two cancel at low frequencies. This is the same failure as §13's σ²/(2R) bias, arriving by a different route, and it has the same cause: a linear operator applied in a curved geometry.

15 Lines: voting, splitting, and sampling

Straight lines are worth a detector of their own: the man-made world is full of them, they survive occlusion in a way that points do not, and a set of lines sharing a vanishing point determines the camera's orientation. Three quite different algorithms find them, and they fail in three different ways.

Method 1 · successive approximation

recursive line simplification, on a chain that is already linked (§14)simplify(chain, ε): draw the chord from the first point to the last find the point p furthest from that chord, at distance d if d ≤ ε: return [first, last] the chord is good enough else: return simplify(first…p, ε) + simplify(p…last, ε) O(n log n) typical, O(n²) worst case. ε is in pixels and is the only parameter, which is its great virtue. Its limitation is structural: it can only simplify a chain that has already been LINKED, so it cannot join collinear fragments separated by an occluder, and a broken line stays broken.

Method 2 · the Hough transform

the parameterisation — never use y = mx + c, whose m is unbounded for vertical linesd = x·cos θ + y·sin θ n̂ = (cos θ, sin θ) is the unit normal θ ∈ [−180°, 180°), d ∈ [−√2, √2] using normalised image coordinates in [−1, 1] the original algorithm — every edgel votes for every line through itfor each edgel (x, y): for each θ in the accumulator: d ← x cos θ + y sin θ; accumulator[θ][d] += 1 A single point sweeps out a SINUSOID in (θ, d). Points that are collinear produce sinusoids that all pass through one cell, and that cell is the line. Cost: one vote per (edgel, θ-bin) pair. the oriented variant — each edgel already knows its own normal, so it votes ONCEθ ← atan2(n_y, n_x) from the edgel's own gradient; d ← x·n_x + y·n_y accumulator[θ][d] += 1 ONE cell, not a whole row Cost drops by the number of θ bins — typically two orders of magnitude — and the accumulator gets far less background clutter, because a point no longer votes for the 179 lines through it that it has no evidence for. The price is that the orientation estimate must be good: an error of δθ puts the vote in the wrong θ bin, and a hybrid that spreads each vote over a few bins around the estimate is the usual compromise.
The bin size is the whole problem. Too fine and one real line's votes scatter across several cells and no peak forms. Too coarse and two nearly parallel lines merge into one, and the fitted θ is quantised. There is no setting that is right for all images, because the correct bin size depends on the accuracy of the edgel positions and orientations, on the length of the lines, and on how many there are. The standard mitigation is to use the accumulator only to find candidates and then re-fit each line by total least squares to its own voting edgels — which recovers full precision and makes the bin size merely a grouping parameter rather than an estimator.

Method 3 · random sampling

Pick two edgels at random, form the line through them, count how many other edgels lie within τ of it, keep the best. Repeat. This is §27's RANSAC applied to lines, and it is worth naming here because the comparison is instructive: no accumulator, so no memory and no bin size; but many more hypotheses must be generated and tested than there are cells in a Hough array, and the number needed grows as described in §27. If the edgel orientations are reliable a single edgel determines a line hypothesis, which changes the sample size s from 2 to 1 and collapses the iteration count enormously.

Where the lines go. Lines that are parallel in 3D meet at a vanishing point in the image (Part 1 §13). Detecting the vanishing points of a scene therefore means grouping detected lines — one common method takes each pair of line segments, computes the candidate vanishing point as the cross product of their homogeneous line vectors v = m̂ᵢ × m̂ⱼ, weights the vote by ‖v‖·lᵢ·lⱼ so that near-parallel and short segments count for little, and votes on a sphere or a cube map. Three mutually orthogonal vanishing points calibrate the camera. That is Part 6's material; this page's job is to supply the line segments.
