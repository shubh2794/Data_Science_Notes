16 Scale space, and the theorem that is not quite a theorem

Every detector so far has a scale parameter that had to be chosen. Scale space is the decision to stop choosing: build the image at all scales, treat scale as a third coordinate, and search in it. That reframing is what makes a detector scale invariant, and it is the single most important idea on this page.

the objectL(x, y; t) = G(·, ·; √t) ∗ I(x, y) t ≥ 0, L(x, y; 0) = I(x, y) A one-parameter FAMILY of images, not a stack of approximations to one image. The parameter t = σ² is the natural one because variances add (part 2 §10): smoothing by t₁ and then by t₂ is smoothing by t₁ + t₂, which makes the family a SEMIGROUP. and the equation it satisfies — which is what makes it more than a definition∂L/∂t = ½·∇²L the HEAT EQUATION, with the image as initial data Blurring an image IS diffusing heat through it. Every property of scale space that matters is a property of diffusion: information is destroyed monotonically, the process is irreversible, and structure can only be REMOVED as t grows, never created. §13's curvature displacement was this fact seen from the side.

The uniqueness result, stated with its conditions attached

The sentence you will read everywhere. "The Gaussian is the unique scale-space kernel." That is a theorem only once you say which axioms you are assuming, and every one of them can be dropped to obtain a different, perfectly good scale space. Stated without its conditions it is folklore, and it has misled people into believing that no other multi-scale representation is legitimate.
the axioms, in the form the result is usually proved under1. LINEARITY and SHIFT INVARIANCE ⇒ smoothing is a convolution 2. the SEMIGROUP property L(·; t₁ + t₂) = G_{t₂} ∗ L(·; t₁) 3. CONTINUITY in t, with L(·; 0) = I 4. POSITIVITY and unit mass a kernel that averages, so no new grey levels 5. ISOTROPY rotationally symmetric 6. NON-ENHANCEMENT OF LOCAL EXTREMA at a spatial maximum ∂L/∂t ≤ 0, at a minimum ∂L/∂t ≥ 0 Under 1–6, the kernel is the Gaussian. Axiom 6 is the substantive one: it is the formal version of "blurring must not invent structure that was not there", and it is what rules out kernels with negative side lobes. and the thing axiom 6 does NOT say, which is where almost everyone over-claimsIn ONE dimension the number of local extrema of L(·; t) is non-increasing in t. That is a theorem, and it is easy to check — on a random 512-sample signal the count falls 185 → 141 → 107 → 85 → 63 → 53 → 51 → 29 → 25 → 17 → 11 → 11 → 9 → 7 → 5 → 5 → 1 across scales 1.0 to 66.5, never once rising. In TWO dimensions it is FALSE. New local extrema CAN be created as the scale grows. Measured on this page's own scene, three sub-octave levels per octave, exactly what the figure counts: σ 0.80 1.01 1.27 1.60 2.02 2.54 3.20 4.03 5.08 6.40 8.06 10.16 extrema 28 27 36 33 26 22 17 10 7 5 5 5 The count RISES from 27 to 36 between σ = 1.01 and 1.27. It is not a tie-breaking artefact: requiring every extremum to beat its neighbours by at least 10⁻³ gives 27, 24, 31, 28, 23, 19, 15, 8, 7, 5, 5, 5 — the same rise, from 24 to 31. Non-enhancement says an extremum's VALUE cannot grow, not that extrema cannot APPEAR. Two ridges merging can create a new saddle and a new maximum between them, and that has no 1-D analogue. The overall trend is downward and the process is still irreversible; but "structure only ever disappears" is a 1-D statement, said in 2-D. and what happens when each is droppeddrop ISOTROPY (5) → AFFINE Gaussian scale space, a family indexed by a 2 × 2 covariance rather than a scalar. §20 uses exactly this. drop the SEMIGROUP (2) → other families become admissible; the cascade property is what forces the exponential form. require TIME CAUSALITY → over a temporal axis you cannot look into the future, and the answer is NOT a Gaussian but a cascade of truncated exponentials. Video is not a third spatial dimension. work on a LATTICE → the sampled Gaussian is not exactly a semigroup, see below.

The discrete case, where the sampled Gaussian is not the right answer

measured: does h(σ₁) ∗ h(σ₂) equal h(√(σ₁² + σ₂²)) on the integer lattice?σ₁ σ₂ sampled Gaussian discrete Gaussian 0.7 0.7 6.295 × 10⁻³ 5.1 × 10⁻⁷ 1.0 1.0 2.918 × 10⁻⁵ 5.6 × 10⁻⁷ 1.5 2.0 1.981 × 10⁻¹⁰ 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ 2.0 3.0 1.519 × 10⁻¹⁰ 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ 3.0 4.0 3.493 × 10⁻¹⁰ 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ (maximum absolute error per tap) The sampled, truncated, renormalised Gaussian breaks the semigroup by 6 × 10⁻³ at σ = 0.7 and by 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ by σ = 1.5. THAT is why every implementation refuses to go below about σ = 0.8 per level: below it the lattice, not the theory, is in charge. The exact discrete analogue is the DISCRETE GAUSSIAN T(n; t) = e⁻ᵗ·I_n(t), built from modified Bessel functions, which satisfies the semigroup exactly and whose second moment is exactly t — verified to 10 decimal places at t = 1, 4 and 9.

How it is actually built

Not by convolving the original image with a wider and wider kernel — the cost of that grows as σ². The semigroup makes it incremental, and Part 2 §23's pyramid makes it cheap: within an octave, smooth incrementally by √(σ² k² − σ²) to step from one level to the next; at the end of an octave, downsample by two and continue, because an image already smoothed to σ = 2 has nothing above the new Nyquist rate to lose (Part 3). The whole pyramid therefore costs about 4/3 of one full-resolution pass, exactly as Part 2 computed.

the standard sub-octave layout, and where k = 2^(1/s) comes froms levels per octave ⇒ k = 2^(1/s) three sub-octave levels ⇒ k = 2^(1/3) = 1.2599 To get s USABLE difference-of-Gaussian levels per octave you must compute s + 3 Gaussian levels, because a DoG level costs one Gaussian pair and a scale-space extremum needs a level above and below it. The scale ratio between the FIRST and LAST Gaussian of an octave is exactly 2, which is what lets the next octave start from the downsampled copy of the level at 2σ.

17 Automatic scale selection

Building the stack is half the idea. The other half is knowing which level to believe — and the answer is that a suitably normalised response has a maximum over scale, at the scale that matches the structure. Scale stops being a parameter and becomes a measurement.

Why the response has to be normalised first

the problem: derivatives shrink with scale, so raw responses cannot be compared across levels∂ⁿ/∂xⁿ (G_σ ∗ I) decays like σ⁻ⁿ for a fixed structure — the smoother the image, the smaller every derivative, regardless of what is in it. the fix: γ-normalised derivatives∂_ξ = σ^γ·∂_x with γ = 1 for the general-purpose case so the normalised Laplacian, an operator of order 2, is σ²·∇²L and what that buys, exactlyFor a bright disc of radius R and unit contrast, the response of σ²∇²G at the centre is |σ²∇²L(0)| = (R²/σ²)·exp(−R²/2σ²) Set d/dσ = 0: σ* = R/√2 exactly, and the PEAK VALUE is 2/e = 0.7357589… INDEPENDENT OF R. That is scale invariance, in one line: the same structure at any size produces the same normalised response, at a scale proportional to its size. Verified by exhaustive search over 400 000 scales: R = 3 → argmax σ = 2.12131 R/√2 = 2.12132 R = 5 → argmax σ = 3.53554 R/√2 = 3.53553 R = 8 → argmax σ = 5.65684 R/√2 = 5.65685 R = 12 → argmax σ = 8.48534 R/√2 = 8.48528 R = 20 → argmax σ = 14.14213 R/√2 = 14.14214 and for a GAUSSIAN blob of standard deviation σ₀ the argmax is σ₀ itself, to 5 places. what the un-normalised response would have done insteadfor R = 8: σ raw ∇²L σ²-normalised 2.000 −0.001342 −0.005367 4.000 −0.033834 −0.541341 ← raw peaks HERE, at σ = R/2 5.657 −0.022991 −0.735759 ← normalised peaks HERE, at R/√2 8.000 −0.009477 −0.606531 12.000 −0.002471 −0.355883 The raw response peaks at σ = R/2 and its peak VALUE is (16/R²)e⁻², which depends on R — so raw responses at two scales are not comparable and a "maximum over scale" computed from them means nothing. The normalised peak is 2/e for every R. The whole content of the γ-normalisation is making the vertical axis mean the same thing at every level.
Scale is a search dimension, not a parameter. This is the sentence to take away. A scale-invariant detector does not choose σ; it evaluates a normalised response over a range of σ and reports the argument of the maximum, exactly as it reports the argument of the spatial maximum. The output is a point in (x, y, σ), and the third coordinate is as much a measurement as the first two. Everything downstream — the descriptor's support radius (§23), the matching that must compare like with like (§25) — depends on that third number being an estimate rather than a setting.
A conversion error that is easy to make. The detected σ is not the radius of the thing detected. For the disc above, σ* = R/√2, so the blob's radius is R = √2·σ*, which is also exactly where Part 2 §13's Laplacian-of-Gaussian crosses zero. Report √2·σ when you draw a circle around a blob detection, or every circle you draw will be 41% too small.

18 Blob detection, and the approximation everyone uses

A corner is a point where two edges meet. A blob is a region that differs from its surroundings. Corners are precise but rare and unstable under scale change; blobs are plentiful, and — because §17 gives them a size — naturally scale invariant. Almost every scale-invariant detector in use is a blob detector.

the two operatorsLoG σ²∇²G_σ ∗ I one filter, the exact object of §17 DoG (G_{kσ} − G_σ) ∗ I = L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ) free, if the stack exists why they are related at all — the heat equation again, differentiated in t∂L/∂σ = σ∇²L ⇒ σ∇²L ≈ (L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ)) / (kσ − σ) so L(·; kσ) − L(·; σ) ≈ (k − 1)·σ²·∇²L — a finite difference in SCALE. The DoG is the scale derivative of the stack, evaluated with a step of (k − 1)σ. The (k − 1) factor is CONSTANT across scale, so it does not disturb the location of the extremum in either space or scale, which is why nobody bothers to divide it out.
The folklore, restated from Part 2 §13 because it matters twice as much here. "A difference of Gaussians with k = 1.6 is the Laplacian of Gaussian." It is a 17.89% approximation to it, and the error grows with k, not shrinks. Measured as the maximum relative shape error against the true LoG after rescaling the two to agree at the centre: k = 1.05 → 2.21%, 1.1 → 4.25%, 1.2 → 7.87%, 1.3 → 10.95%, 1.6 → 17.89%, 2.0 → 23.64%, 2.5 → 27.86%. (Recomputed here at 4 × 10⁵ radial samples the k = 1.6 figure is 17.8846%, and it is independent of σ — the error is a function of k alone, which the figure's σ slider demonstrates by not moving it.) The approximation is exact only in the limit k → 1, where the DoG's amplitude vanishes and it becomes useless. k = 1.6 comes from dividing an octave into three sub-levels — 2^(1/3) = 1.26 for the Gaussian step, with the DoG spanning a wider effective ratio — not from any claim about approximation quality.
So why is a 17.9% approximation acceptable? Because the detector uses the response's argmax, not its value. A smooth 18% distortion of a unimodal function moves its peak a little and changes its height, but does not create or destroy peaks. What the DoG buys is that it is already computed: if you have built the scale-space stack of §16, every adjacent pair of levels is a DoG for free, whereas the true LoG needs a second filter bank. Free beats 18% accurate, when only the argmax is read.

What the two operators respond to, and the sign convention

read the sign∇²L < 0 at the centre of a BRIGHT blob on a dark background ∇²L > 0 at the centre of a DARK blob on a bright background So a blob detector looks for extrema of BOTH signs and records the polarity, which is a genuine extra bit of descriptive information: two blobs of opposite polarity can be ruled out as matches before any descriptor is compared. Some binary matchers exploit exactly this to halve the search (§24). and the reported sizedetected at (x, y, σ) ⇒ the blob's radius is √2·σ in 2D (§17) In N dimensions the factor is √N: √2 in an image, √3 in a volume. Drawing circles of radius σ instead of √2σ is the standard off-by-41% bug.

19 Finding extrema in three dimensions, and throwing most of them away

With the stack built, detection is a local-maximum search in (x, y, σ) instead of in (x, y). The search is trivial. What follows it — three rejection tests and one interpolation — is where a usable detector is separated from a list of noise.

the testD(x, y, σ) is an extremum ⟺ it is greater (or less) than ALL 26 NEIGHBOURS: 8 in its own level, 9 in the level above, 9 in the level below. The cost is low because most candidates fail on the first comparison. The subtlety is that the levels above and below must be at the SAME spatial resolution, which is why an octave computes s + 3 Gaussian levels for s usable DoG levels — the extra ones exist purely to give the top and bottom DoG levels somebody to be compared against.

Step 1 · sub-pixel and sub-scale interpolation

the same quadratic fit as §07, now in three dimensionsD(x + δ) ≈ D + gᵀδ + ½δᵀHδ δ = −H⁻¹g, a 3 × 3 solve per candidate with g and H estimated by central differences in x, y AND σ. If any component of δ exceeds ½, the extremum is closer to a neighbouring sample: move there and refit, up to about five times, then give up. The refined value D + ½gᵀδ is what the next test thresholds — not the raw sample.

Step 2 · reject low contrast

Discard candidates whose interpolated |D| falls below a threshold. Because the DoG response scales linearly with image contrast, this threshold is exposure dependent in the same way §06's Harris threshold is, and the same fix applies: express it relative to the image's own response range, or normalise the image first.

Step 3 · reject edges — and note that you have seen this test before

the DoG responds strongly along an edge, where the extremum is a RIDGE and not a peak ⎡ D_xx D_xy ⎤ H₂ = ⎢ ⎥ the 2 × 2 SPATIAL Hessian of the DoG at the candidate ⎣ D_xy D_yy ⎦ reject if tr(H₂)² / det(H₂) > (r + 1)²/r with r = 10 what that threshold actually saystr²/det = (λ₀ + λ₁)²/(λ₀λ₁) = (r + 1)²/r where r is the ratio of the two curvatures so the test is exactly "the two principal curvatures must not differ by more than a factor of r". At r = 10 the threshold value is (10 + 1)²/10 = 12.1, and the quantity being thresholded has no units and no dependence on contrast. and now compare it with §06Harris: R = det A − k·tr²A > 0 ⟺ tr²A/det A < 1/k Both tests are a BOUND ON tr²/det. They are literally the same inequality, applied to two different matrices — Harris to the structure tensor of FIRST derivatives, the blob detector to the Hessian of SECOND derivatives — and their constants convert exactly: k = 1/12.1 = 0.082645 ⟺ the r = 10 edge-rejection test, admissible ratio 10.0000 k = 0.04 ⟺ admissible ratio 22.96 (much MORE permissive) k = 0.06 ⟺ admissible ratio 14.60 k = 0.10 ⟺ admissible ratio 7.873 = 4 + √15 (more STRICT) Worth noticing: the usual Harris settings 0.04–0.06 accept far more elongated structure than the usual blob edge-rejection at r = 10 does. The two detectors are not merely using the same test with different names, they are deliberately set to different strictness — because Harris has already required BOTH eigenvalues to be large, and the blob detector has not.
why the Hessian and not the structure tensor heredet H₂ < 0 ⇒ a SADDLE, and the candidate is rejected outright — the Hessian is not positive semi-definite and its sign carries information the structure tensor cannot. The structure tensor is always PSD, so it can never report a saddle. §05's warning about confusing the two operators has a practical consequence here.

20 Affine adaptation, in outline

Scale invariance handles a camera moving toward or away from a plane. It does not handle a camera moving around it: a circle on a slanted surface projects to an ellipse, and no isotropic detector can normalise that. The fix is to let the smoothing window itself be an ellipse, and to solve for which one.

the transformation hierarchy this rests on (part 1 §5)a small enough patch of ANY smooth warp is well approximated by an AFFINE map: x′ = Ax + t A a general 2 × 2, six parameters in all Scale invariance handles A = sR (four parameters). Affine invariance handles all six, and for a locally planar surface that is the whole of perspective, to first order. the normalisation, which is the entire ideaCompute the second-moment matrix M (§05's structure tensor, with an ELLIPTICAL window) and transform the patch by M^(−1/2). Under x′ = Ax the second-moment matrix transforms as M′ = A⁻ᵀ M A⁻¹, and therefore M′^(−1/2) x′ = R · M^(−1/2) x for some rotation R Two views of the same patch, each normalised by its OWN M^(−1/2), differ by a pure ROTATION — which §22's orientation assignment already knows how to remove. Six parameters are reduced to one, and that one is already handled.

The iteration

M^(−1/2) is a fixed point, not a formula: normalising changes the M you measureU₀ ← I repeat: 1. warp the patch by U_k and measure M in the WARPED frame 2. U_{k+1} ← M^(−1/2)·U_k, renormalised so that det U = 1 and the larger eigenvalue is 1 (otherwise the iteration drifts in overall scale) 3. re-select the scale, since warping changed it until M's eigenvalue ratio is close enough to 1 — typically λ₁/λ₀ < 1.05, in 3 to 10 iterations. On the figure's clean synthetic patch the iteration converges for every setting the sliders allow: a 70° slant starts at a ratio of 4.20 and reaches 1.0037 in ten steps. On real data it does NOT always converge, and the usual guard is to abandon any point whose ratio exceeds about 6, because the normalisation would then have to stretch the patch further than the sampling can support. Detecting the failure is part of the algorithm, not an optional refinement.
What it costs and when it pays. Affine adaptation multiplies detection cost by the iteration count, roughly 5–10×, and it reduces repeatability on easy pairs because the extra parameters are estimated from noisy data. It pays only when the viewpoint change is large — wide-baseline stereo, matching a façade photographed from two sides of a street, recognising a planar object seen obliquely. For image stitching or video tracking it is a net loss. This is a general pattern worth naming: every invariance is bought with discriminability and with variance in the estimate, and buying one you do not need makes the system worse.
An entirely different route to the same place. Rather than fitting an ellipse to a point, one can detect regions that are stable under a changing threshold: sweep the intensity threshold from black to white, watch each connected component's area as a function of the threshold, and keep the components whose area changes least. Such regions are invariant to any monotonic intensity mapping — not merely affine, but any gamma curve, any tone curve from Part 2 §03 — and to any affine geometric transformation, because both properties follow from the construction rather than being fitted. The region view of this is Part 5's territory; the point here is that photometric invariance by construction is stronger than photometric invariance by normalisation, and §23 has to settle for the weaker kind.
