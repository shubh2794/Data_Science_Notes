01 One change of basis, and what it buys

A grey image is a function on a lattice. So is a filter. So is a sinusoid. The Fourier transform says that the sinusoids are special: they are the only functions that a shift-invariant linear filter cannot change the shape of. Feed a sinusoid into any convolution and a sinusoid of the same frequency comes out, scaled and delayed. That single fact is the whole page.

an image on a lattice→a sum of sinusoids→each one merely scaled by the filter→multiply instead of convolve→back to the lattice
the algebra
Convolution becomes multiplication
A convolution is a linear operator; under wrap-around it is a circulant matrix; every circulant matrix is diagonalised by the same fixed basis — the complex exponentials. So f ∗ h costs one multiply per basis vector once you are in that basis. §16–§17 prove it and measure the residual.
the arithmetic
The FFT makes the change of basis cheap
The change of basis is an N × N matrix product, O(N²). A recursive factorisation drops it to O(N log N). For a 1024 × 1024 image the saving over a separable direct evaluation is 204.8×, measured in §08. Without it the frequency domain would be a theoretical device only.
the catch
Sampling folds the spectrum, irreversibly
A lattice can only carry frequencies below half its rate. Everything above is not lost, it is disguised as something below, and no later processing can undo the disguise. §12–§15. Real images are not band-limited, so the pre-filter is always a compromise, and §15 measures exactly how bad a compromise.
the escape
Localise, and pay for it
A Fourier coefficient is a statement about the whole image; an image is not stationary. Windowing (§21), the short-time transform (§22), the DCT's block structure (§23) and wavelets (§24–§26) are four ways to buy locality, and the uncertainty principle of §20 prices all four.

02 Images as signals, and the one experiment that defines the transform

Before any integral, there is a measurement you could actually perform with a filter and a ruler. Put a sinusoid in, look at what comes out. The output is a sinusoid of the same frequency, with a different amplitude and a different offset. Tabulate that amplitude and that offset, for every frequency, and you have written down the Fourier transform without integrating anything.

Why the sinusoids and nothing else

A convolution is a weighted sum of shifted copies of the input. Shift a sinusoid and you get another sinusoid at the same frequency; add sinusoids of one frequency and you get one sinusoid at that frequency. So the shape survives. Nothing else has that property: shift a square pulse and add the copies and you get a trapezoid, then a smoother thing, then something else again. The sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of shift-invariant convolution, and §07 makes that word literal.

the experiment, in three linesin: s(x) = sin(ωx + φᵢ) out: o(x) = (h ∗ s)(x) = A·sin(ωx + φₒ) SAME ω, different A and φ read: A = the GAIN of h at ω; Δφ = φₒ − φᵢ = the PHASE SHIFT of h at ω the same experiment with a complex probe, which removes the bookkeepings(x) = e^{jωx} = cos ωx + j·sin ωx o(x) = h ∗ s = A·e^{j(ωx + φ)} = ( A·e^{jφ} )·e^{jωx} └──── one complex number ────┘ so the transform is simply the table of those numbersH(ω) = F{ h }(ω) = A(ω)·e^{jφ(ω)} |H| = gain, arg H = phase shift

The complex probe is not mysticism. A real sinusoid is two complex exponentials, cos ωx = (e^{jωx} + e^{−jωx})/2, and carrying them separately means the gain and the shift come out as one complex number rather than two real ones that have to be matched up. The imaginary unit is bookkeeping, and §06 shows the bookkeeping being unwound at the end of every real computation.

This is a definition you can compute with, right now. Part 2 plotted a frequency response for every filter it built and never used an FFT to do it: it summed H(ω) = ∑ₖ h(k)·e^{−jωk} directly over the taps, which is exactly "convolve with a sinusoid and read the amplitude", done algebraically. That is Part 2 §12's whole apparatus, and it is one line, because a filter has a handful of taps. The machinery of §06–§08 exists because an image has a million samples, not because the definition needs it.

What "frequency" means for a picture

In 1-D, a frequency is a number. In 2-D it is a vector (ω_x, ω_y), and the corresponding basis function is a plane wave — a corrugation, constant along one direction and sinusoidal along the perpendicular one.

the 2-D basis function: a corrugation with an orientation and a wavelengths(x, y) = sin(ω_x·x + ω_y·y) = sin( ω · p ) p = (x, y), ω = (ω_x, ω_y) direction of variation : ω/|ω| the corrugation's normal wavelength : 2π/|ω| crest-to-crest, in pixels orientation in the plot: atan2(ω_y, ω_x) ROTATED 90° from the stripes you see

That last line catches everyone once. A picture of vertical stripes varies along x and is constant along y, so its energy sits on the ω_x axis — a horizontal pair of dots in the spectrum. The spectrum is rotated a quarter turn from the thing you are looking at, always.

The defining experiment: a sinusoid in, a scaled and shifted sinusoid out change the frequency and watch only two numbers change — the gain and the shift

03 The continuous Fourier transform, and the pulse that generates everything

The experiment of §02 is a recipe, not a formula. The formula comes from asking how much of e^{jωx} a signal contains, and answering it the way you always answer "how much of this basis vector is in that vector" — with an inner product. In infinite dimensions the inner product is an integral.

the forward transform — an inner product against the conjugate basis functionF(ω) = F{ f }(ω) = ∫ f(x)·e^{−jωx} dx over x from −∞ to ∞ the inverse — the same sum, run the other way, with the 2π that ω costsf(x) = F⁻¹{ F }(x) = (1/2π)·∫ F(ω)·e^{+jωx} dω over ω from −∞ to ∞ the same pair in ordinary frequency, where the 2π disappears entirelyF(f) = ∫ f(x)·e^{−j2πfx} dx f(x) = ∫ F(f)·e^{+j2πfx} df THREE conventions are in common use and they differ only in where the 2π lands: in front of the inverse (used above), split as 1/√(2π) on both, or absent because the exponent carries 2πf. Nothing depends on the choice except the constants in Parseval's theorem, and every formula on this page says which convention it is in.

The pulse, computed rather than quoted

One worked integral is enough to generate half the transform pairs in §05, because everything else follows from it by the properties in §04. Take a pulse of height A from −T/2 to T/2 and zero elsewhere.

the pulse, integrated directlyF(ω) = ∫ A·e^{−jωx} dx over x from −T/2 to T/2 = A·[ e^{−jωx} / (−jω) ] evaluated at ±T/2 = A·( e^{+jωT/2} − e^{−jωT/2} ) / (jω) and now Euler's identity, sin θ = ( e^{jθ} − e^{−jθ} ) / 2j, collapses itF(ω) = (2A/ω)·sin(ωT/2) = A·T·sinc(ωT/2) where sinc(u) = sin(u)/u F(0) = A·T the area, as it must be

Three things to read off it, and they will be used repeatedly. One: F(0) is the signal's integral — the DC term is always the mean, in every version of the transform. Two: the first zero is at ωT/2 = π, i.e. ω = 2π/T — narrow pulse, wide transform, and the product of the two widths does not depend on T. That is the uncertainty principle of §20 in its crudest form. Three: the sinc has infinitely many side lobes, alternating in sign, decaying only as 1/ω. That slow decay and that sign change are the entire indictment of the box filter, and Part 2 §10 spent two figures on their consequences without being allowed to say why.

Which functions have a transform, honestly. The integral converges absolutely when ∫|f| < ∞, which excludes a constant, a sinusoid, and an impulse — the three things every treatment uses on the first page. The repair is not a hedge: the transform is extended to tempered distributions, in which F{1} = 2π·δ(ω) and F{δ} = 1 are theorems rather than abuses. Everything on this page is either absolutely integrable, or a distribution whose transform is one of the four standard ones, or a finite vector, where the question does not arise at all. The convergence machinery is Calculus's; the practical statement is that no signal you will meet in a computer causes trouble, because it is finite and bounded.
Fourier series, and why it is the same theorem. If f is periodic with period L, only the frequencies ω_k = 2πk/L can appear, and the integral becomes a sum: f(x) = ∑ₖ c_k·e^{j2πkx/L} with c_k = (1/L)∫ f(x)·e^{−j2πkx/L} dx. Periodic in space ⇒ discrete in frequency. §12 shows the dual: discrete in space ⇒ periodic in frequency. The DFT of §06 is both, which is why it is a finite object. Gibbs' phenomenon lives here: truncating the series of a discontinuous function at any order leaves an overshoot at the jump that does not shrink — measured on a square wave, the partial sums peak at 1.17860 for 501 terms against a limit of (2/π)·Si(π) = 1.178980, an overshoot of 8.949% of the total jump, forever. That is ringing, and §18 shows the same number appearing when a filter has a sharp edge in frequency instead of in space.

04 The properties, each with the one-line reason it is true

Nine properties do all the work. Every one of them is a change of variable inside the defining integral, and none takes more than two lines. They are worth deriving once rather than memorising, because the derivation is always the same move.

All positional information — where the edges are, which way the roof slopes, where the eyes sit — is in the PHASE, and only in the phase. §11 is the demonstration.
Parseval, checked. On the running signal f = [3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6] the two sides of the discrete Parseval relation ∑ₙ|f(n)|² = (1/N)·∑ₖ|F(k)|² agree to the last bit; on a random complex vector of length 32 the relative difference measured 0.0 — not "small", exactly zero, because both sides are the same sum of products reassociated. That is the sanity check every transform implementation should carry, and it catches a wrong normalisation instantly.
The property that is not on the list. There is no useful statement about the transform of max(f, g), or of a median, or of a threshold. The whole apparatus applies to linear operators and to nothing else. Part 2 §15–§17's median, bilateral filter and morphology have no frequency response — not one that is hard to compute, one that does not exist, because those operators do not map a sinusoid to a sinusoid. When a paper reports "the frequency response of a bilateral filter" it means the response measured on one particular image, which is a description of that image as much as of the filter.

06 The discrete Fourier transform: a finite, exact, invertible matrix

Computers hold N numbers, not functions. Replace the integral by a sum over those N samples and the transform stops being analysis and becomes linear algebra: an N × N complex matrix, unitary up to a scale, with an exact inverse. Nothing is approximated. The DFT of a finite vector is not "the Fourier transform, discretised"; it is an exact statement about that vector.

What the bin index means, and where the negative frequencies went

Bin k corresponds to a frequency of k/N cycles per sample, or ω = 2πk/N radians per sample. But e^{−j2π(k+N)n/N} = e^{−j2πkn/N}·e^{−j2πn} = e^{−j2πkn/N} because n is an integer — so the transform is periodic in k with period N. Bin N−1 is bin −1. The honest picture of the frequency axis is therefore a circle, and the useful window on it is k ∈ [−N/2, N/2].

the bin map, which every display of a spectrum silently appliesstored order: k = 0 1 2 … N/2 … N−2 N−1 means: f = 0 +1/N +2/N … ½ … −2/N −1/N cycles per sample DC ────── positive ──────┤├── negative ────── The "fftshift" every plot applies is just rotating this array by N/2 so that DC lands in the middle and the axis reads −½ … 0 … +½. It is a DISPLAY operation and it changes no arithmetic — but doing it before a multiply, and forgetting to undo it, silently modulates the image by (−1)ⁿ. That is §09's checkerboard bug.

07 Why this basis: convolution is diagonal in it

There is nothing sacred about sinusoids as such. They are forced on us by one requirement: we want a basis in which every shift-invariant filter is a diagonal matrix. There is exactly one such basis, and the complex exponentials are it. That claim is a theorem about circulant matrices and it is short.

Every circulant matrix has the same eigenvectors. 1 · origin
DC lands in the corner

Nothing is visible on a linear scale

12 What sampling does, in one picture

Sampling looks like throwing information away, and that is the wrong mental model. What sampling actually does is replicate the spectrum, endlessly, at intervals of the sampling rate. Nothing is lost while the copies stay apart. Everything is lost, irrecoverably, the moment they overlap.

a sampled signal is the original times a comb of impulsescomb(x; Δ) = ∑ₘ δ(x − mΔ) spikes every Δ f_s(x) = f(x) · comb(x; Δ) sampling = MULTIPLICATION and the transform of a comb is a comb, with the spacing invertedF{ comb(x; Δ) } = (1/Δ)·comb(ω; 2π/Δ) Tight in space ⇒ loose in frequency. The spectrum of the sampled signal is the original spectrum, repeated forever, at spacing ω_s = 2π/Δ.
This is the same fact as "the DFT is periodic in k", from §06. There it was proved in one line from e^{−j2πn} = 1; here it is derived from the comb. Both say: discrete in space ⟺ periodic in frequency. And §03's Fourier series said the dual: periodic in space ⟺ discrete in frequency. The DFT is the case where both hold, which is why it is a finite object with a finite frequency axis that wraps.

The three regimes, and what makes them different

f_s > 2·f_max
Oversampled
The copies are separated by a gap. The original spectrum can be cut out with an ideal low-pass filter and the signal recovered exactly. This is not "good enough" — it is exact, and §13 gives the reconstruction formula.
f_s = 2·f_max

The copies just touch. Still recoverable in principle, but the reconstruction filter must be an ideal brick wall with no transition band at all, which is a sinc of infinite extent. Critical sampling is a limit, not a design point.

Undersampled — aliased
The copies overlap and add. In the overlap region, the sum of two different contributions is stored as one number, and there is no operation that separates a sum into its two parts. The damage happens at the moment of sampling and no subsequent filter, model or network undoes it.

13 The sampling theorem, and the reconstruction it promises

The theorem is usually quoted as an inequality and remembered as a rule of thumb. It is neither: it is an exact reconstruction formula, and the inequality is the condition under which that formula converges to the right answer.

the statementIf F(ω) = 0 for all |ω| ≥ ω_max, and the sampling rate satisfies ω_s > 2·ω_max equivalently f_s > 2·f_max, then f is determined EXACTLY by its samples f(nΔ), Δ = 2π/ω_s. and the formula that reconstructs it — Whittaker–Shannon interpolationf(x) = ∑ₙ f(nΔ) · sinc( π(x − nΔ)/Δ ) sinc(u) = sin(u)/u The sinc is the ideal low-pass filter of §05, and this sum is precisely "cut out one copy of the replicated spectrum and throw the rest away". The interpolation kernel is not a design choice here; it is FORCED by the requirement of exactness. why it works, in two linesThe sampled spectrum is ∑ₘ F(ω − mω_s). If the copies do not overlap, then multiplying by a box of half-width ω_s/2 leaves EXACTLY F(ω) and nothing else. Multiplying by a box in frequency is convolving with a sinc in space. Done.
Three things the theorem does not say, each of which is asserted somewhere as though it did.
· It does not say "sample at twice the highest frequency". It says strictly more than twice. Sampling a sinusoid at exactly twice its frequency can give you all zeros — try sin(πn). The equality case fails.
· It does not say anything about non-band-limited signals. If the hypothesis is false the conclusion is not "approximately true"; there is no error bound, and §15 is about what actually happens.
· It does not say the reconstruction is practical. The sinc is infinitely wide and decays as 1/x, so a truncated sinc is not an approximation with a small error — it is a different filter, whose error is a windowing question (§21) rather than a sampling one.

15 The honest version: nothing you photograph is band-limited

The sampling theorem has a hypothesis, and for images that hypothesis is false. An occlusion boundary is a step; a step has a 1/ω spectrum that never terminates. So the theorem does not apply, there is no rate at which sampling becomes lossless, and every pre-filter is a trade rather than a solution. This section states the trade and measures it.

why an image cannot be band-limitedOne object in front of another produces a DISCONTINUITY in radiance. §05: a step has a spectrum decaying as 1/ω, extending to infinity. So band-limited images do not merely fail to occur; they are the wrong model.

16 The convolution theorem, derived

This is the result that Part 2 §21 borrowed first and used most. It says that the messy, order-dependent, O(K)-per-pixel operation of convolution becomes an elementwise product. The derivation is one interchange of integrals and one change of variable.

the theoremF{ f ∗ h } = F(ω) · H(ω) and dually F{ f · h } = (1/2π)·(F ∗ H) the proof, in four linesF{ f ∗ h }(ω) = ∫ [ ∫ f(τ)·h(x − τ) dτ ]·e^{−jωx} dx = ∫ f(τ) [ ∫ h(x − τ)·e^{−jωx} dx ] dτ swap the order = ∫ f(τ) [ e^{−jωτ}·H(ω) ] dτ the SHIFT property, §04 = H(ω)·∫ f(τ)·e^{−jωτ} dτ = H(ω)·F(ω) The only step needing care is the swap, which is Fubini's theorem and needs both integrals to converge absolutely — true whenever f and h are integrable. The rest is the shift property and factoring a constant out of an integral. That is all.
The same proof in the discrete case, with the wrinkle that matters. For finite vectors the interchange is a finite double sum and needs no hypotheses at all — but the shift in h(x − τ) now has to mean something at the ends of the array, and the only choice that makes the shift property exact is wrap-around. So the discrete convolution theorem is a statement about circular convolution, always. §17 is entirely about the consequences, and it is where the classic error lives.

17 Circular versus linear: the error everybody makes once

Multiply two DFTs and invert, and you do not get the convolution you wanted. You get the circular convolution: the one in which the array wraps around, so the last column influences the first. It agrees with the ordinary convolution in the interior and disagrees at the edges by an amount that is not small. This section measures both, on numbers you can check by hand.

Why the wrap-around is not always wrong. The 2-D version, and its cost. 19 Why the Gaussian, and only the Gaussian

The Gaussian appears in every part of this series and is usually justified by "it is smooth and it is what everyone uses". It has four genuine, provable properties that nothing else has at once, and this section proves them and then states exactly where each one stops being true in a computer.

Property 1 — it is its own transform, with the width inverted

the cleanest proof: both sides satisfy the same first-order differential equationLet g(x) = e^{−x²/2σ²}. Then g′(x) = −(x/σ²)·g(x) (∗) Transform both sides, using two properties from §04: F{ g′ } = jω·G(ω) differentiation F{ x·g } = j·dG/dω multiplication by x (the dual of differentiation) So (∗) becomes jω·G = −(1/σ²)·j·dG/dω dG/dω = −σ²·ω·G — the SAME equation, in ω, with σ → 1/σ Its solution is G(ω) = G(0)·e^{−σ²ω²/2}, and G(0) = ∫g = σ√(2π). Done. Verified numerically: a Gaussian sampled on a dense grid, transformed by FFT and compared with σ√(2π)·e^{−σ²ω²/2}, agrees to 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 1, 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 2 and 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 3, with the imaginary part below 10⁻¹⁶ throughout.

Property 2 — the transform is positive, so there is no ringing, ever

e^{−σ²ω²/2} > 0 for every ω. A filter whose response never changes sign cannot invert a band; it can only attenuate. Since ringing beside an edge is a band inversion — the negative lobes of the kernel producing an overshoot — a Gaussian blur has no halos, at any width, on any image. Every other smoothing kernel in Part 2 §10 either has this property because it is a self-convolution (tent, binomial) or lacks it (box, windowed sinc).

Property 3 — it is the only separable, rotationally symmetric kernel

the statement, with its hypotheses, which matterLet f: ℝ² → ℝ be CONTINUOUS, STRICTLY POSITIVE, and both SEPARABLE f(x, y) = g(x)·g(y) ROTATIONALLY SYMMETRIC f(x, y) = F(x² + y²) for some F Then g(x) = g(0)·e^{c·x²} for some constant c, and if f is integrable then c < 0 and f is a Gaussian. the proof, which is Cauchy's functional equation in disguiseRotational symmetry with (x, y) rotated onto (√(x²+y²), 0) gives g(x)·g(y) = g(√(x² + y²))·g(0) for all x, y Put φ(t) = log( g(√t) / g(0) ), defined because g > 0. Then φ(x²) + φ(y²) = φ(x² + y²) i.e. φ(u) + φ(v) = φ(u + v) which is Cauchy's equation. With φ continuous the only solutions are φ(u) = c·u. Hence g(x) = g(0)·e^{c x²}, and c < 0 for an integrable kernel. ∎ The hypotheses are not decoration. Drop CONTINUITY and Cauchy's equation admits monstrous non-measurable solutions. Drop POSITIVITY and g may vanish on a set, breaking the logarithm. And note the degenerate members of the family: c = 0 gives the CONSTANT function, which is indeed separable and rotationally symmetric, and a delta is a distributional limit of the family. The theorem is about the shape, not about the existence of exceptions to carelessly stated versions of it.
Checked, as a rank statement. Separability of a sampled kernel means its matrix has rank 1. Measured singular values of three 11 × 11 kernels: the Gaussian at σ = 2 has numerical rank 1 with s₁/s₂ = 1.4 × 10¹⁶; the box has rank 1 too (it is separable, but it is not rotationally symmetric — it is a square); the disc of radius 5 is rotationally symmetric and has numerical rank 4, with s₁/s₂ = 5.1. Exactly one of the three is both, and it is the Gaussian. This is also why Part 2 §11's 2K-instead-of-K² saving is available for a Gaussian blur and not for a disc blur.

Property 4 — it composes with itself, which makes scale space possible

the semigroup property, one line in frequency (§16)G(σ₁) ∗ G(σ₂) = G( √(σ₁² + σ₂²) ) Blurring twice is blurring once. So a pyramid can be built INCREMENTALLY, each level from the one above, and the result is identical to blurring the original once with the total width. No other kernel family in use has this exactly — a box convolved with a box is a tent, not a box. Equivalently: G(x; σ) is the Green's function of the heat equation, and blurring by increasing σ is running diffusion forward in time with t = σ²/2. That is why scale space is a well-posed evolution and why detail can only ever be DESTROYED as scale increases — the second half of that statement is a theorem about the heat equation, not an empirical observation.
Where all four properties stop being exactly true, which no one says.
· Sampling breaks the self-transform. The transform of a sampled Gaussian is not a Gaussian but the periodised one — the sum of shifted copies, by §12. Measured on a 128-point grid: the sampled Gaussian's DFT matches the periodised continuous transform to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ and matches the single continuous Gaussian to only 7.2 × 10⁻³ at σ = 1, 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ at σ = 1.5, and 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 3. Below about σ = 1.5 a sampled Gaussian is measurably not a Gaussian in frequency.
· Truncation breaks the positivity. The ideal Gaussian's response is positive; a truncated one's is not. Measured: σ = 2 truncated at 3σ dips to −7.6 × 10⁻⁴; truncated at 2σ it dips to −1.5 × 10⁻². So a 2σ-truncated Gaussian can ring, at the 1.5% level. That is small, and it is not zero, and the honest statement of "a Gaussian never rings" carries "if you keep enough of it".
· Renormalising breaks the semigroup slightly. Truncate, renormalise to ∑h = 1, and two blurs no longer compose to exactly √(σ₁² + σ₂²). The error is at the truncation level, so 3σ makes it negligible and 2σ does not.
· And the discrete analogue that is exactly right is the binomial kernel, row n of Pascal's triangle over 2ⁿ: it is exactly separable, exactly non-negative, has H(π) = 0 exactly, composes exactly (binomial(m) ∗ binomial(n) = binomial(m+n)), and converges to the Gaussian. When exactness matters more than the last few per cent of shape, it is the better object, and it is what Part 2 §23's pyramid actually uses.

Why the magnitude of a complex Gabor response is the useful quantity. The even and odd parts respond maximally at different phases of the same texture — the even one peaks on a bar, the odd one on its edge. Their squared sum, the oriented energy, is independent of that phase, so it measures the presence of the structure rather than its alignment with the filter. That is exactly the quadrature-pair construction Part 2 §14 built with steerable filters, arrived at from the frequency side; and it is why oriented-energy features are stable where raw filter responses oscillate.

The one parameter that cannot be chosen

The window width sets the resolution in both domains, by §20, and it is fixed across the whole transform. A narrow window locates events precisely and cannot distinguish nearby frequencies; a wide one does the reverse. Since an image contains both a sharp edge (needs a narrow window) and a slow gradient (needs a wide one), no single choice is right for the whole image. That is not a tuning difficulty; it is a structural limitation of a fixed grid, and it is precisely what the wavelet transform of §24–§26 is built to escape.

25 Lifting: building a wavelet transform without designing a filter

Designing a filter pair that is simultaneously finite, smooth, orthogonal and exactly invertible is a research problem. The lifting scheme sidesteps it entirely by making invertibility structural: every step modifies one half of the samples using only the other half, so undoing it is a matter of running the same arithmetic with the signs flipped.

26 The tiling: what actually distinguishes a wavelet from a windowed transform

Both the short-time transform and the wavelet transform partition the space–frequency plane into cells. By §20 every cell must have area at least ½. The difference between the two methods is entirely in the shape of the cells — and that difference decides which signals each one describes well.

four ways to tile the plane, and what each one isPIXELS / SAMPLES tall, thin columns perfect position, no frequency information at all FOURIER wide, flat rows perfect frequency, no position information at all SHORT-TIME (§22) a UNIFORM grid of identical rectangles one compromise, chosen once, applied everywhere WAVELET rectangles that get SHORTER AND WIDER as frequency rises Δω/ω is CONSTANT: octave bands, i.e. constant RELATIVE bandwidth every cell still has the same AREA — nothing is gained for free, the shape is merely reallocated

Why the wavelet's shape is the right one for images

A high-frequency event in an image is an edge, and an edge is spatially compact — so the analysis of high frequencies should be spatially compact too. A low-frequency event is an illumination gradient across the whole picture, and it needs no spatial precision at all. The wavelet tiling gives short, wide cells at high frequency and long, narrow ones at low frequency, which is exactly that allocation. The short-time transform gives the same rectangle to both, and therefore mis-serves both.

constant relative bandwidth, statedSTFT: Δω = constant absolute bandwidth fixed WAVELET: Δω/ω = constant RELATIVE bandwidth fixed ⇒ octave bands At scale j the wavelet ψ(2^j·x − n) has spatial width ∝ 2^{−j} frequency width ∝ 2^{j} centre frequency ∝ 2^{j} so Δω/ω is INDEPENDENT of j and the product of the two widths is constant — the uncertainty area is preserved at every scale. A wavelet transform does not beat §20; it spends its budget differently at different frequencies.
The cost, stated plainly. A critically sampled wavelet transform is badly shift-variant: move the input one pixel and energy moves between sub-bands, because each level is subsampled. That is fine for compression, where the image is not going to move, and disqualifying for matching, where it is. The fix is to give up critical sampling — an undecimated (à trous) transform, or a steerable pyramid, both overcomplete, both approximately shift-invariant, both useless for compression. This is the same overcomplete-versus-critical trade that Part 2 §23 found between the Laplacian pyramid and the wavelet, arrived at from the other direction.

27 Four things the frequency domain is actually used for

Almost all of the frequency domain's practical value in vision is in four operations. Two are computational shortcuts, one is a repair, and one is a diagnosis that has no spatial equivalent at all.

1 · Fast correlation, for template matching and alignment

correlation by transform — note the conjugate (§04)f ⊗ t = F⁻¹{ F(ω) · T*(ω) } Cost: independent of the template size. Matching a 64 × 64 template against a 1024 × 1024 image costs three transforms rather than 4096 multiplies per pixel. phase correlation, which uses ONLY the phase and is why §11 mattersR(ω) = ( F·G* ) / |F·G*| normalise the magnitude away r(x) = F⁻¹{ R } a sharp PEAK at the displacement If g is f shifted by d, then G = F·e^{−jω·d} and R = e^{+jω·d} exactly, whose inverse transform is δ(x − d) — a single spike, at the shift. Discarding the magnitude makes it invariant to contrast and to any illumination change that is purely multiplicative, which is why it is robust where raw correlation is not.

2 · Deconvolution and the Wiener filter

the naive inverse, and why it explodesobserved: o = b ∗ s + n b = a known blur, n = noise naive: S = O / B = S + N/B Wherever B is small, N/B is enormous. Deconvolution does not fail because the algorithm is poor; it fails because the operator that produced the data was nearly singular, and the noise sits in exactly the directions it destroyed (§07). the Wiener filter — the posterior mean, derived in one line of algebraModel each frequency independently: S ~ N(0, P_s(ω)), N ~ N(0, σ_n²). Minimising the negative log posterior ½·P_n⁻¹(S − O)² + ½·P_s⁻¹S² gives S_opt = O · P_s / (P_s + σ_n²) = O / ( 1 + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) so the filter is W(ω) = 1 / ( 1 + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) Unit gain where the signal dominates; attenuation by P_s/σ_n² where the noise does. With a known blur b as well, the same derivation gives W(ω) = B*(ω) / ( |B(ω)|² + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) which is 1/B where B is large and rolls smoothly to ZERO where B is small, instead of to infinity. That single added term in the denominator is the whole difference between a usable deblur and an explosion of noise. Note what P_s is: the assumed POWER SPECTRUM of the class of images. The usual choice is P_s ∝ 1/ω², the natural-image statistic, and it is a genuine prior — the estimate is only as good as that assumption. See Estimation for what "posterior mean" is doing here.

3 · Notch filtering: removing periodic corruption

Periodic interference — scanner banding, sensor pattern noise, the moiré of a photographed screen, the weave of a fabric under a fingerprint — is compact in frequency and spread out in space. There is no spatial filter that removes it without removing the image; in frequency it is a handful of bright spots that can be zeroed individually. This is the one operation on the page with no spatial equivalent, and it is the reason a spectrum is worth looking at as a diagnostic.

How to do it without making it worse. Zeroing a coefficient outright is multiplying by a box, and a box in frequency is a sinc in space (§05) — so a hard notch rings across the whole image. Use a smooth notch, a Gaussian dip rather than a hole. And remember that the spectrum is conjugate-symmetric: zero the coefficient at (k_x, k_y) and its partner at (−k_x, −k_y), or the result is complex and the imaginary part you discard is a real error.

28 Practice

Every answer below is worked out, and every number has been recomputed independently of the page that quotes it. If a step feels like recall rather than derivation, the section it came from is the one to reread.

Drills

  1. A 512 × 512 image is filtered with an unseparable 31 × 31 kernel. Direct, or by transform? Direct costs 31² = 961 multiplies per pixel. By transform: two 2-D FFTs and an inverse, about 3·log₂512 = 27 per pixel plus the multiply, so roughly 28 — a 34× saving, before counting that the padding to 542 × 542 will be rounded up to 1024 and cost four times as much again, bringing it to about 8×. Still worth it. At K = 5 the direct cost is 25 and the transform loses outright.
  2. Why does the DFT of a real image have N²/2 redundant numbers, and how much does exploiting that save? Hermitian symmetry (§04): F(−k) = F*(k). So half the coefficients are determined by the other half. A real-input FFT stores N(N/2 + 1) complex numbers instead of N² and runs in about half the time. Forgetting the symmetry when you edit a spectrum is worse than wasteful — it makes the inverse complex, and the imaginary part you then discard is a real error.
  3. A sinusoid at 0.62 cycles/sample is sampled. Where does it appear? |((0.62 + 0.5) mod 1) − 0.5| = |0.12 − 0.5| = 0.38. And 0.38 itself would appear at 0.38 — so 0.62 and 0.38 give identical samples and are not distinguishable by any means whatsoever from the sampled data.
  4. Decimating an image by 3: what is the new Nyquist limit, and what does the pre-filter have to kill? The new limit is 1/6 = 0.1667 cycles per original pixel. Everything from there to 0.5 folds back, so the pre-filter must attenuate the band [0.1667, 0.5] — three quarters of the original spectrum. A Gaussian of σ ≈ 0.5r = 1.5 is the usual choice, and by §15's table that is a compromise, not a solution.
  5. Multiply two 8-point DFTs of an 8-sample signal and a 3-tap kernel, invert. How many of the 8 outputs are wrong? Two — the first and the last, i.e. K − 1 = 2 samples, the kernel's reach. Measured on the running example the errors are exactly 1.50 and 0.75 and every other sample agrees to 0.0. Padding to N + K − 1 = 10 fixes it.
  6. Two Gaussian blurs, σ = 3 then σ = 4. What is the combined width, and prove it in one line. √(9 + 16) = 5. In frequency: e^{−9ω²/2}·e^{−16ω²/2} = e^{−25ω²/2}. The spatial proof needs a completed square; the frequency proof is exponent addition.
  7. What is the frequency response of [1,1]/2, and why is it exactly the right anti-alias plate? H(f) = cos(πf) with a half-sample shift. H(0.5) = 0 exactly: it annihilates the Nyquist frequency, which by §15 is the one frequency that folds onto itself and that no other filter can rescue. That is what a birefringent AA filter is doing physically.
  8. Why can a box filter never be a decimation pre-filter? For odd width K = 2R + 1, H(π) = (−1)^R/K ≠ 0. Box-3 leaves −1/3 of the Nyquist frequency standing, box-9 leaves +1/9. It shrinks but never vanishes. And its deepest negative lobe converges to the sinc's −0.2172 and does not shrink at all.
  9. An image and its 90°-rotated copy: which is the same, the magnitude spectrum or the phase? Neither — rotation rotates the whole transform, magnitude and phase alike. It is translation that leaves the magnitude untouched. Confusing the two invariances is the classic error, and §04's shift property is the one that is true.
  10. Reconstruct the running signal from DC and three conjugate pairs. What is the RMSE, exactly? 0.3750. The only omitted coefficient is F(4) = −3, at the Nyquist bin, which contributes (−3/8)·(−1)ⁿ — a constant-magnitude alternating term of size 3/8 = 0.375. The RMSE is that magnitude exactly, because the term has the same size at every sample.
  11. The uncertainty product of a box of half-width 1: compute it. You cannot: σ_ω diverges, because |F|² ~ 1/ω² makes ω²|F|² tend to a constant. Measured on grids of growing extent the "answer" doubles each time the range quadruples — 4.33, 8.69, 17.40, 34.81. A box has no finite bandwidth in this sense.
  12. Why is the DCT better than the DFT on a linear ramp, quantitatively? The DFT's periodic extension puts a jump of 70 between the last sample and the first; the DCT's mirror extension has none. Measured RMSE keeping 4 of 8 basis functions: DCT 0.7326, DFT 10.4201 — a factor of 14.2. On a less extreme photographic block the factor is about 1.5.
  13. How many coefficients does a J-level 2-D wavelet decomposition produce? Exactly N, the pixel count, at every J. Each level replaces its input with three detail bands (¾ of the samples) and one approximation (¼), and recursing on the approximation gives ¾ + ¾·¼ + ¾·1/16 + … = 1. The Laplacian pyramid gives 4/3 instead, and that 1/3 is the whole compression argument.
  14. What does the 5/3 wavelet's high-pass filter do to a linear ramp, and why does that matter? Annihilates it — measured, every detail coefficient is exactly 0 on f(n) = 3n + 7. Two vanishing moments. A smooth image is locally a ramp, so its detail bands are nearly all zero, so they compress to almost nothing. On a quadratic the detail coefficients are a non-zero constant, which is what "exactly two" means.
  15. A 64-sample record, a sinusoid halfway between two bins, no window. How much is the amplitude under-reported? The peak reads 0.65072 of its true value, a loss of 3.73 dB. As N → ∞ this converges to 2/π = 0.63662, i.e. −3.92 dB. With a Hann window the worst case is 0.8489, −1.42 dB.
  16. Which window would you pick to see a −60 dB component next to a strong one, and what does it cost? Blackman: peak side lobe −58.12 dB, so a −60 dB component is right at the edge of visibility and a longer record is the real answer. The cost is a main lobe 3 bins wide against the rectangle's 1, so two components closer than 3 bins will merge. Detection and resolution pull in opposite directions, always.
  17. Why does a magnitude spectrum of almost any photograph have a bright cross through it? The DFT tiles the image; the left and right edges do not match, and neither do the top and bottom. Those two seams are steps, and a step has energy at every frequency along one axis. It is an artefact of the finite record, and windowing removes it.
  18. Deconvolve an image blurred by [1,2,1]/4 at N = 8. What happens? The eigenvalues are [1, 0.8536, 0.5, 0.1464, 0, 0.1464, 0.5, 0.8536]. One of them is exactly zero, so the operator is singular and the Nyquist component of the original is gone — not attenuated, destroyed. No deconvolution recovers it, and a naive 1/H divides by zero. §27's Wiener filter returns 0 there, which is the correct answer.
  19. A texture appears at 0.3 cycles/pixel in an image, and you downsample by 2. What do you see? The new limit is 0.25, so 0.3 folds to |((0.6 + 0.5) mod 1) − 0.5| = 0.4 cycles/sample of the new grid — higher in relative terms than it was. The texture appears finer, not coarser, which is why aliased downsampling often looks sharper.
  20. How would you tell aliasing from genuine detail in an image you were handed? You cannot, from that image alone — that is the content of §14, not a limitation of technique. Evidence comes from elsewhere: the acquisition chain's MTF, a second exposure at a different resolution, or the fact that the "detail" moves incoherently when the camera does. Any method claiming to detect aliasing from a single image is using a prior about natural images, and should say so.
  21. Why is the Gabor filter's magnitude the quantity used in texture features, rather than its response? The even and odd parts of a quadrature pair peak at different phases of the same structure, so a raw response oscillates as the filter slides across a texture. The squared sum is phase-invariant, and it measures presence rather than alignment. Same construction as Part 2 §14's steerable quadrature pair.
  22. You need to align two images that differ by a translation and an overall brightness scale. What is the cheapest robust method? Phase correlation: normalise F·G* by its magnitude and invert. A pure translation gives e^{jω·d} exactly, whose inverse transform is a single spike at d. Discarding the magnitude discards the brightness scale with it, which is precisely why it is robust — and it is §11's lesson used as an algorithm.
  23. The 2-D DFT of a picture of vertical bars: where is the energy? On the horizontal axis of the spectrum, as a symmetric pair of dots. The image varies along x and is constant along y, so ω_y = 0. The spectrum is rotated a quarter turn from the stripes you see, always, and the wavelength is N/|k| pixels.
  24. What is the frequency response of a median filter? It does not have one. A median is not linear, so it does not map a sinusoid to a sinusoid, and §04's whole apparatus is inapplicable. Any measured "response" is a property of the particular image it was measured on. Part 2 §15's non-linear filters are outside this page by construction, not by omission.

  • That sampling multiplies by a comb, that a comb's transform is a comb with the reciprocal spacing, and hence that sampling replicates the spectrum.
