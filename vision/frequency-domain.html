Frequency Domain

Part 2 built filters and then kept apologising for them. It said the box filter rings and the Gaussian does not; that you must blur before you subsample; that an ideal low-pass filter is infinitely wide. It stated four of those results as borrowed and pointed here. This page pays that debt. It is one idea in many costumes: every shift-invariant linear operation on an image is a diagonal matrix in the right basis, that basis is the complex exponentials, and the Fourier transform is the change of coordinates that reveals it. Everything else — the FFT, the sampling theorem, aliasing, the DCT in every JPEG you have ever opened, wavelets — is a consequence, a special case, or a workaround for a place where the idea does not quite fit.

01 One change of basis, and what it buys

A grey image is a function on a lattice. So is a filter. So is a sinusoid. The Fourier transform says that the sinusoids are special: they are the only functions that a shift-invariant linear filter cannot change the shape of. Feed a sinusoid into any convolution and a sinusoid of the same frequency comes out, scaled and delayed. That single fact is the whole page.

an image on a lattice→a sum of sinusoids→each one merely scaled by the filter→multiply instead of convolve→back to the lattice
the algebra
Convolution becomes multiplication
A convolution is a linear operator; under wrap-around it is a circulant matrix; every circulant matrix is diagonalised by the same fixed basis — the complex exponentials. So f ∗ h costs one multiply per basis vector once you are in that basis. §16–§17 prove it and measure the residual.
the arithmetic
The FFT makes the change of basis cheap
The change of basis is an N × N matrix product, O(N²). A recursive factorisation drops it to O(N log N). For a 1024 × 1024 image the saving over a separable direct evaluation is 204.8×, measured in §08. Without it the frequency domain would be a theoretical device only.
the catch
Sampling folds the spectrum, irreversibly
A lattice can only carry frequencies below half its rate. Everything above is not lost, it is disguised as something below, and no later processing can undo the disguise. §12–§15. Real images are not band-limited, so the pre-filter is always a compromise, and §15 measures exactly how bad a compromise.
the escape
Localise, and pay for it
A Fourier coefficient is a statement about the whole image; an image is not stationary. Windowing (§21), the short-time transform (§22), the DCT's block structure (§23) and wavelets (§24–§26) are four ways to buy locality, and the uncertainty principle of §20 prices all four.

27 Four things the frequency domain is actually used for

Almost all of the frequency domain's practical value in vision is in four operations. Two are computational shortcuts, one is a repair, and one is a diagnosis that has no spatial equivalent at all.

1 · Fast correlation, for template matching and alignment

correlation by transform — note the conjugate (§04)f ⊗ t = F⁻¹{ F(ω) · T*(ω) } Cost: independent of the template size. Matching a 64 × 64 template against a 1024 × 1024 image costs three transforms rather than 4096 multiplies per pixel. phase correlation, which uses ONLY the phase and is why §11 mattersR(ω) = ( F·G* ) / |F·G*| normalise the magnitude away r(x) = F⁻¹{ R } a sharp PEAK at the displacement If g is f shifted by d, then G = F·e^{−jω·d} and R = e^{+jω·d} exactly, whose inverse transform is δ(x − d) — a single spike, at the shift. Discarding the magnitude makes it invariant to contrast and to any illumination change that is purely multiplicative, which is why it is robust where raw correlation is not.

2 · Deconvolution and the Wiener filter

the naive inverse, and why it explodesobserved: o = b ∗ s + n b = a known blur, n = noise naive: S = O / B = S + N/B Wherever B is small, N/B is enormous. Deconvolution does not fail because the algorithm is poor; it fails because the operator that produced the data was nearly singular, and the noise sits in exactly the directions it destroyed (§07). the Wiener filter — the posterior mean, derived in one line of algebraModel each frequency independently: S ~ N(0, P_s(ω)), N ~ N(0, σ_n²). Minimising the negative log posterior ½·P_n⁻¹(S − O)² + ½·P_s⁻¹S² gives S_opt = O · P_s / (P_s + σ_n²) = O / ( 1 + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) so the filter is W(ω) = 1 / ( 1 + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) Unit gain where the signal dominates; attenuation by P_s/σ_n² where the noise does. With a known blur b as well, the same derivation gives W(ω) = B*(ω) / ( |B(ω)|² + σ_n²/P_s(ω) ) which is 1/B where B is large and rolls smoothly to ZERO where B is small, instead of to infinity. That single added term in the denominator is the whole difference between a usable deblur and an explosion of noise. Note what P_s is: the assumed POWER SPECTRUM of the class of images. The usual choice is P_s ∝ 1/ω², the natural-image statistic, and it is a genuine prior — the estimate is only as good as that assumption. See Estimation for what "posterior mean" is doing here.

3 · Notch filtering: removing periodic corruption

Periodic interference — scanner banding, sensor pattern noise, the moiré of a photographed screen, the weave of a fabric under a fingerprint — is compact in frequency and spread out in space. There is no spatial filter that removes it without removing the image; in frequency it is a handful of bright spots that can be zeroed individually. This is the one operation on the page with no spatial equivalent, and it is the reason a spectrum is worth looking at as a diagnostic.

How to do it without making it worse. Zeroing a coefficient outright is multiplying by a box, and a box in frequency is a sinc in space (§05) — so a hard notch rings across the whole image. Use a smooth notch, a Gaussian dip rather than a hole. And remember that the spectrum is conjugate-symmetric: zero the coefficient at (k_x, k_y) and its partner at (−k_x, −k_y), or the result is complex and the imaginary part you discard is a real error.

4 · Diagnosis: reading a spectrum to find out what is wrong

What you see in the spectrumWhat it means
A bright cross along the two axesThe boundary discontinuity of the implicit tiling (§21), not content. Window the image, or use a periodic-plus-smooth split, before drawing any conclusion.
Isolated bright dots off the axesGenuine periodic content or interference. Read the wavelength as N/|k| and the orientation as perpendicular to the dot's direction — §09's figure does this for you.
Energy piled up at the Nyquist edgeEither genuine pixel-scale detail, or aliasing folded down from above (§14). The two are indistinguishable in this image alone, which is the whole problem — you must look at the acquisition.
A star or streak through the originMotion blur or an anisotropic PSF: the blur's transform is a sinc along the motion direction, whose zeros appear as dark bands whose spacing gives the blur length.
A hard circular or rectangular cutoffThe image has been resampled or JPEG-compressed by something that low-passed it. Anything you "recover" beyond that boundary is invention.
Periodic corruption, found in the spectrum and removed there no spatial filter can do this; the interference is one dot, and the picture is everywhere else

28 Practice

Every answer below is worked out, and every number has been recomputed independently of the page that quotes it. If a step feels like recall rather than derivation, the section it came from is the one to reread.

Drills

  1. A 512 × 512 image is filtered with an unseparable 31 × 31 kernel. Direct, or by transform? Direct costs 31² = 961 multiplies per pixel. By transform: two 2-D FFTs and an inverse, about 3·log₂512 = 27 per pixel plus the multiply, so roughly 28 — a 34× saving, before counting that the padding to 542 × 542 will be rounded up to 1024 and cost four times as much again, bringing it to about 8×. Still worth it. At K = 5 the direct cost is 25 and the transform loses outright.
  2. Why does the DFT of a real image have N²/2 redundant numbers, and how much does exploiting that save? Hermitian symmetry (§04): F(−k) = F*(k). So half the coefficients are determined by the other half. A real-input FFT stores N(N/2 + 1) complex numbers instead of N² and runs in about half the time. Forgetting the symmetry when you edit a spectrum is worse than wasteful — it makes the inverse complex, and the imaginary part you then discard is a real error.
  3. A sinusoid at 0.62 cycles/sample is sampled. Where does it appear? |((0.62 + 0.5) mod 1) − 0.5| = |0.12 − 0.5| = 0.38. And 0.38 itself would appear at 0.38 — so 0.62 and 0.38 give identical samples and are not distinguishable by any means whatsoever from the sampled data.
  4. Decimating an image by 3: what is the new Nyquist limit, and what does the pre-filter have to kill? The new limit is 1/6 = 0.1667 cycles per original pixel. Everything from there to 0.5 folds back, so the pre-filter must attenuate the band [0.1667, 0.5] — three quarters of the original spectrum. A Gaussian of σ ≈ 0.5r = 1.5 is the usual choice, and by §15's table that is a compromise, not a solution.
  5. Multiply two 8-point DFTs of an 8-sample signal and a 3-tap kernel, invert. How many of the 8 outputs are wrong? Two — the first and the last, i.e. K − 1 = 2 samples, the kernel's reach. Measured on the running example the errors are exactly 1.50 and 0.75 and every other sample agrees to 0.0. Padding to N + K − 1 = 10 fixes it.
  6. Two Gaussian blurs, σ = 3 then σ = 4. What is the combined width, and prove it in one line. √(9 + 16) = 5. In frequency: e^{−9ω²/2}·e^{−16ω²/2} = e^{−25ω²/2}. The spatial proof needs a completed square; the frequency proof is exponent addition.
  7. What is the frequency response of [1,1]/2, and why is it exactly the right anti-alias plate? H(f) = cos(πf) with a half-sample shift. H(0.5) = 0 exactly: it annihilates the Nyquist frequency, which by §15 is the one frequency that folds onto itself and that no other filter can rescue. That is what a birefringent AA filter is doing physically.
  8. Why can a box filter never be a decimation pre-filter? For odd width K = 2R + 1, H(π) = (−1)^R/K ≠ 0. Box-3 leaves −1/3 of the Nyquist frequency standing, box-9 leaves +1/9. It shrinks but never vanishes. And its deepest negative lobe converges to the sinc's −0.2172 and does not shrink at all.
  9. An image and its 90°-rotated copy: which is the same, the magnitude spectrum or the phase? Neither — rotation rotates the whole transform, magnitude and phase alike. It is translation that leaves the magnitude untouched. Confusing the two invariances is the classic error, and §04's shift property is the one that is true.
  10. Reconstruct the running signal from DC and three conjugate pairs. What is the RMSE, exactly? 0.3750. The only omitted coefficient is F(4) = −3, at the Nyquist bin, which contributes (−3/8)·(−1)ⁿ — a constant-magnitude alternating term of size 3/8 = 0.375. The RMSE is that magnitude exactly, because the term has the same size at every sample.
  11. The uncertainty product of a box of half-width 1: compute it. You cannot: σ_ω diverges, because |F|² ~ 1/ω² makes ω²|F|² tend to a constant. Measured on grids of growing extent the "answer" doubles each time the range quadruples — 4.33, 8.69, 17.40, 34.81. A box has no finite bandwidth in this sense.
  12. Why is the DCT better than the DFT on a linear ramp, quantitatively? The DFT's periodic extension puts a jump of 70 between the last sample and the first; the DCT's mirror extension has none. Measured RMSE keeping 4 of 8 basis functions: DCT 0.7326, DFT 10.4201 — a factor of 14.2. On a less extreme photographic block the factor is about 1.5.
  13. How many coefficients does a J-level 2-D wavelet decomposition produce? Exactly N, the pixel count, at every J. Each level replaces its input with three detail bands (¾ of the samples) and one approximation (¼), and recursing on the approximation gives ¾ + ¾·¼ + ¾·1/16 + … = 1. The Laplacian pyramid gives 4/3 instead, and that 1/3 is the whole compression argument.
  14. What does the 5/3 wavelet's high-pass filter do to a linear ramp, and why does that matter? Annihilates it — measured, every detail coefficient is exactly 0 on f(n) = 3n + 7. Two vanishing moments. A smooth image is locally a ramp, so its detail bands are nearly all zero, so they compress to almost nothing. On a quadratic the detail coefficients are a non-zero constant, which is what "exactly two" means.
  15. A 64-sample record, a sinusoid halfway between two bins, no window. How much is the amplitude under-reported? The peak reads 0.65072 of its true value, a loss of 3.73 dB. As N → ∞ this converges to 2/π = 0.63662, i.e. −3.92 dB. With a Hann window the worst case is 0.8489, −1.42 dB.
  16. Which window would you pick to see a −60 dB component next to a strong one, and what does it cost? Blackman: peak side lobe −58.12 dB, so a −60 dB component is right at the edge of visibility and a longer record is the real answer. The cost is a main lobe 3 bins wide against the rectangle's 1, so two components closer than 3 bins will merge. Detection and resolution pull in opposite directions, always.
  17. Why does a magnitude spectrum of almost any photograph have a bright cross through it? The DFT tiles the image; the left and right edges do not match, and neither do the top and bottom. Those two seams are steps, and a step has energy at every frequency along one axis. It is an artefact of the finite record, and windowing removes it.
  18. Deconvolve an image blurred by [1,2,1]/4 at N = 8. What happens? The eigenvalues are [1, 0.8536, 0.5, 0.1464, 0, 0.1464, 0.5, 0.8536]. One of them is exactly zero, so the operator is singular and the Nyquist component of the original is gone — not attenuated, destroyed. No deconvolution recovers it, and a naive 1/H divides by zero. §27's Wiener filter returns 0 there, which is the correct answer.
  19. A texture appears at 0.3 cycles/pixel in an image, and you downsample by 2. What do you see? The new limit is 0.25, so 0.3 folds to |((0.6 + 0.5) mod 1) − 0.5| = 0.4 cycles/sample of the new grid — higher in relative terms than it was. The texture appears finer, not coarser, which is why aliased downsampling often looks sharper.
  20. How would you tell aliasing from genuine detail in an image you were handed? You cannot, from that image alone — that is the content of §14, not a limitation of technique. Evidence comes from elsewhere: the acquisition chain's MTF, a second exposure at a different resolution, or the fact that the "detail" moves incoherently when the camera does. Any method claiming to detect aliasing from a single image is using a prior about natural images, and should say so.
  21. Why is the Gabor filter's magnitude the quantity used in texture features, rather than its response? The even and odd parts of a quadrature pair peak at different phases of the same structure, so a raw response oscillates as the filter slides across a texture. The squared sum is phase-invariant, and it measures presence rather than alignment. Same construction as Part 2 §14's steerable quadrature pair.
  22. You need to align two images that differ by a translation and an overall brightness scale. What is the cheapest robust method? Phase correlation: normalise F·G* by its magnitude and invert. A pure translation gives e^{jω·d} exactly, whose inverse transform is a single spike at d. Discarding the magnitude discards the brightness scale with it, which is precisely why it is robust — and it is §11's lesson used as an algorithm.
  23. The 2-D DFT of a picture of vertical bars: where is the energy? On the horizontal axis of the spectrum, as a symmetric pair of dots. The image varies along x and is constant along y, so ω_y = 0. The spectrum is rotated a quarter turn from the stripes you see, always, and the wavelength is N/|k| pixels.
  24. What is the frequency response of a median filter? It does not have one. A median is not linear, so it does not map a sinusoid to a sinusoid, and §04's whole apparatus is inapplicable. Any measured "response" is a property of the particular image it was measured on. Part 2 §15's non-linear filters are outside this page by construction, not by omission.

Things to be able to derive from a blank page

  • That a convolution maps e^{jωx} to H(ω)·e^{jωx}, by pulling the exponential out of the sum — and hence that the Fourier basis is the eigenbasis of every shift-invariant filter.
  • The transform of a pulse, by integrating e^{−jωx} and applying Euler's identity, and the reciprocal width that falls out of it.
  • The shift property, by substitution, and the corollary that |F| is translation-invariant.
  • The convolution theorem, by swapping two integrals and applying the shift property.
  • That sampling multiplies by a comb, that a comb's transform is a comb with the reciprocal spacing, and hence that sampling replicates the spectrum.
  • The fold rule |((f + ½) mod 1) − ½|, and the fact that f and 1 − f are indistinguishable.
  • The Whittaker–Shannon formula, as "cut out one copy with an ideal low-pass filter", and why the kernel is forced to be a sinc.
  • The radix-2 split, from ω_N² = ω_{N/2} and ω^{k+N/2} = −ω^k, and the recurrence T(N) = 2T(N/2) + N/2.
  • That the DFT matrix satisfies W*W = N·I, and hence that Parseval holds with a 1/N.
  • Hermitian symmetry for real inputs, by conjugating the defining sum.
  • That the Gaussian is its own transform, from the differential equation g′ = −(x/σ²)g and the two properties that turn it into dG/dω = −σ²ωG.
  • That a separable, rotationally symmetric, positive, continuous kernel must be a Gaussian, via Cauchy's functional equation — and the hypotheses that proof needs.
  • The uncertainty inequality, from an integration by parts and one Cauchy–Schwarz, and that its equality case is the Gaussian.
  • That windowing convolves the spectrum with the window's transform, from the multiplication property, and hence what leakage is.
  • Why the DFT product is a circular convolution, and that padding to N + K − 1 repairs it.
  • Why the DCT's mirror extension has no jump while the DFT's periodic extension does, and that this is the entire compression argument.
  • The multi-resolution axioms, and how the refinement equation φ(x) = √2 ∑ h(n) φ(2x − n) follows from V₀ ⊂ V₁.
  • The lifting scheme's predict and update steps, and why reversibility is structural rather than algebraic.
  • That the fast wavelet transform is O(N) from N + N/2 + N/4 + … = 2N.
  • That the wavelet tiling has constant relative bandwidth while the short-time tiling has constant absolute bandwidth, and that both obey the same area bound.
  • The Wiener filter W = 1/(1 + σ_n²/P_s), from a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian likelihood, and why the extra term prevents the explosion.
