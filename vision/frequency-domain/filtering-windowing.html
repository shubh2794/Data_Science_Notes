16 The convolution theorem, derived

This is the result that Part 2 §21 borrowed first and used most. It says that the messy, order-dependent, O(K)-per-pixel operation of convolution becomes an elementwise product. The derivation is one interchange of integrals and one change of variable.

the theoremF{ f ∗ h } = F(ω) · H(ω) and dually F{ f · h } = (1/2π)·(F ∗ H) the proof, in four linesF{ f ∗ h }(ω) = ∫ [ ∫ f(τ)·h(x − τ) dτ ]·e^{−jωx} dx = ∫ f(τ) [ ∫ h(x − τ)·e^{−jωx} dx ] dτ swap the order = ∫ f(τ) [ e^{−jωτ}·H(ω) ] dτ the SHIFT property, §04 = H(ω)·∫ f(τ)·e^{−jωτ} dτ = H(ω)·F(ω) The only step needing care is the swap, which is Fubini's theorem and needs both integrals to converge absolutely — true whenever f and h are integrable. The rest is the shift property and factoring a constant out of an integral. That is all.
The same proof in the discrete case, with the wrinkle that matters. For finite vectors the interchange is a finite double sum and needs no hypotheses at all — but the shift in h(x − τ) now has to mean something at the ends of the array, and the only choice that makes the shift property exact is wrap-around. So the discrete convolution theorem is a statement about circular convolution, always. §17 is entirely about the consequences, and it is where the classic error lives.

What the theorem is actually used for

1 · analysis
Explaining a filter
"Low-pass", "band-pass", "it rings", "it inverts a band" are all statements about H(ω). Every response plotted in Part 2 §12 is H, and every complaint about a kernel is a complaint about the shape of H.
2 · computation
Large-kernel filtering, fast
Transform, multiply, transform back: cost independent of the kernel size. Worth it above about K = 15 for a separable kernel and K = 6 for an unseparable one (§08). Correlation is the same trick with a conjugate, which is how template matching over a whole image is done in one pass.
3 · composition
Reasoning about pipelines
Two filters in series multiply their responses. So blur-then-blur is one blur (variances add, because e^{−σ₁²ω²/2}·e^{−σ₂²ω²/2} = e^{−(σ₁²+σ₂²)ω²/2}), a derivative of a Gaussian is one kernel, and a band-pass is a low-pass minus a lower low-pass. Every algebraic simplification in Part 2 §13 is a product of responses.
4 · inversion
Deconvolution, and why it fails
F = G/H. Where H is small the quotient amplifies noise by 1/H; where H = 0 nothing can be recovered. §27's Wiener filter replaces 1/H with a quotient that stays bounded, and it is the honest version of "sharpen it up".
the variance-addition identity, since it is used constantly and rarely provedG(x; σ₁) ∗ G(x; σ₂) ⟷ e^{−σ₁²ω²/2} · e^{−σ₂²ω²/2} = e^{−(σ₁² + σ₂²)ω²/2} ⟷ G(x; √(σ₁² + σ₂²)) Two Gaussian blurs are one Gaussian blur, and the widths add IN QUADRATURE. In the spatial domain this needs a completed square and a substitution; in frequency it is one line, because exponentials multiply by adding exponents. That asymmetry is the practical case for the transform.

17 Circular versus linear: the error everybody makes once

Multiply two DFTs and invert, and you do not get the convolution you wanted. You get the circular convolution: the one in which the array wraps around, so the last column influences the first. It agrees with the ordinary convolution in the interior and disagrees at the edges by an amount that is not small. This section measures both, on numbers you can check by hand.

the two operations, side by sideLINEAR (f ∗ h)(n) = ∑ₖ f(n − k)·h(k) f taken as ZERO outside its range output length N + K − 1 CIRCULAR (f ⊛ h)(n) = ∑ₖ f((n − k) mod N)·h(k) f taken as PERIODIC output length N F⁻¹{ DFT(f) · DFT(h) } = f ⊛ h — the CIRCULAR one. Always.

Padding to less than that does not.
Said: "I filtered with an FFT and got slightly different numbers from the direct convolution — probably floating point."
Actually: the difference is 1.5 out of a peak of 9 in the example above, and it is confined to a border of width K/2. It is not floating point, it is a different operator. Either pad, or use a padding mode of wrap in the spatial version so that the two genuinely agree — Part 2 §09 lists five border modes and exactly one of them, wrap, is the one the transform implements.
Why the wrap-around is not always wrong. For a texture that really is periodic, or for a filter whose support is tiny relative to the image, or when the border is going to be cropped anyway, the circular result is fine and the padding is wasted work. And for the seamless algorithms — Poisson blending, gradient-domain compositing, periodic texture synthesis — the wrap-around is exactly the boundary condition you want. The rule is not "always pad"; it is "know which operator you invoked".
The 2-D version, and its cost. An M × N image convolved with a K × K kernel needs padding to (M + K − 1) × (N + K − 1), which for a 1024 × 1024 image and a 31 × 31 kernel is 1054 × 1054 — and the FFT would rather have a power of two or a product of small primes, so in practice you pad to 1080 or 1152 or 2048. That rounding is why the crossover in §08 sits higher than the arithmetic alone suggests, and it is why overlap–add and overlap–save exist: split the image into tiles, transform each at a convenient size, and reassemble. The bookkeeping is real and it is the reason a spatial separable convolution stays competitive to surprisingly large kernels.

18 Designing a filter is choosing a shape in two domains at once

A kernel and its frequency response are one object seen twice, and you cannot make one nice without making the other worse. Every filter-design rule of thumb in vision is a consequence of that single constraint, and this section states them as consequences rather than folklore.

19 Why the Gaussian, and only the Gaussian

The Gaussian appears in every part of this series and is usually justified by "it is smooth and it is what everyone uses". It has four genuine, provable properties that nothing else has at once, and this section proves them and then states exactly where each one stops being true in a computer.

Property 1 — it is its own transform, with the width inverted

the cleanest proof: both sides satisfy the same first-order differential equationLet g(x) = e^{−x²/2σ²}. Then g′(x) = −(x/σ²)·g(x) (∗) Transform both sides, using two properties from §04: F{ g′ } = jω·G(ω) differentiation F{ x·g } = j·dG/dω multiplication by x (the dual of differentiation) So (∗) becomes jω·G = −(1/σ²)·j·dG/dω dG/dω = −σ²·ω·G — the SAME equation, in ω, with σ → 1/σ Its solution is G(ω) = G(0)·e^{−σ²ω²/2}, and G(0) = ∫g = σ√(2π). Done. Verified numerically: a Gaussian sampled on a dense grid, transformed by FFT and compared with σ√(2π)·e^{−σ²ω²/2}, agrees to 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 1, 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 2 and 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 3, with the imaginary part below 10⁻¹⁶ throughout.

Property 2 — the transform is positive, so there is no ringing, ever

e^{−σ²ω²/2} > 0 for every ω. A filter whose response never changes sign cannot invert a band; it can only attenuate. Since ringing beside an edge is a band inversion — the negative lobes of the kernel producing an overshoot — a Gaussian blur has no halos, at any width, on any image. Every other smoothing kernel in Part 2 §10 either has this property because it is a self-convolution (tent, binomial) or lacks it (box, windowed sinc).

Property 3 — it is the only separable, rotationally symmetric kernel

the statement, with its hypotheses, which matterLet f: ℝ² → ℝ be CONTINUOUS, STRICTLY POSITIVE, and both SEPARABLE f(x, y) = g(x)·g(y) ROTATIONALLY SYMMETRIC f(x, y) = F(x² + y²) for some F Then g(x) = g(0)·e^{c·x²} for some constant c, and if f is integrable then c < 0 and f is a Gaussian. the proof, which is Cauchy's functional equation in disguiseRotational symmetry with (x, y) rotated onto (√(x²+y²), 0) gives g(x)·g(y) = g(√(x² + y²))·g(0) for all x, y Put φ(t) = log( g(√t) / g(0) ), defined because g > 0. Then φ(x²) + φ(y²) = φ(x² + y²) i.e. φ(u) + φ(v) = φ(u + v) which is Cauchy's equation. With φ continuous the only solutions are φ(u) = c·u. Hence g(x) = g(0)·e^{c x²}, and c < 0 for an integrable kernel. ∎ The hypotheses are not decoration. Drop CONTINUITY and Cauchy's equation admits monstrous non-measurable solutions. Drop POSITIVITY and g may vanish on a set, breaking the logarithm. And note the degenerate members of the family: c = 0 gives the CONSTANT function, which is indeed separable and rotationally symmetric, and a delta is a distributional limit of the family. The theorem is about the shape, not about the existence of exceptions to carelessly stated versions of it.
Checked, as a rank statement. Separability of a sampled kernel means its matrix has rank 1. Measured singular values of three 11 × 11 kernels: the Gaussian at σ = 2 has numerical rank 1 with s₁/s₂ = 1.4 × 10¹⁶; the box has rank 1 too (it is separable, but it is not rotationally symmetric — it is a square); the disc of radius 5 is rotationally symmetric and has numerical rank 4, with s₁/s₂ = 5.1. Exactly one of the three is both, and it is the Gaussian. This is also why Part 2 §11's 2K-instead-of-K² saving is available for a Gaussian blur and not for a disc blur.

Property 4 — it composes with itself, which makes scale space possible

the semigroup property, one line in frequency (§16)G(σ₁) ∗ G(σ₂) = G( √(σ₁² + σ₂²) ) Blurring twice is blurring once. So a pyramid can be built INCREMENTALLY, each level from the one above, and the result is identical to blurring the original once with the total width. No other kernel family in use has this exactly — a box convolved with a box is a tent, not a box. Equivalently: G(x; σ) is the Green's function of the heat equation, and blurring by increasing σ is running diffusion forward in time with t = σ²/2. That is why scale space is a well-posed evolution and why detail can only ever be DESTROYED as scale increases — the second half of that statement is a theorem about the heat equation, not an empirical observation.
Where all four properties stop being exactly true, which no one says.
· Sampling breaks the self-transform. The transform of a sampled Gaussian is not a Gaussian but the periodised one — the sum of shifted copies, by §12. Measured on a 128-point grid: the sampled Gaussian's DFT matches the periodised continuous transform to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ and matches the single continuous Gaussian to only 7.2 × 10⁻³ at σ = 1, 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ at σ = 1.5, and 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁶ at σ = 3. Below about σ = 1.5 a sampled Gaussian is measurably not a Gaussian in frequency.
· Truncation breaks the positivity. The ideal Gaussian's response is positive; a truncated one's is not. Measured: σ = 2 truncated at 3σ dips to −7.6 × 10⁻⁴; truncated at 2σ it dips to −1.5 × 10⁻². So a 2σ-truncated Gaussian can ring, at the 1.5% level. That is small, and it is not zero, and the honest statement of "a Gaussian never rings" carries "if you keep enough of it".
· Renormalising breaks the semigroup slightly. Truncate, renormalise to ∑h = 1, and two blurs no longer compose to exactly √(σ₁² + σ₂²). The error is at the truncation level, so 3σ makes it negligible and 2σ does not.
· And the discrete analogue that is exactly right is the binomial kernel, row n of Pascal's triangle over 2ⁿ: it is exactly separable, exactly non-negative, has H(π) = 0 exactly, composes exactly (binomial(m) ∗ binomial(n) = binomial(m+n)), and converges to the Gaussian. When exactness matters more than the last few per cent of shape, it is the better object, and it is what Part 2 §23's pyramid actually uses.

20 The uncertainty principle: you cannot be sharp in both domains

Every localisation problem in vision — which scale is this feature at, where exactly is this edge, what orientation is this texture — is a request to be precise in space and precise in frequency simultaneously. There is a theorem saying how precise you may be, it is not a practical limitation, and it has an exact constant.

Where this bites, concretely. A filter that responds to a narrow band of frequencies must be spatially wide, so you cannot say both which scale a feature is at and exactly where it is. A Part 4 blob detector localises scale well and position poorly; a corner detector does the reverse. A Gabor filter bank makes the trade explicit through σ: the same filter cannot both separate two nearby orientations and locate the boundary between two textures. And an image pyramid is a decision to sample this trade-off at octave spacing rather than to resolve it.

21 Windowing: the price of looking at a finite piece

Every transform you ever compute is of a finite record, and a finite record is an infinite signal multiplied by a box. By the multiplication property of §04, that multiplication convolves the true spectrum with the box's transform — a sinc — and smears every sharp feature across the whole axis. The smear has a name, a size, and a set of standard remedies.

what taking N samples actually computesobserved(x) = f(x)·w(x) w = the window; a plain truncation is a BOX F{ observed } = (1/2π)·( F ∗ W ) A single true frequency δ(ω − ω₀) therefore appears not as a spike but as a copy of W centred on ω₀. With a box window, W is a sinc — a main lobe of width 2 bins and side lobes at −13.3 dB that decay only as 1/ω. the two things that go wrong, and they are differentSMEARING the main lobe has non-zero width, so two nearby frequencies merge. Cost: resolution. Fixed only by a longer record. LEAKAGE the side lobes put energy at frequencies that are not in the signal. A strong component's side lobes can bury a weak one entirely. Fixed by choosing a smoother window — at the cost of a wider main lobe.

The first trade is real and it is the uncertainty principle of §20 in another costume: going from the rectangle to Hann buys 18.2 dB of side-lobe suppression and costs one whole bin of main-lobe width. But it is not a fixed exchange rate, and the tempting rule "18 dB per bin" is false — Hamming has the same main-lobe width as Hann and a lower peak side lobe, because its coefficients are tuned to cancel the first lobe rather than to make the window smooth. What it pays instead is in the far field:

So the choice is three-way, not two-way — main-lobe width, near side lobes, and far side lobes — and no window wins all three.

There is no window that is narrow and clean, and the choice is a statement about whether you are trying to resolve two close components, detect a weak one just beside a strong one, or detect one a long way from it.

The image version, which is where you have already seen this. A 2-D DFT windows the image with a rectangle — the image boundary. The left edge and the right edge of the implicit tiling almost never match, so there is a discontinuity down the seam, and a discontinuity has energy at every frequency along one axis. That is the bright cross through the middle of most published magnitude spectra.
· Measured, on a smooth diagonal ramp, which has no axis-aligned structure of its own so the entire cross must be the seam: the mean |F| along the two frequency axes beyond |k| = 8 is 13.4128 unwindowed and 0.0162 after a separable Hann window — a reduction of 826× — while the off-axis level is 2 × 10⁻¹⁵, i.e. exactly nothing, both times.
· Measured on a real picture, the running house: 13.0087 on the axes against 1.5439 off them, a ratio of 8.43; after windowing, 4.5248 against 0.7504, a ratio of 6.03. The ratio falls and does not reach 1, because a house genuinely has horizontal and vertical edges. So the honest statement is not "the cross is an artefact" but "the cross is an artefact plus whatever axis-aligned content the picture has, and you cannot separate them by looking".
· And note the second-order effect: a separable window has an axis-concentrated transform of its own, so windowing smears content onto the axes even as it removes the seam. Measuring "energy exactly on the axis lines" therefore rises after windowing on some images; the far tail beyond |k| = 8, where only a discontinuity's 1/k decay reaches, is the measurement that actually isolates the seam.
· If you need to keep the whole image rather than taper it, the standard alternative is periodic-plus-smooth decomposition: split the image into a smooth part that carries the boundary mismatch and a periodic part whose spectrum is clean.
