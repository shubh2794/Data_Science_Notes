02 Images as signals, and the one experiment that defines the transform

Before any integral, there is a measurement you could actually perform with a filter and a ruler. Put a sinusoid in, look at what comes out. The output is a sinusoid of the same frequency, with a different amplitude and a different offset. Tabulate that amplitude and that offset, for every frequency, and you have written down the Fourier transform without integrating anything.

Why the sinusoids and nothing else

A convolution is a weighted sum of shifted copies of the input. Shift a sinusoid and you get another sinusoid at the same frequency; add sinusoids of one frequency and you get one sinusoid at that frequency. So the shape survives. Nothing else has that property: shift a square pulse and add the copies and you get a trapezoid, then a smoother thing, then something else again. The sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of shift-invariant convolution, and §07 makes that word literal.

the experiment, in three linesin: s(x) = sin(ωx + φᵢ) out: o(x) = (h ∗ s)(x) = A·sin(ωx + φₒ) SAME ω, different A and φ read: A = the GAIN of h at ω; Δφ = φₒ − φᵢ = the PHASE SHIFT of h at ω the same experiment with a complex probe, which removes the bookkeepings(x) = e^{jωx} = cos ωx + j·sin ωx o(x) = h ∗ s = A·e^{j(ωx + φ)} = ( A·e^{jφ} )·e^{jωx} └──── one complex number ────┘ so the transform is simply the table of those numbersH(ω) = F{ h }(ω) = A(ω)·e^{jφ(ω)} |H| = gain, arg H = phase shift

The complex probe is not mysticism. A real sinusoid is two complex exponentials, cos ωx = (e^{jωx} + e^{−jωx})/2, and carrying them separately means the gain and the shift come out as one complex number rather than two real ones that have to be matched up. The imaginary unit is bookkeeping, and §06 shows the bookkeeping being unwound at the end of every real computation.

This is a definition you can compute with, right now. Part 2 plotted a frequency response for every filter it built and never used an FFT to do it: it summed H(ω) = ∑ₖ h(k)·e^{−jωk} directly over the taps, which is exactly "convolve with a sinusoid and read the amplitude", done algebraically. That is Part 2 §12's whole apparatus, and it is one line, because a filter has a handful of taps. The machinery of §06–§08 exists because an image has a million samples, not because the definition needs it.

What "frequency" means for a picture

In 1-D, a frequency is a number. In 2-D it is a vector (ω_x, ω_y), and the corresponding basis function is a plane wave — a corrugation, constant along one direction and sinusoidal along the perpendicular one.

the 2-D basis function: a corrugation with an orientation and a wavelengths(x, y) = sin(ω_x·x + ω_y·y) = sin( ω · p ) p = (x, y), ω = (ω_x, ω_y) direction of variation : ω/|ω| the corrugation's normal wavelength : 2π/|ω| crest-to-crest, in pixels orientation in the plot: atan2(ω_y, ω_x) ROTATED 90° from the stripes you see

That last line catches everyone once. A picture of vertical stripes varies along x and is constant along y, so its energy sits on the ω_x axis — a horizontal pair of dots in the spectrum. The spectrum is rotated a quarter turn from the thing you are looking at, always.

The defining experiment: a sinusoid in, a scaled and shifted sinusoid out change the frequency and watch only two numbers change — the gain and the shift

03 The continuous Fourier transform, and the pulse that generates everything

The experiment of §02 is a recipe, not a formula. The formula comes from asking how much of e^{jωx} a signal contains, and answering it the way you always answer "how much of this basis vector is in that vector" — with an inner product. In infinite dimensions the inner product is an integral.

the forward transform — an inner product against the conjugate basis functionF(ω) = F{ f }(ω) = ∫ f(x)·e^{−jωx} dx over x from −∞ to ∞ the inverse — the same sum, run the other way, with the 2π that ω costsf(x) = F⁻¹{ F }(x) = (1/2π)·∫ F(ω)·e^{+jωx} dω over ω from −∞ to ∞ the same pair in ordinary frequency, where the 2π disappears entirelyF(f) = ∫ f(x)·e^{−j2πfx} dx f(x) = ∫ F(f)·e^{+j2πfx} df THREE conventions are in common use and they differ only in where the 2π lands: in front of the inverse (used above), split as 1/√(2π) on both, or absent because the exponent carries 2πf. Nothing depends on the choice except the constants in Parseval's theorem, and every formula on this page says which convention it is in.

The pulse, computed rather than quoted

One worked integral is enough to generate half the transform pairs in §05, because everything else follows from it by the properties in §04. Take a pulse of height A from −T/2 to T/2 and zero elsewhere.

the pulse, integrated directlyF(ω) = ∫ A·e^{−jωx} dx over x from −T/2 to T/2 = A·[ e^{−jωx} / (−jω) ] evaluated at ±T/2 = A·( e^{+jωT/2} − e^{−jωT/2} ) / (jω) and now Euler's identity, sin θ = ( e^{jθ} − e^{−jθ} ) / 2j, collapses itF(ω) = (2A/ω)·sin(ωT/2) = A·T·sinc(ωT/2) where sinc(u) = sin(u)/u F(0) = A·T the area, as it must be

Three things to read off it, and they will be used repeatedly. One: F(0) is the signal's integral — the DC term is always the mean, in every version of the transform. Two: the first zero is at ωT/2 = π, i.e. ω = 2π/T — narrow pulse, wide transform, and the product of the two widths does not depend on T. That is the uncertainty principle of §20 in its crudest form. Three: the sinc has infinitely many side lobes, alternating in sign, decaying only as 1/ω. That slow decay and that sign change are the entire indictment of the box filter, and Part 2 §10 spent two figures on their consequences without being allowed to say why.

Which functions have a transform, honestly. The integral converges absolutely when ∫|f| < ∞, which excludes a constant, a sinusoid, and an impulse — the three things every treatment uses on the first page. The repair is not a hedge: the transform is extended to tempered distributions, in which F{1} = 2π·δ(ω) and F{δ} = 1 are theorems rather than abuses. Everything on this page is either absolutely integrable, or a distribution whose transform is one of the four standard ones, or a finite vector, where the question does not arise at all. The convergence machinery is Calculus's; the practical statement is that no signal you will meet in a computer causes trouble, because it is finite and bounded.
Fourier series, and why it is the same theorem. If f is periodic with period L, only the frequencies ω_k = 2πk/L can appear, and the integral becomes a sum: f(x) = ∑ₖ c_k·e^{j2πkx/L} with c_k = (1/L)∫ f(x)·e^{−j2πkx/L} dx. Periodic in space ⇒ discrete in frequency. §12 shows the dual: discrete in space ⇒ periodic in frequency. The DFT of §06 is both, which is why it is a finite object. Gibbs' phenomenon lives here: truncating the series of a discontinuous function at any order leaves an overshoot at the jump that does not shrink — measured on a square wave, the partial sums peak at 1.17860 for 501 terms against a limit of (2/π)·Si(π) = 1.178980, an overshoot of 8.949% of the total jump, forever. That is ringing, and §18 shows the same number appearing when a filter has a sharp edge in frequency instead of in space.

04 The properties, each with the one-line reason it is true

Nine properties do all the work. Every one of them is a change of variable inside the defining integral, and none takes more than two lines. They are worth deriving once rather than memorising, because the derivation is always the same move.

PropertySignal → transformWhy, and what it is used for here
Linearitya·f + b·g → a·F + b·GThe integral is linear. Used constantly and never remarked on: it is what lets a signal be decomposed at all.
Shiftf(x − x₀) → F(ω)·e^{−jωx₀}Substitute u = x − x₀; the extra factor e^{−jωx₀} comes out of the integral. The magnitude is unchanged — |e^{−jωx₀}| = 1 — so a translated image has an identical magnitude spectrum and a completely different phase. §10 and §11 are about that sentence.
Modulationf(x)·e^{jω₀x} → F(ω − ω₀)The dual of shift: multiplying by a complex sinusoid in space translates the spectrum. This is how §09's spectrum gets recentred and how the Gabor filter of §22 is built.
Reversalf(−x) → F*(ω)Substitute u = −x, which flips the sign in the exponent, which conjugates. Combined with the next row this is why correlation and convolution differ by a conjugate.
Convolutionf ∗ h → F·HThe theorem the page is built on. Derived in full in §16 rather than asserted here.
Correlationf ⊗ h → F·H*Correlation is convolution with a reflected kernel, and reflection conjugates. So the two agree exactly when H is real, i.e. when h is symmetric — the same condition Part 2 §08 found by a different route.
Multiplicationf·h → (1/2π)·(F ∗ H)The dual of convolution, and the reason §21's windowing smears a spectrum: multiplying by a window in space convolves the spectrum with the window's transform.
Differentiationf′(x) → jω·F(ω)Integrate by parts once; the boundary term vanishes for decaying f. Differentiation multiplies by ω, so it amplifies high frequencies linearly — which is the entire noise problem of Part 2 §13, stated as one factor.
Scalingf(a·x) → (1/|a|)·F(ω/a)Substitute u = ax. Squeeze in space, stretch in frequency, and the area is conserved. This is the formal version of "small things have big spectra", and it is what Part 2 §28's resampling-rate argument is really using.
Hermitian symmetryf real ⟺ F(−ω) = F*(ω)Conjugating the integral flips the sign of ω. So a real image's spectrum is redundant: half of it is a mirror-conjugate of the other half, the magnitude is even and the phase is odd. Every real FFT implementation exploits this for a factor of two in time and space.
Parseval / Plancherel∫|f|² dx = (1/2π)·∫|F|² dωEnergy is conserved by the transform — it is a unitary map, a rotation of the signal space. Everything that later says "keep the biggest coefficients" is standing on this: coefficient energy is signal energy, so discarding a small coefficient causes a small error, measurable in advance.
shift, derived, because it is the one that surprises peopleF{ f(x − x₀) } = ∫ f(x − x₀)·e^{−jωx} dx put u = x − x₀, x = u + x₀ = ∫ f(u)·e^{−jω(u + x₀)} du = e^{−jωx₀}·∫ f(u)·e^{−jωu} du = e^{−jωx₀}·F(ω) and therefore, since |e^{−jωx₀}| = 1 exactly| F{ f(x − x₀) } | = | F(ω) | for EVERY shift x₀ The magnitude spectrum is shift-INVARIANT. Move the object anywhere in the frame and the magnitude picture is bit-for-bit identical. All positional information — where the edges are, which way the roof slopes, where the eyes sit — is in the PHASE, and only in the phase. §11 is the demonstration.
Parseval, checked. On the running signal f = [3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6] the two sides of the discrete Parseval relation ∑ₙ|f(n)|² = (1/N)·∑ₖ|F(k)|² agree to the last bit; on a random complex vector of length 32 the relative difference measured 0.0 — not "small", exactly zero, because both sides are the same sum of products reassociated. That is the sanity check every transform implementation should carry, and it catches a wrong normalisation instantly.
The property that is not on the list. There is no useful statement about the transform of max(f, g), or of a median, or of a threshold. The whole apparatus applies to linear operators and to nothing else. Part 2 §15–§17's median, bilateral filter and morphology have no frequency response — not one that is hard to compute, one that does not exist, because those operators do not map a sinusoid to a sinusoid. When a paper reports "the frequency response of a bilateral filter" it means the response measured on one particular image, which is a description of that image as much as of the filter.

05 The transform pairs worth knowing by heart

Ten pairs cover essentially every filter in vision. Each is derived from the pulse of §03 plus a property from §04 — none needs a fresh integral — and each one explains a behaviour that Part 2 had to describe empirically.

SignalTransformWhat it explains
impulse
δ(x)		1Flat, over all frequencies. An impulse contains every frequency in equal amount — which is why the impulse response is the filter, and why probing with an impulse recovers the kernel exactly. Part 2 §08 used this to tell correlation from convolution.
shifted impulse
δ(x − x₀)		e^{−jωx₀}Unit magnitude, linear phase. A pure delay changes nothing but the phase, and changes it by an amount proportional to frequency. Linear phase = no shape distortion, which is why symmetric kernels are the ones you can trust.
constant
1		2π·δ(ω)The dual of the impulse. A flat field is pure DC. It also says that the mean of an image occupies exactly one coefficient, and that removing it is a one-sample edit.
cosine
cos(ω₀x)		π[δ(ω−ω₀) + δ(ω+ω₀)]Two spikes, symmetric about zero. A real sinusoid always appears twice. Half of every spectrum you look at is the mirror image of the other half — §04's Hermitian symmetry, made visible.
box
box(x/T)		T·sinc(ωT/2)Derived in §03. Infinite sidelobes, alternating sign, 1/ω decay. The box filter inverts the bands where the sinc is negative and never really removes the high ones. Measured: box-3's response reaches −0.3333 at ω = π, box-5 dips to −0.2500, box-9 to −0.2266 — the first sidelobe does not shrink with kernel size.
sinc
sinc(x)		box(ω)The same pair read backwards, by the near-symmetry of the transform. The ideal low-pass filter — a perfect brick wall in frequency — is a sinc in space: infinitely wide, and it rings. There is no free lunch and this pair is where it is priced.
tent
max(0, 1 − |x|)		sinc²(ω/2)A tent is a box convolved with itself, so its transform is a sinc squared — non-negative everywhere, decaying as 1/ω². One convolution buys you the sign and one extra order of decay. This is why Part 2 §27's linear interpolation is so much better behaved than nearest-neighbour.
Gaussian
G(x; σ)		e^{−σ²ω²/2} = G(ω; 1/σ)Its own transform, with the width inverted. Positive everywhere, so no sidelobes and no ringing, ever. §19 proves it and §20 shows it is the unique minimiser of the space–frequency uncertainty product.
Laplacian of Gaussian
(x²/σ⁴ − 1/σ²)·G(x;σ)		−ω²·e^{−σ²ω²/2} × constTwo derivatives is (jω)² = −ω², times a Gaussian. The product is a band-pass response: zero at DC, zero at infinity, one peak in between at ω = √2/σ. That peak is the "scale" a LoG selects, and it is why Part 2 §13's blob detector has a preferred size.
Gabor
cos(ω₀x)·G(x;σ)		½[G(ω−ω₀;1/σ) + G(ω+ω₀;1/σ)]Modulation (§04) applied to the Gaussian: two Gaussian bumps centred on ±ω₀. Narrow, tunable, non-negative — the most frequency-selective filter you can build at a given spatial width, by §20. §22 builds the whole time–frequency picture out of it.
windowed sinc
sinc(x/a)·w(x)		box ∗ WBy the multiplication property: truncating a sinc convolves the brick wall with the window's transform, rounding its corners and raising sidelobes. Add lobes and the wall gets sharper. That trade is §21, and it is the same trade Part 2 §27's Lanczos kernel is making.
The derivative filter's phase, which the tables usually hide. The central difference [−1, 0, 1]/2 has response −j·sin ω under the convolution convention: purely imaginary. Its magnitude |sin ω| is what gets plotted, but the interesting part is the −j, which is a 90° phase shift at every frequency — exactly what the differentiation property jω demands, since a derivative turns a cosine into a sine. Numerically, the closed form −j·sin ω matches a direct sum over the taps to 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶; the sign flips if you switch to the correlation convention, which is the trap.
