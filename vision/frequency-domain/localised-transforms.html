22 Localising in both domains: the windowed transform and the Gabor filter

A Fourier coefficient is a statement about the entire image. Images are not stationary — the sky and the grass have nothing in common — so a global spectrum describes an average that occurs nowhere. The obvious fix is to transform a window at a time, and the obvious fix has one parameter that cannot be chosen well.

the short-time (windowed) Fourier transformS(x₀, ω) = ∫ f(x)·w(x − x₀)·e^{−jωx} dx A two-parameter family: WHERE (x₀) and WHAT FREQUENCY (ω). The window w has a fixed width, and that width is chosen ONCE for the whole analysis. the same thing read as a filter bank, which is more useful in visionS(x₀, ω) = ( f ∗ g_ω )(x₀) with g_ω(x) = w(−x)·e^{+jωx} Each frequency's slice is the image CONVOLVED with a modulated window. With a Gaussian window that kernel is the GABOR filter of §05: a Gaussian envelope times a complex sinusoid, whose transform is a Gaussian bump at ±ω₀. the 2-D version used for texture and orientationg(x, y) = e^{−(x² + y²)/2σ²} · e^{ j·2πf₀·(x·cosθ + y·sinθ) } four free parameters: σ (extent), f₀ (frequency), θ (orientation), and the aspect ratio if the envelope is elliptical. Its REAL part is an even filter, its IMAGINARY part an odd one, and the magnitude of the complex response is a PHASE-INVARIANT measure of "how much of this frequency, at this orientation, here".
Why the magnitude of a complex Gabor response is the useful quantity. The even and odd parts respond maximally at different phases of the same texture — the even one peaks on a bar, the odd one on its edge. Their squared sum, the oriented energy, is independent of that phase, so it measures the presence of the structure rather than its alignment with the filter. That is exactly the quadrature-pair construction Part 2 §14 built with steerable filters, arrived at from the frequency side; and it is why oriented-energy features are stable where raw filter responses oscillate.

The one parameter that cannot be chosen

The window width sets the resolution in both domains, by §20, and it is fixed across the whole transform. A narrow window locates events precisely and cannot distinguish nearby frequencies; a wide one does the reverse. Since an image contains both a sharp edge (needs a narrow window) and a slow gradient (needs a wide one), no single choice is right for the whole image. That is not a tuning difficulty; it is a structural limitation of a fixed grid, and it is precisely what the wavelet transform of §24–§26 is built to escape.

The short-time transform: one window width, applied everywhere no width works for both halves of the signal — that is the point, not a bug

23 The DCT: why compression does not use the DFT

Every JPEG, every MPEG frame, every H.264 block is a discrete cosine transform, not a Fourier transform. The reason is not that cosines are cheaper — a DCT is computed through an FFT — but that the DFT makes a specific, avoidable mistake at the edges of a block, and the DCT does not.

the DCT-II, orthonormal, which is the one everything usesF(k) = α(k)·∑_{n=0}^{N−1} f(n)·cos( π(n + ½)k / N ) α(0) = √(1/N), α(k) = √(2/N) for k > 0 inverse: f(n) = ∑_{k=0}^{N−1} α(k)·F(k)·cos( π(n + ½)k / N ) The half-sample offset (n + ½) is what makes the basis symmetric about the block's EDGES rather than about its samples. The real reason: what each transform assumes happens outside the block

extend the block [a b c d] beyond its ends — the transform's implicit assumptionDFT assumes PERIODIC extension: … a b c d | a b c d | a b c d … ↑ a JUMP from d back to a DCT assumes EVEN (mirror) extension: … c b a | a b c d | d c b a … ↑ CONTINUOUS: a meets a, d meets d A photographic block is a smooth gradient far more often than it is periodic. The DFT's periodic extension manufactures a step at every block boundary, that step needs every frequency, and the coefficients that should have been zero are not. The DCT's mirror extension has no jump — only a kink — so its coefficients decay much faster. That is the whole argument, and it is geometric rather than algebraic.

The ramp is the worst case for a periodic extension — a jump of 70 between the last sample and the first — and blocks of a smooth photograph are ramps far more often than they are anything else.
Two more reasons, both real. It is real-valued: N real inputs give N real outputs, with no conjugate-symmetric half to store or to get wrong, and quantisation acts on real numbers directly. And it is close to optimal: for a signal modelled as a first-order Markov process with high correlation — which is a decent model of a photographic row — the DCT basis converges to the Karhunen–Loève basis, i.e. to the principal components of that process. So it decorrelates nearly as well as the data-optimal transform while being fixed, fast and separable. "Nearly optimal, and you do not have to send the basis" is the whole design.
What the DCT gives up. There is no convolution theorem for it: multiplying two DCTs and inverting does not convolve the signals, because the mirror extension is not a group. So the DCT is a coding transform, not a filtering one — and the block structure that makes it work is also the source of the blocking artefacts in an over-compressed image, since each 8 × 8 block is quantised independently and its reconstruction no longer agrees with its neighbour's at the shared edge. Lapped and overlapped variants exist precisely to fix that, and so do the wavelet codecs of §24.

24 Wavelets: multi-resolution analysis, properly

Part 2 §25 gave the sub-band picture and pointed here for the theory. Here it is. A wavelet basis is not "a Fourier transform with a window"; it is a nested sequence of approximation spaces, and everything else — the filters, the pyramid, the lifting scheme — is a consequence of that nesting.

the axioms of a multi-resolution analysisA ladder of closed subspaces of L²(ℝ): … ⊂ V₋₁ ⊂ V₀ ⊂ V₁ ⊂ V₂ ⊂ … each Vⱼ is "signals at resolution 2^j" 1. NESTED Vⱼ ⊂ V_{j+1} a coarse signal is a fine one 2. DENSE closure( ∪ Vⱼ ) = L² fine enough captures everything 3. TRIVIAL ∩ Vⱼ = {0} coarse enough captures nothing 4. SCALING f(x) ∈ Vⱼ ⟺ f(2x) ∈ V_{j+1} the ladder is SELF-SIMILAR 5. SHIFT f(x) ∈ V₀ ⟺ f(x − n) ∈ V₀ for integer n 6. THAT is the decomposition; the filters below are the way to compute it. the two-scale relation, which forces the filtersBecause φ ∈ V₀ ⊂ V₁ and V₁ is spanned by φ(2x − n), the scaling function must satisfy φ(x) = √2 · ∑ₙ h(n)·φ(2x − n) the REFINEMENT equation ψ(x) = √2 · ∑ₙ g(n)·φ(2x − n) with g(n) = (−1)ⁿ·h(1 − n) The filters h and g are not chosen and then found to work: they are DETERMINED by the demand that the spaces nest. Everything a wavelet family is — its smoothness, its support, its vanishing moments — is a property of the sequence h.

Wavelets and the Fourier transform are answering different questions. The Fourier basis has perfect frequency localisation and none in space; the standard basis (pixels) has the reverse. A wavelet basis is a specific, structured compromise between them — and unlike the short-time transform of §22, the compromise is different at every scale: wide in space and narrow in frequency for the coarse levels, narrow in space and wide in frequency for the fine ones. §26 draws that as a tiling and it is the clearest single picture of the difference.

25 Lifting: building a wavelet transform without designing a filter

Designing a filter pair that is simultaneously finite, smooth, orthogonal and exactly invertible is a research problem. The lifting scheme sidesteps it entirely by making invertibility structural: every step modifies one half of the samples using only the other half, so undoing it is a matter of running the same arithmetic with the signs flipped.

the three stages, forwardSPLIT even(n) = f(2n) odd(n) = f(2n+1) no arithmetic at all PREDICT d(n) = odd(n) − P( even ) the odd sample's SURPRISE UPDATE s(n) = even(n) + U( d ) put back what the split lost and backward, which needs no algebra whatsoeverUNDO UPDATE even(n) = s(n) − U( d ) same U, sign flipped UNDO PREDICT odd(n) = d(n) + P( even ) same P, sign flipped MERGE f(2n) = even(n), f(2n+1) = odd(n) Invertibility does not depend on P or U being anything in particular. Choose them badly and the transform is useless; it is still EXACTLY invertible. That is the whole trick, and it holds in integer arithmetic with rounding — which is how LOSSLESS image compression is built. the CDF 5/3 pair, which is JPEG 2000's reversible modePREDICT d(n) = odd(n) − ½·( even(n) + even(n+1) ) UPDATE s(n) = even(n) + ¼·( d(n−1) + d(n) ) the filters that produces — MEASURED by feeding it impulses, not derivedlow-pass (row of the analysis matrix for s): [ −1/8, 1/4, 3/4, 1/4, −1/8 ] ∑ = 1 high-pass (row of the analysis matrix for d): [ −1/2, 1, −1/2 ] ∑ = 0 Both read off directly by transforming a unit impulse at each position and recording the coefficient. Five taps and three taps: the "5/3" in the name.
Two vanishing moments, verified. Feed the 5/3 transform a linear ramp f(n) = 3n + 7 on 33 samples with whole-sample mirror extension, one level: every detail coefficient is exactly 0, all sixteen of them, including at the boundaries. Run three levels on 256 samples and 221 of 224 are exactly zero — the three survivors are at the boundaries of the shortened coarser levels, where the mirror extension of an already-truncated ramp is no longer a ramp. Feed it a quadratic and the detail coefficients are a non-zero constant of magnitude 1. So the high-pass filter annihilates constants and ramps and nothing beyond — exactly two vanishing moments. That is the whole compression argument in one experiment: a smooth image is locally a ramp, so its detail bands are nearly all zero, so they cost almost nothing to store.
Round trip, verified. 32 random samples through the forward lifting and back: max |f − reconstruction| = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. Haar on 16 random samples: 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁶, with ∑f² = ∑L² + ∑H² exactly. Both are round-off. Note the difference in kind, though: Haar is orthonormal so it preserves energy exactly, while the 5/3 pair is biorthogonal and does not — it is symmetric and short instead, and symmetry is worth more than orthogonality for images because a symmetric filter has linear phase and does not displace edges.

Why the recursion is O(N), not O(N log N)

the cost, summed over levelsOne level on N samples: a constant number of operations per sample, so c·N. Recurse on the LOW half only: c·N + c·N/2 + c·N/4 + … = 2·c·N The fast wavelet transform is LINEAR in the number of samples — faster than the FFT's N log N — because each level halves the work rather than repeating it. In 2-D the series is 1 + ¼ + 1/16 + … = 4/3, so a full 2-D decomposition costs 4/3 of one filtering pass, and it produces EXACTLY N coefficients (Part 2 §25).

26 The tiling: what actually distinguishes a wavelet from a windowed transform

Both the short-time transform and the wavelet transform partition the space–frequency plane into cells. By §20 every cell must have area at least ½. The difference between the two methods is entirely in the shape of the cells — and that difference decides which signals each one describes well.

four ways to tile the plane, and what each one isPIXELS / SAMPLES tall, thin columns perfect position, no frequency information at all FOURIER wide, flat rows perfect frequency, no position information at all SHORT-TIME (§22) a UNIFORM grid of identical rectangles one compromise, chosen once, applied everywhere WAVELET rectangles that get SHORTER AND WIDER as frequency rises Δω/ω is CONSTANT: octave bands, i.e. constant RELATIVE bandwidth every cell still has the same AREA — nothing is gained for free, the shape is merely reallocated

Why the wavelet's shape is the right one for images

A high-frequency event in an image is an edge, and an edge is spatially compact — so the analysis of high frequencies should be spatially compact too. A low-frequency event is an illumination gradient across the whole picture, and it needs no spatial precision at all. The wavelet tiling gives short, wide cells at high frequency and long, narrow ones at low frequency, which is exactly that allocation. The short-time transform gives the same rectangle to both, and therefore mis-serves both.

constant relative bandwidth, statedSTFT: Δω = constant absolute bandwidth fixed WAVELET: Δω/ω = constant RELATIVE bandwidth fixed ⇒ octave bands At scale j the wavelet ψ(2^j·x − n) has spatial width ∝ 2^{−j} frequency width ∝ 2^{j} centre frequency ∝ 2^{j} so Δω/ω is INDEPENDENT of j and the product of the two widths is constant — the uncertainty area is preserved at every scale. A wavelet transform does not beat §20; it spends its budget differently at different frequencies.
The cost, stated plainly. A critically sampled wavelet transform is badly shift-variant: move the input one pixel and energy moves between sub-bands, because each level is subsampled. That is fine for compression, where the image is not going to move, and disqualifying for matching, where it is. The fix is to give up critical sampling — an undecimated (à trous) transform, or a steerable pyramid, both overcomplete, both approximately shift-invariant, both useless for compression. This is the same overcomplete-versus-critical trade that Part 2 §23 found between the Laplacian pyramid and the wavelet, arrived at from the other direction.
