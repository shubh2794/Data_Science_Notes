Image Classification

Give the network a whole image and ask for one label. It is the simplest vision task to state and the one the field was built on. Almost every backbone you will meet, from CNNs to Vision Transformers, was first judged by its ImageNet top-1 accuracy. The pretrained classifier then became the starting point for detection, segmentation and nearly everything else. This page covers the objective, the benchmarks, the forty-year arc of architectures, the training tricks that now matter as much as the architecture, and the things a single accuracy number hides: calibration, per-class failure and distribution shift.

01 The task: one image in, one distribution over labels out

A classifier is a function f: ℝ^(H×W×3) → Δ^(K−1). It maps an image to a probability vector over K fixed classes. Every architecture on this page is a different way of building f, and every training trick is a different way of choosing it.

pixels x→backbone (conv / attention)→feature map→global pool or [CLS]→vector h ∈ ℝᵈ→linear head W h + b→logits z ∈ ℝᴷ→softmax→p
  • The backbone does almost all the work, and the head is linear. Everything before the last layer is a learned feature extractor. The last layer is a plain multinomial logistic regression on those features. That split is why a classifier transfers so well: you keep the extractor and swap the head (§08).
  • The spatial axes have to disappear somewhere. A CNN collapses its final h×w×d map with global average pooling. A ViT reads a [CLS] token or averages its patch tokens. Either way the label is invariant to where the object sits, which is what classification wants and exactly what detection and segmentation must undo.
  • The assumption that breaks first is "one object per image". Many ImageNet photos contain several valid classes. When the labels allow several at once, the problem is multi-label (§09), and softmax is the wrong output.
Where the neighbouring material lives. The convolution itself, pooling and receptive fields are on Convolutional Networks and Conv Arithmetic & Receptive Fields. Patch embedding and the encoder are on Vision Transformers. Specific model cards are on Vision Models, EfficientNet and CLIP. This page is about the task: what is optimised, how it is scored, and what goes wrong.

02 Softmax cross-entropy, and the gradient it actually sends

The objective is the negative log-likelihood of the true class under a categorical distribution whose logits the network produces. It is worth knowing in three forms: the loss, the loss as a divergence from a target, and its gradient. The gradient is the one that explains label smoothing, temperature and calibration.

softmax with temperature T, and the cross-entropy against a target distribution qpₖ = exp(zₖ/T) / ∑ⱼ exp(zⱼ/T) L(z, q) = −∑ₖ qₖ · log pₖ = H(q) + KL(q ‖ p) one-hot target (q = e_y): the familiar formL = −log p_y = −z_y/T + log ∑ⱼ exp(zⱼ/T) the gradient w.r.t. the logits, for any target q∂L/∂zₖ = (pₖ − qₖ) / T
  • "Probability minus target" is the whole learning signal. The true class is pushed up by 1 − p_y. Every wrong class is pushed down by its own probability, so the most confusable classes get the strongest push. That is the logic of Losses & Backpropagation, specialised to a K-way head.
  • With a one-hot target the loss has no minimum, only an infimum. −log p_y → 0 needs z_y − zⱼ → ∞. Once the training set is fitted, the network can keep lowering the loss by scaling its logits up without changing a single prediction. That runaway is the root cause of the over-confidence in §11.
  • The H(q) term is a floor you cannot go below. With a soft target (label smoothing, mixup, distillation) the loss bottoms out at the target's entropy, not at zero. Only the KL part measures fit, so comparing raw losses across smoothing settings is meaningless.
Five logits, one target, and the gradient that the head receives drag the logit bars; then raise ε and watch the target stop being one-hot
  • At the default (cat true, ε = 0, T = 1) the model gives cat p = 0.585, the loss is 0.536 and the gradient on the cat logit is −0.415. Drag the cat bar up and the loss keeps falling with no end in sight.
  • Set ε = 0.10. The target becomes (0.92, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02), the floor H(q) rises to 0.390, and the gradient on truck flips sign to −0.004. Truck is now below its target of 0.02, so the loss pulls it back up. Label smoothing sets a finite best gap of log(0.92/0.02) = 3.83 nats between the true logit and every other one. Past that gap, pushing further raises the loss.
  • Temperature changes p without changing the argmax. At T = 0.5 cat reaches 0.807; at T = 2 it drops to 0.402. The predicted class is the same in both. This is the single fact that makes temperature scaling (§11) safe to apply after training.

03 Datasets and benchmarks: what "ImageNet top-1" does and does not mean

The field's progress is mostly written in one number: top-1 accuracy on the 50 000-image validation set of the ILSVRC-2012 subset of ImageNet (1000 classes, about 1.28 M training images). It is worth knowing what that number measures before trusting a 0.3-point gain.

top-k accuracy over a labelled set of n imagestop-k = (1/n) ∑ᵢ 𝟙[ yᵢ ∈ the k classes with the largest pᵢ ] top-1 is plain accuracy; top-5 forgives the model for ranking the right answer anywhere in its first five
DatasetScaleWhat it is used for
MNIST / Fashion-MNIST70 k grey 28×28, 10 classesSmoke tests and teaching. Solved; differences between methods are noise.
CIFAR-10 / CIFAR-10060 k colour 32×32, 10 / 100 classesFast ablations of augmentation and regularisation. Low resolution, so results do not always carry over to large images.
ImageNet-1k (ILSVRC-2012)≈1.28 M train · 50 k val · 1000 classesThe standard benchmark and the standard pretraining set. Fine-grained in places (over a hundred dog breeds), coarse elsewhere.
ImageNet-21k, JFT, web image–text pairs14 M to billionsPretraining only. ViTs need this scale to beat CNNs (§06), and CLIP replaces class labels with captions entirely.
ImageNet-V2, -C, -R, -Sketch, ObjectNettest sets onlyMeasuring robustness: how much accuracy survives a new sample, a corruption, a rendition or a pose change (§12).
The trap. Reading top-1 as "fraction of images understood". It is the agreement with one annotator's single label per image, and that label is sometimes wrong and often incomplete. Re-annotation studies of the ImageNet validation set found a few percent of the labels to be errors, and many images contain more than one valid class. Near the top of the leaderboard, some "mistakes" are correct answers to a question the label did not ask. Multi-label re-annotations and top-5 exist to soften exactly this.

04 Before deep learning: hand-crafted features and linear classifiers

Until 2012 the pipeline had the same shape as today's: a feature extractor followed by a linear classifier. The extractor was designed by hand instead of learned, and that one difference accounts for most of the accuracy gap.

image→dense SIFT / HOG patches→encode against a visual vocabulary→spatial-pyramid pooling→linear SVM
  • Local descriptors such as SIFT and HOG are histograms of oriented gradients over small cells. They are built to be invariant to illumination and small shifts, as Features & Matching derives. They are good at describing a patch and know nothing about what "dog" means.
  • Bag of visual words clusters the descriptors with k-means and represents an image as a histogram of cluster assignments. Fisher vectors and VLAD keep first- and second-order statistics of the residuals to each cluster instead of counts, which made the strongest pre-2012 ImageNet entries.
  • Spatial pyramids pool the codes over a 1×1, 2×2 and 4×4 grid and concatenate, bringing back coarse layout. This is the hand-built ancestor of the pooling hierarchy a CNN learns.
  • The classifier was a linear SVM because the encoded vectors were huge (tens of thousands of dimensions) and kernels did not scale. The best ImageNet systems of 2010–2011 sat at roughly 26–28% top-5 error.
What survived. The overall structure: local filtering, non-linear encoding, pooling, a linear readout. A CNN is that pipeline with every stage learned end to end by gradient descent. The intuitions behind the hand-designed filters are on Image Processing and the Classical Vision hub, and they are still the best way to understand what a network's first layer converges to.

05 The CNN arc: LeNet → AlexNet → VGG → ResNet → EfficientNet

Each step in the lineage removed one obstacle to going deeper or wider. Read the table for what the step unlocked, not just for the accuracy column.

ModelYearSizeImageNetWhat it changed

  • Two biases did the heavy lifting: locality and weight sharing. They make a CNN translation-equivariant and very sample-efficient, which is why it beat everything on 1.28 M images. See Convolutional Networks for the argument in full.
  • Not every gain in the table is architectural. Later re-trainings of a plain ResNet-50 with a modern recipe (long schedules, the augmentation of §07, label smoothing, cosine learning rate) add several top-1 points with no change to the network. Compare architectures only under the same recipe.

06 Transformers and language supervision: ViT and CLIP zero-shot

Two later shifts changed what a classifier is. The ViT dropped convolution's built-in assumptions and let scale supply them. CLIP dropped the fixed label set, so the class list becomes an input at test time.

Vision Transformers

A ViT cuts the image into 16×16 patches, embeds each as a token, adds position embeddings and runs a standard Transformer encoder. The classifier reads the [CLS] token. With weak inductive bias it loses to ResNets when trained on ImageNet-1k alone. It wins once it is pretrained on ImageNet-21k or larger (ViT-H/14 reached about 88.5% top-1 after JFT-300M pretraining). DeiT closed much of the small-data gap with strong augmentation and distillation, and convolutional designs modernised in response (ConvNeXt). The comparison is laid out on ViT vs CNN.

CLIP: the label set becomes a prompt

zero-shot classification with a contrastively trained image encoder f and text encoder gtₖ = g("a photo of a {classₖ}.") / ‖·‖ one text embedding per class name u = f(x) / ‖f(x)‖ p(k | x) = softmax_k( τ · uᵀtₖ ) τ is the learned logit scale the text embeddings are the rows of a linear head W that nobody trained on this label set
  • A classifier you never trained. CLIP's largest model reaches about 76% ImageNet top-1 zero-shot, roughly matching a supervised ResNet-50, without seeing one of ImageNet's 1.28 M training labels. Prompt templates and ensembling over templates are worth several points (CLIP: Zero-Shot Transfer).
  • It is strongest where labels are scarce and weakest on fine or abstract categories: counting, distances, satellite and medical imagery, specific aircraft variants. A linear probe or fine-tune on CLIP features usually beats zero-shot when a few labelled examples per class exist.
  • Its robustness is the headline result and belongs in §12: zero-shot CLIP loses far less accuracy under distribution shift than an ImageNet-trained model of equal in-distribution accuracy.

07 Regularising the input and the target: augmentation, mixup, CutMix, label smoothing

A modern ImageNet recipe spends as much effort on what the network sees as on the network. Augmentation tells the model which changes should not change the label. Soft targets tell it not to be certain.

baseline · always on
Random-resized crop + flip
Crop a random 8–100% area at a random aspect ratio, resize to 224, flip horizontally half the time. Teaches scale and position invariance. Its side effect is that objects look larger in training than in the centre-cropped test images, which is why testing at a slightly higher resolution helps (the FixRes effect).
policy · two knobs
RandAugment
For each image apply N operations drawn uniformly from a fixed list of about 14 (rotate, shear, translate, colour, contrast, posterize, solarize, equalize…), all at one shared magnitude M. It replaced searched policies (AutoAugment) with a two-parameter grid search and matched them.
mix two images
mixup
Blend two images pixel-wise and blend their one-hot labels by the same weight. The model is pushed to behave linearly between examples, which smooths the decision boundary.
paste two images
CutMix
Cut a rectangle out of one image and paste it from another. The label weight is the pasted area fraction. Every pixel stays natural, which suits localisation better than mixup's ghosted blends.
mixup — λ ~ Beta(α, α), with α around 0.2 on ImageNetx̃ = λ·xᵢ + (1 − λ)·xⱼ ỹ = λ·yᵢ + (1 − λ)·yⱼ CutMix — binary mask M zero inside a random box of area (1 − λ)·HWx̃ = M ⊙ xᵢ + (1 − M) ⊙ xⱼ ỹ = λ·yᵢ + (1 − λ)·yⱼ, λ = 1 − box area / HW label smoothing with ε over K classes (usually ε = 0.1)qₖ = (1 − ε)·𝟙[k = y] + ε/K optimum logit gap z_y − zₖ = log( (1 − ε + ε/K) / (ε/K) ) finite for any ε > 0
  • All three soft-target methods train on H(q) + KL(q‖p) with a non-one-hot q, so the logits stay bounded (§02's figure shows the finite gap). The effect is a regulariser on the head, and usually better calibration.
  • Label smoothing has a known cost. It squeezes each class's penultimate features into tight, equally spaced clusters and throws away information about which wrong classes are similar. That makes the network a worse distillation teacher even when it is a better classifier.
  • Stack carefully. Mixup or CutMix plus label smoothing plus heavy RandAugment can over-regularise a small model and leave it under-confident, the reverse failure in §11. Weight decay, dropout, stochastic depth and the rest are covered on Normalization & Regularization.

08 Transfer learning and fine-tuning recipes

Almost nobody trains an image classifier from scratch. You start from a backbone pretrained on ImageNet, a larger labelled set or image–text pairs, and choose how much of it to move. The choice depends on two things: how much labelled data you have, and how far your images are from the pretraining images.

RegimeWhat is trainedWhen it is the right call
Linear probeOnly a new head on frozen featuresTens of examples per class, or a domain close to the pretraining data. Cheap, hard to overfit, and the honest measure of representation quality.
Partial fine-tuneHead + the last stage(s); early layers frozenMedium data. Early filters (edges, colour blobs) are generic. Later ones are task-specific and benefit most from moving.
Full fine-tuneEverything, with a learning rate 10–100× below the from-scratch rateThousands of examples per class, or a domain far from the pretraining data (medical, satellite, documents).
LP-FTLinear probe first, then full fine-tune from that headWhen out-of-distribution robustness matters. A random head sends large gradients that distort good features before the head is sensible, and probing first avoids that.
  1. Match the preprocessing the backbone was trained with: resolution, normalisation mean/std, colour order. Getting it wrong costs points silently, with no error raised.
  2. Use discriminative learning rates: smaller for early layers, larger for the head, with warm-up and cosine decay. Use a light version of the §07 augmentation.
  3. Mind batch norm. In small-batch fine-tuning, freeze its running statistics or switch to evaluation-mode BN, or the statistics drift to a noisy estimate.
  4. Select by validation loss and calibrated accuracy, not by the final epoch. Fine-tuning overfits fast on small sets.
Better ImageNet models usually transfer better, but the correlation is weaker for fine-grained or far-domain tasks, and is broken by regularisers that hurt the penultimate features (label smoothing again). The full treatment, covering feature generality by layer, the data size × similarity grid, forgetting, domain adaptation and parameter-efficient methods, is on Fine-Tuning & Transfer Learning.

09 Multi-label versus multi-class: when softmax is the wrong output

Softmax makes the classes compete for one unit of probability, which encodes "exactly one of these is true". If an image can be both beach and dog, that assumption is false. The fix is to make each class an independent yes/no question.

multi-class: one softmax, one categorical labelp = softmax(z) L = −log p_y predict argmaxₖ pₖ multi-label: K sigmoids, K binary labels yₖ ∈ {0,1}pₖ = σ(zₖ) = 1 / (1 + e^(−zₖ)) L = −∑ₖ [ yₖ·log pₖ + (1 − yₖ)·log(1 − pₖ) ] predict { k : pₖ ≥ tₖ }
Multi-class (softmax)Multi-label (sigmoid + BCE)
Output constraint∑ pₖ = 1; classes are mutually exclusiveEach pₖ ∈ [0,1] independently; any subset is possible, including none
Decisionargmax, no threshold neededA threshold per class. Tune tₖ on validation data, because 0.5 is rarely right for rare classes
ImbalanceMild: every example is a positive for some classSevere: most labels are negatives for most classes. Use positive weights, focal or asymmetric losses (focal loss), and see Data Sampling & Imbalance
Headline metricTop-1 / top-5 accuracyMean average precision over classes, plus per-class F₁ at the chosen thresholds
Typical dataImageNet, CIFAR, species IDPhoto tagging, Open Images, chest X-ray findings, attributes
The trap. Training multi-label data with softmax and taking the top few classes. Softmax divides probability between co-occurring labels, so an image with three true tags gets about a third each and none clears a sensible threshold. Worse, the model learns that tags compete when they actually co-occur. The same distinction appears for text on Text Classification.

Accuracy is one number from a K×K table, and it weights classes by how often they occur. The table itself says which classes are confused with which and whether a rare class is being sacrificed.

  • The rare class is where the model is failing, and accuracy barely shows it. Fox recall is 0.5: half of all foxes are called cats or dogs. Because fox is only 12.5% of the data, it moves accuracy far less than it moves macro-F₁ or balanced accuracy.
  • For single-label multi-class, micro-averaged precision, recall and F₁ all equal accuracy. Every error is a false positive for one class and a false negative for another, so reporting "micro-F₁" next to accuracy adds nothing.
  • Read the off-diagonal for structure. Cat↔dog confusion (8 and 10) is between visually similar classes and is often a labelling question. A large off-diagonal between dissimilar classes usually points to a data bug: a mislabelled folder, or a background shortcut. Model Interpretability tools such as saliency maps are how you check which.
The general methodology (held-out splits, cross-validation, threshold-free curves, confidence intervals on a metric, leakage) is on Model Evaluation. Two vision-specific leaks deserve naming: near-duplicate images split across train and test, and pretraining sets that already contain the benchmark's test images.

11 Calibration: is "90% confident" right 90% of the time?

A classifier is calibrated if, among all the predictions it makes with confidence 0.9, about 90% are correct. Accuracy says nothing about this. A model can be right more often than another and still be badly wrong about how sure it should be, which matters for thresholds, abstention, triage and any downstream system that consumes p.

expected calibration error over B equal-width confidence bins BᵦECE = ∑ᵦ (|Bᵦ| / n) · | acc(Bᵦ) − conf(Bᵦ) | temperature scaling — one scalar fitted on held-out data by minimising NLL; the argmax is unchangedp̂ = softmax(z / T) T* = argmin_T −∑ᵢ log softmax(zᵢ / T)_{yᵢ}
  • Modern deep networks tend to be over-confident. §02's runaway logits are the mechanism. Depth, width, batch norm and long training at low weight decay all make it worse, while accuracy keeps rising. Temperature scaling with T > 1 is a strong, nearly free fix, and it cannot change accuracy because it rescales all logits equally.
  • ECE is a convenient summary with real flaws: it depends on the bin count, it is noisy for small sets, and a model can game it by always predicting its average accuracy. Pair it with a proper scoring rule such as NLL or the Brier score, and read the diagram itself.
  • Calibration is not robust to shift. Temperature fitted on in-distribution validation data under-corrects on shifted data, where accuracy falls faster than confidence (§12).
One fixed set of predictions, rescaled by a temperature: the reliability diagram, ECE and NLL move; accuracy does not slide T until the bars sit on the diagonal
  • The default model is right 67.3% of the time and claims 84.4%. ECE is 17.1% and every bar sits below the diagonal. The labels were drawn from softmax(z) and the model reports softmax(2.5·z), so the right answer is known to be T = 2.5.
  • Slide to T ≈ 2.4. Mean confidence falls to about 66%, ECE to about 2%, and NLL from 1.280 to 0.928. Accuracy reads 67.3% the whole time. On the grid, NLL bottoms out at T ≈ 2.43, the grid point nearest the true 2.5.
  • ECE never reaches zero, even at the true temperature. With 3000 samples the residual is finite-sample noise, and the ECE minimum (T ≈ 2.33) is not at the true T. NLL, a proper score, finds the right temperature. ECE only approximately does, which is why T is fitted on NLL.
  • Switch to the under-confident model, the over-regularised failure of §07. The bars rise above the diagonal (green), ECE is about 16% at T = 1, and the fix is T < 1.

12 Robustness and distribution shift, then the takeaways

An i.i.d. test set measures interpolation. Deployed classifiers meet new cameras, new lighting, sketches, compression artefacts and objects in unusual poses, and accuracy can fall by tens of points. This is the same problem Monitoring & Drift treats from the operations side.

Test setWhat shiftsWhat it revealed
ImageNet-V2A fresh sample collected with the original procedureAccuracy drops of roughly 11–14 points across models, yet the ranking of models is almost perfectly preserved. A small change in the collection process is already a shift.
ImageNet-C15 synthetic corruptions (noise, blur, weather, JPEG) × 5 severitiesNetworks are brittle to perturbations humans ignore. Augmentation that targets the corruption helps on that corruption and less on others.
ImageNet-R, -SketchRenditions: paintings, cartoons, sketchesImageNet CNNs rely heavily on texture over shape. Remove the texture and much of the accuracy goes with it.
ObjectNetControlled rotations, viewpoints and backgroundsLarge drops for supervised models, showing how much they lean on canonical poses and backgrounds.
  • Effective robustness is accuracy under shift beyond what in-distribution accuracy predicts. For most models, shifted accuracy lies on a line set by ImageNet accuracy, so almost nothing helps except being more accurate. Zero-shot CLIP lies well above that line. Fine-tuning it on ImageNet pulls it back towards the line, which is the motivation for LP-FT and weight-space ensembling (CLIP robustness).
  • What helps in practice: more diverse pretraining data (the biggest lever), shape-biasing and corruption augmentation, test-time adaptation of normalisation statistics, and monitoring the input distribution in production so you know when you have left the training support.

Takeaways

  • A classifier is a learned feature extractor plus a linear softmax head, trained on cross-entropy whose gradient is p − q.
  • The architecture arc (hand-crafted → LeNet → AlexNet → VGG → ResNet → EfficientNet → ViT → CLIP) is a story of removing obstacles to scale. Above all it is a story of learning the features instead of designing them.
  • The recipe matters as much as the network: augmentation, mixup/CutMix and label smoothing regularise input and target. Compare models only under the same recipe.
  • Start from a pretrained backbone and pick probe, partial or full fine-tune by data size and domain distance.
  • Use sigmoids for multi-label, report per-class metrics for imbalanced classes, calibrate with a temperature, and test under shift before trusting a top-1 number.
