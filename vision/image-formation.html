Image Formation

A photograph is the output of a physical process that ran once, in the world, and then threw most of its input away. This part reconstructs that process forwards — from a point in a room, through a rigid transformation into the camera's frame, through a division by depth that destroys the third dimension, through a lens that bends the straight lines it was supposed to preserve, off a surface whose reflectance decides how bright it looks, onto a grid of silicon wells that count photons badly, and out through a pipeline of gain, colour interpolation and gamma that ends in three eight-bit numbers. Every later part of this series is an attempt to invert some stage of what follows. Nothing here is estimated from data; everything here is defined, so that parts 6 and 7 have something precise to estimate.

01 The forward model, and what this page does not cover

Vision is the inverse of graphics, and you cannot invert a function you have not written down. This part writes it down. The whole of image formation is one composition of maps, and every section below is a magnifying glass held over one arrow in it.

a 3D point→[R|t] into the camera frame→divide by z→lens distortion→K into pixels→× surface reflectance→× lens vignetting→integrate over the pixel→count photons→gain, demosaic, gamma→8 bits

The chain splits cleanly into three questions, and the source material splits the same way. Where does a scene point land in the image — that is geometry, and it is §02 to §18. How bright and what colour is it — that is photometry, and it is §19 to §23. What number is finally stored — that is the sensor and the image signal processor, §24 and §25. The geometric half is by far the longest, because it is the half that later parts invert.

The two things that are irreversibly destroyed

loss 1
Depth
Every point on a ray through the camera centre maps to the same pixel. A single image is consistent with an infinite family of scenes, and no amount of processing recovers the one that was there. Parts 6 and 7 add a second view, which is the only honest fix.
loss 2
Spectrum
A pixel's three numbers are three inner products of a continuous spectrum against three sensor curves. Infinitely many spectra give the same triple — they are called metamers — and §22 is about why the eye has exactly the same defect and why that is what makes colour reproduction possible at all.
loss 3
Everything above Nyquist
Detail finer than twice the pixel pitch does not vanish; it comes back disguised as coarser detail. That is aliasing, it is a sampling question rather than a formation question, and it is Part 3's.
not a loss
Distortion
Radial distortion looks like damage but is not: it is an invertible map, up to the point where it stops being monotone. §17 works out exactly where that point is, and it is closer than most people assume.

02 2D primitives: points, lines, conics

Before anything can be transformed it has to be written down, and the choice of how to write a line down is not neutral. The representation chosen here — a line as a triple, exactly as symmetric with a point as a point is with it — is what makes the whole of the next section possible.

Points

the ordinary wayx = (x, y) ∈ ℝ² written as a column, x = [x, y]ᵀ the projective way, which §03 justifiesx̃ = (x̃, ỹ, w̃) ∈ ℙ² with x̃ ∼ λx̃ for every λ ≠ 0 ℙ² = ℝ³ ∖ {(0,0,0)} under that equivalence — the PROJECTIVE PLANE converting back, when it is possible at allx̃ = (x̃, ỹ, w̃) = w̃ · (x, y, 1) = w̃ · x̄ so x = x̃/w̃, y = ỹ/w̃ w̃ = 0 ⟹ no inhomogeneous form exists. These are the IDEAL POINTS, or POINTS AT INFINITY.

Two objects are being kept apart deliberately. x̃ is a whole equivalence class — a line through the origin of ℝ³ with the origin removed. x̄ = (x, y, 1) is one chosen member of that class, the augmented vector. Every formula below that ends in a division is really a step from x̃ back down to x̄.

Lines

A 2D line is also a homogeneous triple, and the equation relating a point to a line is a dot product that is symmetric in the two.

Notice what has just happened. "Two lines meet in a point" is now true without exception. Parallel lines are no longer a special case to be tested for and branched around; they meet at a point whose last coordinate happens to be zero, and the cross product neither knows nor cares. That single removal of a special case is most of the argument for projective coordinates, and §03 is the rest of it.

Conics

a conic is the zero set of a homogeneous quadratic formx̃ᵀ Q x̃ = 0 with Q symmetric 3 × 3, defined up to scale written out, this is the familiar general second-degree equationa x² + b xy + c y² + d x + e y + f = 0 ⎡ a b/2 d/2 ⎤ Q = ⎢ b/2 c e/2 ⎥ 5 degrees of freedom: 6 entries, minus 1 for scale ⎣ d/2 e/2 f ⎦ which conic it is, is decided by the top-left 2 × 2 blockdet[[a, b/2], [b/2, c]] = ac − b²/4 > 0 ellipse (circle when a = c, b = 0) = 0 parabola < 0 hyperbola and the classification is exactly a statement about points at infinitythe conic meets l̃∞ in 0 real points → ellipse; 1 → parabola; 2 → hyperbola

That last line is the reward for the whole projective apparatus, and it is worth pausing on. A parabola and an ellipse are not different kinds of curve; they are the same kind of curve differing in how they cross the horizon. A parabola is an ellipse tangent to the line at infinity; a hyperbola is one that cuts it twice, and its two "branches" are one connected curve that passes through infinity and comes back. This is also why a circle photographed at an angle can come out as a hyperbola: the projective transformation of §05 moved the line at infinity.

Conics matter later — the image of a circle is a conic, the absolute conic is what metric calibration is really estimating — but this series uses them only as far as Part 4's ellipse fitting and Part 7's calibration. Five degrees of freedom means five points in general position determine a conic uniquely, which is the fact those parts will need.

Duality, in one line. Points and lines in ℙ² obey identical algebra: swap the words "point" and "line", swap "lies on" and "passes through", and every true statement stays true. "Two points determine a line" and "two lines determine a point" are the same theorem read twice. That is why the same cross product computes both, and why §06's rule for transforming a line is forced to be what it is.

03 Homogeneous coordinates, and the three problems they solve

Adding a coordinate that carries no information looks like a bad trade. It buys three things that are individually convenient and jointly decisive, and the third one is the reason a camera can be a matrix at all.

The construction

Take the plane ℝ² and embed it in ℝ³ as the affine plane w = 1. Now declare two vectors in ℝ³ equivalent when one is a non-zero multiple of the other. Each equivalence class is a line through the origin with the origin deleted, and almost every such line pierces the plane w = 1 exactly once — so almost every class is a point of the original plane. The exceptions are the lines that lie flat in the plane w = 0: parallel to the embedded copy, they never pierce it. Those classes are the new points, one for every direction in the plane, and they are precisely the points at infinity.

the projective plane, three equivalent descriptionsℙ² = (ℝ³ ∖ {0}) / ∼ where x̃ ∼ ỹ ⟺ x̃ = λỹ, λ ≠ 0 = { lines through the origin of ℝ³ } = ℝ² ∪ { one point per direction } the plane, plus a circle of directions with antipodes glued so a point of ℙ² is a whole ray, and dividing by w̃ just picks the representative that sits on w = 1λ(x̃, ỹ, w̃) all name ONE point; dehomogenise: (x̃/w̃, ỹ/w̃) when w̃ ≠ 0

Problem 1 — translation is not linear, and homogeneous coordinates make it linear

In ℝ², x ↦ x + t is not a linear map: it does not fix the origin, so no 2×2 matrix implements it. The consequence is that a rotation is a matrix, a scale is a matrix, and a translation is an addition, so a chain of transformations becomes an alternating mess of multiplies and adds that cannot be pre-multiplied into one object. Lift to ℝ³ and the translation becomes a shear, which is linear.

the whole of affine geometry becomes matrix multiplication ⎡ 1 0 t_x ⎤ ⎡ x ⎤ ⎡ x + t_x ⎤ ⎢ 0 1 t_y ⎥ ⎢ y ⎥ = ⎢ y + t_y ⎥ a SHEAR in ℝ³, restricted to the plane w = 1 ⎣ 0 0 1 ⎦ ⎣ 1 ⎦ ⎣ 1 ⎦ and therefore a chain of transformations is ONE matrix, computed oncerotate about the point c = T(c) · R(θ) · T(−c) — three matrices, multiplied, then applied N times

Problem 2 — the exceptions disappear

"Two distinct lines meet in exactly one point" is false in the Euclidean plane and true in the projective plane. "Two distinct points lie on exactly one line" is true in both. Removing the exception is not just tidiness: a piece of code that computes intersections no longer needs a parallelism test, and a piece of code that fits a vanishing point no longer needs a special case for a horizontal one. §02 showed the cross product doing it without noticing.

Problem 3 — perspective is a linear map, but only up here

This is the decisive one. Perspective projection divides by depth, and division is emphatically not linear. But in homogeneous coordinates the division is not performed by the matrix at all — it is performed by the act of reading the answer back out, i.e. by dehomogenisation. The matrix stays linear; the non-linearity is pushed into the equivalence relation, where it costs nothing.

perspective, as a linear map on ℙ³ → ℙ² ⎡ 1 0 0 0 ⎤ ⎡ X ⎤ ⎡ X ⎤ the matrix is linear; ⎢ 0 1 0 0 ⎥ ⎢ Y ⎥ = ⎢ Y ⎥ ∼ (X/Z, Y/Z) the DIVISION happens when you ⎣ 0 0 1 0 ⎦ ⎢ Z ⎥ ⎣ Z ⎦ dehomogenise, not inside it ⎣ 1 ⎦

Everything the rest of this page does with a matrix depends on that. P = K[R|t] is a single 3×4 matrix precisely because rotation, translation, projection and pixel scaling can all be written as linear maps on homogeneous coordinates and therefore composed by multiplication. Without the extra coordinate there is no camera matrix; there is a procedure.

What the figure is showing, stated precisely

  • Moving the λ slider does not move the point. That is the whole content of "defined up to scale". The marker slides all the way through the origin and out the other side, and the pierce point is unchanged — including for negative λ, which is why the projective plane glues antipodal directions together.
  • Lowering the elevation sends the pierce point outward. At elevation ψ the pierce point sits at distance cot ψ from the origin of the plane, so it goes to infinity like 1/ψ. There is no elevation at which it is "very far but still there and then suddenly gone" — it is finite for every ψ > 0 and undefined at exactly ψ = 0.
  • A line in the plane is a plane through the origin in ℝ³. Turn on the parallel-lines option: two parallel lines become two distinct planes, and two distinct planes through the origin always intersect in a line through the origin. That intersection line lies inside w̃ = 0, which is the geometric reason parallel lines meet at infinity — and the reason the set of ideal points is itself a line, the line at infinity.
  • The right-hand panel is what a camera would see. The ideal point is not off the edge of the frame; it is not in the frame's coordinate system at all. What a camera actually records in its place is the vanishing point of §13 — and a vanishing point is finite, because the camera's own projection maps the plane at infinity somewhere concrete.
The trap. Homogeneous coordinates are sometimes described as "adding a 1 at the end". That is the description of x̄, the augmented vector, not of x̃. The distinction bites the moment a matrix produces a last coordinate that is not 1: a homogeneous vector must be divided through before its first two entries mean anything. Forgetting the division is the single most common bug in hand-written projection code, and it is silent — the output is a plausible-looking pair of numbers that is wrong by a factor that varies across the image.

The four-parameter count is the same kind of argument that will decide, in §14, whether the calibration matrix has five, seven or eight degrees of freedom. Both times the recipe is: write down an over-parameterised representation, then subtract one for each direction you can move the parameters without moving the object. Over-parameterisation is not a mistake — it is usually the numerically better choice — but knowing the true count tells you how many independent measurements you need before the problem is determined, which is exactly what Part 7 will be counting.

The symmetric alternative, for completeness. A representation that treats all directions alike takes the six independent entries of the skew-symmetric 4×4 matrix L = p̃q̃ᵀ − q̃p̃ᵀ. It is homogeneous — one scale to remove — and satisfies det L = 0, a single quadratic constraint, which brings six down to 6 − 1 − 1 = 4 as required. The four-vs-six tension is real but rarely worth the machinery in practice: estimating a direction plus a point on the visible portion of the line is usually enough, and line segments want the two endpoints anyway.

05 The 2D transformation hierarchy — the one table to memorise

Five transformations, nested one inside the next, each one a strictly larger group than the last. Every alignment problem in vision is a choice of one of these five, and choosing wrongly is the most common modelling error in the subject: too small a group and the model cannot fit the data, too large and it fits the noise as well.

Where the degree-of-freedom counts come from. Not memorised — derived, in one line each. Projective: a 3×3 matrix has 9 entries and is defined up to scale, so 9 − 1 = 8. Affine: the bottom row is pinned to [0 0 1], leaving the 2×3 block, so 6. Similarity: 2 for t, 1 for θ, 1 for s, so 4. Rigid: drop the scale, 3. Translation: drop the angle, 2. Equivalently, in terms of point correspondences: each matched point gives two equations, so a homography needs 4 points, an affine 3, a similarity 2, a rigid 2 (one over-determined), a translation 1. Those counts are the minimal sample sizes Part 6's robust estimator will draw.

They are groups, and nested ones

Each level is closed under composition and inversion, and each is a subgroup of the next. That is not decoration: it means the composition of two homographies is a homography, so a chain of warps can be collapsed into one matrix and applied once — which is why image stitching resamples the source image exactly once rather than once per pair.

The modelling error. "Use a homography, it is the most general." Choose the smallest group that can express the true motion. Two images from a camera on a tripod that only rotated are related by a homography exactly (§16); two images of a flat wall are related by a homography exactly; almost everything else is not, and a homography fitted to it is a lie that fits well in the middle and diverges at the edges.

06 Composing transforms, and how a line transforms

Two short results that get used constantly and misremembered constantly: matrix multiplication does not commute, so the order of a chain is part of its meaning; and a line does not transform the way a point does — it transforms by the inverse transpose.

Order is meaning

Transformations compose right-to-left, matching the order the matrices are applied to the vector. H₂H₁x means "do H₁, then H₂". The two orders of the same pair are genuinely different maps.

Why R has three degrees of freedom, and why that is awkward

A 3×3 rotation matrix has nine entries, but orthonormality RᵀR = I imposes six independent constraints — three unit-length conditions on the columns and three mutual orthogonality conditions — leaving 9 − 6 = 3. The determinant condition |R| = +1 costs nothing further; it merely selects one of the two disconnected components of the orthogonal group, ruling out reflections.

the group of 3D rotationsSO(3) = { R ∈ ℝ³ˣ³ : RᵀR = I, det R = +1 } dimension 3 the count9 free entries − 3 (columns are unit) − 3 (columns are mutually orthogonal) = 3 and the awkward fact that all of §09–§11 is aboutSO(3) is a 3-dimensional manifold that is NOT homeomorphic to ℝ³. It is closed and curved, so NO set of three numbers can parameterise it smoothly everywhere. Every 3-parameter chart must break somewhere. Euler angles break at gimbal lock (§09); axis/angle breaks at θ = 0 and θ = π (§10). The escape is to use FOUR numbers (§11).

That is a theorem, not a warning about sloppy implementations: it is a consequence of the topology of SO(3), which is real projective 3-space. Any continuous map from an open subset of ℝ³ onto SO(3) must either fail to be injective or fail to be a local homeomorphism somewhere. Nothing you can code will avoid it; you can only choose where the failure happens and whether you notice.

The difference between 2D and 3D, in one sentence. In the plane, a rotation is one number and the number composes by addition — θ₁ + θ₂, commutatively, with no singularities. In space, a rotation is three numbers, they do not compose by addition, they do not commute, and no three-number scheme is free of a breakdown. Everything hard about 3D pose comes from that sentence.

Numerical hygiene. This is why so much pose code carries quaternions instead: renormalising a 4-vector is one division.

09 Euler angles, and the exact place they fail

Three angles about three axes. It is the parameterisation everybody meets first, it is the one every user interface exposes, and it is the one that is wrong for almost every purpose in this series. The reasons are specific and provable, not stylistic.

The convention, written out

The source material declines to give the formula on the grounds that Euler angles are a bad idea. That is good advice and bad pedagogy: you cannot recognise a failure you have never seen. Here is the convention this series uses whenever Euler angles appear at all.

There are twenty-four conventions, and they are all called "Euler angles". The single most useful fact about the family is that an intrinsic sequence equals the same axes reversed, taken extrinsically: intrinsic Z–Y–X is identical to extrinsic X–Y–Z. Every "the angles from library A do not work in library B" bug is somewhere in that list of 24.

Failure 1 — the composition rule is not addition

Averaging two orientations by averaging their Euler angles is therefore meaningless, and so is interpolating between them by interpolating the angles — the intermediate orientations wobble off the shortest path in a way that depends on the arbitrary choice of convention. §11 fixes exactly this.

Interpolation: slerp

Three ways to interpolate the same two orientations — only one of them travels at constant speed the step sizes are measured, not asserted: each bar is the geodesic angle between consecutive frames
  • The slerp bars are all the same height, and the others are not. Constant angular speed is not a detail: it is what makes an interpolated camera path look like a camera move rather than a lurch.
  • nlerp follows the identical path but at a varying speed. 13 Perspective: one division, and everything it costs

The whole of perspective is x = X/Z, y = Y/Z. Every distinctive property of a photograph — that distant things are small, that parallel lines converge, that a horizon exists, that depth is unrecoverable — is a consequence of that single quotient, and each of them can be derived from it in a line or two.

The map

inhomogeneousx̄ = P_z(p) = ( X/Z , Y/Z , 1 ) homogeneous, where it is linear — drop w rather than z ⎡ 1 0 0 0 ⎤ x̃ = ⎢ 0 1 0 0 ⎥ p̃ the CANONICAL camera: centre at the origin, axis along +z, f = 1 ⎣ 0 0 1 0 ⎦ the 3 × 4 matrix has a one-dimensional null space — spanned by (0, 0, 0, 1), the camera centre — and that null space IS the information the projection destroys

Four consequences, each derived in one line

consequence 1
Depth is gone
Every point λp on the ray through p gives the same (X/Z, Y/Z). The pre-image of a pixel is a whole ray, and no operation on one image narrows it. Recovering depth needs a second constraint — another view, another moment, or an assumption.
consequence 2
Size ∝ 1/depth
An object of height h at depth Z subtends fh/Z pixels. Double the distance, halve the image size — exactly, with no approximation. This is the relation §12's affine models linearise away.
consequence 3
Straight lines stay straight
A 3D line together with the camera centre spans a plane; that plane cuts the image plane in a line, and every point of the 3D line projects onto it. So straightness survives projection exactly. A real photograph bends lines anyway — that is the lens, not the projection, and it is §17.
consequence 4
Parallel lines converge
A family of parallel lines shares one point at infinity, and the projection maps that point somewhere finite. That image is the vanishing point, and it depends only on the direction, never on which line of the family you picked.

  • Change the rail direction and the vanishing point slides along the horizon.   • Change the camera height and the horizon does not move either. Height changes where the ground is in the image, not where its infinity is. A camera on the floor and a camera on a roof, both level, put the horizon on exactly the same row.
  • Pitch moves the horizon; roll tilts it. Those are the only two things that do. That is why the horizon is an attitude sensor and not a position sensor.
  • Watch the depth-uncertainty figure as the probe approaches the horizon. It is not a numerical artefact; dz/dv genuinely diverges. Anything you want to measure by this trick must be near enough that its row is well clear of c_y.

14 Camera intrinsics: the calibration matrix K

Perspective projection produces a pair of dimensionless ratios. A sensor produces integer row and column indices. K is the affine map between them, and its five entries are the only things about a camera's optics that a linear model can express.

Where it comes from

The honest derivation runs backwards, from pixels to rays, because pixels are what is physically measurable. A pixel index (x_s, y_s) names a cell on a silicon array. Scaling by the pixel pitches (sₓ, s_y) turns indices into millimetres on the sensor; the sensor's origin sits at some point c_s in space and its plane has some orientation R_s. Composing those gives a map from pixel indices to 3D rays.

How many degrees of freedom does K really have?

Three different answers circulate, and all three are correct about different questions. This is worth untangling because it recurs whenever someone says a calibration is "under-determined".

answer A
Eight
If you treat K as an arbitrary 3×3 homogeneous matrix — which is what an estimator that does not impose structure will return — it has 9 − 1 = 8 degrees of freedom.
answer B
Seven
Physically, M_s has 3 for R_s, 3 for c_s and 2 for (sₓ, s_y) = 8; but the distance of the sensor from the centre cannot be separated from the pixel pitch by any image measurement, so one of those eight is unobservable. Seven remain.
answer C
Five — the one that is used
Because K is only ever seen through P = K[R|t], and you may insert R₁ᵀR₁ between them: K → KR₁ and [R|t] → R₁ᵀ[R|t] gives the identical P. The sensor's true orientation and the camera's are not separately observable. Fixing the ambiguity by demanding K be upper triangular leaves exactly 5.
the ambiguity, in one lineP = K[R|t] = (KR₁)(R₁ᵀ[R|t]) for ANY rotation R₁ — the same P, different K and R and the fix, which is a convention rather than a discoverydemand K upper triangular. The clash of notation to be ready for. In numerical linear algebra R means the upper-triangular factor of a QR decomposition. In computer vision R means an orthogonal rotation. Decomposing the camera matrix uses RQ — upper-triangular times orthogonal — so the sentence "compute the RQ decomposition of M to get K and R" contains the letter R twice with two different meanings. Each entry of K, acting alone — the same rays, five different sensors nothing about the scene changes; only the map from rays to pixels does
  • The principal point slides the image; it does not reshape it. Which is exactly why cropping a photograph invalidates a stored calibration: the pixel indices all shift, so (cₓ, c_y) must shift with them, by precisely the crop offset.
  • Two different focal lengths is a pure anisotropic scale. It cannot be distinguished from a scene that is genuinely stretched, which is why a = f_y/fₓ is fixed to 1 whenever anything else is being estimated.
  • Skew slants the columns and leaves the rows alone. That asymmetry is the signature of the upper-triangular convention: the skew term multiplies Y_c/Z_c and lands in the x equation only.
  • None of these sliders changes the scene, the pose, or the rays. Everything on the right of the panel is a property of the sensor and its mounting. That separation — optics in K, geometry in [R|t] — is the whole point of factoring P at all.

A pinhole has infinite depth of field and admits almost no light. A lens trades the first for the second. Everything photographers argue about — aperture, depth of field, bokeh, the darkening at the corners — is that one trade, and all of it is derivable from a single equation.

19 Photometric formation: where the numbers come from

Geometry decides where a scene point lands. Photometry decides what value is stored there, and it is a chain of three multiplications: how much light arrives, what fraction the surface sends towards the camera, and how much of that the optics deliver to the well.

Light sources

source type
Point source
A single location, an intensity, and a spectrum L(λ). Its irradiance falls as 1/r², because the same energy spreads over a sphere whose area grows as r². A source at infinity — the sun — has no falloff across a scene and gives parallel rays, which is why sunlit shadows are sharp and parallel.
source type
Area source
A finite emitting surface, e.g. a ceiling panel. source type

L(v̂; λ) — a colour for every incident direction, and nothing else. It assumes every source is at infinity, so it cannot express falloff or parallax, but it captures a whole complicated sky or courtyard in one lookup. A photograph of a mirrored sphere is a cheap way to acquire one.
what is ignored
Everything after the first bounce
The model below is local: light goes source → surface → camera, once. Real light bounces repeatedly, so surfaces are lit by their neighbours (colour bleeding), corners are darker than flats (ambient occlusion), and shadows are not black. Those are §20's global-illumination footnote, and every one of them breaks shape-from-shading.

The two quantities that must not be confused

RADIANCE — the property of a surface or a ray, and the thing an image measuresL = power per unit projected area per unit solid angle W · m⁻² · sr⁻¹ It is constant along a ray in a vacuum. That invariance is why a wall looks equally bright from one metre and from ten — the 1/r² falloff in power is exactly cancelled by the 1/r² shrinkage of the solid angle a pixel subtends. IRRADIANCE — the property of a receiving surface, e.g. the sensorE = power per unit area W · m⁻² and §18's relation is exactly the bridge between themE = L · (π/4)(d/f)² cos⁴α scene radiance in, pixel irradiance out

The invariance of radiance along a ray is the single most useful fact in photometric vision, and it is the reason a pixel value can be treated as a property of the surface patch rather than of the distance to it. It fails the moment there is fog, smoke or water between the surface and the lens, which is precisely why every de-hazing method begins by writing down how much it fails by.

22 Colour: three numbers for an infinite-dimensional thing

Light arriving at a pixel is a whole function of wavelength. Three numbers come out. Colour science is the study of what survives that collapse, and its central discovery is that three is not a limitation of cameras but a fact about eyes — which is exactly why colour reproduction is possible at all.

Trichromacy

The human retina has rods, used at low light levels and giving no colour at all, and three types of cone: S (short, blue-ish), M (medium, green-ish) and L (long, red-ish). There are roughly 6 million cones, almost all within a few degrees of the fovea, and roughly 100 million rods spread across the rest of the retina — which is why peripheral and night vision are sharp in neither detail nor colour, and why looking slightly to one side of a faint star makes it visible.

Metamerism is illuminant-dependent.
The additive/subtractive confusion, resolved. Red + green light gives yellow because the two together excite the L and M cones in the same ratio that a true 570 nm yellow does — no new wavelength is created; the eye simply cannot tell the mixture from the pure line. Additive primaries (red, green, blue) add emitted light and are what a display does. Subtractive primaries (cyan, magenta, yellow) remove wavelengths by absorption and are what ink does. 23 Gamma: the non-linearity in every image you will ever load

A stored pixel value is not proportional to the light that made it. It is roughly proportional to the light raised to the power 1/2.2. This is the single most consequential fact about image data for anyone doing physics with it, and it is invisible until it bites.

Where it came from, and why it stayed

the historical cause: a cathode-ray tube's brightness was a power law in its input voltageB = V^γ with γ ≈ 2.2 so the camera pre-compensated, and the two cancelled end to endY′ = Y^(1/γ) at the camera Ŷ = (Y′)^γ = Y at the display typical 1/γ = 0.45 the accident that made it outlive the hardwarenoise added in transmission was ADDED to Y′ and then SQUASHED by the display's exponent in the dark regions, where the eye is most sensitive to relative differences. Gamma encoding is, accidentally, a near-optimal perceptual quantisation — so it survived the death of the CRT and is baked into every 8-bit format in use.

Why it is the right thing to do anyway

The eye responds to relative luminance differences — roughly 1% is the discrimination threshold across a wide range. A linear 8-bit encoding spends its codes uniformly in luminance, which means enormous relative steps in the shadows and wastefully fine ones in the highlights.

A silicon photosite counts photons; it has no idea what colour they were. Three-chip cameras exist and are expensive. Almost every camera instead puts a mosaic of coloured filters over one sensor, measures one channel per pixel, and interpolates the other two — so two thirds of the numbers in an RGB image were never measured.

Demosaicing, and the artefacts each method leaves

method
Nearest / bilinear
Average the available neighbours of the same colour. Cheap, and wrong at every edge: it produces zipper artefacts — alternating light and dark pixels along a high-contrast edge, because the interpolation stencil straddles the edge differently on the two phases of the mosaic — and false colour, coloured fringes where a purely luminance edge is split unevenly between the channels.
method
Constant hue / colour ratio
Interpolate R − G and B − G rather than R and B. The colour differences are far smoother than the channels themselves across an edge — an edge is mostly a luminance event — so interpolating them and adding the well-sampled green back removes most of the false colour for almost no extra cost.
method
Gradient-corrected / edge-directed
Estimate the local edge direction first and interpolate along it, never across it. This is what removes zippers, and it is what every camera actually ships. alternative
No mosaic at all
Three sensors behind a beam splitter, or stacked photodiodes exploiting the wavelength-dependence of silicon's absorption depth. Both measure all three channels at every location and neither needs demosaicing; both cost more, and the stacked design trades colour separation for it.
Why this matters upstream of everything else. Demosaicing is a spatially varying, non-linear operation applied before you ever see the image. It correlates neighbouring pixels, so per-pixel independent noise assumptions are already false at the first step; it manufactures high-frequency structure that a corner detector will happily find; and it is applied before the gamma of §23, so undoing gamma does not undo it. Anything demanding genuinely independent measurements — noise calibration, high-precision photometry, some stereo matching — should start from the raw mosaic, not from the developed image.

Translation 2 DoF → 1 point; rigid 3 → 2; similarity 4 → 2; affine 6 → 3; projective 8 → 4. Those are exactly the minimal sample sizes Part 6's RANSAC will draw, and they are why homography estimation is described as a "4-point" problem.
  5. Four collinear points at x = 0, 1, 2.5, 4 are photographed. What survives? The cross ratio (2.5 × 3)/(1.5 × 4) = 1.25, exactly, under any projective map. The simple ratio |AB|/|BC| = 0.6667 survives an affine map but not a projective one — under the page's example homography it becomes 0.75.
  6. ZYX Euler angles (α, β, γ) = (10°, 90°, 40°). Give an equivalent triple with γ = 0. At β = +90° only α − γ matters, and it is −30°. So (−30°, 90°, 0°) is the same rotation. There are infinitely many others, all with α − γ = −30°.
  7. Write the rotation matrix of the unit quaternion q = (0, 0, 1, 0). x = y = 0, z = 1, w = 0, so R₀₀ = 1 − 2(0 + 1) = −1, R₁₁ = −1, R₂₂ = 1 − 2(0 + 0) = +1, all off-diagonals zero: R = diag(−1, −1, 1), a 180° turn about z, with det R = +1. The half-angle is 90°, so w = cos 90° = 0 ✓.
  8. Two orientations satisfy tr(R₁ᵀR₂) = 1.4. How far apart are they? θ = arccos((1.4 − 1)/2) = arccos(0.2) = 78.463°. Equivalently, if their unit quaternions have dot product 0.9, then θ = 2 arccos(0.9) = 51.684° — a different pair, and the two formulas are consistent readings of the same geodesic distance.
  9. A lens has κ₁ = −0.35 (and κ₂ = 0). What is the largest normalised radius that can still be undistorted? 1/√(−3κ₁) = 1/√1.05 = 0.9759. If the frame's corner radius exceeds that — with f = 500 on 640 × 480 it is 0.8, so it does not — the map folds and the inverse does not exist.
  10. A camera 1.2 m above flat ground, f = 700, c_y = 240. A ground point appears at row 400. How far away is it, and what is one pixel of row error worth? z = fh/(v − c_y) = 700 × 1.2/160 = 5.25 m. dz/dv = fh/(v − c_y)² = 840/25600 = 0.0328 m, about 3.3 cm per pixel. At row 250 the same pixel would be worth 840/100 = 8.4 m.
  11. A matte surface of albedo 0.6 is lit by a unit source 60° from its normal. What radiance leaves it? ρL[n̂·l̂]⁺ = 0.6 × 1 × cos 60° = 0.30 — and the same for every viewing direction, which is what "Lambertian" means.
  12. How much brighter does pure green look than pure blue, at equal RGB code? By the BT.709 luminance weights, 0.7152/0.0722 = 9.906×. Which is also why converting to greyscale with (R + G + B)/3 makes blue skies far too light.
  13. An sRGB image has a pixel at code 128. What fraction of full linear luminance is that? 128/255 = 0.50196 > 0.04045, so L = ((0.50196 + 0.055)/1.055)^2.4 = 0.2159 — 21.6%, not 50%. The pure γ = 2.2 approximation gives 0.2195, within 0.4 percentage points.
  14. A photosite collects 400 electrons; read noise is 4 e⁻. What is the SNR, and what would double the exposure buy? σ = √(400 + 16) = 20.396, SNR = 400/20.396 = 19.61. Doubling the light gives 800/√(800 + 16) = 28.01, a factor of 1.428 — essentially the √2 = 1.414 that shot noise alone would predict, with a sliver extra because the read-noise term did not double.
  15. A pixel sits 25° off the optical axis. How much light does it lose to natural vignetting? cos⁴ 25° = 0.6747 — it receives 67% of what an on-axis pixel does, before any lens defect is considered.
  16. A 12-megapixel Bayer sensor: how many of each colour are actually measured? 6 M green, 3 M red, 3 M blue. The "12-megapixel RGB image" it produces contains 36 M numbers, of which 12 M were measured and 24 M were interpolated.
  17. Going from f/4 to f/8: how much light is lost, and what happens to the depth of field? The aperture diameter halves, so the area quarters: two stops, one quarter the light. The depth of field roughly doubles, since it scales with N. Compensating with exposure time means four times as long, and therefore four times the motion blur.
  18. A 35 mm lens focused at 700 mm: where is the sensor, and what is the magnification? 1/z_i = 1/35 − 1/700 = 0.0271429, so z_i = 36.842 mm and m = z_i/z_o = 0.05263. Its hyperfocal distance at f/8 with c = 0.02 mm is 35²/(8 × 0.02) + 35 = 7691 mm = 7.69 m.
