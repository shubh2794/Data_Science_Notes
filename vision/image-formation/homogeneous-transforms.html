02 2D primitives: points, lines, conics

Before anything can be transformed it has to be written down, and the choice of how to write a line down is not neutral. The representation chosen here — a line as a triple, exactly as symmetric with a point as a point is with it — is what makes the whole of the next section possible.

Points

the ordinary wayx = (x, y) ∈ ℝ² written as a column, x = [x, y]ᵀ the projective way, which §03 justifiesx̃ = (x̃, ỹ, w̃) ∈ ℙ² with x̃ ∼ λx̃ for every λ ≠ 0 ℙ² = ℝ³ ∖ {(0,0,0)} under that equivalence — the PROJECTIVE PLANE converting back, when it is possible at allx̃ = (x̃, ỹ, w̃) = w̃ · (x, y, 1) = w̃ · x̄ so x = x̃/w̃, y = ỹ/w̃ w̃ = 0 ⟹ no inhomogeneous form exists. These are the IDEAL POINTS, or POINTS AT INFINITY.

Two objects are being kept apart deliberately. x̃ is a whole equivalence class — a line through the origin of ℝ³ with the origin removed. x̄ = (x, y, 1) is one chosen member of that class, the augmented vector. Every formula below that ends in a division is really a step from x̃ back down to x̄.

Lines

A 2D line is also a homogeneous triple, and the equation relating a point to a line is a dot product that is symmetric in the two.

the line as a triple, and the incidence relationl̃ = (a, b, c) x̄ · l̃ = ax + by + c = 0 normalised so the first two entries are a unit normall = (n̂ₓ, n̂_y, d) = (n̂, d) with ‖n̂‖ = 1 then n̂ is the unit normal to the line, and −d is its SIGNED DISTANCE from the origin, and for any point, x̄ · l is the signed perpendicular distance from x to the line the polar form, which is what a Hough accumulator votes in — see part 4n̂ = (cos θ, sin θ) so a line is the pair (θ, d), and (θ, d) covers every line exactly once for θ ∈ [0, 2π), d ∈ ℝ the one line that cannot be normalisedl̃∞ = (0, 0, 1) the LINE AT INFINITY: n̂ = 0, so it has no direction and no distance. It contains exactly the ideal points, since (0,0,1) · (x̃, ỹ, 0) = 0 for every such point.

The two constructions that make the representation pay

Notice what has just happened. "Two lines meet in a point" is now true without exception. Parallel lines are no longer a special case to be tested for and branched around; they meet at a point whose last coordinate happens to be zero, and the cross product neither knows nor cares. That single removal of a special case is most of the argument for projective coordinates, and §03 is the rest of it.

Conics

a conic is the zero set of a homogeneous quadratic formx̃ᵀ Q x̃ = 0 with Q symmetric 3 × 3, defined up to scale written out, this is the familiar general second-degree equationa x² + b xy + c y² + d x + e y + f = 0 ⎡ a b/2 d/2 ⎤ Q = ⎢ b/2 c e/2 ⎥ 5 degrees of freedom: 6 entries, minus 1 for scale ⎣ d/2 e/2 f ⎦ which conic it is, is decided by the top-left 2 × 2 blockdet[[a, b/2], [b/2, c]] = ac − b²/4 > 0 ellipse (circle when a = c, b = 0) = 0 parabola < 0 hyperbola and the classification is exactly a statement about points at infinitythe conic meets l̃∞ in 0 real points → ellipse; 1 → parabola; 2 → hyperbola

That last line is the reward for the whole projective apparatus, and it is worth pausing on. A parabola and an ellipse are not different kinds of curve; they are the same kind of curve differing in how they cross the horizon. A parabola is an ellipse tangent to the line at infinity; a hyperbola is one that cuts it twice, and its two "branches" are one connected curve that passes through infinity and comes back. This is also why a circle photographed at an angle can come out as a hyperbola: the projective transformation of §05 moved the line at infinity.

Conics matter later — the image of a circle is a conic, the absolute conic is what metric calibration is really estimating — but this series uses them only as far as Part 4's ellipse fitting and Part 7's calibration. Five degrees of freedom means five points in general position determine a conic uniquely, which is the fact those parts will need.

Duality, in one line. Points and lines in ℙ² obey identical algebra: swap the words "point" and "line", swap "lies on" and "passes through", and every true statement stays true. "Two points determine a line" and "two lines determine a point" are the same theorem read twice. That is why the same cross product computes both, and why §06's rule for transforming a line is forced to be what it is.

03 Homogeneous coordinates, and the three problems they solve

Adding a coordinate that carries no information looks like a bad trade. It buys three things that are individually convenient and jointly decisive, and the third one is the reason a camera can be a matrix at all.

The construction

Take the plane ℝ² and embed it in ℝ³ as the affine plane w = 1. Now declare two vectors in ℝ³ equivalent when one is a non-zero multiple of the other. Each equivalence class is a line through the origin with the origin deleted, and almost every such line pierces the plane w = 1 exactly once — so almost every class is a point of the original plane. The exceptions are the lines that lie flat in the plane w = 0: parallel to the embedded copy, they never pierce it. Those classes are the new points, one for every direction in the plane, and they are precisely the points at infinity.

the projective plane, three equivalent descriptionsℙ² = (ℝ³ ∖ {0}) / ∼ where x̃ ∼ ỹ ⟺ x̃ = λỹ, λ ≠ 0 = { lines through the origin of ℝ³ } = ℝ² ∪ { one point per direction } the plane, plus a circle of directions with antipodes glued so a point of ℙ² is a whole ray, and dividing by w̃ just picks the representative that sits on w = 1λ(x̃, ỹ, w̃) all name ONE point; dehomogenise: (x̃/w̃, ỹ/w̃) when w̃ ≠ 0

Problem 1 — translation is not linear, and homogeneous coordinates make it linear

In ℝ², x ↦ x + t is not a linear map: it does not fix the origin, so no 2×2 matrix implements it. The consequence is that a rotation is a matrix, a scale is a matrix, and a translation is an addition, so a chain of transformations becomes an alternating mess of multiplies and adds that cannot be pre-multiplied into one object. Lift to ℝ³ and the translation becomes a shear, which is linear.

the whole of affine geometry becomes matrix multiplication ⎡ 1 0 t_x ⎤ ⎡ x ⎤ ⎡ x + t_x ⎤ ⎢ 0 1 t_y ⎥ ⎢ y ⎥ = ⎢ y + t_y ⎥ a SHEAR in ℝ³, restricted to the plane w = 1 ⎣ 0 0 1 ⎦ ⎣ 1 ⎦ ⎣ 1 ⎦ and therefore a chain of transformations is ONE matrix, computed oncerotate about the point c = T(c) · R(θ) · T(−c) — three matrices, multiplied, then applied N times

Problem 2 — the exceptions disappear

"Two distinct lines meet in exactly one point" is false in the Euclidean plane and true in the projective plane. "Two distinct points lie on exactly one line" is true in both. Removing the exception is not just tidiness: a piece of code that computes intersections no longer needs a parallelism test, and a piece of code that fits a vanishing point no longer needs a special case for a horizontal one. §02 showed the cross product doing it without noticing.

Problem 3 — perspective is a linear map, but only up here

This is the decisive one. Perspective projection divides by depth, and division is emphatically not linear. But in homogeneous coordinates the division is not performed by the matrix at all — it is performed by the act of reading the answer back out, i.e. by dehomogenisation. The matrix stays linear; the non-linearity is pushed into the equivalence relation, where it costs nothing.

perspective, as a linear map on ℙ³ → ℙ² ⎡ 1 0 0 0 ⎤ ⎡ X ⎤ ⎡ X ⎤ the matrix is linear; ⎢ 0 1 0 0 ⎥ ⎢ Y ⎥ = ⎢ Y ⎥ ∼ (X/Z, Y/Z) the DIVISION happens when you ⎣ 0 0 1 0 ⎦ ⎢ Z ⎥ ⎣ Z ⎦ dehomogenise, not inside it ⎣ 1 ⎦

Everything the rest of this page does with a matrix depends on that. P = K[R|t] is a single 3×4 matrix precisely because rotation, translation, projection and pixel scaling can all be written as linear maps on homogeneous coordinates and therefore composed by multiplication. Without the extra coordinate there is no camera matrix; there is a procedure.

A point of the plane is a ray through the origin — drag the ray flat and watch the point leave at exactly w̃ = 0 the ray is parallel to the image plane and never lands

What the figure is showing, stated precisely

  • Moving the λ slider does not move the point. That is the whole content of "defined up to scale". The marker slides all the way through the origin and out the other side, and the pierce point is unchanged — including for negative λ, which is why the projective plane glues antipodal directions together.
  • Lowering the elevation sends the pierce point outward. At elevation ψ the pierce point sits at distance cot ψ from the origin of the plane, so it goes to infinity like 1/ψ. There is no elevation at which it is "very far but still there and then suddenly gone" — it is finite for every ψ > 0 and undefined at exactly ψ = 0.
  • A line in the plane is a plane through the origin in ℝ³. Turn on the parallel-lines option: two parallel lines become two distinct planes, and two distinct planes through the origin always intersect in a line through the origin. That intersection line lies inside w̃ = 0, which is the geometric reason parallel lines meet at infinity — and the reason the set of ideal points is itself a line, the line at infinity.
  • The right-hand panel is what a camera would see. The ideal point is not off the edge of the frame; it is not in the frame's coordinate system at all. What a camera actually records in its place is the vanishing point of §13 — and a vanishing point is finite, because the camera's own projection maps the plane at infinity somewhere concrete.
The trap. Homogeneous coordinates are sometimes described as "adding a 1 at the end". That is the description of x̄, the augmented vector, not of x̃. The distinction bites the moment a matrix produces a last coordinate that is not 1: a homogeneous vector must be divided through before its first two entries mean anything. Forgetting the division is the single most common bug in hand-written projection code, and it is silent — the output is a plausible-looking pair of numbers that is wrong by a factor that varies across the image.

04 3D primitives: points, planes, lines, quadrics

Everything in §02 and §03 lifts one dimension without changing a word. The one thing that does not lift cleanly is the 3D line, and the reason is a counting argument worth doing, because it is the same argument that decides how many parameters a camera has.

Points and planes are dual, exactly as points and lines were

3D pointsp = (X, Y, Z) ∈ ℝ³ p̃ = (X̃, Ỹ, Z̃, w̃) ∈ ℙ³ p̄ = (X, Y, Z, 1) 3D planes, and the incidence relation — the exact analogue of a 2D linem̃ = (a, b, c, d) p̄ · m̃ = aX + bY + cZ + d = 0 normalised: m = (n̂, d) with ‖n̂‖ = 1, n̂ the unit normal and −d the signed distance to the origin the plane at infinity, which cannot be normalised, and contains every ideal pointm̃∞ = (0, 0, 0, 1) since (0,0,0,1) · (X̃, Ỹ, Z̃, 0) = 0 always a normal in spherical form — note the WARNING that comes with itn̂ = (cos θ cos φ, sin θ cos φ, sin φ) this does NOT sample directions uniformly — the density piles up at the poles by a factor 1/cos φ, which is why (θ, φ) is a poor accumulator for a 3D Hough transform and polar (θ, d) was fine in 2D

In ℙ³ the duality is between points and planes, not points and lines. Three points span a plane and three planes meet in a point; there is no cross-product shortcut because the cross product is a three-dimensional accident. The 4×4 analogue exists but is a determinant of a 3×4 matrix, computed one minor at a time.

3D lines, and the degrees-of-freedom argument

two points, and the segment between themr = (1 − λ)p + λq 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1 gives the SEGMENT; all λ ∈ ℝ gives the line in homogeneous form, where the second point may be at infinityr̃ = μp̃ + λq̃ and if q̃ = (d̂, 0), then d̂ is the DIRECTION and r = p + λd̂ counting: the endpoint form has six parameters but a line has only four2 endpoints × 3 coordinates = 6 parameters − 1 for sliding the first point along the line − 1 for sliding the second point along the line = 4 genuine degrees of freedom a minimal parameterisation: pin the two points to two fixed planese.g. the (x, y) where the line crosses z = 0 and where it crosses z = 1 → exactly 4 numbers but this breaks for lines parallel to those planes, which is why no single chart covers all lines

The four-parameter count is the same kind of argument that will decide, in §14, whether the calibration matrix has five, seven or eight degrees of freedom. Both times the recipe is: write down an over-parameterised representation, then subtract one for each direction you can move the parameters without moving the object. Over-parameterisation is not a mistake — it is usually the numerically better choice — but knowing the true count tells you how many independent measurements you need before the problem is determined, which is exactly what Part 7 will be counting.

The symmetric alternative, for completeness. A representation that treats all directions alike takes the six independent entries of the skew-symmetric 4×4 matrix L = p̃q̃ᵀ − q̃p̃ᵀ. It is homogeneous — one scale to remove — and satisfies det L = 0, a single quadratic constraint, which brings six down to 6 − 1 − 1 = 4 as required. The four-vs-six tension is real but rarely worth the machinery in practice: estimating a direction plus a point on the visible portion of the line is usually enough, and line segments want the two endpoints anyway.

Quadrics

the 3D analogue of a conic — spheres, ellipsoids, cylinders, cones, hyperboloidsp̃ᵀ Q p̃ = 0 Q symmetric 4 × 4, up to scale ⟹ 10 − 1 = 9 degrees of freedom worked — the unit sphereX² + Y² + Z² − 1 = 0 ⟺ Q = diag(1, 1, 1, −1) check: (X, Y, Z, 1)ᵀ diag(1,1,1,−1) (X, Y, Z, 1) = X² + Y² + Z² − 1 ✓

Quadrics play no further role in this series, but the count is worth carrying: nine parameters means nine points determine a quadric, and the fact that a sphere's silhouette in an image is a conic is the basis of several calibration tricks that Part 7 mentions.

05 The 2D transformation hierarchy — the one table to memorise

Five transformations, nested one inside the next, each one a strictly larger group than the last. Every alignment problem in vision is a choice of one of these five, and choosing wrongly is the most common modelling error in the subject: too small a group and the model cannot fit the data, too large and it fits the noise as well.

The five levels

TRANSLATION — 2 DoFx′ = x + t x′ = [I | t] x̄ I is 2 × 2 RIGID / EUCLIDEAN — 3 DoF: one angle, two offsetsx′ = Rx + t x′ = [R | t] x̄ R = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], RRᵀ = I, |R| = +1 SIMILARITY (scaled rotation) — 4 DoFx′ = sRx + t x′ = [sR | t] x̄ = [[a, −b, tₓ], [b, a, t_y]] x̄ with a = s cos θ, b = s sin θ the constraint a² + b² = 1 is DROPPED; s = √(a² + b²) is whatever it turns out to be AFFINE — 6 DoF: an arbitrary 2 × 3 matrixx′ = A x̄ = [[a₀₀, a₀₁, a₀₂], [a₁₀, a₁₁, a₁₂]] x̄ PROJECTIVE (homography, perspective transform) — 8 DoFx̃′ = H̃ x̃ H̃ an arbitrary 3 × 3 matrix, defined only up to scale x′ = (h₀₀x + h₀₁y + h₀₂) / (h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂) y′ = (h₁₀x + h₁₁y + h₁₂) / (h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂) and THAT DIVISION is the entire difference between affine and projective

Where the degree-of-freedom counts come from. Not memorised — derived, in one line each. Projective: a 3×3 matrix has 9 entries and is defined up to scale, so 9 − 1 = 8. Affine: the bottom row is pinned to [0 0 1], leaving the 2×3 block, so 6. Similarity: 2 for t, 1 for θ, 1 for s, so 4. Rigid: drop the scale, 3. Translation: drop the angle, 2. Equivalently, in terms of point correspondences: each matched point gives two equations, so a homography needs 4 points, an affine 3, a similarity 2, a rigid 2 (one over-determined), a translation 1. Those counts are the minimal sample sizes Part 6's robust estimator will draw.

They are groups, and nested ones

Each level is closed under composition and inversion, and each is a subgroup of the next. That is not decoration: it means the composition of two homographies is a homography, so a chain of warps can be collapsed into one matrix and applied once — which is why image stitching resamples the source image exactly once rather than once per pair.

Three lines of that table carry almost all the practical content. The similarity multiplies area by s², exactly. The affine multiplies area by |det A|, exactly, and that is the geometric meaning of a determinant. The projective multiplies area by nothing — the scale factor is different at every point, because the denominator h₂₀x + h₂₁y + h₂₂ varies across the plane. That single fact is why a photograph of a tiled floor shows tiles of different sizes, and why the "area" column stops being meaningful at the last row.

One shape, five levels — drive each level's parameters and watch which measurements stop moving green = this quantity is invariant at this level, red = it has changed
  • Start at translation and walk up. At each step exactly one row of the measurement table turns red, and it turns red permanently. That staircase is the hierarchy; nothing else needs memorising.
  • The projective level is the only one where the grid stops being a grid. Under affine, parallel lines stay parallel — the transformed grid is a lattice of parallelograms. Under projective, the two families converge to two vanishing points, and the line joining those two points is the image of the line at infinity. That line is the horizon of §13, and it is the single visual signature of a perspective image.
  • Setting both tilt sliders to zero collapses projective to affine. The bottom row becomes [0, 0, 1], the denominator becomes the constant 1, and the division does nothing. Affine is not "approximately" a homography; it is exactly the homography that fixes the line at infinity.
  • The cross ratio stays put at every level, including the last. It is the invariant of projective geometry in the same way length is the invariant of Euclidean geometry — the one number four points on a line always agree about, whatever camera looked at them.
The modelling error. "Use a homography, it is the most general." A homography has 8 parameters; fitting it to a scene that only translated wastes 6 of them on noise, and the extra freedom will happily bend the image to explain mismatched points. Choose the smallest group that can express the true motion. Two images from a camera on a tripod that only rotated are related by a homography exactly (§16); two images of a flat wall are related by a homography exactly; almost everything else is not, and a homography fitted to it is a lie that fits well in the middle and diverges at the edges.

06 Composing transforms, and how a line transforms

Two short results that get used constantly and misremembered constantly: matrix multiplication does not commute, so the order of a chain is part of its meaning; and a line does not transform the way a point does — it transforms by the inverse transpose.

Order is meaning

Transformations compose right-to-left, matching the order the matrices are applied to the vector. H₂H₁x means "do H₁, then H₂". The two orders of the same pair are genuinely different maps.

worked — rotate 90° then translate by (3, 0), versus translate then rotateR = R(90°) = [[0, −1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]] T = [[1, 0, 3], [0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 1]] T·R = [[0, −1, 3], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]] applied to (1, 0): → (3, 1) R·T = [[0, −1, 0], [1, 0, 3], [0, 0, 1]] applied to (1, 0): → (0, 4) the standard idiom this forces: to rotate ABOUT a point c, sandwichH = T(c) · R(θ) · T(−c) move c to the origin, rotate, move back with c = (2, 1), θ = 90°: H = [[0, −1, 3], [1, 0, −1], [0, 0, 1]], and H·(2,1,1) = (2,1,1) ✓ c is fixed

The sandwich is worth internalising because the same shape recurs in three dimensions for rotating a camera about a point that is not its centre, and again in §16 as the change-of-basis that turns "the camera moved" into "the world moved the other way".

Co-vectors: a line transforms by H⁻ᵀ

A point is a column that gets multiplied. A line is defined by an equation, and equations transform contravariantly to whatever satisfies them. The derivation is three lines and worth doing once, because getting it wrong produces a plausible-looking line that is subtly in the wrong place.

the requirement: if x̃ is on l̃, then H̃x̃ must be on the transformed line l̃′l̃ᵀx̃ = 0 and we want l̃′ᵀ(H̃x̃) = 0 for every such x̃ insert the identity H̃⁻¹H̃ and read off the answerl̃ᵀx̃ = l̃ᵀ(H̃⁻¹H̃)x̃ = (H̃⁻ᵀl̃)ᵀ(H̃x̃) = 0 sol̃′ = H̃⁻ᵀ l̃ the INVERSE TRANSPOSE, not H̃ equivalently, since H̃ is homogeneous, l̃′ ∼ adj(H̃)ᵀ l̃ — no inverse needed, which matters when H̃ is near-singular and the same rule in 3D, for a plane or a surface NORMALm̃′ = M̃⁻ᵀ m̃ — which is why a normal must NOT be transformed with the model matrix in a renderer
The bug. Transforming a surface normal with the same matrix as the vertices. Under a non-uniform scale — squash a sphere into an ellipsoid — the vertices flatten and so, wrongly, do the normals, which then stop being perpendicular to the surface and the shading of §21 goes visibly wrong. Normals are co-vectors: transform them by the inverse transpose of the upper 3×3 block. For a rotation, R⁻ᵀ = R, which is exactly why the bug hides until someone adds a scale.

Two transformations that do not fit the hierarchy

restricted
Stretch / squash
x′ = sₓx + tₓ, y′ = s_yy + t_y. Four parameters, a restricted affine — but it is not a subgroup containing the similarities, because composing it with a rotation leaves the family. Useful for correcting non-square pixels and for anamorphic lenses; awkward to place in the table, which is why it is not in it.
approximation
Planar surface flow
x′ = a₀ + a₁x + a₂y + a₆x² + a₇xy and its partner. Eight parameters, and it is the second-order Taylor expansion of a homography for small motion. Its attraction is that it is linear in the unknowns aₖ, so estimating it is least squares rather than an iterative solve — the trade Part 6 weighs.
interpolant
Bilinear
x′ = a₀ + a₁x + a₂y + a₆xy and its partner. Also eight parameters, and it interpolates the motion of four corners exactly. But it does not preserve straight lines except those parallel to the axes, so it is a warp, not a projective model — do not use it where a homography is meant.

07 The 3D hierarchy

Why R has three degrees of freedom, and why that is awkward

A 3×3 rotation matrix has nine entries, but orthonormality RᵀR = I imposes six independent constraints — three unit-length conditions on the columns and three mutual orthogonality conditions — leaving 9 − 6 = 3. The determinant condition |R| = +1 costs nothing further; it merely selects one of the two disconnected components of the orthogonal group, ruling out reflections.

the group of 3D rotationsSO(3) = { R ∈ ℝ³ˣ³ : RᵀR = I, det R = +1 } dimension 3 the count9 free entries − 3 (columns are unit) − 3 (columns are mutually orthogonal) = 3 and the awkward fact that all of §09–§11 is aboutSO(3) is a 3-dimensional manifold that is NOT homeomorphic to ℝ³. It is closed and curved, so NO set of three numbers can parameterise it smoothly everywhere. Every 3-parameter chart must break somewhere. Euler angles break at gimbal lock (§09); axis/angle breaks at θ = 0 and θ = π (§10). The escape is to use FOUR numbers (§11).

That is a theorem, not a warning about sloppy implementations: it is a consequence of the topology of SO(3), which is real projective 3-space. Any continuous map from an open subset of ℝ³ onto SO(3) must either fail to be injective or fail to be a local homeomorphism somewhere. Nothing you can code will avoid it; you can only choose where the failure happens and whether you notice.

The difference between 2D and 3D, in one sentence. In the plane, a rotation is one number and the number composes by addition — θ₁ + θ₂, commutatively, with no singularities. In space, a rotation is three numbers, they do not compose by addition, they do not commute, and no three-number scheme is free of a breakdown. Everything hard about 3D pose comes from that sentence.
