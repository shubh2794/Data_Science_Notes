18 Optics: the thin lens, aperture, focus and vignetting

A pinhole has infinite depth of field and admits almost no light. A lens trades the first for the second. Everything photographers argue about — aperture, depth of field, bokeh, the darkening at the corners — is that one trade, and all of it is derivable from a single equation.

The lens law

the thin lens equation1/z_o + 1/z_i = 1/f z_o = distance to the object, z_i = distance to its focused image workedf = 100 mm, z_o = 5000 mm ⟹ 1/z_i = 1/100 − 1/5000 = 0.0098 ⟹ z_i = 102.0408 mm magnification z_i/z_o = 0.020408, so a 1 m object images 20.4 mm across the limit that connects a lens to the pinhole model of §13z_o → ∞ ⟹ z_i = f. So focusing at infinity puts the sensor exactly f behind the lens, and the pinhole camera of focal length f is the lens focused at infinity. That is the ONLY sense in which the f in K is "the focal length of the lens". For a subject at 5 m and f = 100 mm the sensor is at 102.04 mm, 2% out — small, but the reason the effective f of a macro lens differs noticeably from its marked value.

Aperture, f-number, and the circle of confusion

the f-number is nothing but a way of writing the aperture diameterN = f/# = f / d ⟹ d = f / N f/2 on a 50 mm lens means d = 25 mm; f/8 means d = 6.25 mm. the standard series, and why it looks arbitrary1, 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22, 32 — successive powers of √2 because AREA ∝ d² ∝ 1/N², so a factor √2 in N halves the light. One "stop" = one factor of two. and one stop of aperture is interchangeable with one stop of exposure time: f/2 at 1/250 s delivers the same light as f/2.8 at 1/125 s. the CIRCLE OF CONFUSION — how big a point becomes when the sensor is in the wrong placec = d · |Δz_i| / z_i by similar triangles on the converging cone worked, f = 100 mm, z_i = 102.04 mm, sensor 1 mm out of position: at f/2 (d = 50 mm): c = 50 × 1/102.04 = 0.490 mm — badly out of focus at f/8 (d = 12.5 mm): c = 12.5 × 1/102.04 = 0.1225 mm — four times sharper, for the same error a pinhole is the limit d → 0: c = 0 for every z_i, which is why a pinhole never needs focusing.

Depth of field

the hyperfocal distance — focus here and everything from H/2 to infinity is acceptably sharpH = f²/(N c) + f and the near and far limits for a subject at z_onear = z_o(H − f) / (H + z_o − 2f) far = z_o(H − f) / (H − z_o) (far = ∞ once z_o ≥ H) worked, 50 mm lens, c = 0.02 mm, subject at 3 m f/1.4 H = 89.3 m sharp from 2.904 m to 3.103 m total 0.199 m f/2.8 H = 44.7 m sharp from 2.814 m to 3.212 m total 0.398 m f/5.6 H = 22.4 m sharp from 2.650 m to 3.457 m total 0.807 m f/11 H = 11.4 m sharp from 2.382 m to 4.052 m total 1.670 m every stop roughly doubles the depth of field — and costs a stop of light.

For computer vision the relevant consequence is not aesthetic. A shallow depth of field blurs part of the scene, and blur destroys the high frequencies that every feature detector in Part 4 depends on. Stopping down recovers them but forces a longer exposure, which introduces motion blur, or a higher gain, which introduces noise (§25). There is no setting that avoids all three; the choice is which degradation the algorithm downstream tolerates best.

Vignetting: the cos⁴ law

The corners of an image are darker than the centre even with a perfectly uniform scene, and the largest part of the effect is pure geometry rather than a lens defect.

four independent factors, each one a cosine1. the surface patch is foreshortened as seen from the lens × cos α 2. it is further away: r_o = z_o/cos α, and irradiance falls as 1/r_o² × cos² α 3. the aperture, seen obliquely, is an ellipse d × d cos α × cos α multiplying them gives the FUNDAMENTAL RADIOMETRIC RELATIONE = L · (π/4) · (d/f)² · cos⁴ α = L · (π/4) · (1/N²) · cos⁴ α scene radiance L, pixel irradiance E, off-axis angle α. Note the 1/N² — the f-number again. how much it costs, on the running camera α = 10° → cos⁴ = 0.9406 α = 20° → 0.7797 α = 30° → 0.5625 the frame corner is at arctan(400/500) = 38.66° off axis → cos⁴ = 0.372 so the corners receive 37% of the light the centre does, from geometry alone.

That is a large effect, and it matters for anything photometric: stereo matching that compares intensities across a wide-angle image, exposure blending in a panorama, or a background model that expects a flat response. It is smooth and radially symmetric, so it calibrates out easily — but only if someone remembers it exists. Mechanical vignetting, from internal occlusion by the lens barrel, is separate, is not described by a formula, and unlike the cos⁴ term it reduces when you stop down.

aberration
The five Seidel aberrations
Spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, curvature of field, and distortion — the third-order departures from ideal imaging that a single glass element cannot avoid. Compound lenses exist to cancel them against each other. Only the last one is modelled in §17; the others appear as a spatially varying blur.
aberration
Chromatic
The refractive index of glass varies with wavelength, so each colour focuses at a slightly different distance and with a slightly different magnification. The transverse part is a per-channel radial distortion and calibrates out with §17's model applied three times. The longitudinal part is a per-channel blur and is much harder to undo, because the attenuated high frequencies are not recoverable.
model failure
No single nodal point
A compound lens has a front and a rear nodal point, not one centre. For most work only the front one matters — it is the point to rotate about when shooting a parallax-free panorama. Fisheye and catadioptric systems have no single centre at all, and then the only honest model is a per-pixel lookup from pixel to 3D ray, not to a direction.
One lens, three sliders — focus, aperture, and the depth of field that falls out of them the blur circle is computed from the ray cone, not drawn to taste

19 Photometric formation: where the numbers come from

Geometry decides where a scene point lands. Photometry decides what value is stored there, and it is a chain of three multiplications: how much light arrives, what fraction the surface sends towards the camera, and how much of that the optics deliver to the well.

a source emits L(λ)→falls off as 1/r²→foreshortened by cos θᵢ→scattered by the BRDF→vignetted by cos⁴α→integrated over the pixel→counted as photons

Light sources

source type
Point source
A single location, an intensity, and a spectrum L(λ). Its irradiance falls as 1/r², because the same energy spreads over a sphere whose area grows as r². A source at infinity — the sun — has no falloff across a scene and gives parallel rays, which is why sunlit shadows are sharp and parallel.
source type
Area source
A finite emitting surface, e.g. a ceiling panel. It produces penumbrae — soft shadow edges whose width is set by the ratio of source size to distance — and every shading model that assumes point sources gets shadow boundaries wrong. Even the sun must be treated as an area source if you care about penumbra width.
source type
Environment map
L(v̂; λ) — a colour for every incident direction, and nothing else. It assumes every source is at infinity, so it cannot express falloff or parallax, but it captures a whole complicated sky or courtyard in one lookup. A photograph of a mirrored sphere is a cheap way to acquire one.
what is ignored
Everything after the first bounce
The model below is local: light goes source → surface → camera, once. Real light bounces repeatedly, so surfaces are lit by their neighbours (colour bleeding), corners are darker than flats (ambient occlusion), and shadows are not black. Those are §20's global-illumination footnote, and every one of them breaks shape-from-shading.

The two quantities that must not be confused

RADIANCE — the property of a surface or a ray, and the thing an image measuresL = power per unit projected area per unit solid angle W · m⁻² · sr⁻¹ It is constant along a ray in a vacuum. That invariance is why a wall looks equally bright from one metre and from ten — the 1/r² falloff in power is exactly cancelled by the 1/r² shrinkage of the solid angle a pixel subtends. IRRADIANCE — the property of a receiving surface, e.g. the sensorE = power per unit area W · m⁻² and §18's relation is exactly the bridge between themE = L · (π/4)(d/f)² cos⁴α scene radiance in, pixel irradiance out

The invariance of radiance along a ray is the single most useful fact in photometric vision, and it is the reason a pixel value can be treated as a property of the surface patch rather than of the distance to it. It fails the moment there is fog, smoke or water between the surface and the lens, which is precisely why every de-hazing method begins by writing down how much it fails by.

20 The BRDF and the reflection equation

A surface's entire optical character — matte, glossy, metallic, brushed — is one four-dimensional function. Everything else in this section is a cheap approximation to it, and knowing which corner of it each approximation covers is what tells you when the approximation will embarrass you.

Definition

the bidirectional reflectance distribution functionf_r(θᵢ, φᵢ, θ_r, φ_r; λ) how much of what arrives from (θᵢ, φᵢ) leaves towards (θ_r, φ_r) four angles, plus wavelength — angles measured in the local frame (d̂ₓ, d̂_y, n̂) on the surface two properties that hold for physical reasons, not by convention1. HELMHOLTZ RECIPROCITY: f_r(v̂ᵢ, v̂_r) = f_r(v̂_r, v̂ᵢ) — swap the light and the eye, same answer. 2. ENERGY CONSERVATION: ∫ f_r(v̂ᵢ, v̂_r) cos θ_r dv̂_r ≤ 1 for every v̂ᵢ. A model that violates this makes surfaces emit more than they receive, and a renderer built on it produces images that get brighter with each bounce. ISOTROPIC surfaces have no preferred direction on the surface, dropping 4D to 3Df_r(θᵢ, θ_r, |φ_r − φᵢ|; λ) or equivalently f_r(v̂ᵢ, v̂_r, n̂; λ) the exceptions are anisotropic: brushed metal, satin, hair — where the scratch direction matters

The reflection equation

integrate the arriving light against the BRDF, weighted by foreshorteningL_r(v̂_r; λ) = ∫ Lᵢ(v̂ᵢ; λ) · f_r(v̂ᵢ, v̂_r, n̂; λ) · cos⁺θᵢ dv̂ᵢ with the clamp that stops light arriving from below the surfacecos⁺θᵢ = max(0, cos θᵢ) = max(0, v̂ᵢ · n̂) = [v̂ᵢ · n̂]⁺ and for a finite set of point sources, the integral collapses to a sumL_r(v̂_r; λ) = Σᵢ Lᵢ(λ) · f_r(v̂ᵢ, v̂_r, n̂; λ) · [v̂ᵢ · n̂]⁺
This is the local rendering equation. The full rendering equation adds an emitted term and, crucially, lets Lᵢ itself be the L_r of every other surface in the scene — making it an integral equation rather than an integral. Solving it is what ray tracing and radiosity do: ray tracing follows individual rays through many bounces and suits scenes dominated by specular transport; radiosity sets up and solves a large linear system over patch-to-patch form factors, and suits diffuse scenes. Neither is needed to read an image, but both explain effects a local model cannot: colour bleeding, soft shadows, darkening in creases, and caustics.

21 Shading: from a normal to a pixel value, and back

The diffuse term is worth its own section, because it is the one component whose dependence on geometry is simple enough to invert — and because the exact sense in which it cannot be inverted is one of the sharpest results in the subject.

The Lambertian sphere, worked

a matte surface of albedo ρ under one distant source of intensity L, direction l̂I(x, y) = ρ · L · max(0, n̂(x, y) · l̂) on a sphere of radius R at the origin, seen orthographically, the normal is freen̂(x, y) = ( x/R , y/R , √(1 − (x² + y²)/R²) ) for x² + y² ≤ R² so the image of a Lambertian sphere is, exactlyI(x, y) = ρL · max(0, (x lₓ + y l_y)/R + l_z√(1 − (x²+y²)/R²) ) and the TERMINATOR — the curve where the surface turns away from the light — is where n̂ · l̂ = 0that is a great circle on the sphere, and its orthographic image is an ELLIPSE. A perfect circle only when the light is exactly behind the camera (l̂ = viewing direction), in which case the terminator is the silhouette itself and the whole visible sphere is lit. the cosine, tabulated — this is what "shading" actually isθᵢ = 0° → n̂·l̂ = 1.0000 θᵢ = 60° → 0.5000 θᵢ = 30° → 0.8660 θᵢ = 90° → 0.0000 and beyond it, clamped to 0 θᵢ = 45° → 0.7071 the clamp is a SELF-SHADOW: the surface faces away.

Two kinds of shadow, which behave completely differently

shadow type
Attached (self) shadow
Where n̂ · l̂ ≤ 0 — the surface has turned away from the light. It is a local property, computable from the normal alone, and its boundary is the terminator. Its edge is soft even with a point source, because the cosine goes to zero smoothly.
shadow type
Cast shadow
Where something else is between the surface and the light. It is a global property — you cannot decide it without knowing the rest of the scene — and with a point source its edge is a step. That difference in edge profile is often the only way to tell the two apart in one image.
what breaks
Inter-reflection
Real shadows are not black: they are lit by everything else in the room. The ambient term is a crude constant stand-in, and the residual — darkening in creases and corners where less of the environment is visible — is what "ambient occlusion" names and what shape-from-shading systematically misreads as curvature.

Inverting it: shape from shading, and the exact ambiguity

Given one image of a known-albedo Lambertian surface under a known light, can the normals be recovered? Partly — and the part that cannot be is a precise, three-parameter family, not a vague uncertainty.

what one pixel gives youone equation, n̂ · l̂ = I/(ρL), in the TWO unknowns of a unit normal. So a pixel constrains n̂ to a CONE about l̂ — a one-parameter family, not a point. Smoothness or integrability assumptions are what pick one out, and they are assumptions. with THREE images under three different known lights — PHOTOMETRIC STEREO — it is exactstack the three equations: I = L · (ρn̂) with the ROWS of the 3 × 3 matrix L being l̂₁ᵀ, l̂₂ᵀ, l̂₃ᵀ ρn̂ = L⁻¹I, then ρ = ‖L⁻¹I‖ and n̂ = L⁻¹I / ‖L⁻¹I‖ — albedo and normal, separately, in closed form. The condition is that the three light directions be linearly independent, i.e. not coplanar. and if the lights are UNKNOWN, a residual ambiguity survives no matter how many images you takereplacing the surface's normal field n̂ by A⁻ᵀn̂ and every light l̂ by Al̂, for any invertible A, leaves every product n̂ · l̂ unchanged — so all such scenes produce identical images. Imposing integrability (the normals must come from an actual surface) cuts the 9 parameters of A down to 3: this is the GENERALISED BAS-RELIEF ambiguity, and it is not removable from shading alone. It is why a bas-relief carving, flattened to a few centimetres of depth, looks like a full-depth figure under the right light.
The everyday version. A crater photographed from above looks like a dome when the image is turned upside down. Both interpretations are exactly consistent with the pixels; the eye disambiguates them with a prior that light comes from above. That is not a defect of human vision — it is the correct response to a genuinely underdetermined problem, and any algorithm doing shape from shading is making the same guess, whether or not it admits to it.
A Lambertian sphere with a movable light — drag in the panel to move it turn on the specular term and watch the highlight track the mirror direction, not the light
  • The brightest point of the diffuse sphere is where the normal points at the light — not the centre of the disc, unless the light is directly behind the camera. Its image position is exactly (R lₓ, R l_y), which is a direct read-out of the light direction and is the basis of the simplest light-direction estimators.
  • The terminator is an ellipse, and its eccentricity encodes the light's elevation. When the light is in the image plane the ellipse degenerates to a straight line through the sphere's centre — exactly half the disc is lit.
  • Turn up k_s. The highlight appears somewhere else entirely: at the point whose mirror direction points at the camera, which for a viewer along the axis is halfway between the light direction and the view direction. Diffuse maximum and specular maximum coincide only when the light is at the camera.
  • The iso-intensity contours are the curves of constant n̂ · l̂, i.e. circles of constant angle from the light on the sphere — a family of nested ellipses in the image. Each one is a whole cone of possible normals, which is the picture of why one image is not enough.
