Image Processing

Part 1 finished with an array: a rectangular grid of numbers, produced by a known optical and electronic process, with the depth and the spectrum already thrown away. 01 Three kinds of operator, and what this page does not cover

Every operator on this page is classified by one question: how much of the input is an output pixel allowed to see? The answer determines the cost, the invariances, the artefacts, and — crucially — what can be said about the result.

02 Point operators: one pixel in, one pixel out

The simplest possible image operator ignores every pixel but one. That constraint sounds crippling and is in fact the reason point operators are the workhorse of the whole pipeline: they cannot blur, cannot invent structure, cannot move an edge, and can be implemented as a 256-entry lookup table.

The definition

a point operator, continuous and discreteg(x) = h(f(x)) one input image g(x) = h(f₀(x), …, f_n(x)) several input images, combined pixelwise g(i, j) = h(f(i, j)) on the lattice, which is all we ever compute the two that matter most: GAIN and BIASg(x) = a·f(x) + b a > 0 is the GAIN — loosely, "contrast" b is the BIAS — loosely, "brightness" and their spatially varying versions, which are still point operators at each pixelg(x) = a(x)·f(x) + b(x) a graduated filter; a vignetting correction; a flat-field division

"Loosely" is doing real work in that box. Gain and bias are not contrast and brightness in any perceptual sense; they are the slope and intercept of a straight line. What they do to the two summary statistics is exact and worth memorising, because it is the reason the pair is used at all.

Linear, and not

Multiplicative gain is a linear operator in the exact algebraic sense: it obeys superposition, h(f₀ + f₁) = h(f₀) + h(f₁). Adding a bias does not — a(f₀ + f₁) + b ≠ (af₀ + b) + (af₁ + b) unless b = 0. The affine map is therefore not linear, it is affine, and the distinction is exactly the one Part 1 §03 solved with an extra coordinate. Nobody bothers here, because a point operator is never composed into a matrix chain.

Genuinely non-linear point operators are everywhere and are all just differently shaped curves through the same [0,1] → [0,1] box:

  • Gamma, g = f^γ. Defined and justified in Part 1 §23. §03 below only concerns where in a pipeline you must undo it.
  • Squaring, g = f². Not linear, and used precisely because it is not: the square of a band-pass filtered signal is a local energy estimate, which is how §14's oriented energy and Part 4's corner responses are built.
  • Thresholding, θ(f, t) = 1 if f ≥ t else 0. The most violently non-linear operator on the page, and the gateway to §18's morphology. Choosing t well is Part 5's problem, not this page's.
  • Quantisation / posterisation, g = round(f·L)/L. A staircase. It is what an eight-bit container does to you whether you ask or not, and §07 is about what happens when a point operator amplifies its steps.
  • Colour matrices, g = M f with f a 3-vector. Still a point operator — it looks at one pixel — but a vector-valued one. White balance, RGB→YCbCr, and the primaries conversion of Part 1 §23 are all this.

Compositing and matting, the two-image case

The multi-image form h(f₀, f₁) covers dissolves, differencing, and — most importantly — compositing a foreground over a background using a per-pixel opacity α. This is a point operator with three inputs, and it is the reason images carry a fourth channel at all.

That count is the honest statement of why matting is hard, and it is the same shape of argument as Part 1 §22's metamers: the measurement is a projection and the projection has a kernel. Blue-screening works only because it legislates B, which removes three unknowns and makes the system determined.

The trap. Blending, resizing and filtering are all weighted averages, and a weighted average of gamma-encoded code values is not the gamma encoding of the weighted average. Averaging code 0 and code 255 gives code 127.5, whose linear luminance is 0.2176 — but the true midpoint in light is 0.5, which encodes to code 186.1. That is a 59-code-value error, and it is why a downsampled image with fine black-and-white detail comes out too dark. §03 says what to do about it.

03 Tone curves, clipping, and where gamma bites

Every point operator on an eight-bit image is a curve from 256 inputs to 256 outputs. Drawing the curve, rather than reasoning about the formula, tells you immediately what it will destroy — because destruction is visible as a flat segment or as a segment that leaves the box.

The lookup table is the operator

For an eight-bit image, every scalar point operator, however exotic, is one array T[0…255]. Compute it once and the per-pixel cost is one memory read, regardless of whether the formula involved a power, an exponential or a spline. Three consequences follow immediately and are worth stating because people rediscover them painfully:

  • Composing point operators is composing tables. Two operators in sequence is T₂[T₁[·]], one table. There is never a reason to make two passes over the pixels.
  • The table is where the information loss is visible. If T maps two distinct inputs to the same output, those two values are now indistinguishable and no later operator can separate them. Count the distinct values in T and you have counted exactly how many grey levels survive.
  • A monotone table is invertible only where it is strictly increasing. A flat run in T is a permanent merge; a clipped run at 0 or 255 is a permanent collapse.

Clipping, and why it is not the same as compression

what the display container forcesg = clip(a·f + b, 0, 255) Above 255: every value maps to 255. The DERIVATIVE of the operator is zero there. Below 0: every value maps to 0. Same. the alternative — a soft shoulder, which compresses instead of clippingg = 255 · (1 − exp(−a·f/255)) or a smoothstep, or an S-curve Slope falls smoothly to a small positive number, never to exactly zero. Contrast in the highlights is reduced; it is not deleted.

The difference matters because clipping is not recoverable and compression is. A clipped highlight has no information in it at all — every pixel in it is the number 255 — so Part 4's gradient there is exactly zero and no detector will find anything. A compressed highlight has a small gradient, which is noisy but present.

Where gamma bites, in one rule

The rule. Operators that are weighted averages of pixel values must be done in linear light: blurring, resizing, pyramid construction, compositing, blending, demosaicing. Operators that are monotone reshapings of a single value can be done in either space, and are usually done in the encoded space because that is where the code values are perceptually spaced: tone curves, thresholds, histogram equalisation. The full argument for why the encoding exists is Part 1 §23; the operational content is this one sentence, and violating it is the single most common cause of images that are "slightly wrong and nobody can say why".

Concretely: to blur an sRGB image correctly you decode with the sRGB transfer function, filter, then re-encode. Skipping the round trip darkens every blurred edge, because the encoding is concave and Jensen's inequality then guarantees that the average of the encoded values is below the encoding of the average. That inequality is not an approximation — it is the definition of concavity — and it is the rigorous version of the 127.5-versus-186.1 trap in §02.

What the figure is showing, stated precisely

  • The slope of the curve is the local contrast gain. Where T′(v) > 1 the histogram is stretched apart and gaps open between output levels; where T′(v) < 1 it is squeezed and levels merge. Total pixel count is conserved, so the output histogram is the input histogram divided by the slope. That is the change-of-variables formula for a density, and it is the entire mechanism behind §05.
  • Posterising to L levels leaves exactly L spikes, no matter what the input looked like. The distinct-level readout drops to L immediately. Nothing downstream can undo it.
  • Clipping shows up as a bar at an end of the histogram, and as a flat run in the curve. Push the gain up with the bias at zero and watch the count of clipped pixels rise while the standard deviation stops rising with it — the spread saturates because the container is full.
  • The default is the identity — a = 1, b = 0, γ = 1 — so the two histograms coincide and no pixel is clipped. Every claim above is something you have to move a slider to see.

04 Histograms and the cumulative distribution

A histogram throws away every pixel's location and keeps only how many pixels had each value. That is a catastrophic loss of information and exactly the right summary for choosing a point operator, because a point operator is blind to location too.

The percentile reading is the one that matters. Ask "what fraction of the class scored at or below this grade?" and the answer is c. That is the whole idea of §05: if you can map every grade to its percentile, the grades become uniformly spread by construction — because percentiles are, by definition, uniformly spread.

Three things a histogram cannot tell you

  • Where anything is. An image and any permutation of its pixels have identical histograms. A photograph of a face and the same pixels shuffled into noise are indistinguishable to every operator on §02's list. This is why point operators can never sharpen, denoise or segment.
  • Whether the image is in focus. Blur redistributes values only slightly; the histogram of a blurred image is close to that of the sharp one. Sharpness lives in the joint statistics of neighbouring pixels, which is §08 onwards.
  • What the "right" exposure is. A histogram piled against the left edge might be an under-exposed photograph or a correct photograph of a dark scene. Nothing in the numbers distinguishes them, which is why every automatic exposure algorithm is a heuristic with an assumption bolted on — most commonly the grey-world assumption that the scene averages to middle grey.
Per-channel or on luminance? Equalising R, G and B independently changes the hue of every pixel, because it applies three different curves to the three components of one colour. The usual fix is to convert to a space with a separate lightness axis, apply the curve to lightness only, and convert back — using the colour spaces of Part 1 §23. The cost is that the colour gamut can be exceeded after the round trip, requiring a clip that reintroduces exactly the problem §03 warned about.

05 Histogram equalisation, and what it actually guarantees

Take the tone curve to be the cumulative distribution itself. The result is the one automatic contrast adjustment that needs no parameters at all — and the one whose guarantee is most often overstated.

The construction

That cancellation is the whole idea and it is worth staring at. The curve is steep exactly where pixels are crowded, and steepness is what spreads them apart. The map does not know or care what the image contains; it only knows that a percentile axis is uniform by construction.

What is guaranteed, and what is not

The overstatement. "Histogram equalisation makes the histogram flat." On a discrete image it does not, and cannot. T is a function on {0,…,255}; it can only merge levels, never split one. Every pixel that had value v still has one common value T(v) afterwards, so a tall spike stays a tall spike — it just moves. What equalisation actually delivers is that the cumulative distribution is pushed towards the diagonal, which is a much weaker and entirely true statement. The figure measures both, and they disagree.
The theorem that is true. T is a deterministic function of the pixel value, so by the data-processing property of entropy, H(T(X)) ≤ H(X), with equality if and only if T is injective on the levels that actually occur. Equalisation can never increase the information in an image; usually it decreases it. On the 64-pixel patch above, T happens to be injective and the entropy is unchanged at 5.2504 bits exactly. On a 40 000-pixel low-contrast image occupying 136 levels, the same procedure collapses them to 83 and the entropy falls from 6.2147 to 6.1324 bits. It looks better and contains less.

The interpolation in T_α is not a hack. Full equalisation optimises a criterion — uniformity of the CDF — that nobody actually wants; what people want is "more contrast where it matters, without the mud". α between 0.3 and 0.6 is the usual answer, and §06's local version is the other one.

Equalisation's transfer curve is steep where the histogram is tall, and a tall histogram bin in a small, flat block is usually noise — a hundred pixels that all happen to sit within a couple of code values of each other. 07 Why histogram equalisation is often a bad idea

It is the default demonstration in every image processing course and it is rarely the right thing to run on a photograph. Five separate objections, each of which is a real failure mode rather than a matter of taste.

objection 1
It amplifies noise exactly where noise is worst
Dark regions have the most pixels per code value in an under-exposed image, so the CDF is steepest there, so the gain is largest there. But dark regions are also where Part 1 §25's shot noise has the worst signal-to-noise ratio. The operator therefore applies its maximum gain to the noisiest part of the image. The clip limit of §06 exists entirely because of this.
objection 2
It cannot create levels, only merge them
T is a map on 256 symbols. Where it is steep it leaves gaps — output levels that no pixel occupies — and where it is shallow it merges distinct inputs irreversibly. Gaps are visible as banding on smooth gradients; merges are quiet and permanent. The entropy inequality of §05 is the formal statement.
objection 3
It destroys radiometric meaning
After equalisation a pixel's value encodes its rank in this image, not the light that fell on the sensor. Any downstream step that assumed radiometric consistency — stereo matching across two views, temporal differencing, HDR merging, physically based shading estimation — is now comparing two different monotone reparameterisations. If it must be applied, apply the same curve to every image in the set.
objection 4
It optimises a criterion nobody wants
Uniformity of the grey-level distribution is not a perceptual goal. Real photographs have most of their pixels in the midtones and a long thin tail in the highlights, and forcing that tail to occupy a quarter of the range is what makes an equalised image look flat and "muddy". §05's α blend and §06's local version both exist to back away from the criterion.
objection 5
It is content-dependent and therefore unstable
Two consecutive frames of a video that differ only by a bright object entering the corner get two different curves, so the whole background changes brightness. Equalisation applied per frame flickers. Any video use needs the curve temporally smoothed or computed once on a reference frame.
when it is right
Machine consumption, not viewing
Equalisation is a legitimate normaliser when the downstream consumer cares about ordering and not magnitudes: matching under unknown illumination, medical and astronomical images where the interesting structure is genuinely in a narrow band, and as a preprocessing step before a detector that was trained on equalised inputs. Its very defect — that it discards absolute level — is the property being bought.
The practical replacement. For display, a gentle S-curve in linear light plus a mild local operator does everything equalisation was reaching for without any of the above. For analysis, prefer normalising the mean and variance (a two-parameter affine map, invertible, entropy-preserving up to quantisation) over normalising the whole distribution. Both are point operators; both are one lookup table; neither is fashionable, and both are better.

08 Correlation, convolution, and the sign that trips everyone

Two formulas that differ by one minus sign, give identical answers for every symmetric kernel, and give mirror-image answers for every asymmetric one. Nearly every published sign error in this subject is this one, and the way to be immune is to know what each formula does to an impulse.

The two definitions, side by side

CORRELATION — slide the kernel over the image and take an inner productg(i, j) = ∑_{k,l} f(i + k, j + l) · h(k, l) written g = f ⊗ h CONVOLUTION — the same, with the offset into f REVERSEDg(i, j) = ∑_{k,l} f(i − k, j − l) · h(k, l) written g = f ∗ h = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(i − k, j − l) — the same sum, re-indexed and hence, alwaysf ∗ h = f ⊗ h̃ where h̃(k, l) = h(−k, −l) is h rotated 180°. If h is symmetric under 180° rotation — every box, tent, binomial and Gaussian on this page — then h̃ = h and the two operators are IDENTICAL. Which is exactly why the distinction stays invisible until you build a gradient or a Sobel filter.

What every library actually does. Almost every image processing routine named "filter2D", "conv", or "convolution" in a vision library computes correlation. What is special about the Gaussian, honestly

property 1
Variances add under composition
G_σ₁ ∗ G_σ₂ = G_σ with σ² = σ₁² + σ₂². Verified: two truncated sampled Gaussians of σ = 1.5 and 2.0 convolve to a kernel of measured variance 6.2481 against the predicted 2.25 + 4 = 6.25. This is what lets §23's pyramid build each level from the previous one, and what makes scale a one-dimensional parameter you can walk along.
property 2
Separable, exactly
exp(−(x²+y²)/2σ²) = exp(−x²/2σ²)·exp(−y²/2σ²). This is algebra, not approximation, and it is the reason the Gaussian is affordable at all. §11 counts the saving.
property 3
Rotationally symmetric
It is the only function that is both separable and circularly symmetric — the two conditions together force the exponential of a negative quadratic. A box filter is separable but square, so it blurs 41% further along its diagonal than along its axes, and every subsequent orientation measurement inherits that bias.
property 4
Non-negative response, no ringing
The Gaussian's frequency response is a Gaussian and is therefore positive everywhere. A box filter's is not: box-3's response reaches −0.3333 at the Nyquist frequency, and box-9's dips to −0.2266. A negative response means the filter inverts that band — which is why a box blur applied to a fine stripe pattern can come out with the stripes reversed, and why box filters ring at edges.
property 5, with a caveat
"Its own Fourier transform"
True of the continuous Gaussian, and borrowed here from Part 3 — §21 states it as borrowed. It is not exactly true of the sampled and truncated kernel: the discrete kernel's response at the Nyquist frequency is about 2·exp(−σ²π²/2), which for σ = 1 is 0.014384 against the measured 0.014387, and for larger σ is swamped by the truncation error entirely.
property 6
Box iterated → Gaussian
Convolving box filters converges to a Gaussian by the central limit theorem, and fast: three passes of box-3 give a 7-tap kernel of variance exactly 2 with excess kurtosis −0.50, four passes give −0.375, five give −0.30 — all heading to 0. Since a box pass costs O(1) per pixel with a running sum, this is the standard trick for a cheap large blur.

12 What makes a filter a good one — the moment conditions

"Good" is not a matter of taste. A handful of sums over the kernel's taps decide whether it changes brightness, whether it shifts the image, how wide it really is, whether it can be used before subsampling, and how accurately it reproduces smooth signals. All of them are two-line calculations, and all of them are checkable before you ever run the filter on an image.

What it costs, and its two characteristic artefacts

  • It is worse than a mean on Gaussian noise. The median of K independent standard normal samples has variance approaching (π/2)·σ²/K against the mean's σ²/K — about 57% more. Measured over 400 000 trials, the variance ratio is 1.349 at K = 3, 1.433 at K = 5, 1.494 at K = 9 and 1.559 at K = 51, climbing towards π/2 = 1.5708. Robustness is bought, not free.
  • It is not separable and not linear. The median of row medians is not the median of the window. A "separable median" is a genuinely different, cheaper operator, not a factorisation. The cost is O(K² log K) naive, O(K) with an incrementally maintained histogram, and O(1) per pixel with the constant-time algorithms that maintain column histograms across a scanline.
  • It rounds corners. A sharp convex corner has fewer than half of its window inside the object, so the median votes for the background and the corner is clipped. Repeated application converges to shapes with no corners at all — which is the point of the "root signal" analysis of median filters, and the reason the median is not an edge-preserving filter in the way the bilateral filter is.
  • It cannot be analysed in the frequency domain. No impulse response, no transfer function, no convolution theorem. The tools of §21 simply do not apply.

16 The bilateral filter, and why it preserves edges

A Gaussian averages over a neighbourhood because pixels that are close are probably similar. That assumption fails at exactly one place — an edge — and it fails there catastrophically. The bilateral filter repairs it by weighting on both closeness and similarity, which costs it linearity, shift-invariance and the entire frequency-domain toolkit.

Two kernels, multiplied

Read the weight at a pixel sitting on an edge. Neighbours on the same side of the edge are close in space and close in value, so both factors are near 1 and they contribute fully. Neighbours across the edge are close in space but far in value, so the range factor collapses towards zero and they contribute almost nothing. The filter has, without being told where the edge is, restricted its own support to one side of it. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why the effective kernel is different at every pixel — hence not shift-invariant, hence not a convolution.

The vector distance matters. On a colour image the range kernel must use ‖f(i,j) − f(k,l)‖, the distance between the two colour vectors, not three independent per-channel filters. Filtering the channels separately lets one channel average across an edge while another does not, which produces coloured fringes at every boundary. Taking the difference in a perceptually uniform space rather than in RGB improves it further, using Part 1 §23's colour spaces.

On a binary image the only thing a filter can do is change the shape of the set of ones. Morphology is the algebra of those changes, and it has a unifying trick: every operator is a convolution with a binary structuring element followed by a threshold, and the operators differ only in where the threshold is put.

What each one is for

  • Opening removes what is thinner than the structuring element and leaves everything else almost untouched. It is the shape-based analogue of a low-pass filter: specks, hairline bridges between blobs, and thin protrusions vanish; a wide region loses only the sharpness of its corners. A dot survives an opening only if the element fits entirely inside it — which is a precise, checkable criterion, not a heuristic.
  • Closing fills what is thinner than the structuring element: pinholes, hairline cracks, the gap between two nearly-touching regions. Same criterion from the other side.
  • Opening then closing — in that order — is the standard cleanup for a thresholded image: remove the noise specks first, then seal the holes. The reverse order seals the specks into the object.
  • The morphological gradient, dilate(f) − erode(f), is a one-pixel-wide boundary. It is the binary answer to §13's gradient magnitude and it is exact rather than approximate.

Two semi-global operators on a binary image. Neither has a fixed window — each pixel's answer can depend on a region arbitrarily far away — and both are nonetheless computable in a couple of raster passes, which is why they are cheap enough to be everywhere.

The trap. The same two-pass scheme with √2 on the diagonals does not give the Euclidean distance transform. A scalar minimum is not enough: the nearest background pixel's direction has to be propagated too, so the algorithm must carry a vector (Δi, Δj) per pixel and compare squared lengths. The scalar city-block transform over-estimates the Euclidean distance by up to a factor of √2 = 1.4142 — the measured worst-case over-estimate on the figure's shape is 0.8377 pixels, and the two-pass 3–4 chamfer approximation, still integer and still two passes, cuts that to 0.1711. Exact Euclidean transforms exist in O(N) time, but they are a different algorithm, not a tweak to this one.
  • The ridges of the distance transform are the skeleton — the medial axis — because a ridge point is equidistant from two or more boundaries. This is the "grassfire" reading: light the boundary and D is the time at which the fire reaches each pixel; the ridges are where two fronts meet.
  • The signed distance transform computes D for the object and for its complement and negates one. The result is smooth, zero exactly on the boundary, and is the standard implicit representation of a curve — the level-set function of Part 5, and the feathering weight of Part 6's stitching.
  • Chamfer matching precomputes D once for a template and then scores an alignment by summing D over the transformed points of the other shape. The alignment problem becomes a lookup, which is why the distance transform shows up in shape matching and in Part 7's point-set registration.

21 Four facts borrowed from the frequency domain

This page has been quoting frequency-domain reasoning since §10 — "the box filter's response goes negative", "the Gaussian has no sidelobes", "you must blur before you subsample". Those are all consequences of a theory that belongs to Part 3. This section states exactly what is being borrowed, and stops.

Scope. The Fourier transform, the DFT and the FFT, the sampling theorem, the Nyquist limit and the full account of aliasing are Part 3's. Nothing below is derived here. Four results are stated, used, and credited forward; if any of them looks like it needs justification, that is because it does, and Part 3 is where it gets one.
BORROWED 1 · the convolution theoremF{ f ∗ h } = F{f} · F{h} Convolution in space is MULTIPLICATION in frequency. Every "this filter passes low frequencies and attenuates high ones" claim on this page is really a claim about the shape of F{h}, which is why §10 and §12 plot H(ω) at all. It is also why a large-kernel convolution can be computed by two transforms and a multiply — exactly, but only under WRAP padding (§09), which is the mode that makes the convolution matrix circulant. BORROWED 2 · a Gaussian's transform is a Gaussian, with the width invertedF{ G(x; σ) } = G(ω; 1/σ) up to normalisation Two consequences used repeatedly above. It is POSITIVE everywhere, so a Gaussian has no sidelobes and cannot invert a band or ring at an edge (§10). And it is the unique shape that is its own transform, so the space–frequency uncertainty product σ_x·σ_ω is MINIMISED by it — the Gaussian is as localised in both domains at once as anything can be. That is the real reason it is the default scale-space kernel, not tradition. BORROWED 3 · a box in space is a sinc in frequencyF{ box } = sinc, and sinc oscillates and changes sign, forever. This is the whole of §10's complaint about the box filter: the negative lobes mean some bands are INVERTED rather than attenuated, and the slow 1/ω decay of the envelope means high frequencies are never really removed. Dually, an IDEAL low-pass filter — a box in frequency — is a sinc in space, which is infinitely wide and rings; §27's windowed sinc is the compromise between the two. BORROWED 4 · sampling replicates the spectrum, and overlap is irreversibleSampling at spacing 1 makes the spectrum periodic with period 2π. Content above the NYQUIST frequency π folds back into the band and is indistinguishable from real low- frequency content once it is there. Fold rule, verified: a sinusoid at f cycles/sample, decimated by 2, reappears at f_apparent = | ((2f + ½) mod 1) − ½ | in cycles/sample of the new grid. f = 0.30 → 0.400 f = 0.40 → 0.200 f = 0.45 → 0.100 Measured from the DFT of the decimated signal: 0.3984, 0.1992, 0.1016. ✓

Borrowed fact 4 is the one that makes §22 mandatory rather than advisory. It also explains the shape of §12's conditions: H(π) = 0 is the demand that the filter kill the one frequency that folds exactly onto DC, and the coset condition is the same demand re-read as "the two sampling phases must see the same average".

The one thing this page does say about frequency and means. A filter's response is a property of the filter, and it is measurable without any Fourier theory at all: feed in cos(ωk) and read off the amplitude of what comes out. That is what H(ω) = ∑ₖ h(k)·e^{−jωk} computes, and it is why every response plotted on this page is a direct sum over the taps rather than an FFT. The theory is Part 3's; the arithmetic is one line.

22 Interpolation and decimation, and why the order matters

Changing the sampling rate is two different problems that look like one. Going up needs an interpolation kernel and is fundamentally safe. Going down needs a low-pass filter first, and skipping it destroys the image in a way that no amount of later processing repairs.

Upsampling by r

the interpolation sum — one kernel, evaluated at the output grid's positionsg(i, j) = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(i − r·k, j − r·l) Read it two ways, and both are useful: · SUPERPOSITION — each input sample k contributes a scaled copy of h centred at r·k · GATHER — each output pixel i collects from the inputs under h centred at i The second is what you implement; the first is what you reason with. the POLYPHASE view, which is how it is actually codedThe offset (i mod r) takes only r distinct values, so h(i − r·k) takes only r distinct TAP SETS. 24 Multi-resolution blending

Joining two images along a seam has one parameter that cannot be got right: the width of the transition. Too narrow and the seam is visible; too wide and the two images ghost through each other. The resolution of the paradox is that the right width is different for every spatial frequency — and a pyramid is exactly a decomposition by spatial frequency.

The dilemma, stated precisely

width → 0
Hard splice
A step discontinuity in intensity wherever the two images differ in exposure, white balance or vignetting — which is always. The eye finds a straight intensity step at once, far more readily than it finds either image's texture wrong.
width → large
Feathering / cross-dissolve
The low-frequency step is now invisible. But over the whole transition band both images' textures are present at half strength, superimposed and unaligned. That is ghosting, and it is worse than the seam because it looks like motion blur that should not be there.
width per band
Multi-resolution blend
Blend each frequency band over a transition as wide as that band's own wavelength. Low frequencies get a wide, gentle ramp — no step. High frequencies get a narrow, abrupt one — no ghosting. Both objections are satisfied simultaneously, because they were never objections to the same band.
the algorithm, in four lines1. build Laplacian pyramids L^A and L^B of the two images 2. build a GAUSSIAN pyramid G^M of the binary mask 3. blend band by band: L^out_l = G^M_l · L^A_l + (1 − G^M_l) · L^B_l 4. collapse L^out back to an image, by §23's reconstruction why step 2 uses a GAUSSIAN pyramid of the mask, and this is the whole trickThe mask's level l has been blurred and subsampled l times, so its transition width is roughly 2^l pixels IN THAT LEVEL'S COORDINATES — which is exactly the wavelength the band L_l carries. The transition width therefore scales with the band automatically, with no parameter to choose. That is why the mask gets a Gaussian pyramid and the images get Laplacian ones: one is a weight, the others are content.

Everything generalises. The mask does not need to be a straight line — any binary region works, and the same construction blends an arbitrary cut-out. With more than two sources the mask becomes an integer label image and each band is a weighted sum over labels, which is how a panorama with a dozen exposures is composited in Part 6. And nothing here requires the sources to be aligned well, only aligned; the multi-resolution blend hides small misregistration in exactly the bands where it would be visible.

The prerequisite everyone skips. Blending is a weighted average of pixel values, so by §03's rule it must be done in linear light. Blending gamma-encoded code values darkens the transition band, and the darkening is worst exactly in the middle of the seam where the weights are equal — producing a dark stripe along the join that looks like a shadow and is entirely an artefact of the encoding.

25 Wavelets, in outline

A pyramid and a wavelet transform are both multi-resolution decompositions localised in space and in frequency. Two things separate them: a pyramid is overcomplete and a wavelet transform is not, and a wavelet transform is orientation-selective while a pyramid band is not. This section is a sketch — the transform theory is Part 3's.

The one-level split, and what makes it invertible

1-D: split into a low-pass and a high-pass half, each at half the rateL = (f ∗ h_lo) ↓2 H = (f ∗ h_hi) ↓2 N samples in, N/2 + N/2 = N out. NOTHING is added — this is the tight-frame property the Laplacian pyramid's 4/3 does not have. Recurse on L only. 2-D, separably — one level produces four sub-bandsLL low in x, low in y → the next level's input, ¼ of the pixels LH low in x, high in y → HORIZONTAL edges HL high in x, low in y → VERTICAL edges HH high in both → diagonal detail and corners, the sparsest band Three of the four (¾ of the pixels) are stored; one is recursed on. Total across all levels: ¾ + ¾·¼ + ¾·1/16 + … = 1 exactly. Same count as the image.

26 Geometric transformations: forward vs inverse warping

Point operators change an image's range; warps change its domain. There are two obvious ways to implement one, the obvious one is wrong, and the reason is a counting argument that takes one line.

the two kinds of operator, side by sidepoint operator: g(x) = h( f(x) ) the VALUE is transformed — §02 geometric warp: g(x) = f( h(x) ) the DOMAIN is transformed — this section

The transformation h itself is Part 1 §05's hierarchy — translation, rigid, similarity, affine, projective — and this page does not re-derive it. Part 1 defined the map; this section applies it to pixels; Part 6 estimates it from correspondences. All that is at stake here is what happens to the lattice.

Forward warping, and why it fails

the obvious algorithmfor every pixel x in the SOURCE f: x′ = h(x) write f(x) into g at x′
  • x′ is not an integer. Round it and the output jitters as the transform animates; splat it over the four nearest neighbours with bilinear weights and you must accumulate a weight image and normalise at the end — which is §09's normalised-zero construction again, and which blurs.
  • Holes. When the transform magnifies, there are fewer source pixels than destination pixels, so some destinations are never written. This is not an edge case; it is arithmetic. Under a magnification by r, the fraction of the destination left unwritten is about 1 − 1/r².
  • Overlaps. When it minifies, several sources land on one destination and the last write wins — which throws away the others and aliases, instead of averaging them.

why 1
No holes, ever
The loop is over the destination, so every destination pixel is visited exactly once and assigned exactly once. Holes are not reduced; they are impossible. The measured hole count is 0 for every transform, by construction.
why 2
The hard part becomes a solved problem
The remaining question is "what is f at a non-integer location?", which is interpolation — §27, a subject with kernels, error orders and anti-aliasing theory. Forward warping's question, "how do I distribute a value over neighbours I do not control?", has none of that.
the cost
h must be invertible
Every transform in Part 1's hierarchy is: invert the 3 × 3 matrix. Radial distortion (Part 1 §17) is not invertible in closed form, and its inverse only exists at all below the fold radius. A dense flow field from Part 6 may not be invertible at all — which is why flow is usually estimated in the inverse direction from the start, as "where did this pixel come from", precisely so that the warp can be done by gathering.
the remaining trap
Minification still aliases
Inverse warping fixes holes. It does not fix the fact that when the transform minifies, one destination pixel covers many source pixels, and point-sampling f there is exactly §22's unfiltered decimation. That needs a space-varying pre-filter, which is §28.

27 Interpolation kernels: nearest, bilinear, bicubic, windowed sinc

Inverse warping reduced everything to one question: what is f at a non-integer location? Every answer is a convolution with a continuous kernel evaluated at fractional offsets, and the kernels differ in cost, in accuracy order, and in whether they overshoot.

the resampling sum — the same formula as §22's interpolation, at arbitrary offsetsg(x, y) = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(x − k, y − l) x, y real, k, l integer 2-D is the outer product of two 1-D kernels for all four below, so the cost is the square of the tap count: nearest 1, bilinear 4, bicubic 16, a 4-lobe sinc 64.

The four kernels

NEAREST NEIGHBOUR — 1 taph(x) = 1 for |x| < ½, else 0 Order 0: it does not even reproduce a constant continuously — the output is piecewise constant, so a rotated edge becomes a staircase. Its only virtues are speed and that it never invents a value the image did not contain, which is why it is the ONLY correct choice for label images, segmentation masks and index maps. BILINEAR (the tent) — 4 taps in 2-Dh(x) = 1 − |x| for |x| < 1, else 0 Order 2, verified: the interpolation error on a smooth function falls as Δ², measured 1.9994 on sin x. Reproduces bilinear functions EXACTLY — verified to 10⁻¹⁵ — but not quadratics: on f = i², bilinear at row 2.5 returns 6.5 against the true 6.25. NON-NEGATIVE, so it can never overshoot: a step edge stays inside [0, 1] always. Its defect is a visible crease at every sample: the interpolant is C⁰, not C¹. BICUBIC (Keys) — 16 taps in 2-D, one free parameter a ⎧ 1 − (a+3)x² + (a+2)|x|³ for |x| < 1 h(x) = ⎨ a(|x| − 1)(|x| − 2)² for 1 ≤ |x| < 2 ⎩ 0 otherwise For every a: h(0) = 1 and h(±1) = h(±2) = 0, so it INTERPOLATES — the curve passes through the samples. And h′(1) = a exactly: a is the slope the kernel imposes at the first sample away from the centre, i.e. how much overshoot is being asked for. WINDOWED SINC (Lanczos, Hann-windowed) — 2W taps per axish(x) = sinc(x) · window(x/W) sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx) The unwindowed sinc is the IDEAL interpolator (§21's borrowed fact 3) and is infinitely wide. Windowing truncates it; the more lobes kept, the closer to ideal and the more ringing is retained rather than removed. Highest fidelity of the four, and the most visible ringing on hard edges.

The default that is wrong. Most image editors ship a = −1 as "bicubic". It is a first-order interpolator with 14.8% overshoot on a step. It is popular because its error is a mild sharpening rather than a blur, and sharpened output looks better on a screen. That is a legitimate aesthetic choice and an illegitimate numerical one: for measurement, registration, MIP-map construction or anything that will be differenced against another resampled image, use a = −1/2. The difference is three orders of magnitude at fine sampling.
Interpolating versus approximating. A kernel with h(0) = 1 and h(k) = 0 at every other integer interpolates: the curve passes through the data. The cubic B-spline — the binomial [1,4,6,4,1]/16 in its discrete guise — does not: h(±1) ≠ 0, so it approximates, producing a smoother curve that misses every sample. That is a defect for resampling and a virtue for representation, and it is fixable: pre-filter the samples with the B-spline's inverse (a short recursive filter) and the resulting interpolant does pass through the data while keeping the B-spline's smoothness. Which is why "cubic spline interpolation" and "cubic convolution" are different algorithms with confusingly similar names.

28 The resampling rate, MIP-maps and anisotropic filtering

Interpolation answers "what is f between the samples?" That is the right question only when the transform magnifies. When it minifies, one output pixel covers many input pixels and the right question is "what is the average of f over the region this pixel came from?" — which needs a pre-filter whose width varies from pixel to pixel.

29 Practice

Every answer below is worked out, and every number has been recomputed independently. If a step feels like recall rather than derivation, the section it came from is the one to reread.

Drills

  1. A Gaussian of σ = 1.5 is truncated at 3σ. How many taps, and what does separability save? R = ⌈4.5⌉ = 5, so K = 11. Direct 2D is 11² = 121 multiplies per pixel, separable is 2 × 11 = 22 — a saving of 5.5×, which is K/2 exactly. The kernel keeps 99.98% of the untruncated mass.
  2. Blur with σ = 3, then blur the result with σ = 4. What is the total blur, and can you do it in one pass? Variances add: σ = √(9 + 16) = 5 exactly. One pass with σ = 5, and it is cheaper: 31 taps once rather than 19 and 25 in sequence. This is §08's associativity plus §10's composition property, and it is what makes §23's pyramid incremental.
  3. Which box filter has the same second moment as a Gaussian of σ = 2? (K² − 1)/12 = 4 gives K = 7 exactly. They match in that one number and in nothing else: box-7's frequency response dips to −0.2 while the Gaussian's never goes negative, so the box will invert a band that the Gaussian merely attenuates.
  4. A 9 × 9 median filter: how many pixels of the window can be arbitrary before the output is? ⌊(81 − 1)/2⌋ = 40 of 81, i.e. 49.4%. The mean's answer is 0 — a single outlier of magnitude M moves it by M/81, without bound.
  5. What is the storage overhead of a Laplacian pyramid in 2D, and what would it be for a volume? 2D: ∑ 4⁻ᵏ = 1/(1 − ¼) = 4/3 ≈ 1.3333. 3D: ∑ 8⁻ᵏ = 1/(1 − ⅛) = 8/7 ≈ 1.1429. The overhead falls with dimension, because the levels shrink faster.
  6. How many bits does an integral image of a 16-bit 4096 × 4096 image need? The largest entry is 65535 × 4096 × 4096 = 1 099 494 850 560, needing 40 bits. A 32-bit integer overflows; a 64-bit one is required, and a 64-bit float would still be exact here since 40 < 53 mantissa bits.
  7. Equalise an image whose histogram is already perfectly flat. What happens? c(v) = (v+1)/256, so T(v) = round(255(v+1)/256): 0 → 1, 127 → 128, 255 → 255. Almost the identity, but shifted up by one and with 0 and 255 both mapping into the interior — the operator is not idempotent on the lattice even though it is in the continuum, purely because of rounding.
  8. Bilinear interpolation at the exact centre of the 2 × 2 block [[0, 1], [1, 0]]. All four weights are ¼, so the answer is 0.5. Note that no bicubic or sinc kernel can beat this — the answer is forced by symmetry — and note also that no interpolant can recover the fact that the block was a checkerboard.
  9. Forward-warp an image with a magnification of 3. What fraction of the destination is left unwritten? 1 − 1/3² = 88.9%. Nine destination pixels for every source pixel, and only one of the nine is written. Inverse warping leaves 0% at any magnification.
  10. Correlate a Sobel x kernel with the ramp f(i, j) = j. What comes out, and what does that say about scale? Exactly 8, where the true derivative is 1. The tent [1,2,1] sums to 4 and the difference [−1,0,1] has ∑k·h = 2, so the operator estimates 8·∂f/∂x. Scharr's factor is 16 × 2 = 32. Comparing gradient magnitudes across two libraries without checking this factor is the classic silent bug.
  11. Where does the Laplacian-of-Gaussian of σ = 2 cross zero? At r = √2·σ = 2.8284 in 2D. That radius is what sets the size of blob a LoG responds to, and it is why Part 4's blob detector reports r = √2·σ rather than σ.
  12. Unsharp masking with γ = 1.5 and a 5 × 5 σ = 1 Gaussian: what is the combined kernel's centre tap, and what is its sum? The Gaussian's centre tap is 0.16210, so the combined centre is 1 + 1.5 − 1.5 × 0.16210 = 2.25685. The sum is (1 + γ)·1 − γ·1 = 1 exactly, for any γ and any unit-sum blur — which is why sharpening never changes the average brightness.
  13. What does a four-level Gaussian pyramid cost, relative to one full-resolution filtering pass? 1 + ¼ + 1/16 + 1/64 = 1.328125. Building the entire multi-scale representation costs a third more than filtering once, and that bound holds however many levels you add.
  14. CLAHE with a clip factor of 2 on 64 × 64 blocks, 8-bit: what is the clip height? β = c·N/L = 2 × 4096/256 = 32 counts. Any grey level occupied by more than 32 of the block's 4096 pixels has its excess redistributed, which bounds the local contrast gain at roughly 2.
  15. Decimating by 3: what is the new Nyquist frequency, and what pre-filter width? 0.5/3 = 0.1667 cycles/sample in the original grid's units. Pre-filter at about σ = 0.5r = 1.5, i.e. a 9- to 11-tap Gaussian. Reusing a σ = 1 kernel would leave a third of the doomed band unfiltered.
  16. A 5 × 5 square structuring element: how many comparisons per pixel, and can you do better? 25 naive. It decomposes as 1×5 ⊕ 5×1, so two passes of 5 give 10 — §11's separability, in the morphological algebra. Only flat, convex elements decompose this way; a disc does not, exactly.
  17. At Δ = 0.05, how much more accurate is bicubic a = −0.5 than bilinear? 3.13 × 10⁻⁴ / 1.12 × 10⁻⁶ = 279×. And bicubic a = −1 is worse than bilinear at that spacing, by a factor of 8.5, because it is first order and bilinear is second.
  18. Haar-transform [3, 1, 4, 1] and check the energy. L = [(3+1)/√2, (4+1)/√2] = [2.8284, 3.5355], H = [(3−1)/√2, (4−1)/√2] = [1.4142, 2.1213]. Energies: 9+1+16+1 = 27 and 8 + 12.5 + 2 + 4.5 = 27. Exactly equal, because the transform is orthonormal — it is a rotation of ℝ⁴.
  19. A structuring element is a 3 × 3 cross. What is |S|, and what is the erosion threshold? |S| = 5, not 9 — the size is the number of ones, not the bounding box. Erosion is θ(c, 5), majority is θ(c, 3).
  20. Your noise has standard deviation 3 code values and the edges you care about are steps of 40. What σ_r should the bilateral filter use? Anywhere comfortably inside (3, 40); around 10 is the usual choice. Below 3 it refuses to average genuine noise and does nothing; above 40 it averages across the edges and degenerates into a Gaussian. If your noise were 30 and your edges 40, no value would work, and that is the honest answer rather than a tuning failure.
  21. Why does box-3 fail as a decimation pre-filter when it passes ∑h = 1 and ∑k·h = 0? H(π) = ⅓ − ⅓ + ⅓ = ⅓ ≠ 0. Its two cosets sum to (1 ± ⅓)/2, i.e. ⅓ and ⅔, so the two sampling phases of a constant field come out different and subsampling manufactures a checkerboard.
  22. A destination pixel's footprint in the source is an ellipse 12 texels long and 1.5 wide. What MIP level, and what does that cost? From the major axis, l = log₂12 = 3.585; from the minor, l = log₂1.5 = 0.585. Choosing the major blurs by the anisotropy ratio 8:1; choosing the minor aliases along the long axis. Anisotropic filtering takes 8 samples along the major axis at level 0.585, which is the right answer and costs 8 lookups.
