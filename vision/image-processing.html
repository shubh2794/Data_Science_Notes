Image Processing

Part 1 finished with an array: a rectangular grid of numbers, produced by a known optical and electronic process, with the depth and the spectrum already thrown away. It handed over a function on a lattice and nothing more. This part is everything that happens to that array before anyone tries to interpret it — the operators that map one image to another. There are exactly three kinds, and the whole page is organised by how much of the input each output pixel is allowed to look at: a point operator sees one pixel, a neighbourhood operator sees a small window, and a global operator — pyramids, warps, integral images — restructures the lattice itself. Nothing here recognises anything. The point is to arrive at Part 4 with an array whose gradients mean what they appear to mean.

01 Three kinds of operator, and what this page does not cover

Every operator on this page is classified by one question: how much of the input is an output pixel allowed to see? The answer determines the cost, the invariances, the artefacts, and — crucially — what can be said about the result.

an array from part 1→point operator→neighbourhood operator→resample / warp→an array with the same meaning and better statistics
reach: 1 pixel
Point operators
g(x) = h(f(x)). The output at a pixel depends only on the input at that pixel. Brightness, contrast, gamma, thresholds, colour matrices, lookup tables. Cost is O(1) per pixel and can be a 256-entry table. §02–§07. Histogram equalisation is still a point operator: the table is computed globally, but the map applied is per-pixel.
reach: a window
Neighbourhood operators
The output at a pixel depends on a fixed-shape window around it. If the combination is a weighted sum, the operator is linear and shift-invariant and everything in the theory applies: §08–§14. If it is not — median, bilateral, morphology — the theory mostly does not apply and the operator is usually better: §15–§19.
reach: the whole array
Global / multi-scale operators
The output lattice is not the input lattice. Pyramids change the sampling rate (§22–§25), warps change the coordinate system (§26–§28), integral images (§20) precompute a global running sum so that a later local query costs three additions. These are where the sampling theory of Part 3 starts to bite.
What this page does not cover, and where it lives. Part 2 owns the operators. It does not own the theory that explains them, nor the detectors built on top of them.
· The camera, projection, colour spaces, gamma and the sensor are Part 1. This page assumes an array already exists and never asks where it came from. Gamma in particular is defined in Part 1 §23; §03 only says where in the pipeline you must undo it.
· The Fourier transform, the DFT, sampling, the Nyquist limit and aliasing are Part 3. This page borrows exactly four facts from it, states them as borrowed in §21, and uses them to explain filter behaviour. It does not derive them.
· Edge, corner and blob detection, descriptors and matching are Part 4. The gradient, Laplacian-of-Gaussian and steerable filters are built here (§13–§14) because they are filters; the non-maximum suppression and hysteresis that turn a gradient magnitude into an edge map are not.
· Thresholding as a segmentation method — Otsu, region growing, watershed — is Part 5. §18 thresholds only to have a binary image to do morphology on.
· Estimating the warp of §26 from correspondences is Part 6. This page applies a warp you already know; Part 6 finds one you do not.
· Learned filters are Convolutional Networks. A convolution layer is §08's correlation with the kernel learned rather than designed, and it inherits this page's padding modes, its stride, its separability trick and its receptive-field arithmetic wholesale. §08 makes the correspondence explicit and then stops.
· Probability, the CDF and the probability integral transform that §05 leans on are Descriptive Statistics; the entropy argument in §07 is Statistics.

The worked array that runs through the page

One 8 × 8 patch of eight-bit values recurs from §04 to §20, so that a histogram, a convolution, a median, a morphological count and an integral image can all be read off the same numbers and checked by hand. It is deliberately small enough to add up in your head and structured enough to have a real edge in it.

Two features of this patch matter later. It has a ramp, not a step — the transition from the dark left column to the bright right column takes three or four pixels, which is what a real optical edge looks like once Part 1's lens blur has acted. And its histogram is bimodal with a thin waist: there is a cluster in the high 40s and low 50s, another in the 120s and 130s, and only four of the sixty-four pixels lie strictly between 70 and 90. 29 Practice

Every answer below is worked out, and every number has been recomputed independently. If a step feels like recall rather than derivation, the section it came from is the one to reread.

Drills

  1. A Gaussian of σ = 1.5 is truncated at 3σ. How many taps, and what does separability save? R = ⌈4.5⌉ = 5, so K = 11. Direct 2D is 11² = 121 multiplies per pixel, separable is 2 × 11 = 22 — a saving of 5.5×, which is K/2 exactly. The kernel keeps 99.98% of the untruncated mass.
  2. Blur with σ = 3, then blur the result with σ = 4. What is the total blur, and can you do it in one pass? Variances add: σ = √(9 + 16) = 5 exactly. One pass with σ = 5, and it is cheaper: 31 taps once rather than 19 and 25 in sequence. This is §08's associativity plus §10's composition property, and it is what makes §23's pyramid incremental.
  3. Which box filter has the same second moment as a Gaussian of σ = 2? (K² − 1)/12 = 4 gives K = 7 exactly. They match in that one number and in nothing else: box-7's frequency response dips to −0.2 while the Gaussian's never goes negative, so the box will invert a band that the Gaussian merely attenuates.
  4. A 9 × 9 median filter: how many pixels of the window can be arbitrary before the output is? ⌊(81 − 1)/2⌋ = 40 of 81, i.e. 49.4%. The mean's answer is 0 — a single outlier of magnitude M moves it by M/81, without bound.
  5. What is the storage overhead of a Laplacian pyramid in 2D, and what would it be for a volume? 2D: ∑ 4⁻ᵏ = 1/(1 − ¼) = 4/3 ≈ 1.3333. 3D: ∑ 8⁻ᵏ = 1/(1 − ⅛) = 8/7 ≈ 1.1429. The overhead falls with dimension, because the levels shrink faster.
  6. How many bits does an integral image of a 16-bit 4096 × 4096 image need? The largest entry is 65535 × 4096 × 4096 = 1 099 494 850 560, needing 40 bits. A 32-bit integer overflows; a 64-bit one is required, and a 64-bit float would still be exact here since 40 < 53 mantissa bits.
  7. Equalise an image whose histogram is already perfectly flat. What happens? c(v) = (v+1)/256, so T(v) = round(255(v+1)/256): 0 → 1, 127 → 128, 255 → 255. Almost the identity, but shifted up by one and with 0 and 255 both mapping into the interior — the operator is not idempotent on the lattice even though it is in the continuum, purely because of rounding.
  8. Bilinear interpolation at the exact centre of the 2 × 2 block [[0, 1], [1, 0]]. All four weights are ¼, so the answer is 0.5. Note that no bicubic or sinc kernel can beat this — the answer is forced by symmetry — and note also that no interpolant can recover the fact that the block was a checkerboard.
  9. Forward-warp an image with a magnification of 3. What fraction of the destination is left unwritten? 1 − 1/3² = 88.9%. Nine destination pixels for every source pixel, and only one of the nine is written. Inverse warping leaves 0% at any magnification.
  10. Correlate a Sobel x kernel with the ramp f(i, j) = j. What comes out, and what does that say about scale? Exactly 8, where the true derivative is 1. The tent [1,2,1] sums to 4 and the difference [−1,0,1] has ∑k·h = 2, so the operator estimates 8·∂f/∂x. Scharr's factor is 16 × 2 = 32. Comparing gradient magnitudes across two libraries without checking this factor is the classic silent bug.
  11. Where does the Laplacian-of-Gaussian of σ = 2 cross zero? At r = √2·σ = 2.8284 in 2D. That radius is what sets the size of blob a LoG responds to, and it is why Part 4's blob detector reports r = √2·σ rather than σ.
  12. Unsharp masking with γ = 1.5 and a 5 × 5 σ = 1 Gaussian: what is the combined kernel's centre tap, and what is its sum? The Gaussian's centre tap is 0.16210, so the combined centre is 1 + 1.5 − 1.5 × 0.16210 = 2.25685. The sum is (1 + γ)·1 − γ·1 = 1 exactly, for any γ and any unit-sum blur — which is why sharpening never changes the average brightness.
  13. What does a four-level Gaussian pyramid cost, relative to one full-resolution filtering pass? 1 + ¼ + 1/16 + 1/64 = 1.328125. Building the entire multi-scale representation costs a third more than filtering once, and that bound holds however many levels you add.
  14. CLAHE with a clip factor of 2 on 64 × 64 blocks, 8-bit: what is the clip height? β = c·N/L = 2 × 4096/256 = 32 counts. Any grey level occupied by more than 32 of the block's 4096 pixels has its excess redistributed, which bounds the local contrast gain at roughly 2.
  15. Decimating by 3: what is the new Nyquist frequency, and what pre-filter width? 0.5/3 = 0.1667 cycles/sample in the original grid's units. Pre-filter at about σ = 0.5r = 1.5, i.e. a 9- to 11-tap Gaussian. Reusing a σ = 1 kernel would leave a third of the doomed band unfiltered.
  16. A 5 × 5 square structuring element: how many comparisons per pixel, and can you do better? 25 naive. It decomposes as 1×5 ⊕ 5×1, so two passes of 5 give 10 — §11's separability, in the morphological algebra. Only flat, convex elements decompose this way; a disc does not, exactly.
  17. At Δ = 0.05, how much more accurate is bicubic a = −0.5 than bilinear? 3.13 × 10⁻⁴ / 1.12 × 10⁻⁶ = 279×. And bicubic a = −1 is worse than bilinear at that spacing, by a factor of 8.5, because it is first order and bilinear is second.
  18. Haar-transform [3, 1, 4, 1] and check the energy. L = [(3+1)/√2, (4+1)/√2] = [2.8284, 3.5355], H = [(3−1)/√2, (4−1)/√2] = [1.4142, 2.1213]. Energies: 9+1+16+1 = 27 and 8 + 12.5 + 2 + 4.5 = 27. Exactly equal, because the transform is orthonormal — it is a rotation of ℝ⁴.
  19. A structuring element is a 3 × 3 cross. What is |S|, and what is the erosion threshold? |S| = 5, not 9 — the size is the number of ones, not the bounding box. Erosion is θ(c, 5), majority is θ(c, 3).
  20. Your noise has standard deviation 3 code values and the edges you care about are steps of 40. What σ_r should the bilateral filter use? Anywhere comfortably inside (3, 40); around 10 is the usual choice. Below 3 it refuses to average genuine noise and does nothing; above 40 it averages across the edges and degenerates into a Gaussian. If your noise were 30 and your edges 40, no value would work, and that is the honest answer rather than a tuning failure.
  21. Why does box-3 fail as a decimation pre-filter when it passes ∑h = 1 and ∑k·h = 0? H(π) = ⅓ − ⅓ + ⅓ = ⅓ ≠ 0. Its two cosets sum to (1 ± ⅓)/2, i.e. ⅓ and ⅔, so the two sampling phases of a constant field come out different and subsampling manufactures a checkerboard.
  22. A destination pixel's footprint in the source is an ellipse 12 texels long and 1.5 wide. What MIP level, and what does that cost? From the major axis, l = log₂12 = 3.585; from the minor, l = log₂1.5 = 0.585. Choosing the major blurs by the anisotropy ratio 8:1; choosing the minor aliases along the long axis. Anisotropic filtering takes 8 samples along the major axis at level 0.585, which is the right answer and costs 8 lookups.

Things to be able to derive from a blank page

  • That an affine point operator scales the standard deviation by a and shifts the mean by b, and that it leaves the histogram's shape untouched.
  • The probability integral transform, in the three lines of §05, and why it makes the CDF uniform rather than the histogram flat.
  • H(T(X)) ≤ H(X) for any deterministic T, and hence that equalisation cannot create information.
  • That correlation reflects the kernel and convolution reproduces it, by substituting an impulse — not by recalling which is which.
  • That every linear shift-invariant operator is a convolution, from writing the image as a sum of shifted impulses.
  • Why zero padding returns 4/9 at the corner of a 3 × 3 box on a constant image, and why the normalised-zero construction returns exactly 1.
  • K = vhᵀ ⟺ rank K = 1, and the K² versus 2K count that follows.
  • The five moment conditions, and the two-line proof that the coset condition is H(0) = 1 and H(π) = 0 restated.
  • ∂(G ∗ f) = (∂G) ∗ f, and hence why every derivative filter in vision is a derivative-of-Gaussian.
  • ∇²G = ((x² + y²)/σ⁴ − 2/σ²)·G, its zero crossing at r = √2σ, and that it is rank 2.
  • That G_ûû = u²G_xx + 2uv·G_xy + v²G_yy, by expanding (ux + vy)², and hence that three filters steer every orientation.
  • That the mean minimises ∑w|f − g|² and the median minimises ∑w|f − g|, and that this is the whole difference between them.
  • The bilateral weight as a product of two Gaussians, and why the range factor collapses across an edge and not along one.
  • That every morphological operator is θ(f ⊗ S, t) for some t, and that dilation and erosion are t = 1 and t = |S|.
  • The two-pass city-block distance transform, and why the same trick does not give the Euclidean one.
  • The integral image recurrence and its four-corner query, both from inclusion–exclusion.
  • Why decimation without pre-filtering aliases, and why the fold is irreversible.
  • REDUCE, EXPAND, L_l = G_l − EXPAND(G_{l+1}), and that the reconstruction is exact by construction, independently of the kernel.
  • The 4/3 storage ratio, as a geometric series.
  • Why a multi-resolution blend needs a Gaussian pyramid of the mask and Laplacian pyramids of the images.
  • The lifting scheme's predict and update steps, and why reversibility is structural rather than algebraic.
  • Why forward warping leaves holes — the 1 − 1/r² counting argument — and why inverse warping cannot.
  • That the Keys cubic interpolates for every a, that h′(1) = a, and that only a = −1/2 reaches third order.
  • That the Jacobian's singular values are the local resampling rates, that l = log₂ r, and that no single r describes an ellipse.
