15 Non-linear filtering: the median and its relatives

Every filter so far has been a weighted sum, which is exactly what makes it vulnerable: one absurd pixel, weighted, is an absurd contribution. Rank filters throw the weights away and use the ordering instead, and buy an enormous amount of robustness for it.

The median

the operatorg(i, j) = median{ f(i+k, j+l) : (k, l) ∈ the window } 3 × 3 window → 9 values → sort → take the 5th. Worked, on a 3 × 3 patch: ⎡ 2 8 7 ⎤ unsorted: 2 8 7 4 0 6 3 5 7 ⎢ 4 0 6 ⎥ sorted: 0 2 3 4 5 6 7 7 8 ⎣ 3 5 7 ⎦ the 5th value is 5 — that is the output. Note the MEAN of the same window is 4.667. The median is not an average of anything. what it is optimising — the L1 analogue of the meanmean = argmin_g ∑_{k,l} w(k,l)·|f(i+k, j+l) − g|² p = 2 → a weighted MEAN median = argmin_g ∑_{k,l} w(k,l)·|f(i+k, j+l) − g|¹ p = 1 → a weighted MEDIAN Same objective, different exponent. p = 2 is correlation (§08) after normalising by ∑w; p = 1 is the weighted median. Everything robust in this subject is an exponent change.

Breakdown: the number that explains everything

how many of the K samples can be arbitrarily corrupted before the output is arbitraryMEAN: 0. One outlier of size M moves the output by M/K, without limit. MEDIAN: ⌊(K−1)/2⌋. Until half the window is corrupted the output is still one of the good values, and it cannot be dragged outside their range at all. K = 3 → 1 outlier tolerated (33.3%) K = 9 → 4 (44.4%) K = 5 → 2 (40.0%) K = 25 → 12 (48.0%) → 50% asymptotically measured on the §17 figure — 201 samples, 5% replaced by 0 or 1, a step of 0.25 → 0.75 residual sd RMSE fraction of the error removed unfiltered 0.1262 0.1129 — box, K = 9 0.0381 0.0367 67.5% Gaussian, σ = 2 0.0451 0.0410 63.7% median, K = 9 0.0000 0.0001 99.9% ← exact recovery bilateral, σ_d = 2, σ_r = 0.30 0.1031 0.0915 19.0% ← §16 fails

The median's near-zero is not a rounding artefact: with fewer than half the window corrupted, the median of the window is literally one of the uncorrupted values, and on a locally constant signal that value is exactly right. The residual 0.0001 is entirely the handful of samples on the soft edge itself. That is what a 50% breakdown point buys, and it is why the median is the standard answer to salt-and-pepper noise, dead pixels and cosmic-ray hits — while the bilateral filter, whose range kernel refuses on principle to average a pixel with neighbours it disagrees with, removes only 19% of the same error.

What it costs, and its two characteristic artefacts

  • It is worse than a mean on Gaussian noise. The median of K independent standard normal samples has variance approaching (π/2)·σ²/K against the mean's σ²/K — about 57% more. Measured over 400 000 trials, the variance ratio is 1.349 at K = 3, 1.433 at K = 5, 1.494 at K = 9 and 1.559 at K = 51, climbing towards π/2 = 1.5708. Robustness is bought, not free.
  • It is not separable and not linear. The median of row medians is not the median of the window. A "separable median" is a genuinely different, cheaper operator, not a factorisation. The cost is O(K² log K) naive, O(K) with an incrementally maintained histogram, and O(1) per pixel with the constant-time algorithms that maintain column histograms across a scanline.
  • It rounds corners. A sharp convex corner has fewer than half of its window inside the object, so the median votes for the background and the corner is clipped. Repeated application converges to shapes with no corners at all — which is the point of the "root signal" analysis of median filters, and the reason the median is not an edge-preserving filter in the way the bilateral filter is.
  • It cannot be analysed in the frequency domain. No impulse response, no transfer function, no convolution theorem. The tools of §21 simply do not apply.
the family it sits in — all of them "rank" or "order-statistic" filtersmin / erosion take the 0th order statistic — §18's morphology max / dilation take the last — §18 median take the middle — this section α-trimmed mean discard the smallest and largest αK, average the rest α = 0 → the mean; α → 0.5 → the median. The link to morphology. Erosion is the minimum over the structuring element and dilation is the maximum. Once that is seen, §18's entire algebra is the algebra of rank filters at the two extremes of the ordering, and the "majority" operator it also defines is the median in disguise. The median is the exact midpoint of an operator family whose endpoints are the two morphological primitives.

16 The bilateral filter, and why it preserves edges

A Gaussian averages over a neighbourhood because pixels that are close are probably similar. That assumption fails at exactly one place — an edge — and it fails there catastrophically. The bilateral filter repairs it by weighting on both closeness and similarity, which costs it linearity, shift-invariance and the entire frequency-domain toolkit.

Two kernels, multiplied

the operator — note the normalisation, which is not optional ∑_{k,l} f(k, l)·w(i, j, k, l) g(i, j) = ─────────────────────────── ∑_{k,l} w(i, j, k, l) the DOMAIN kernel — ordinary Gaussian closeness in space. Data-independent.d(i, j, k, l) = exp( −((i−k)² + (j−l)²) / (2σ_d²) ) the RANGE kernel — Gaussian similarity in VALUE. This is the whole idea.r(i, j, k, l) = exp( −‖f(i, j) − f(k, l)‖² / (2σ_r²) ) and the productw(i, j, k, l) = exp( −((i−k)² + (j−l)²)/(2σ_d²) − ‖f(i,j) − f(k,l)‖²/(2σ_r²) )

Read the weight at a pixel sitting on an edge. Neighbours on the same side of the edge are close in space and close in value, so both factors are near 1 and they contribute fully. Neighbours across the edge are close in space but far in value, so the range factor collapses towards zero and they contribute almost nothing. The filter has, without being told where the edge is, restricted its own support to one side of it. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why the effective kernel is different at every pixel — hence not shift-invariant, hence not a convolution.

The vector distance matters. On a colour image the range kernel must use ‖f(i,j) − f(k,l)‖, the distance between the two colour vectors, not three independent per-channel filters. Filtering the channels separately lets one channel average across an edge while another does not, which produces coloured fringes at every boundary. Taking the difference in a perceptually uniform space rather than in RGB improves it further, using Part 1 §23's colour spaces.

What the two parameters do, and the limits

RegimeConditionBehaviour
Gaussian limitσ_r → ∞The range kernel becomes 1 everywhere and the operator collapses exactly to a normalised Gaussian blur. Every edge-preserving property is gone. This is the correct sanity check that an implementation is right.
Identity limitσ_r → 0Only pixels of exactly the centre's value contribute, so g = f. Nothing is filtered.
The useful bandσ_r ≈ 1–3 × the noise sdNoise, whose excursions are small, is averaged away; edges, whose steps are large, are not crossed. The rule of thumb is real: choose σ_r above the noise level and below the contrast of the edges you want to keep. If those two intervals do not overlap, no bilateral filter will help.
Cartoon / staircaseiterated, or σ_r largeRepeated application drives smooth gradients into piecewise-constant plateaux with false edges between them — the staircase effect. Sometimes wanted (stylisation), usually not.
The failure mode people are surprised by. The bilateral filter cannot remove shot noise. A pixel that is wildly different from its neighbours is, by the range kernel's own logic, surrounded by pixels it should not average with — so the weights collapse to the centre pixel alone and it is reproduced exactly. Measured on the §17 figure: with 5% of samples replaced by 0 or 1, the bilateral filter (σ_d = 2, σ_r = 0.30) left an RMSE of 0.0915 against the unfiltered 0.1129 — it removed 19% of the error, where a 9-tap median removed 99.9% and even a plain Gaussian removed 64%. The right order is: median first to kill the outliers, then bilateral to smooth what remains.

Iterating it: adaptive smoothing and anisotropic diffusion

restrict the domain kernel to the 4 nearest neighbours and it becomes two constantsd = 1 at the centre d = λ = exp(−1/2σ_d²) at the four N₄ neighbours d ≈ 0 everywhere else then the bilateral update, iterated, rearranges into an explicit diffusion stepf⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾(i,j) = f⁽ᵗ⁾(i,j) + (η/(1 + ηR)) · ∑_{N₄} r(i,j,k,l)·[ f⁽ᵗ⁾(k,l) − f⁽ᵗ⁾(i,j) ] with R = ∑_{N₄} r. This is the discrete ANISOTROPIC DIFFUSION equation: a heat equation whose conductivity r(·) is switched OFF across large gradients, so heat (intensity) flows along edges and not across them. the general form — r is called the edge-stopping function or diffusion coefficientr(x) any monotonically decreasing function with r(x) → 0 as x → ∞. Common choices: exp(−x²/2σ_r²) the Gaussian, i.e. the bilateral filter 1/(1 + x²/σ_r²) the Perona–Malik Lorentzian Each corresponds to minimising a different ROBUST PENALTY on the image gradient, which is why this section and part 5's energy minimisation are the same subject.
Where iterated smoothing goes wrong, and the fix. With no term pulling the result back towards the original image, anisotropic diffusion converges to a constant image — every edge eventually loses. Running "a few iterations" is therefore a stopping rule masquerading as an algorithm. The principled version adds a data-fidelity term and minimises ‖g − f‖² + λ·(smoothness penalty on ∇g), which turns denoising into an optimisation rather than a process. That is Part 5's subject, and it is also where the guided filter and the joint (cross) bilateral filter live: the same weights computed from a different guide image — a flash photograph, a high-resolution colour image, an aligned depth map — so the edges enforced are the guide's rather than the noisy input's.

17 Box, Gaussian, median and bilateral, on the same noisy edge

All four claim to remove noise. They differ in what they remove with it, and the difference is measurable. This section fixes one test signal, three metrics, and lets the numbers decide.

The three metrics

1 · RESIDUAL NOISE — the standard deviation of the error in the flat regions onlyσ_res = sd{ g(i) − f_clean(i) : i in a flat region } lower is better Measured away from the edge, so it is purely a measure of smoothing power. 2 · EDGE WIDTH — the 10–90% rise distance across the transitionwidth = (position where g first reaches 90% of the step) − (position where g first reaches 10% of the step) lower is better A clean sharp edge has width 0 or 1 samples; a heavily blurred one has many. This is the metric that punishes filters for what they destroyed. 3 · RMSE — against the noise-free signal, over the whole recordRMSE = √( mean( (g − f_clean)² ) ) lower is better The honest summary, because it charges for both errors at once.

Four conclusions the table earns rather than asserts. First, the box filter's advantage in raw noise reduction is real — 0.0169 against the Gaussian's 0.0198 — and it pays for it with the worst edge here, so "the box is a bad Gaussian" is fair but not free. Second, the median matches the linear filters' noise reduction while leaving the edge exactly as it found it, which is why its RMSE is the best of the set on this signal. Third, the bilateral filter's behaviour is entirely governed by σ_r: too small and it is the identity, too large and it is a Gaussian, and only in between does it buy anything — which is §16's rule, made quantitative. Fourth, and least comfortable: on a clean Gaussian-noise problem with a soft edge, the median is not beaten by the more sophisticated filter. Its advantage disappears in two dimensions, where the median rounds corners and the bilateral filter does not — but that is a claim about 2-D shapes, not about this 1-D signal, and the table cannot support it.

The decision procedure, compressed. Is the noise heavy-tailed or impulsive? Median first — nothing else survives it. Are edges the thing you are about to measure? Bilateral, with σ_r set from a measurement of the noise, not a guess. Do you need the frequency response to be knowable, or the operation to commute with a warp or a derivative? Gaussian — it is the only one of the four with a theory. Is this a running sum inside an inner loop where nothing downstream will look at an edge? Box, via the integral image of §20, for O(1) per pixel at any radius.

18 Binary morphology: filtering by shape instead of by value

On a binary image the only thing a filter can do is change the shape of the set of ones. Morphology is the algebra of those changes, and it has a unifying trick: every operator is a convolution with a binary structuring element followed by a threshold, and the operators differ only in where the threshold is put.

The unification

first, a binary image — usually from a threshold, §02θ(f, t) = 1 if f ≥ t, else 0 then count how many ones of the structuring element land on ones of the imagec = f ⊗ S the integer COUNT at every pixel, 0 ≤ c ≤ |S| |S| = the number of ones in S (its SIZE, not its bounding box) and every standard operator is one threshold on that countdilate(f, S) = θ(c, 1) "at least one" — grows the object erode(f, S) = θ(c, |S|) "all of them" — shrinks it majority = θ(c, |S|/2) "more than half" — the binary MEDIAN of §15 open(f, S) = dilate(erode(f, S), S) — erode then dilate close(f, S) = erode(dilate(f, S), S) — dilate then erode the classical set-theoretic definitions, which are the same operatorsf ⊕ S = { p : (Ŝ)_p ∩ f ≠ ∅ } Minkowski sum — dilation f ⊖ S = { p : (S)_p ⊆ f } Minkowski difference — erosion

The algebra, which is what makes morphology worth having

PropertyStatementWhy it matters
Dualityerode(f) = ¬dilate(¬f)Erosion and dilation are the same operator viewed from the background. Verified exactly. So an implementation only needs one of them.
Extensivityopen(f) ⊆ f ⊆ close(f)Opening only ever removes, closing only ever adds. Neither can move a boundary in both directions, which is why they are safe cleanup operators.
Idempotenceopen(open(f)) = open(f)
close(close(f)) = close(f)		Verified exactly. Running an opening twice does nothing. This is not true of dilation or erosion, which compound: (f ⊖ S) ⊖ S = f ⊖ (S ⊕ S), eroding by a larger element.
Increasingf ⊆ g ⇒ op(f) ⊆ op(g)All four are monotone, which is what lets them be built out of rank filters (§15) at all.
Decompositionf ⊕ (S₁ ⊕ S₂) = (f ⊕ S₁) ⊕ S₂The morphological analogue of §11's separability. A 5 × 5 square dilation is two passes with a 1 × 5 and a 5 × 1 line — 2K comparisons instead of K². Only flat, convex elements decompose this way.

What each one is for

  • Opening removes what is thinner than the structuring element and leaves everything else almost untouched. It is the shape-based analogue of a low-pass filter: specks, hairline bridges between blobs, and thin protrusions vanish; a wide region loses only the sharpness of its corners. A dot survives an opening only if the element fits entirely inside it — which is a precise, checkable criterion, not a heuristic.
  • Closing fills what is thinner than the structuring element: pinholes, hairline cracks, the gap between two nearly-touching regions. Same criterion from the other side.
  • Opening then closing — in that order — is the standard cleanup for a thresholded image: remove the noise specks first, then seal the holes. The reverse order seals the specks into the object.
  • The morphological gradient, dilate(f) − erode(f), is a one-pixel-wide boundary. It is the binary answer to §13's gradient magnitude and it is exact rather than approximate.
  • The top-hat, f − open(f), isolates exactly what the opening removed: bright details smaller than the element. On a grey image it is the classical background-subtraction operator for uneven illumination. The bottom-hat, close(f) − f, does the same for dark details.
HIT-OR-MISS — the only operator that tests foreground AND background at onceHMT(f; S₁, S₂) = erode(f, S₁) ∩ erode(¬f, S₂) with S₁ ∩ S₂ = ∅ S₁ says "these pixels must be 1", S₂ says "these must be 0", and the remaining pixels are DON'T CARE. It fires only where the local pattern matches exactly, which makes it a binary template matcher — and the basis of thinning, thickening and the skeletonisation algorithms that repeatedly delete pixels a hit-or-miss test permits. GREY-LEVEL morphology — the same operators on a non-binary imagedilate(f, S)(i,j) = max over the element erode(f, S)(i,j) = min over the element These are the two extreme rank filters of §15, so grey-level morphology and rank filtering are literally the same subject. Every algebraic property above survives.

19 Distance transforms and connected components

Two semi-global operators on a binary image. Neither has a fixed window — each pixel's answer can depend on a region arbitrarily far away — and both are nonetheless computable in a couple of raster passes, which is why they are cheap enough to be everywhere.

The distance transform

the definition — distance to the nearest background pixelD(i, j) = min { d(i − k, j − l) : b(k, l) = 0 } for every pixel with b(i,j) = 1 the two metrics that mattercity block (Manhattan) d₁(k, l) = |k| + |l| 4-connected steps Euclidean d₂(k, l) = √(k² + l²) the true distance chamfer d(k, l) ≈ a·max + b·min a cheap rational approximation the two-pass raster algorithm for d₁ — this is the whole implementationinitialise: D = 0 where b = 0, D = ∞ where b = 1 forward pass, top-left to bottom-right: D(i,j) ← min( D(i,j), D(i−1,j) + 1, D(i,j−1) + 1 ) backward pass, bottom-right to top-left: D(i,j) ← min( D(i,j), D(i+1,j) + 1, D(i,j+1) + 1 ) Two passes, four comparisons per pixel, exact for d₁. Verified identical to a reference chamfer implementation on the figure's shape, cell for cell.
The trap. The same two-pass scheme with √2 on the diagonals does not give the Euclidean distance transform. A scalar minimum is not enough: the nearest background pixel's direction has to be propagated too, so the algorithm must carry a vector (Δi, Δj) per pixel and compare squared lengths. The scalar city-block transform over-estimates the Euclidean distance by up to a factor of √2 = 1.4142 — the measured worst-case over-estimate on the figure's shape is 0.8377 pixels, and the two-pass 3–4 chamfer approximation, still integer and still two passes, cuts that to 0.1711. Exact Euclidean transforms exist in O(N) time, but they are a different algorithm, not a tweak to this one.
  • The ridges of the distance transform are the skeleton — the medial axis — because a ridge point is equidistant from two or more boundaries. This is the "grassfire" reading: light the boundary and D is the time at which the fire reaches each pixel; the ridges are where two fronts meet.
  • The signed distance transform computes D for the object and for its complement and negates one. The result is smooth, zero exactly on the boundary, and is the standard implicit representation of a curve — the level-set function of Part 5, and the feathering weight of Part 6's stitching.
  • Chamfer matching precomputes D once for a template and then scores an alignment by summing D over the transformed points of the other shape. The alignment problem becomes a lookup, which is why the distance transform shows up in shape matching and in Part 7's point-set registration.

20 Integral images: any rectangle sum in three additions

One precomputation, one pass, and afterwards the sum over any axis-aligned rectangle — at any position, of any size — costs four lookups and three additions. Not approximately: exactly, in integer arithmetic, with no dependence on the rectangle's area at all.

the summed-area tables(i, j) = ∑_{k ≤ i} ∑_{l ≤ j} f(k, l) the sum of everything up and to the left built in one raster pass by inclusion–exclusions(i, j) = s(i−1, j) + s(i, j−1) − s(i−1, j−1) + f(i, j) (the two partial sums double-count the corner block, so it is subtracted once) Three additions per pixel, or two if a running row sum is carried separately. and queried by inclusion–exclusion again — the four corners of the rectangleS(i₀…i₁, j₀…j₁) = s(i₁, j₁) − s(i₁, j₀−1) − s(i₀−1, j₁) + s(i₀−1, j₀−1)

What it costs you

  • Precision. The table holds a sum of up to M·N pixel values, so it needs log₂M + log₂N extra bits. For an 8-bit 1920 × 1080 image the largest entry is 255 × 1920 × 1080 = 528 768 000, needing 29 bits — fine in a 32-bit integer, and a 32-bit float would silently lose the low bits, because a float has only 24 bits of mantissa. Squared sums, needed for a variance, want 64 bits.
  • Shape. Only axis-aligned rectangles. A rotated rectangle needs a second, rotated table; a disc cannot be done at all. Every rectangle-based feature in the literature is rectangle-based because of this constraint.
  • A box filter is all you get. An integral image gives you means over rectangles, so it gives you §10's box filter — with §12's failed Nyquist condition and §10's negative frequency response intact. A cheap Gaussian is three or four box passes (§10), which is still O(1) per pixel and is what fast implementations actually do.
What it is used for. Local mean and local variance in constant time — var = S₂/n − (S₁/n)² from two tables — which gives adaptive thresholding and normalised cross-correlation for free. Multi-scale rectangle features for cascaded detection. Box approximations to second-derivative filters in fast blob detectors. And the sum-of-squared-differences windows of Part 7's stereo matching, where the window size is a parameter being searched over and the running sum's fixed-K restriction bites.
