21 Four facts borrowed from the frequency domain

This page has been quoting frequency-domain reasoning since §10 — "the box filter's response goes negative", "the Gaussian has no sidelobes", "you must blur before you subsample". Those are all consequences of a theory that belongs to Part 3. This section states exactly what is being borrowed, and stops.

Scope. The Fourier transform, the DFT and the FFT, the sampling theorem, the Nyquist limit and the full account of aliasing are Part 3's. Nothing below is derived here. Four results are stated, used, and credited forward; if any of them looks like it needs justification, that is because it does, and Part 3 is where it gets one.
BORROWED 1 · the convolution theoremF{ f ∗ h } = F{f} · F{h} Convolution in space is MULTIPLICATION in frequency. Every "this filter passes low frequencies and attenuates high ones" claim on this page is really a claim about the shape of F{h}, which is why §10 and §12 plot H(ω) at all. It is also why a large-kernel convolution can be computed by two transforms and a multiply — exactly, but only under WRAP padding (§09), which is the mode that makes the convolution matrix circulant. BORROWED 2 · a Gaussian's transform is a Gaussian, with the width invertedF{ G(x; σ) } = G(ω; 1/σ) up to normalisation Two consequences used repeatedly above. It is POSITIVE everywhere, so a Gaussian has no sidelobes and cannot invert a band or ring at an edge (§10). And it is the unique shape that is its own transform, so the space–frequency uncertainty product σ_x·σ_ω is MINIMISED by it — the Gaussian is as localised in both domains at once as anything can be. That is the real reason it is the default scale-space kernel, not tradition. BORROWED 3 · a box in space is a sinc in frequencyF{ box } = sinc, and sinc oscillates and changes sign, forever. This is the whole of §10's complaint about the box filter: the negative lobes mean some bands are INVERTED rather than attenuated, and the slow 1/ω decay of the envelope means high frequencies are never really removed. Dually, an IDEAL low-pass filter — a box in frequency — is a sinc in space, which is infinitely wide and rings; §27's windowed sinc is the compromise between the two. BORROWED 4 · sampling replicates the spectrum, and overlap is irreversibleSampling at spacing 1 makes the spectrum periodic with period 2π. Content above the NYQUIST frequency π folds back into the band and is indistinguishable from real low- frequency content once it is there. Fold rule, verified: a sinusoid at f cycles/sample, decimated by 2, reappears at f_apparent = | ((2f + ½) mod 1) − ½ | in cycles/sample of the new grid. f = 0.30 → 0.400 f = 0.40 → 0.200 f = 0.45 → 0.100 Measured from the DFT of the decimated signal: 0.3984, 0.1992, 0.1016. ✓

Borrowed fact 4 is the one that makes §22 mandatory rather than advisory. It also explains the shape of §12's conditions: H(π) = 0 is the demand that the filter kill the one frequency that folds exactly onto DC, and the coset condition is the same demand re-read as "the two sampling phases must see the same average".

The one thing this page does say about frequency and means. A filter's response is a property of the filter, and it is measurable without any Fourier theory at all: feed in cos(ωk) and read off the amplitude of what comes out. That is what H(ω) = ∑ₖ h(k)·e^{−jωk} computes, and it is why every response plotted on this page is a direct sum over the taps rather than an FFT. The theory is Part 3's; the arithmetic is one line.

22 Interpolation and decimation, and why the order matters

Changing the sampling rate is two different problems that look like one. Going up needs an interpolation kernel and is fundamentally safe. Going down needs a low-pass filter first, and skipping it destroys the image in a way that no amount of later processing repairs.

Upsampling by r

the interpolation sum — one kernel, evaluated at the output grid's positionsg(i, j) = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(i − r·k, j − r·l) Read it two ways, and both are useful: · SUPERPOSITION — each input sample k contributes a scaled copy of h centred at r·k · GATHER — each output pixel i collects from the inputs under h centred at i The second is what you implement; the first is what you reason with. the POLYPHASE view, which is how it is actually codedThe offset (i mod r) takes only r distinct values, so h(i − r·k) takes only r distinct TAP SETS. Precompute r small filters, select one by the output pixel's phase, and the inner loop has no fractional arithmetic in it at all.

Downsampling by r — and the mandatory pre-filter

the decimation sum: filter and subsample, fused so the discarded outputs are never computedg(i, j) = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(r·i − k, r·j − l) Note the r on the OUTPUT index, where interpolation had it on the input index. That single difference is the whole distinction, and the fusion is why decimation costs the same as one filter pass, not one filter pass plus a copy. and the kernel must be STRETCHED, not reusedTo decimate by r, the pre-filter's cut-off must move from π to π/r, so its width in space must grow by r: σ ≈ 0.5·r is the usual rule, giving σ = 1 for r = 2. Reusing a fixed σ = 1 kernel at r = 4 leaves three quarters of the band unfiltered.
The mistake that looks harmless. Taking every r-th pixel with no pre-filter. Measured on the figure's chirp, whose local frequency rises to 0.45 cycles/sample, decimated by 4: the naive result differs from an ideally band-limited decimation with an RMS error of 0.28458, against 0.07090 for one pre-blurred with a Gaussian of σ = 0.5r — the naive version is 4.01× worse. A windowed sinc pre-filter does better still, at 0.04585; the linear [1,2,1]/4, far too narrow for r = 4, only reaches 0.13633. And the error is not noise. It is coherent, structured content at the wrong frequency — the fine stripes come back as coarse stripes that were never in the scene, and no later filter can remove them because they are now indistinguishable from real detail.

23 Gaussian and Laplacian pyramids

Repeat §22's decimation and you get a stack of images at halving resolutions. Record what each step threw away instead of the smoothed result, and you get a representation that is band-pass at every level, costs one third extra storage, and reconstructs the original exactly.

The generating kernel

the classical five-tap family, parameterised by its centre weight a[ c, b, a, b, c ] with b = 1/4 and c = 1/4 − a/2 Those two constraints are exactly §12's conditions, solved: ∑h = 2c + 2b + a = (1/2 − a) + 1/2 + a = 1 ✓ for every a coset sums even = a + 2c = 1/2, odd = 2b = 1/2 ✓ for every a so the WHOLE FAMILY passes, and a is free. a is then chosen by the second momenta = 0.6 → [−0.05, 0.25, 0.60, 0.25, −0.05] variance 0.10 (negative lobes) a = 0.5 → [ 0.00, 0.25, 0.50, 0.25, 0.00] variance 0.50 (the tent) a = 0.4 → [ 0.05, 0.25, 0.40, 0.25, 0.05] variance 0.90 a = 0.375 → [ 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 ]/16 variance 1.00 ← the BINOMIAL a = 0.3 → [ 0.10, 0.25, 0.30, 0.25, 0.10] variance 1.30 a = 3/8 is the value at which the kernel becomes exactly the binomial [1,4,6,4,1]/16: non-negative, integer, implementable in shifts and adds, and closest to a Gaussian. Repeated convolution of it converges to a Gaussian by the central limit theorem — as does repeated convolution of ANY unit-sum kernel with finite variance, so this is a property of iteration, not a special property of the binomial.

down one level — blur with h, then keep every other sample, in both directionsG_{l+1} = REDUCE(G_l) = (G_l ∗ h) ↓2 G₀ = the original image up one level — insert zeros, then filter with 2h. The factor of 2 is not optional.EXPAND(G_{l+1}) = (G_{l+1} ↑2) ∗ 2h Upsampling by inserting zeros halves the average value, because half the samples are now zero. On the smoother image the figure uses it comes out exactly 0, for every kernel offered — including the one with negative lobes, and including no pre-blur at all. It is EXACT.

The cost, counted

storage — the geometric series that gives the famous 4/3level 0 has N pixels, level 1 has N/4, level 2 has N/16, … total = N·(1 + ¼ + 1/16 + …) = N · 1/(1 − ¼) = (4/3)·N Measured on a 64 × 64 array with 5 levels: 5456 coefficients against 4096 pixels, a ratio of 1.33203 — approaching 4/3 = 1.33333 from below, as it must for a finite stack. The figure's 128 × 96 image with 4 levels gives 16 368 against 12 288: the same 1.33203. arithmetic — also a geometric series, and also boundedFiltering every level costs (1 + ¼ + 1/16 + …) = 4/3 times the cost of filtering level 0 alone. A whole pyramid is cheaper than 1.34 single-scale passes.
Why "Laplacian" is a misnomer, and what it really is. The band L_l = G_l − EXPAND(G_{l+1}) is a difference of two low-pass filtered images, i.e. a DoG, not a Laplacian of Gaussian. §13 measured how far apart those are: at a scale ratio of 1.6 the DoG differs from the true LoG by 17.9% in shape. The name is historical and harmless as long as nobody differentiates twice on the strength of it.
The variant that trades exactness for less aliasing. Burt and Adelson's original also admits a version in which L_l is formed by subtracting the directly blurred image rather than the expand-of-the-reduced one. It avoids one down-and-up round trip, so it aliases less — and it is no longer self-inverting, because the Laplacian levels no longer contain everything the reduction discarded. Perfect reconstruction and minimum aliasing are genuinely in tension here; which you want depends on whether the pyramid is being analysed or reconstructed.

24 Multi-resolution blending

Joining two images along a seam has one parameter that cannot be got right: the width of the transition. Too narrow and the seam is visible; too wide and the two images ghost through each other. The resolution of the paradox is that the right width is different for every spatial frequency — and a pyramid is exactly a decomposition by spatial frequency.

The dilemma, stated precisely

width → 0
Hard splice
A step discontinuity in intensity wherever the two images differ in exposure, white balance or vignetting — which is always. The eye finds a straight intensity step at once, far more readily than it finds either image's texture wrong.
width → large
Feathering / cross-dissolve
The low-frequency step is now invisible. But over the whole transition band both images' textures are present at half strength, superimposed and unaligned. That is ghosting, and it is worse than the seam because it looks like motion blur that should not be there.
width per band
Multi-resolution blend
Blend each frequency band over a transition as wide as that band's own wavelength. Low frequencies get a wide, gentle ramp — no step. High frequencies get a narrow, abrupt one — no ghosting. Both objections are satisfied simultaneously, because they were never objections to the same band.
the algorithm, in four lines1. build Laplacian pyramids L^A and L^B of the two images 2. build a GAUSSIAN pyramid G^M of the binary mask 3. blend band by band: L^out_l = G^M_l · L^A_l + (1 − G^M_l) · L^B_l 4. collapse L^out back to an image, by §23's reconstruction why step 2 uses a GAUSSIAN pyramid of the mask, and this is the whole trickThe mask's level l has been blurred and subsampled l times, so its transition width is roughly 2^l pixels IN THAT LEVEL'S COORDINATES — which is exactly the wavelength the band L_l carries. The transition width therefore scales with the band automatically, with no parameter to choose. That is why the mask gets a Gaussian pyramid and the images get Laplacian ones: one is a weight, the others are content.

Everything generalises. The mask does not need to be a straight line — any binary region works, and the same construction blends an arbitrary cut-out. With more than two sources the mask becomes an integer label image and each band is a weighted sum over labels, which is how a panorama with a dozen exposures is composited in Part 6. And nothing here requires the sources to be aligned well, only aligned; the multi-resolution blend hides small misregistration in exactly the bands where it would be visible.

The prerequisite everyone skips. Blending is a weighted average of pixel values, so by §03's rule it must be done in linear light. Blending gamma-encoded code values darkens the transition band, and the darkening is worst exactly in the middle of the seam where the weights are equal — producing a dark stripe along the join that looks like a shadow and is entirely an artefact of the encoding.

25 Wavelets, in outline

A pyramid and a wavelet transform are both multi-resolution decompositions localised in space and in frequency. Two things separate them: a pyramid is overcomplete and a wavelet transform is not, and a wavelet transform is orientation-selective while a pyramid band is not. This section is a sketch — the transform theory is Part 3's.

The one-level split, and what makes it invertible

1-D: split into a low-pass and a high-pass half, each at half the rateL = (f ∗ h_lo) ↓2 H = (f ∗ h_hi) ↓2 N samples in, N/2 + N/2 = N out. NOTHING is added — this is the tight-frame property the Laplacian pyramid's 4/3 does not have. Recurse on L only. 2-D, separably — one level produces four sub-bandsLL low in x, low in y → the next level's input, ¼ of the pixels LH low in x, high in y → HORIZONTAL edges HL high in x, low in y → VERTICAL edges HH high in both → diagonal detail and corners, the sparsest band Three of the four (¾ of the pixels) are stored; one is recursed on. Total across all levels: ¾ + ¾·¼ + ¾·1/16 + … = 1 exactly. Same count as the image.

Haar, the smallest example, worked

the orthonormal Haar pairL(n) = ( f(2n) + f(2n+1) ) / √2 H(n) = ( f(2n) − f(2n+1) ) / √2 inverse — the same operation, run backwardsf(2n) = ( L(n) + H(n) ) / √2 f(2n+1) = ( L(n) − H(n) ) / √2 verified on 8 random samples: max |f − reconstruction| = 1.11 × 10⁻¹⁶, and the energy is preserved exactly — ∑f² = ∑L² + ∑H² to the last bit. The √2 is what makes the transform ORTHONORMAL, hence energy-preserving, hence a rotation of the sample space.

Lifting: the construction that makes filter design unnecessary

Designing a pair of filters that are simultaneously finite, smooth, and exactly invertible is hard. Lifting sidesteps the whole problem: split the samples into evens and odds, then apply operations that are trivially reversible because each stage modifies one half using only the other half.

the two lifting stepssplit: even(n) = f(2n), odd(n) = f(2n+1) PREDICT: d(n) = odd(n) − ½·( even(n) + even(n+1) ) the odd sample's surprise UPDATE: s(n) = even(n) + ¼·( d(n−1) + d(n) ) put back what the split lost and the inverse, which needs no algebra at alleven(n) = s(n) − ¼·( d(n−1) + d(n) ) odd(n) = d(n) + ½·( even(n) + even(n+1) ) merge. Reversibility is structural: run the same steps with the signs flipped, in the opposite order. It holds even in integer arithmetic with rounding, which is how LOSSLESS image compression is built. the filters this is secretly applying — computed by feeding it impulses, not derivedhigh-pass: [ −1/2, 1, −1/2 ] ∑h = 0 ✓ low-pass: [ −1/8, 1/4, 3/4, 1/4, −1/8 ] ∑h = 1 ✓ Both measured directly from the lifting steps above, exactly. This is the CDF 5/3 pair. Two vanishing moments on the high-pass side means it annihilates any linear ramp — which is exactly why the detail bands of a smooth image are nearly all zero.

Pyramid or wavelet?

PropertyLaplacian pyramidWavelet transform
Coefficient count4/3 × N — overcompleteExactly N — a tight frame. This is the compression argument, and the only unambiguous win.
OrientationEach band is isotropic. No orientation information at all.Three oriented sub-bands per level: horizontal, vertical, diagonal. But only three, and they are axis-aligned — a 45° edge lands in HH with no way to tell it from a corner.
Shift invariancePoor — every level is subsampled.Worse. Shifting the input by one pixel moves energy between sub-bands, so a critically sampled wavelet transform is badly shift-variant. This is the reason it is excellent for compression and poor for matching.
SimplicityOne kernel, two operations, obviously invertible.Filter design is a subject in itself — unless lifting is used, which makes it as simple as the pyramid.
Steerable pyramids, which fix the two defects at once. Replace the checkerboard 2 × 2 frequency split with a radial and angular one: annuli in frequency for scale, wedges for orientation. The result is deliberately overcomplete — which removes the aliasing that critical sampling forces — is orientation-selective at any number of orientations, is self-inverting, and is approximately shift-invariant. It is the right tool for texture analysis and denoising, and the wrong one for compression, which is the trade in one sentence. The steering machinery is §14's, applied across scales.
