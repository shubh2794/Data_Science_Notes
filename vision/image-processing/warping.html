26 Geometric transformations: forward vs inverse warping

Point operators change an image's range; warps change its domain. There are two obvious ways to implement one, the obvious one is wrong, and the reason is a counting argument that takes one line.

the two kinds of operator, side by sidepoint operator: g(x) = h( f(x) ) the VALUE is transformed — §02 geometric warp: g(x) = f( h(x) ) the DOMAIN is transformed — this section

The transformation h itself is Part 1 §05's hierarchy — translation, rigid, similarity, affine, projective — and this page does not re-derive it. Part 1 defined the map; this section applies it to pixels; Part 6 estimates it from correspondences. All that is at stake here is what happens to the lattice.

Forward warping, and why it fails

the obvious algorithmfor every pixel x in the SOURCE f: x′ = h(x) write f(x) into g at x′
  • x′ is not an integer. Round it and the output jitters as the transform animates; splat it over the four nearest neighbours with bilinear weights and you must accumulate a weight image and normalise at the end — which is §09's normalised-zero construction again, and which blurs.
  • Holes. When the transform magnifies, there are fewer source pixels than destination pixels, so some destinations are never written. This is not an edge case; it is arithmetic. Under a magnification by r, the fraction of the destination left unwritten is about 1 − 1/r².
  • Overlaps. When it minifies, several sources land on one destination and the last write wins — which throws away the others and aliases, instead of averaging them.

And a pure ROTATION still leaves 13% holes at 30°, with no scaling anywhere — because a rotated integer lattice does not land on an integer lattice.

Inverse warping, and why it wins

the algorithm that is actually usedfor every pixel x′ in the DESTINATION g: x = ĥ(x′) where ĥ = h⁻¹ g(x′) = RESAMPLE f at the non-integer location x
why 1
No holes, ever
The loop is over the destination, so every destination pixel is visited exactly once and assigned exactly once. Holes are not reduced; they are impossible. The measured hole count is 0 for every transform, by construction.
why 2
The hard part becomes a solved problem
The remaining question is "what is f at a non-integer location?", which is interpolation — §27, a subject with kernels, error orders and anti-aliasing theory. Forward warping's question, "how do I distribute a value over neighbours I do not control?", has none of that.
the cost
h must be invertible
Every transform in Part 1's hierarchy is: invert the 3 × 3 matrix. Radial distortion (Part 1 §17) is not invertible in closed form, and its inverse only exists at all below the fold radius. A dense flow field from Part 6 may not be invertible at all — which is why flow is usually estimated in the inverse direction from the start, as "where did this pixel come from", precisely so that the warp can be done by gathering.
the remaining trap
Minification still aliases
Inverse warping fixes holes. It does not fix the fact that when the transform minifies, one destination pixel covers many source pixels, and point-sampling f there is exactly §22's unfiltered decimation. That needs a space-varying pre-filter, which is §28.
Forward warping leaves holes; inverse warping cannot the hole count is measured, and it is zero for inverse warping at every setting

27 Interpolation kernels: nearest, bilinear, bicubic, windowed sinc

Inverse warping reduced everything to one question: what is f at a non-integer location? Every answer is a convolution with a continuous kernel evaluated at fractional offsets, and the kernels differ in cost, in accuracy order, and in whether they overshoot.

the resampling sum — the same formula as §22's interpolation, at arbitrary offsetsg(x, y) = ∑_{k,l} f(k, l) · h(x − k, y − l) x, y real, k, l integer 2-D is the outer product of two 1-D kernels for all four below, so the cost is the square of the tap count: nearest 1, bilinear 4, bicubic 16, a 4-lobe sinc 64.

The four kernels

NEAREST NEIGHBOUR — 1 taph(x) = 1 for |x| < ½, else 0 Order 0: it does not even reproduce a constant continuously — the output is piecewise constant, so a rotated edge becomes a staircase. Its only virtues are speed and that it never invents a value the image did not contain, which is why it is the ONLY correct choice for label images, segmentation masks and index maps. BILINEAR (the tent) — 4 taps in 2-Dh(x) = 1 − |x| for |x| < 1, else 0 Order 2, verified: the interpolation error on a smooth function falls as Δ², measured 1.9994 on sin x. Reproduces bilinear functions EXACTLY — verified to 10⁻¹⁵ — but not quadratics: on f = i², bilinear at row 2.5 returns 6.5 against the true 6.25. NON-NEGATIVE, so it can never overshoot: a step edge stays inside [0, 1] always. Its defect is a visible crease at every sample: the interpolant is C⁰, not C¹. BICUBIC (Keys) — 16 taps in 2-D, one free parameter a ⎧ 1 − (a+3)x² + (a+2)|x|³ for |x| < 1 h(x) = ⎨ a(|x| − 1)(|x| − 2)² for 1 ≤ |x| < 2 ⎩ 0 otherwise For every a: h(0) = 1 and h(±1) = h(±2) = 0, so it INTERPOLATES — the curve passes through the samples. And h′(1) = a exactly: a is the slope the kernel imposes at the first sample away from the centre, i.e. how much overshoot is being asked for. WINDOWED SINC (Lanczos, Hann-windowed) — 2W taps per axish(x) = sinc(x) · window(x/W) sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx) The unwindowed sinc is the IDEAL interpolator (§21's borrowed fact 3) and is infinitely wide. Windowing truncates it; the more lobes kept, the closer to ideal and the more ringing is retained rather than removed. Highest fidelity of the four, and the most visible ringing on hard edges.

The default that is wrong. Most image editors ship a = −1 as "bicubic". It is a first-order interpolator with 14.8% overshoot on a step. It is popular because its error is a mild sharpening rather than a blur, and sharpened output looks better on a screen. That is a legitimate aesthetic choice and an illegitimate numerical one: for measurement, registration, MIP-map construction or anything that will be differenced against another resampled image, use a = −1/2. The difference is three orders of magnitude at fine sampling.
Interpolating versus approximating. A kernel with h(0) = 1 and h(k) = 0 at every other integer interpolates: the curve passes through the data. The cubic B-spline — the binomial [1,4,6,4,1]/16 in its discrete guise — does not: h(±1) ≠ 0, so it approximates, producing a smoother curve that misses every sample. That is a defect for resampling and a virtue for representation, and it is fixable: pre-filter the samples with the B-spline's inverse (a short recursive filter) and the resulting interpolant does pass through the data while keeping the B-spline's smoothness. Which is why "cubic spline interpolation" and "cubic convolution" are different algorithms with confusingly similar names.

28 The resampling rate, MIP-maps and anisotropic filtering

Interpolation answers "what is f between the samples?" That is the right question only when the transform magnifies. When it minifies, one output pixel covers many input pixels and the right question is "what is the average of f over the region this pixel came from?" — which needs a pre-filter whose width varies from pixel to pixel.

The Jacobian is the local resampling rate

linearise the warp at each destination pixel ⎡ ∂x/∂x′ ∂x/∂y′ ⎤ the local affine approximation to the inverse map A = ⎢ ⎥ — exact for an affine warp, and the right local model ⎣ ∂y/∂x′ ∂y/∂y′ ⎦ for a projective one, where it varies across the image and read off what the destination pixel's unit square maps tothe unit square at x′ pulls back to a PARALLELOGRAM in the source, of area |det A|. |det A| > 1 → minification: many source pixels per destination pixel. PRE-FILTER. |det A| < 1 → magnification: interpolate, §27, and no pre-filter is needed. |det A| = 1 → area-preserving, but possibly still anisotropic — see below. the scalar rate, and its two defensible definitionsr = max singular value of A → suppresses aliasing, blurs the sharp direction r = min singular value of A → keeps detail, aliases along the squashed direction There is no single r that is right, because the required filter is genuinely ELLIPTICAL and a single number cannot describe an ellipse. That is the whole problem. and the correct filter, stated so it is clear what is being approximatedPre-filter with a kernel whose frequency response is the ideal low-pass transformed by A⁻ᵀ — an oriented, sheared, generally NON-SEPARABLE filter, different at every pixel. Everything below is an approximation to this.

Three approximations, in increasing order of quality

MethodWhat it doesWhat it gets wrong
MIP-mapPrecompute a pyramid (§23), each level pre-filtered with a good filter rather than a cheap one. At each destination pixel compute l = log₂ r, sample the two levels bracketing l, and blend linearly by the fractional part — bilinearly within each level, hence "trilinear". Cost: 8 texel reads, constant.It is isotropic. One scalar r is chosen for a footprint that is generally an ellipse, so it either blurs the sharp axis (if r = max) or aliases along the squashed one (if r = min). A surface at a grazing angle is the standard failure: the footprint is enormously elongated and MIP-mapping is forced to blur it into mush. Storage: the same 4/3 as §23.
Elliptical weighted averageCompute the actual elliptical footprint from A and integrate the source under a Gaussian whose inverse covariance is that ellipse. Not an approximation to the shape at all.The kernel is a Gaussian, so it is simultaneously too blurry in the passband and too leaky in the stopband — it manages to blur and alias at once. And it is slow: the cost grows with the footprint's area, without bound.
Anisotropic filteringTake several MIP-map samples along the major axis of the elliptical footprint, at a level chosen from the minor axis, and average them. The number of samples is the anisotropy ratio, capped — the "16×" of a graphics driver's settings.Nothing structural; it is the right idea. The cap on the sample count means it degrades to plain MIP-mapping at extreme grazing angles, and the cost is linear in the ratio.
MIP-map level selection, workedl = log₂ r r = 1 → l = 0 (full resolution) r = 2 → l = 1 r = 4 → l = 2 r = 5.66 → l = 2.5 fractional l: sample levels ⌊l⌋ and ⌈l⌉ and blend by frac(l). Without the blend, the level switches abruptly and a visible seam appears in the image wherever the rate crosses a power of two — the classic "MIP band". the multi-pass alternative, for a parametric warpA general 2-D oriented filter is expensive; a SHEAR is separable. Any rotation factors into three shears, so a rotation can be done as three 1-D resampling passes — each of which is a cheap polyphase filter (§22). The catch: a shear can compress a direction before a later pass expands it, so it may be necessary to UPSAMPLE first in order not to lose the band a later pass will need.
