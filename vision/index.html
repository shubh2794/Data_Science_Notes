Computer Vision

One question, asked seven ways: what can be recovered about a scene from the numbers a camera produced? This is the overview — the map of the subject, the pipeline that connects the parts, and a one-screen cheat sheet. The notes live on seven sub-pages, ordered so each one supplies what the next assumes.

Computer Vision Geometry-first Classical pipeline 7 sub-topics

scope The classical half of vision

This domain owns the part of vision that is geometry and signal processing — the physics of how an image was formed, and what can be measured from it without learning anything from data.

The learned half lives in Deep Learning, and this domain links to it rather than repeating it: CNNs for learned filters, Vision Transformers for patch-based attention, GANs and Diffusion Models for generation, and Contrastive Learning for representation. The two halves are not rivals: a convolution layer is the filtering of §03 with the kernel learned instead of designed, and a network still needs calibrated cameras and rectified stereo pairs to work in three dimensions.

why The thread that runs through it

Vision is an inverse problem: the world threw away information on the way to the sensor, and every technique here is an attempt to put a constraint back.

a scene→projection→sampling→pixels→features→correspondence→geometry recovered
  • Projection destroys depth, and nothing recovers it from one image alone. Every 3D method here — stereo, structure from motion, shape from X — works by supplying a second constraint: another viewpoint, another moment, or an assumption about the world.
  • Scale is a choice, not a property. A corner at one blur level is an edge at another and nothing at a third. Scale space exists because "the right amount of smoothing" cannot be decided in advance, which is why detectors search across it.
  • Filtering is multiplication in the frequency domain. That single fact explains why Gaussians are everywhere, why downsampling without blurring aliases, and why a separable kernel is cheap.
  • Correspondence is the hard part. Given matched points, the geometry is linear algebra. Getting matches that are actually correct is where the difficulty and most of the engineering lives — hence descriptors, ratio tests, and RANSAC.
  • Robustness beats precision. A least-squares fit is destroyed by a handful of wrong matches, so classical vision leans on estimators that expect to be lied to.
