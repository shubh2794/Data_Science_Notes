Segmentation

An image is a rectangle of numbers with no seams in it. Segmentation is the act of drawing seams anyway — cutting the pixel grid into groups that "go together" — and the uncomfortable fact that organises this entire part is that there is no single correct answer to cut towards. Two people shown the same photograph will not draw the same regions, and neither will they be wrong. So every algorithm below is not a solver for a well-posed problem; it is a criterion — a statement of what "going together" is going to mean today — plus an optimiser for it. Read each method twice: once for the criterion, which is the honest content, and once for the optimiser, which is the engineering. When a segmentation looks bad, it is almost always the criterion that was wrong.

01 What segmentation is, and what this page does not cover

Every method on this page takes the same input — one array of pixels — and produces the same kind of output: a label per pixel. What separates them is entirely what they are willing to call similar, and how far apart in the image two similar pixels are allowed to be.

pixels→a similarity criterion→an objective→an optimiser→a label map→connected components→regions

The subject organises into four families, and the order below is roughly the order in which they were invented, which is also roughly the order of how much of the image each one looks at before deciding anything.

family 1 · §04–§08
Thresholding
Decide each pixel on its own value alone. The whole image contributes only through the histogram, and spatial arrangement enters afterwards, through connected components. Cheapest thing that works, and it works far more often than its reputation suggests.
family 2 · §09–§13
Region-based
Grow, split or merge spatially connected sets under a homogeneity predicate. Regions are connected by construction, so you never have to clean up afterwards — but the answer depends on the order you visited things in.
family 3 · §14–§19
Contour & density
Evolve a boundary downhill in an energy (snakes, level sets), or climb uphill to the modes of a density fitted to the pixels (mean shift). Both are continuous optimisation, and both land in local optima.
family 4 · §20–§25
Graph-based
Make the image a weighted graph and cut it. Two very different cuts appear: the normalised cut, which is NP-hard and gets relaxed into an eigenvector problem, and the minimum cut of a binary energy, which is solved exactly in polynomial time by max-flow.

02 There is no correct segmentation, and saying so early saves a lot of grief

Almost every other problem in this series has a ground truth that exists whether or not anyone measured it. A 3D point really is somewhere; a homography really does or does not relate two images. A segmentation is not like that. The partition is not a property of the scene; it is a property of the question being asked about the scene.

Consider a photograph of a person in a striped shirt. How many regions is that? One — the person. Two — person and background. Three — head, shirt, trousers. Forty — every stripe. All four answers are defensible, and none of them is recoverable from the pixels, because the pixels are identical in all four cases. The information that decides between them lives outside the image: in what the segmentation is for.

This has three consequences that shape everything below.

  • Every algorithm has a granularity knob, and it is never optional. The number of clusters k, the mean-shift bandwidth h, the merge threshold τ, the number of recursive normalised cuts, the superpixel count — these look like nuisance hyperparameters and they are not. They are the place where you supply the missing question. An algorithm that claims not to have one has hidden it in a constant.
  • Human annotators disagree with each other, substantially. On the standard hand-labelled benchmarks, the different people who segmented the same photograph produce partitions that differ as much from one another as good algorithms differ from any one of them. This is not annotator sloppiness; it is the ill-posedness showing up as data.
  • Therefore a benchmark number is a comparison against one particular reading of the image. A score of 0.7 against annotator A and 0.5 against annotator B does not mean the algorithm got worse; it means B drew different seams. Any evaluation that averages over annotators is averaging over incompatible answers — §26 is where this gets uncomfortable.

The honest framing, then, is not "find the segmentation" but: the algorithm proposes a nested family of partitions, and something outside the algorithm picks a level. Most of the good methods below are best understood that way — a hierarchy, plus a cut through it.

One scene, a whole ladder of defensible partitions — and three people who each stopped at a different rung drag the granularity slider; no setting agrees with all three annotators, and none is wrong
  • The default has the right number of regions and the wrong regions. Annotator A drew five; the slider starts at five; the adjusted Rand index between them is 0.445. Matching the count is not remotely the same as matching the partition, and any method that is tuned by counting regions is being tuned on the wrong quantity.
  • Slide to the granularity that best matches annotator A, then switch to annotator B. At K = 10 the agreement with A is 0.941; the same partition scores 0.803 against B, whose own optimum is K = 8. There is no single setting that is simultaneously optimal against all three. The annotators do not agree with each other either — 0.789, 0.936 and 0.812 pairwise — and that residual is irreducible: it is what "the answer depends on the question" looks like as a number.
  • The machine can outscore a human, and that is a fact about the metric. At K = 8 the intensity merge agrees with annotator B at 0.858, which is higher than B's agreement with either of the other two people. Nobody should conclude that the algorithm reads the scene better than a person does. It means the metric rewards a particular kind of similarity, and that a machine tuned to that metric can win on it while still being wrong about what the picture contains. §26 returns to this.
  • The machine's ladder is nested; the annotators' answers are not on it. Agglomerative merging by intensity produces a strictly nested family of partitions, so at every rung each region is a union of finer regions. Annotator B groups the door with the tree trunk, because both are wood, while the wall that lies between them is masonry. {door, trunk} is never a region at any rung of the intensity ladder — those two atoms only ever end up together when everything does, at K = 1 — so no choice of granularity produces B's answer. A better criterion might; a better granularity cannot.
  • This is the argument for over-segmenting deliberately. If you cannot know the right level, produce a partition that is safely finer than any level you might want — one that does not merge across any boundary the reader might care about — and let a later stage group them. That idea has a name and it is superpixels, §25.

03 The formal statement, and exactly where it stops determining anything

There is a standard four-condition definition of a segmentation. It is worth writing down precisely, because seeing what it does not pin down is the fastest route to understanding why there are so many algorithms.

Let R be the whole image domain — the set of all pixel positions — and let P be a homogeneity predicate, a function that takes a set of pixels and returns true when that set is "uniform enough". A segmentation is a finite collection of subsets R₁, …, R_n satisfying four conditions.

the four conditions on a segmentation1. ⋃ᵢ Rᵢ = R complete — every pixel is labelled 2. Rᵢ ∩ Rⱼ = ∅ for i ≠ j disjoint — no pixel is labelled twice 3. P(Rᵢ) = true for every i each region is internally homogeneous 4. P(Rᵢ ∪ Rⱼ) = false for Rᵢ adjacent Rⱼ no two neighbours could be merged

Conditions 1 and 2 say the output is a partition. Condition 3 says the regions are good. Condition 4 — the one that is usually skipped over — says the segmentation is maximal: it forbids the trivial answer of one region per pixel, which satisfies 1, 2 and 3 for any sane P. Most implementations also require each Rᵢ to be connected, which is a fifth condition and is not implied by the other four.

The three things this definition leaves completely open

gap 1
P is not given
"Homogeneous enough" is exactly the modelling decision. Range below a threshold? Variance below a threshold? Every pixel within 2σ of the region mean? A unimodal histogram? Each is a different P, each satisfies the definition, and each gives a different answer on the same image.
gap 2
The solution is not unique
For almost any interesting P, many partitions satisfy all four conditions at once. The definition is a feasibility condition, not an optimisation problem — it has a solution set, not a solution. Which one you get is decided by the algorithm's visiting order, not by the definition.
gap 3
P is usually not hereditary
A predicate is hereditary if P(A) = true implies P(B) = true for every B ⊆ A. "Range ≤ τ" is hereditary; "the histogram is unimodal" is not, and neither is "variance ≤ τ" restricted to sub-regions of unequal size. Without heredity, splitting a good region can produce a bad one, and the recursion in §10 stops behaving.
Merging and splitting are not dual. Run the same predicate top-down and bottom-up and you get different answers. The cleanest counterexample is a chessboard. Take P = "the mean grey level of the region is within tolerance of every pixel's" — no: take P = "the region's mean lies in the middle band". Applied top-down, the whole chessboard has mean grey exactly 0.5, the predicate passes immediately, and the algorithm returns one region and stops. Applied bottom-up from single pixels, no black pixel will ever merge with a white one, and the algorithm returns the 64 squares. Same predicate, same image, opposite answers, and the reason is that condition 3 is checked at different scales. Any time a paper reports "region merging" or "region splitting" results, that choice is doing real work.

Three ways to turn a feasibility condition into an algorithm

  1. Fix a rule and a visiting order, and accept the answer it lands on. Region growing (§09), split-and-merge (§10) and the watershed (§12) do this. Fast, deterministic given the order, and the order matters.
  2. Define a global cost and minimise it. Replace conditions 3 and 4 with a number — a sum of a data term measuring how well pixels fit their region and a boundary term penalising the seams. Snakes (§14), level sets (§16), normalised cuts (§21) and graph cuts (§23) all do this. The criterion becomes explicit and arguable, which is progress; the optimisation becomes the hard part.
  3. Model the pixels as samples from a density and find its structure. Mean shift (§18) and mixture models do this. The criterion moves entirely into feature space, and spatial connectivity has to be put back by hand afterwards.
The trap. Reading condition 4 as "the algorithm cannot be improved". It says only that no two adjacent regions can be merged under this predicate. A segmentation can satisfy all four conditions and still be visibly, badly wrong — every region internally uniform, no mergeable pair, and the seams in all the wrong places. Satisfying the definition is necessary and nowhere near sufficient, which is precisely why §26 exists.

04 Global thresholding: the whole image, through a one-dimensional keyhole

The simplest possible segmenter compares each pixel against one number. It has no idea where the pixel is, what is next to it, or what shape anything has. Everything it knows about the image is the histogram — and the histogram is invariant to shuffling the pixels.

global thresholdB(x, y) = 1 if I(x, y) > t, 0 otherwise t ∈ {0, 1, …, L − 1}

That single line is doing a lot of assuming, and it is worth making the assumptions explicit before choosing t, because when thresholding fails it is always one of these that broke.

AssumptionWhat it means in the imageWhat breaks it, and what to do
Two classesEvery pixel is either object or background — no third material, no "partly".Three materials need two thresholds (multi-level Otsu, §05). Anti-aliased and blurred edges genuinely are "partly", and no threshold handles them; that is what soft segmentation and matting are for.
Separable in valueThe two classes occupy different, barely overlapping ranges of intensity.If the ranges overlap, no threshold is good — the best one still misclassifies, and §06 quantifies exactly how much. Change the feature, not the threshold: texture energy, colour distance, gradient magnitude.
Uniform illuminationThe same material has the same value everywhere in the frame.The most common failure by far. A shading gradient shifts one class's range across the image until it crosses the other's. §07 is the fix.
Spatial structure is irrelevantA pixel's own value is sufficient evidence.Noise breaks this instantly: an isolated bright speck in the background is indistinguishable from an object pixel. The repair is not a better threshold but a post-pass — smooth before, or filter components by size after (§08).

Choosing t: five families, in rough order of how much they assume

  • Fixed / manual. Somebody typed a number. Perfectly respectable when the imaging is controlled — a backlit part on a conveyor, a document scanner — and indefensible otherwise.
  • Percentile. "The object is 12% of the frame", so put t at the 88th percentile of the histogram. Uses prior knowledge of size rather than of brightness, which is sometimes the thing you actually know.
  • Valley seeking. Smooth the histogram and put t at the minimum between the two largest peaks. Intuitive, and fragile: the answer depends entirely on how much you smoothed, and a histogram with no valley returns nonsense without complaining.
  • Variance / discriminant criteria. Score every candidate t by how well it separates the two induced classes, and take the best. This is Otsu's method, §05, and it is the default for good reasons.
  • Entropy and model-fitting criteria. Maximise the sum of the entropies of the two classes, or fit a two-Gaussian mixture and put t where the posteriors cross. The mixture route is the only one that is Bayes-optimal when its model is right — and §06 shows Otsu landing very close to it anyway.
Thresholding is one-dimensional clustering, and it is worth saying so out loud. The histogram is a weighted set of points on a line; a threshold is a partition of that line into two intervals. Everything Clustering & k-Means says about choosing k, about clusters of unequal size, and about criteria that reward compactness applies verbatim — with the enormous simplification that in one dimension with two clusters you can afford to enumerate every candidate and take the exact optimum. §05 shows that Otsu is precisely that exhaustive enumeration, and that what it finds is the exact global optimum of 2-means on the grey levels.

05 Otsu's method, derived — and why two opposite-sounding criteria are the same criterion

Otsu's method is usually quoted as "maximise the between-class variance" and sometimes as "minimise the within-class variance". Both are correct, they are not two methods, and the reason is a one-line identity worth deriving rather than believing.

Setup and notation

Let the image have L grey levels and let p(l) = n_l / N be the normalised histogram, so ∑ p(l) = 1. A threshold t splits the levels into class C₁ = {0, …, t} and class C₂ = {t+1, …, L−1}. Everything below is a function of t and of the histogram alone — no pixel positions are ever consulted.

class weights, means and variancesP₁(t) = ∑_{l=0}^{t} p(l) P₂(t) = ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} p(l) = 1 − P₁(t) μ₁(t) = (1/P₁) ∑_{l=0}^{t} l·p(l) μ₂(t) = (1/P₂) ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} l·p(l) σ₁²(t) = (1/P₁) ∑_{l=0}^{t} (l − μ₁)² p(l) σ₂²(t) = (1/P₂) ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} (l − μ₂)² p(l) μ_G = ∑_{l} l·p(l) σ_T² = ∑_{l} (l − μ_G)² p(l)

μ_G and σ_T² are the global mean and variance of the whole histogram. Neither depends on t. Hold on to that; it is the entire argument.

The two candidate criteria

within-class (intra-class) variance — the weighted average of the two spreadsσ_W²(t) = P₁(t)·σ₁²(t) + P₂(t)·σ₂²(t) between-class (inter-class) variance — the spread of the two means about the global meanσ_B²(t) = P₁(t)·(μ₁(t) − μ_G)² + P₂(t)·(μ₂(t) − μ_G)²

A good threshold should make each class tight — small σ_W² — and the two classes far apart — large σ_B². Those sound like two different demands. They are one demand.

The decomposition, in full

Start from the total variance and split the sum at t, adding and subtracting the relevant class mean inside each piece.

step 1 — split and insert the class meansσ_T² = ∑_{l≤t} (l − μ₁ + μ₁ − μ_G)² p(l) + ∑_{l>t} (l − μ₂ + μ₂ − μ_G)² p(l) step 2 — expand each square into three terms = ∑_{l≤t} [ (l−μ₁)² + 2(l−μ₁)(μ₁−μ_G) + (μ₁−μ_G)² ] p(l) + ∑_{l>t} [ … same with μ₂ … ] step 3 — the cross terms are exactly zero, because μ₁ is the mean of class 1∑_{l≤t} (l − μ₁) p(l) = P₁μ₁ − μ₁P₁ = 0 and likewise for class 2 step 4 — what is leftσ_T² = [ P₁σ₁² + P₂σ₂² ] + [ P₁(μ₁−μ_G)² + P₂(μ₂−μ_G)² ] = σ_W²(t) + σ_B²(t)
The identity, and the conclusion. σ_W²(t) + σ_B²(t) = σ_T² for every t, and σ_T² does not depend on t. So as t sweeps the grey levels, whatever σ_B² gains, σ_W² loses, exactly. Maximising the between-class variance and minimising the within-class variance are the same optimisation, and so is maximising the ratio σ_B²/σ_W² = σ_B²/(σ_T² − σ_B²), which is monotone increasing in σ_B². Three criteria, one argmax. This is the analysis-of-variance decomposition, and it is the same identity that underlies Fisher's discriminant.

Otsu maximises σ_B² rather than minimising σ_W² for a purely computational reason: σ_B² can be evaluated in constant time per candidate from two running sums, whereas σ_W² naively needs the class variances. Simplify σ_B² using μ_G = P₁μ₁ + P₂μ₂ and P₂ = 1 − P₁:

the two useful closed formsμ₁ − μ_G = P₂(μ₁ − μ₂) μ₂ − μ_G = P₁(μ₂ − μ₁) ⇒ σ_B²(t) = P₁P₂²(μ₁−μ₂)² + P₂P₁²(μ₁−μ₂)² = P₁P₂(P₁+P₂)(μ₁−μ₂)² ⇒ σ_B²(t) = P₁(t)·P₂(t)·(μ₁(t) − μ₂(t))² ← the interpretable form σ_B²(t) = (μ_G·ω(t) − m(t))² / (ω(t)·(1 − ω(t))) ← the computable form where ω(t) = ∑_{l≤t} p(l) and m(t) = ∑_{l≤t} l·p(l) (the unnormalised first moment)

The second form is what an implementation runs: sweep t from 0 upward, keep the running sums ω and m, evaluate one expression, remember the best. Cost: one pass to build the histogram, one pass of length L over it. For 8-bit images that is 256 evaluations regardless of image size, which is why Otsu is essentially free.

The interpretable form σ_B² = P₁P₂(μ₁−μ₂)² says something the "between-class variance" name hides: the criterion is the squared gap between the class means, weighted by how balanced the split is. The factor P₁P₂ is maximised at a 50/50 split, so Otsu quietly prefers balanced classes — which is exactly the assumption that breaks when the object occupies 2% of the frame. §06 has the counterexample.

Otsu is exact 2-means on the grey levels — not an approximation to it. The quantity σ_W² = P₁σ₁² + P₂σ₂² is, up to the factor N, the k-means objective: the total within-cluster sum of squares. Minimising it over thresholds only ever considers partitions into two intervals. That loses nothing, because in one dimension a nearest-centroid assignment always is an interval partition — the boundary is the midpoint of the two centroids. Enumerating the L−1 thresholds therefore enumerates every partition k-means could possibly converge to. On the worked histogram, brute force over all 65 534 non-trivial two-way partitions of the sixteen levels gives a minimum within-cluster sum of squares of 2.270753, attained by the mask 11111111 00000000 — precisely the interval split at t = 7, and precisely σ_W²(7). So on a one-dimensional histogram, unlike everywhere else, k-means has no local-minimum problem and no initialisation to get wrong. Multi-level Otsu

Nothing in the derivation is specific to two classes. With K classes cut by thresholds t₁ < … < t_{K−1}, the between-class variance generalises to σ_B² = ∑_k P_k(μ_k − μ_G)², the identity σ_T² = σ_W² + σ_B² still holds, and the optimum is still the argmax. What does not generalise is the cost: exhaustive search is O(L^{K−1}), so 8-bit three-class Otsu is 32 640 evaluations — fine — and five-class is 8.6 billion, which is not. Dynamic programming over the cumulative moments reduces this to O(KL²), which is the standard implementation. A cheaper and more common hack is recursive Otsu: threshold, then re-threshold the larger class. It is not the same answer as joint multi-level Otsu, and it is not optimal, but it is one line.

06 When is a histogram bimodal enough to bother — and what Otsu does when it is not

Otsu's method always returns a threshold. It returns one for a picture of a blank wall. The criterion has a maximum for any histogram whatsoever, because it is zero at both ends and positive in between, so "it found a threshold" carries no information at all about whether a threshold was the right idea.

The exact condition, for the model everyone has in mind

Take the standard mental picture: object and background are both Gaussian in intensity, with the same spread σ, means separated by d, and equal areas. Is the resulting histogram bimodal? There is a clean answer.

Put the two means at ±a with a = d/2, and set σ = 1 without loss of generality. The mixture, up to a constant, is f(x) = e^{−(x−a)²/2} + e^{−(x+a)²/2}. By symmetry x = 0 is always a critical point; the whole question is whether it is a peak or a valley.

second derivative of the symmetric two-Gaussian mixturef″(x) = [(x−a)² − 1]·e^{−(x−a)²/2} + [(x+a)² − 1]·e^{−(x+a)²/2} f″(0) = (a² − 1)e^{−a²/2} + (a² − 1)e^{−a²/2} = 2(a² − 1)·e^{−a²/2} so the sign of f″(0) is the sign of a² − 1a < 1 ⇒ f″(0) < 0 ⇒ x = 0 is a maximum ⇒ unimodal a > 1 ⇒ f″(0) > 0 ⇒ x = 0 is a minimum ⇒ bimodal, with two peaks either side in the original units, with a = d/2σthe histogram is bimodal ⟺ d > 2σ
Two standard deviations, exactly. Equal-weight, equal-variance Gaussian classes produce a visibly two-humped histogram if and only if their means are more than 2σ apart. Below that the two humps have already fused into one, even though the classes are perfectly well defined and a threshold still separates them tolerably. So "the histogram has no valley" is not the same statement as "thresholding will not work" — the valley disappears long before the threshold becomes useless. At d = 2σ exactly, the best achievable error is already down to about 16%, and at d = 1.5σ, where the histogram is a single smooth hump with no hint of structure, the best possible threshold still gets 77% of pixels right.

Unequal class sizes make it much worse. The minority class has to be pushed considerably further out before it raises a bump of its own on the flank of the majority hump. Measured on the figure's own grid:

The failure mode that matters: P₁P₂ wants a balanced split

Recall §05's interpretable form, σ_B² = P₁P₂(μ₁ − μ₂)². The factor P₁P₂ peaks at P₁ = P₂ = ½, so among thresholds with similar mean separation, Otsu prefers the one that halves the image. When the object really is about half the frame this bias costs nothing. When the object is 5% of the frame it is catastrophic: Otsu drags the threshold deep into the background hump to fabricate a more balanced split.

The claim to be careful about. "Otsu is optimal." It is optimal for its own criterion, and its criterion is not the error rate. With a 5% object separated by a generous 4σ — a histogram that is genuinely bimodal — Otsu picks a threshold with a 26.8% misclassification rate where the Bayes-optimal threshold achieves 0.8%. That is a factor of 33, on an easy image, with the histogram doing exactly what the textbook says it should. The fix is not a better search; the search was already exact. The fix is a different criterion — fit a two-component mixture and threshold where the posteriors cross, or use a method that knows the class prior.

What to do when the histogram is unimodal

  • Check whether it should be. A unimodal histogram from a scene that visibly contains two things means the feature is wrong, not the threshold. Intensity is often the wrong feature: try local gradient magnitude, a texture energy, a colour-opponent channel, or the distance from a fitted background model.
  • Use a unimodal-specific rule. The best known takes the line from the histogram's peak to its far tail end and puts the threshold at the point of the histogram furthest from that line — a corner-finding rule rather than a valley-finding one. It has no valley to depend on and degrades gracefully.
  • Use the prior. If you know the object covers roughly π of the frame, a percentile threshold at 1 − π is often dramatically better than Otsu, precisely because it supplies the information P₁P₂ is guessing at.
  • Do not trust the separability number as a bimodality test. The normalised criterion η = σ_B²(t*)/σ_T² ∈ [0, 1] is often quoted as a quality score. It measures how much of the variance the split explains, which is not the same thing. The disastrous 5%-object case above scores η = 0.548; a balanced pair separated by only 1.5σ, where Otsu is exactly Bayes-optimal and cannot be improved, scores a higher 0.656. η ranks them backwards on the question you care about.

07 Adaptive thresholding: when one number cannot cover the frame

The most common reason a global threshold fails has nothing to do with the algorithm that chose it. It is that the same material is not the same brightness in different parts of the picture, so no single number can separate the classes anywhere except locally.

Why illumination is fatal, in one equation

A pixel's value is, to a first approximation, the product of what the surface is and how much light fell on it:

the multiplicative image modelI(x, y) = R(x, y) · S(x, y) R = reflectance (the thing you want) S = shading (the thing you do not) taking logs turns the product into a sum, which is why this is called the homomorphic viewlog I = log R + log S and log S is low-frequency — it varies slowly across the frame

Segmenting means recovering a property of R. A global threshold on I asks whether R·S > t, which is R > t/S(x, y) — a threshold on reflectance that silently moves as you cross the image. If S varies by more than the contrast ratio between the two materials, the dark material in the bright corner becomes brighter than the light material in the dark corner, and the two classes are no longer separable by any constant.

The exact condition for a global threshold to survive. Let the two materials have reflectances R_o < R_b and let shading range over [S_min, S_max]. A constant threshold exists if and only if the two value ranges do not overlap: R_o·S_max < R_b·S_min, that is S_max/S_min < R_b/R_o. The permissible illumination variation is exactly the material contrast ratio. Ink at 90 on paper at 210 gives a ratio of 2.33, so a shading field that swings by more than 2.33× has already destroyed global thresholding, whatever criterion picked the threshold. Printed text under a desk lamp routinely swings by more than that across one page.

Three local rules, and what each is trying to fix

All of them replace the constant t with a field t(x, y) estimated from a window W of side 2w+1 centred on the pixel. Write μ_W and σ_W for the local mean and standard deviation.

mean minus offset — the simplest, and often enought(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) − C Niblack — scale the offset by the local contrastt(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) + k·σ_W(x, y), k ≈ −0.2 for dark objects Sauvola — shrink the offset to nothing where the window is flatt(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) · [ 1 + k·( σ_W(x, y)/R − 1 ) ], k ≈ 0.2, R ≈ 128

Read the third one carefully, because the algebra is the whole idea. When the window has strong contrast, σ_W → R, the bracket → 1, and t → μ_W: it behaves like the plain mean rule. When the window is flat — nothing in it but background and noise — σ_W → 0, the bracket → 1 − k, and t → 0.8·μ_W, a threshold well below everything present, so nothing fires. Niblack does the opposite: with σ_W → 0 its threshold collapses onto μ_W itself, and half the noise in an empty region falls below it.

The failure everyone hits first. Running a local rule and getting a snowstorm of speckle in every blank area. It is not a bug and no amount of tuning C removes it. A local rule has to invent a boundary inside every window, because it has no way to know the window contains only one class. Measured on the figure's own blank band with an 8-pixel half-window: the mean-minus-offset rule fires on 13.67% of blank pixels and Niblack on 25.69%, while Sauvola fires on 0.00% — because Sauvola is the one rule of the three that was designed to notice that the window is flat.

Choosing the window size: a two-sided constraint, and both sides bite

  • Too small and the window fits inside an object. Then μ_W is the object's own mean, the threshold tracks the object, and the interior of every thick stroke is labelled background — objects come out hollow. The window must be larger than the widest object feature.
  • Too large and the window spans the illumination change it was supposed to be immune to. In the limit w → ∞ the local rule is the global rule and inherits all its failures. The window must be smaller than the scale on which the shading varies.
  • If those two constraints do not leave a gap, no window size works, and that is a real possibility — a thick object under fast-varying light. The answer then is not a better window but the next paragraph.

The alternative that is often better: estimate the shading and divide it out

Rather than making the threshold vary, make the image stop varying. Estimate S — by a morphological opening with a structuring element larger than every object (the "rolling ball" background), by a heavy low-pass filter, or by fitting a low-order polynomial or plane to the image — then form I/Ŝ, or equivalently log I − log Ŝ, and threshold that globally. The advantage is that after correction you get a single, interpretable, image-wide threshold back, and every diagnostic from §05 and §06 becomes usable again. The morphology this needs belongs to Part 2.

08 From a binary map to regions: connected components, and the paradox in the grid

A threshold produces a binary image, not a segmentation. Nothing so far has grouped anything. The step that turns "these pixels are bright" into "these are three objects" is connected-component labelling, and it is where spatial information finally enters a thresholding pipeline.

The definition, and the choice hiding inside it

Two foreground pixels are in the same component when a path of foreground pixels joins them, each consecutive pair being neighbours. Everything turns on what "neighbour" means, and there are two conventions on a square grid.

4-connectivity — the von Neumann neighbourhoodN₄(x, y) = { (x±1, y), (x, y±1) } distance ‖·‖₁ = 1 8-connectivity — the Moore neighbourhoodN₈(x, y) = N₄(x, y) ∪ { (x±1, y±1) } distance ‖·‖_∞ = 1

This looks like a taste question. It is not: the two conventions give different numbers of objects, different numbers of holes, and — if you choose the same one for foreground and background — a topological contradiction.

The Jordan curve paradox, on eleven by eleven pixels

The Jordan curve theorem says a simple closed curve in the plane divides it into exactly two pieces, an inside and an outside, and the curve itself is connected. Draw a diagonal diamond ring of 16 pixels on an 11 × 11 grid and count.

ConventionForeground piecesBackground piecesVerdict
4 for both162The "curve" is 16 isolated dots and is not connected at all — yet it still separates inside from outside. Not a curve, but it does divide the plane.
8 for both11The contradiction. The ring is a single connected closed curve, and the inside and the outside are also connected — to each other, through the diagonal gaps. Jordan fails.
8 foreground, 4 background12Connected curve, two background pieces. Jordan holds. This is the standard choice, and the reason libraries default to 8-connectivity for objects.
4 foreground, 8 background161Also consistent, and appropriate when the objects are the light regions rather than the dark ones. The rule is simply that the two must differ.
The rule to remember. Foreground and background must use opposite connectivities. Using 8 for both makes thin diagonal structures both connect and fail to separate; using 4 for both shatters every diagonal line into dust. Which of the two mixed conventions you pick decides whether diagonal contact counts as touching, and that is a modelling decision about your objects, not a mathematical one. The alternative fix, used in some morphology systems, is to work on a hexagonal grid, where every neighbour is at the same distance and the question never arises.

The algorithm

Labelling is one pass with a union–find structure, and it is worth knowing because the same structure reappears in the graph-based merging of §11 and in the watershed of §12.

  1. Raster scan. For each foreground pixel, look at the already-visited neighbours — for 8-connectivity that is the pixel to the left and the three above. If none is labelled, mint a new label. If one is, copy it.
  2. Record collisions. If two visited neighbours carry different labels, both are correct and the labels are equivalent: union(a, b). This is a U-shaped object being met from both arms before the join is seen.
  3. Second pass. Replace every label by find(label), and renumber consecutively.

Cost is O(N·α(N)) where α is the inverse Ackermann function — constant for any conceivable image. The flood-fill alternative (one depth- or breadth-first search per component) is O(N) and simpler to write, but touches the image in scattered order and is slower in practice on large images for cache reasons.

What you get once you have components

  • Area — the pixel count. The single most useful filter there is: drop everything below a minimum area and most threshold noise disappears.
  • Perimeter — the count of boundary edges, or a chain-code length. Beware: the naive count over-estimates diagonal boundaries by up to √2, so a "circularity" computed from it is biased.
  • Bounding box, centroid, second moments — the centroid (x̄, ȳ) and the covariance of the pixel coordinates give an equivalent ellipse, its orientation and its elongation, all in one pass.
  • Euler number E = C − H, components minus holes. Cheap, and a genuinely topological descriptor — but it depends on the connectivity convention exactly as the counts above do, so it must be quoted together with the convention.
  • Compactness 4πA/P², which is 1 for a circle and small for a ragged shape. Useful for rejecting noise clusters, and sensitive to the perimeter bias just mentioned.

09 Region growing: the oldest idea, and the one failure that defines it

Start from a seed pixel, repeatedly annex any neighbour that is similar enough, stop when nothing else qualifies. Three lines of pseudocode, no parameters except a tolerance, and regions that are connected by construction. It is the first thing anyone writes and it fails in a characteristic, instructive way.

Leakage, and why it is not fixable by tuning

Two regions that ought to be separate almost always touch somewhere: a few pixels where the boundary is blurred, or noise happened to bridge the gap, or the objects genuinely abut. A region-growing front only needs one pixel of the boundary to be within tolerance. It goes through, and on the other side it is inside a large uniform region where every neighbour qualifies, so it fills the whole thing.

The failure is a cliff, not a slope: the region is exactly right, exactly right, exactly right, and then it is twice the size it should be. On the figure below the running-mean variant first admits a pixel of the neighbour at a tolerance of 23.50; at a tolerance of 28 it has taken 22 of the neighbour's pixels, and at 30 — a nudge of two grey levels — it has taken 633 of them. The parameter has no safe setting because the safe setting is a knife edge whose position depends on the noise in a handful of pixels.

The four repairs, and what each costs

  1. Grow on the gradient, not on the intensity. Require the step between the candidate and its already-accepted neighbour to be small, rather than the candidate's distance to the region mean. A blurred boundary is still a run of small steps, so this helps less than it sounds, but it makes the criterion local and removes the runaway feedback.
  2. Order the front by cost, not by arrival. Keep the boundary pixels in a priority queue ordered by |I(p) − μ_R| and always take the cheapest. The region then grows outward in order of similarity rather than in order of geometry, so the weak boundary is crossed last instead of first. This is seeded region growing, and it is one line of difference from the naive version and much better behaved.
  3. Make the seeds compete. Grow all regions at once and let each pixel go to whichever front reaches it first under its own predicate. A front arriving at the neck from one side meets a front arriving from the other, and neither crosses. Measured on the figure's image, two competing seeds never let a single pixel cross the neck, at any tolerance the figure can search — where a single seed has swallowed the whole neighbour by a tolerance of 40. (Above about 88 both regions do leak into the background, which is a different failure and one that competition cannot fix, because there is no third seed to race.) This is the single most effective fix, and taken to its limit with one seed per local minimum it is the watershed of §12.
  4. Constrain the result afterwards. Cap the region area, require compactness, or reject a region whose growth curve shows a sudden jump. Crude, and sometimes the only thing that works.

10 Splitting, merging, and the quadtree that sits between them

Region growing works upward from single pixels. The opposite strategy starts with the whole image and cuts. Neither alone is any good; the combination is, and understanding why is understanding what each half contributes.

Splitting: recursive subdivision under a predicate

  1. Start with the whole image as one region.
  2. For each region, test the homogeneity predicate P. If it holds, stop; the region is a leaf.
  3. If it fails, cut the region into four equal quadrants and recurse on each.
  4. Stop also at a minimum block size, typically 1×1 or 2×2.

The bookkeeping is a quadtree: the root is the image, every internal node has four children, and the leaves are the regions. On a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ image the tree has depth at most n, the total work is O(N) if the predicate is evaluated from summed-area tables, and the result is a partition into axis-aligned dyadic squares.

Which is precisely the problem. Every region boundary is a horizontal or vertical line at a power-of-two coordinate. A diagonal edge comes out as a staircase; a circle comes out as a pixelated approximation; and two halves of one smooth object that happen to straddle the image's centre line are never in the same region, no matter how similar they are, because splitting never puts anything back together.

The predicates, and the trap in each

Predicate P(R)Hereditary?Behaviour, and where it misleads
max(R) − min(R) ≤ τyesThe safest choice, because heredity guarantees that splitting a good region gives good children, so the recursion is monotone. Ruinously sensitive to a single outlier pixel — one hot pixel splits a block all the way down.
σ²(R) ≤ τnoRobust to outliers, which is why it is popular. But a checkerboard of two values has a large variance and splits forever, while a smooth ramp of the same range has a smaller variance and does not — variance cannot distinguish texture from a gradient.

Heredity is not a technicality. If P is hereditary — true on a set implies true on every subset — the recursion terminates cleanly, the leaves are the maximal dyadic blocks that satisfy P, and the answer does not depend on the order of the recursion. If it is not, a block can fail the test while all four of its children fail it too and their children pass, so the depth reached is not a monotone function of the data, and two implementations that visit the tree in different orders can produce different leaves. Range is hereditary. Variance is not.

Merging: repairing what splitting broke

After splitting, run a pass that fuses adjacent leaves whose union still satisfies the predicate:

the merge conditionmerge Rᵢ and Rⱼ if Rᵢ adjacent Rⱼ and P(Rᵢ ∪ Rⱼ) = true stop when condition 4 of §03 holds — no adjacent pair can still be merged

Merging repairs three of splitting's four sins. It reunites the halves of an object that straddled a cut line; it eliminates the artificial boundary between two identical adjacent blocks; and it lets a region take a non-rectangular shape, because a union of squares need not be a square. What it does not repair is the staircase: the leaf boundaries are still on dyadic lines, so a diagonal edge remains a diagonal edge quantised to blocks, and the finest step is the smallest leaf. That is the permanent cost of the representation, and the reason quadtree segmentation lost to the graph and energy methods later on this page.

Merging also introduces the order-dependence that splitting did not have. Merging R₁ into R₂ changes R₂'s statistics, which may now prevent a merge with R₃ that would have succeeded a moment earlier. Greedy merging in increasing order of dissimilarity — the same idea as single-link agglomerative clustering — is the usual compromise, and §11 turns it into something with an actual guarantee.

11 The region adjacency graph, and a merge rule that adapts to each region

Once regions exist, the natural object is not the image any more but a graph: one node per region, an edge between every pair that touches, and a weight on each edge saying how different they are. Merging becomes edge contraction, and the whole of §10's merge pass becomes a question about which edges to contract.

the region adjacency graphG = (V, E) V = {R₁, …, R_n} E = { (i, j) : Rᵢ touches Rⱼ } w(i, j) = a dissimilarity — |μᵢ − μⱼ|, a colour distance, a histogram distance, an edge strength along the shared boundary

The RAG is a genuine compression: an image with a million pixels might have a few thousand regions and a few tens of thousands of edges, so an algorithm that would be hopeless on pixels becomes cheap. That is the reason superpixels (§25) exist, and the reason the graph methods of §20 to §23 are usually run on regions rather than raw pixels.

Why a single global threshold on w cannot work

The obvious rule is "contract every edge with w < T". It fails for a reason that is worth measuring rather than asserting. Consider an image with two smooth halves separated by a small step, and a patch of high-frequency texture sitting in one of them. Two edge populations:

The fix is to compare each candidate edge not against a constant but against the region's own internal variability.

The graph-based merging predicate

Define, for a region R and a pair of adjacent regions:

internal difference — the largest edge in the region's minimum spanning treeInt(R) = max_{e ∈ MST(R)} w(e) difference between two regions — the weakest edge joining themDif(R₁, R₂) = min_{e = (v₁, v₂), v₁ ∈ R₁, v₂ ∈ R₂} w(e) the comparison, with a size-dependent slack τ(R) = k/|R|MInt(R₁, R₂) = min( Int(R₁) + k/|R₁| , Int(R₂) + k/|R₂| ) merge exactly when there is no evidence of a boundarymerge R₁, R₂ ⟺ Dif(R₁, R₂) ≤ MInt(R₁, R₂)

Read what this says. Two regions stay apart only if the weakest link between them is stronger than the strongest link inside either of them (plus slack). A texture patch has a large Int, so it tolerates a strong external edge and stays whole; a smooth half has a tiny Int, so even a modest step at its border counts as evidence and survives. The threshold is per-region and is read off the data.

The slack τ(R) = k/|R| exists because Int(R) is meaningless for a small region — a single pixel has no internal edges at all, so Int = 0 and it would never merge with anything. The slack is large for small regions and vanishes for large ones, so k is not a threshold and not a region count: it is a preferred region size. Doubling k makes regions bigger; it does not make the criterion stricter.

The algorithm, and what is actually guaranteed

  1. Sort all edges by weight, ascending. On a pixel grid with 4- or 8-neighbours this is O(N log N), or O(N) with a counting sort on integer weights.
  2. Process edges in order. For edge (a, b) with weight w, find the current regions R_a, R_b. If they are already the same, skip.
  3. Because edges arrive in ascending order, this edge is Dif(R_a, R_b) — no other edge between them has yet been seen, so none is smaller. Merge if w ≤ MInt, and set the new region's Int to w, which is also correct because w is the largest edge in the merged MST.
  4. Optionally, a final pass absorbing every region below a minimum area into its most similar neighbour.
What the guarantee is, stated exactly — and what it is not. Call a segmentation too fine if some pair of adjacent regions in it satisfies the merge condition, so there is no evidence of a boundary between them. Call it too coarse if some proper refinement of it is not too fine. The theorem is that the greedy algorithm above produces a segmentation that is neither too fine nor too coarse, and that such a segmentation exists and can be found in near-linear time.

This is not a claim that the result minimises any global objective, and it is not a uniqueness claim. Both properties are defined with respect to the very predicate the algorithm uses, so the theorem says the greedy procedure is self-consistent, not that the answer is the best partition of the image by some independent standard. Change w, or change k, and you get a different segmentation which is equally "neither too fine nor too coarse". The guarantee is real and it is much stronger than most merging heuristics offer; it is simply a guarantee about the algorithm's relationship to its own criterion, which is the pattern this whole page keeps running into.

12 The watershed: read the image as a landscape and flood it

Treat the grey value as an elevation. The image becomes a terrain: dark regions are valleys, bright ridges are mountain chains. Punch a hole at the bottom of every valley and let water rise uniformly from below. Where two rising lakes are about to meet, build a dam. When the whole terrain is submerged, the dams are the segmentation.

The three kinds of point

  • Regional minima — connected sets of pixels from which every path leads strictly upward. Every one of them seeds a lake.
  • Catchment basin points — a raindrop landing here runs down into one particular minimum. These are the region interiors.
  • Watershed points — a raindrop landing here could run into two or more different minima. These are the dams, and they form the boundaries.

The definition is entirely determined by the terrain: no thresholds, no seeds, no parameters at all. That is the watershed's great virtue and, in the same breath, its defect — §13.

You almost never flood the image itself

Images are not landscapes of the right shape. Objects are usually not dark blobs separated by bright ridges — they are regions of roughly constant value separated by edges. So the transform is run on the gradient magnitude, where uniform interiors become flat valleys and object boundaries become the ridges the dams will be built on.

what actually gets floodedg(x, y) = ‖∇(G_σ * I)(x, y)‖ smooth first, then take the gradient magnitude and for colour, one common choiceg = max over channels, or the norm of the per-channel gradients, or a structure-tensor magnitude

The smoothing is not optional and its scale σ is the single most consequential parameter in the whole pipeline, because every spurious local minimum of g becomes a region. The gradient operators live in Part 4 and the smoothing in Part 2; this section takes g as given.

The flooding algorithm

The classic implementation sorts pixels by value once, then sweeps levels upward, which is what makes it near-linear.

  1. Sort all pixels by g. With integer values this is a counting sort in O(N + L).
  2. At level n, take the set of pixels with g = n and find its connected components.
  3. For each such component q, look at what it touches in the already-flooded set C[n−1]:
    · nothing — a new minimum has been reached; open a new basin;
    · exactly one basin — q is part of that basin's rise; absorb it;
    · two or more basins — a ridge has been crested; build a dam inside q and do not let the basins merge.
  4. Increment the level and repeat to the maximum.

An equivalent and more common implementation replaces the level sweep with a priority queue: initialise it with the minima, repeatedly pop the lowest-valued boundary pixel, assign it to its neighbour's basin if all labelled neighbours agree, and mark it as a dam if they do not. Written that way, the watershed is exactly §09's competitive region growing with the global rule "always take the cheapest pixel next" — and that is why it does not leak: the front cannot cross a ridge until every pixel below the ridge has already been claimed.

Persistence: how deep is a basin, really? Every minimum except the global one eventually merges into a deeper neighbour, at the level of the lowest saddle joining them. The difference between that saddle level and the minimum's own depth is the minimum's persistence — the depth of water it can hold before spilling over. It is the honest measure of how significant a basin is, it is invariant to how wide the basin is, and it is the quantity every over-segmentation fix is secretly thresholding. On the landscape below, the five minima have persistences of 8.20, 17.31, 23.02, 29.85 and infinity — and reading those five numbers tells you in advance exactly how the region count will fall as you filter.

13 Over-segmentation, and the three ways out of it

The watershed has no parameters, which sounds like a virtue until you notice what it implies: the number of regions is decided entirely by the number of local minima in the gradient image, and a noisy gradient image has thousands of them. The transform does exactly what it promises and the answer is useless.

On the figure below — six discs on a flat background, moderate noise, a moderate pre-smooth — the gradient magnitude has 264 regional minima, so the raw watershed returns 264 regions for an image with seven. Its adjusted Rand index against the truth is 0.019: statistically indistinguishable from shredding the image at random. This is not an edge case; it is what the raw watershed does on every real image.

Where the spurious minima come from

  • Noise. Independent noise in the image becomes noise in the gradient, and every local dip is a minimum. Halving the noise roughly halves the count, and never removes it.
  • Texture. Real surfaces have gradient structure at every scale. A patch of fabric or foliage is a mountain range of minima, all of them real features of the image and none of them a region boundary.
  • Quantisation and compression. Eight-bit values and block-transform artefacts create flat plateaus and small steps that are genuine minima of the digitised signal and have no counterpart in the scene.
  • Interiors that are not flat. Shading across an object makes its interior a gentle slope with several tiny dips rather than one basin.

Fix 1 — smooth harder

Increasing the pre-smoothing σ destroys shallow minima and reduces the count. It is the cheapest option and the least controllable: at σ = 1.0 the figure's gradient has 637 minima, at σ = 1.6 it has 264, and at σ = 2.4 it has 88 — still more than twelve times too many, and by then the boundaries have moved. Smoothing removes small minima and large ones alike, and it displaces every edge it survives. Nobody has ever found the right σ this way; it is a first pass, not a fix.

Fix 2 — filter the minima by persistence

§12's persistence is the principled version. Suppress every minimum whose persistence is below h, and flood from what remains. In morphology this is the h-minima transform, implemented by grey-scale reconstruction of g + h under g; on the merge tree it is simply deleting the subtrees that never held more than h of water.

It works, and it is sharply sensitive. On the figure: h = 5 gives 11 regions, h = 8 gives 10, h = 12 gives 8, and h = 16 gives exactly 7 at an adjusted Rand index of 0.928. Four values, four different answers, and only the reader knowing there are six discs can tell which was right.

Fix 3 — impose the minima

The practical answer abandons the pretence that the region count can be discovered. Supply a set of markers and modify the terrain so that its regional minima are exactly the markers and nothing else — that is minima imposition, and it guarantees the region count in advance.

minima imposition, in the morphological formset f(x) = 0 on the marker set M set f(x) = g_max everywhere else then flood the grey-scale reconstruction of min(g + 1, f) under f in practice, and equivalently for the partition, simply seed the flooding at M and nowhere else

Two kinds of marker are used together, and both are necessary:

internal markers
One per object
A click, a scribble, a stroke — or automatic: the regional maxima of a distance transform, the output of a crude detector, the connected components of a coarse threshold. Each one becomes exactly one region.
external markers
One or more for the background
Without them the background has no seed, and the object basins expand until they collide with each other rather than stopping at the objects' edges. The image border, or the watershed lines of a coarse first pass, are the usual choices.

With six internal markers and the border as the external marker, the figure's marker-controlled watershed returns 7 regions at an adjusted Rand index of 0.928 — the same accuracy the best possible h achieves, without having to find it. What changed is not the algorithm but who supplies the region count, and that is the honest characterisation of every interactive segmentation method on this page.

Why the score is 0.928 and not 1. Even a perfect marker set does not give a perfect segmentation, because the watershed line sits on the ridge of the smoothed gradient, and smoothing widened that ridge and pulled it slightly off the true edge. A one-pixel boundary displacement over a few thousand boundary pixels is worth a few percent of any region-overlap score. This is the boundary-localisation cost of pre-smoothing, and it is the reason §26 insists on reporting a boundary measure alongside a region measure — they penalise different mistakes.

14 Active contours: a curve that falls downhill into an edge

Everything so far has grouped pixels. A snake does the opposite: it represents the answer as a curve and moves the curve until it is happy. That change of representation buys sub-pixel boundaries and built-in smoothness, and costs you topology, initialisation and any hope of a global optimum.

The energy

Let the contour be f(s) = (x(s), y(s)), parameterised by arc length s. Its energy is a sum of an internal term that depends only on the curve's shape and an external term that depends only on the image.

internal energy — the curve's own opinion about its shapeE_int = ∫ [ α(s)·‖f_s(s)‖² + β(s)·‖f_ss(s)‖² ] ds the first-derivative term is elasticity: it resists stretching the second-derivative term is rigidity: it resists bending discretised on N samples with spacing h, which is what an implementation minimisesE_int = ∑ᵢ α(i)·‖f(i+1) − f(i)‖² / h² + ∑ᵢ β(i)·‖f(i+1) − 2f(i) + f(i−1)‖² / h⁴ external energy — the image's opinion, usually just the edge termE_edge = ∑ᵢ −‖∇I(f(i))‖² or E_edge = ∑ᵢ −|(G_σ * ∇²I)(f(i))|² optional user constraintsE_spring = ∑ᵢ kᵢ·‖f(i) − d(i)‖² attract point i to an anchor d(i) E_balloon = −p · (area enclosed) a constant outward pressure along the normal

The α term makes the curve behave like a rubber band and the β term like a thin metal strip. Setting β = 0 at one control point lets a corner form there, which is how a snake is allowed to have corners at all; leaving β > 0 everywhere guarantees a smooth curve and guarantees it will round off every genuine corner in the object.

Why a snake shrinks, derived

This is worth doing because it is the source of the single most common complaint about snakes, and because the answer is three lines.

Take a closed snake of N equally spaced points on a circle of radius r, with no image force at all. Consecutive points are 2πr/N apart, so:

the elasticity term on a circle of radius r∑ᵢ ‖f(i+1) − f(i)‖² = N · (2πr/N)² = 4π²r² / N ∝ r² the rigidity term on the same circle — second difference ≈ curvature × spacing²‖f(i+1) − 2f(i) + f(i−1)‖ ≈ (1/r)·(2πr/N)² = 4π²r / N² ∑ᵢ ‖·‖² = N · (4π²r/N²)² = 16π⁴r² / N³ ∝ r²

Both internal terms are proportional to r², so both are minimised at r = 0. A snake with no image force collapses to a point, and a snake whose image force is weak collapses past the boundary it was supposed to find. There are exactly two remedies: initialise outside the object so the shrinkage is doing useful work, or add the balloon term with the pressure pointing outward, which is a constant force with no image justification whatsoever and has to be turned off once the curve arrives.

Minimising it

Taking the variation of E_int gives a fourth-order term, which discretises into a pentadiagonal circulant matrix A = αD₂ + βD₄ — the second- and fourth-difference operators. The standard update is semi-implicit: treat the internal energy implicitly, which is unconditionally stable, and the image force explicitly, which is cheap.

one semi-implicit step, γ the step size(I + γA)·f^(t+1) = f^(t) + γ·F_ext(f^(t)) ⇒ f^(t+1) = (I + γA)⁻¹ ( f^(t) + γ·F_ext(f^(t)) ) and the image force is the downhill direction of the external energyF_ext = −∇E_ext = w_edge · ∇‖∇(G_σ * I)‖²

(I + γA) is banded and circulant, so it is factored once and reused at every step, and a step costs O(N). The whole evolution of a hundred-point snake for a hundred iterations is microseconds; the expensive part was never the optimisation.

Snakes are worth understanding not because they are the state of the art — they have not been for decades — but because their four failure modes are the four problems every later contour method was built to solve, and each solution is named after the failure it fixes.

The interactive cousin. If a user is present, the global-optimum problem becomes easy. It snaps to the boundary in real time, it is globally optimal on that graph rather than locally optimal, and it costs one shortest-path computation per mouse move. 16 Level sets: stop moving the curve, move a surface underneath it

The trick is to stop representing the contour at all. Keep instead a scalar function φ(x, y) over the whole image, and define the contour to be wherever it crosses zero. Moving the contour is then a matter of changing φ, and the contour can split, merge, vanish and appear without anything special happening, because none of those events is a change to the representation.

the implicit representationC(t) = { (x, y) : φ(x, y, t) = 0 } φ < 0 inside, φ > 0 outside (or the reverse; pick one and keep it) the usual choice for φ: the signed distance to the contour, for which‖∇φ‖ = 1 everywhere the outward normal is n̂ = ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ the curvature is κ = div( ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ ) moving the contour with normal speed F becomes a PDE on φ∂φ/∂t = F · ‖∇φ‖

That last line is the whole method. Any evolution you can express as "move every point of the contour along its normal at speed F" becomes a partial differential equation on a fixed grid, and grids do not care about topology.

The edge-driven version: geodesic active contours

g is an edge-stopping function — near 1 in flat areas, near 0 on an edgeg(I) = 1 / (1 + ‖∇(G_σ * I)‖²) the evolution, in the two equivalent forms∂φ/∂t = ‖∇φ‖ · div( g(I) · ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ ) = g(I)·‖∇φ‖·κ + ∇g(I) · ∇φ the first term smooths — curvature flow, switched off on an edge because g is small there the second term attracts — it slides the contour down the gradient of g, onto the edge

Read the second form. The first term moves the contour along its curvature — it shortens and straightens the curve — but it is multiplied by g, so it switches itself off on an edge and leaves the curve where it is. The second term slides the contour down the gradient of g, that is, toward the places where g is small, that is, onto the edges. It is the level-set analogue of the snake's E_edge and it inherits the snake's capture-range problem exactly: ∇g is zero wherever the image is flat.

The region-driven version, which does not need edges at all

The important step is to stop using the gradient. Instead of asking the contour to sit on an edge, ask it to make the statistics inside and outside as different as possible:

the two-phase piecewise-constant energyE(C, c₁, c₂) = λ₁·∫_inside (I − c₁)² dx + λ₂·∫_outside (I − c₂)² dx + μ·Length(C) for a fixed contour the optimal constants are just the two means, in closed formc₁ = mean of I inside C c₂ = mean of I outside C and the level-set gradient flow, with a smoothed delta so the whole grid can move∂φ/∂t = δ_ε(φ) · [ μ·κ − λ₁(I − c₁)² + λ₂(I − c₂)² ] δ_ε(φ) = (1/π) · ε / (ε² + φ²)

This is the two-phase case of a much older piecewise-smooth segmentation model, and it has three properties the edge version lacks. It has global reach — every pixel contributes to c₁ and c₂, so the contour is driven from anywhere in the image, not only from within a few pixels of an edge. It works on boundaries that have no gradient at all, such as a slow ramp between two regions with different means. And it is far less sensitive to noise, because it integrates over regions rather than differentiating.

What it costs

  • You now solve a PDE on the whole grid. A snake moved 72 points; a level set updates every pixel of φ at every step. The standard fix is the narrow band: only update φ within a few pixels of the zero set, and rebuild the band when the contour leaves it. Cost falls from O(N) per step to O(√N)-ish, at the price of real bookkeeping.
  • φ does not stay a distance function. The evolution destroys ‖∇φ‖ = 1, and once φ becomes very flat or very steep near the zero set the numerics degrade. The remedy is periodic reinitialisation — recompute the signed distance to the current zero set, typically with a fast marching method — which is an extra step with no physical meaning that nonetheless has to be tuned.
  • The topology freedom is not always wanted. If you know there is exactly one object, a method that will happily return three is a liability, and constraining a level set to stay connected is awkward. This is a real reason parametric contours survive.
  • It is still a local minimum. The energy is non-convex in φ and the flow is gradient descent. Region-based versions are much less sensitive to initialisation than edge-based ones, but "much less sensitive" is not "insensitive".

17 Pixels as samples: the density view of segmentation

Forget the image grid for a moment. Take every pixel's feature vector — its colour, or its colour and position — and throw the points into feature space. Segmentation becomes: find the clusters in this cloud, then map the cluster labels back onto the grid.

That reframing is what connects this part of vision to the whole of cluster analysis. It is also where all the leverage is, because it separates two decisions that are usually tangled together: what makes two pixels similar (the feature vector and its metric) and what makes a group a group (the clustering criterion).

The bandwidth is the model

h plays exactly the role that k plays in k-means, τ plays in region growing and h plays in the persistence filter: it is the place where the granularity of §02 gets supplied. 18 Mean shift: gradient ascent that needs no gradient and no step size

Once the density is defined, the clusters are its modes and the cluster memberships are the basins of attraction. Actually computing f everywhere is out of the question in more than two dimensions. Mean shift finds the modes without ever evaluating f at all — and the reason is a two-line rearrangement that turns the gradient into a weighted average.

The derivation

Differentiate the estimate. Writing k′ for the derivative of the profile and g = −k′:

the gradient of the density estimate∇f(x) = ∇ ∑ᵢ k( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) = ∑ᵢ k′(·) · 2(x − xᵢ)/h² ∝ ∑ᵢ (xᵢ − x) · g( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) now factor out the total weight — this is the whole trick∇f(x) ∝ [ ∑ᵢ gᵢ ] · ( ∑ᵢ xᵢ gᵢ / ∑ᵢ gᵢ − x ) where gᵢ = g( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) the bracketed vector is the mean shiftm(x) = ∑ᵢ xᵢ·gᵢ / ∑ᵢ gᵢ − x = (weighted mean of the neighbours) − (where you are) so the update is: move to the weighted mean, and repeaty_{k+1} = y_k + m(y_k) = ∑ᵢ xᵢ·g(‖y_k − xᵢ‖²/h²) / ∑ᵢ g(‖y_k − xᵢ‖²/h²)

Three consequences follow, and each of them is a reason the method is used.

  • The mean shift vector points along the density gradient. Not approximately — exactly, because the only thing separating them is the positive scalar ∑gᵢ. So the iteration is gradient ascent, and it never needs the gradient computed.
  • The step size is automatic and correct. The scalar that was divided out, ∑gᵢ, is large where the density is high. Dividing by it means the step is short in dense regions — near a mode, where you want to slow down — and long in sparse ones. This is why mean shift converges without a line search, and why naive gradient ascent on the same f does not: with a fixed step size it overshoots the peaks and crawls across the flats.
  • It converges, and the proof is short. For a monotonically decreasing convex profile k, the density is non-decreasing along the iteration, f(y_{k+1}) ≥ f(y_k), and the sequence is bounded, so it converges to a stationary point. With the Epanechnikov kernel the derivative kernel is a uniform ball, the update becomes "move to the mean of the points inside the ball", and it terminates in a finite number of steps. With the Gaussian kernel the trajectory is smoother and the results are usually better, but convergence near a mode is asymptotic and slow.
Mean shift is the watershed run backwards. The watershed of §12 starts at every minimum and floods upward, assigning each pixel to the minimum it drains into. Mean shift starts at every sample and climbs upward, assigning each sample to the maximum it ascends to. Same construction, same basins-of-attraction definition of a region, opposite sign — and the same failure mode, over-segmentation from spurious extrema, controlled by the same kind of smoothing parameter. If you understand one, you have understood the other.

The procedure, and what it costs

  1. Start a mode estimate at every sample xᵢ.
  2. Iterate the weighted-mean update until the shift falls below a tolerance.
  3. Merge converged points that end up within a small distance of one another; those groups are the modes.
  4. Label each original sample by the mode its trajectory reached. That labelling is the segmentation.

Cost is the problem: each iteration of each trajectory touches every sample, so a full run is O(n²) per iteration with n = the number of pixels. The standard accelerations are to subsample the starting points and assign the rest by nearest trajectory; to use a spatial data structure so only samples within the kernel's support are visited (immediate, and exact, for the Epanechnikov kernel because its support is finite); or to evaluate a coarse version of f on a low-resolution grid and locate its stationary points there.

19 The joint domain: putting position back into the feature vector

Clustering pixels by colour alone throws away the one thing that makes an image an image. Two objects on opposite sides of the frame that happen to be the same colour become one cluster, and a cluster that is scattered across the picture is not a region.

The fix is not to post-process. It is to put position into the feature vector, so that spatial proximity is part of what "similar" means:

With colour the feature vector is five-dimensional: (x, y, L*, u*, v*). The perceptual colour space matters here — Euclidean distance in it is meant to approximate perceived difference, so one h_r is meaningful across the whole gamut, which it is not in raw RGB. That is Part 1's subject.

The relationship to the bilateral filter, stated precisely. The kernel above is the bilateral filter's kernel — a spatial term times a range term, exactly. The difference is what moves. The bilateral filter computes the same weighted average and writes it into the pixel's colour, leaving the pixel where it is; it is an edge-preserving smoother. Mean shift writes the weighted average back into both the colour and the position, so the sample migrates through the joint space toward its mode and can be clustered when it gets there. One is a filter, the other is a segmentation, and the only difference in the code is which coordinates you update. The filter belongs to Part 2.

  • M is not part of the model at all. It is the same post-hoc minimum-size pass as §11's and §13's, and it exists because mean shift, like every mode-finding method, produces a long tail of tiny regions along boundaries.

Every remaining method on this page starts by throwing away the grid and keeping a graph. One node per pixel — or, much more often, one node per superpixel — an edge between pairs that are close enough to compare, and a weight on each edge saying how much they belong together. Segmentation is then a question about cutting a graph, and graph theory has answers.

21 Normalised cuts: the right criterion, an NP-hard problem, and an honest relaxation

Cutting a graph into two pieces has an obvious cost — the total weight of the edges you severed. Minimising that cost is a classical, polynomial-time problem. It is also completely useless for segmentation, and understanding why produces the correct criterion in one step.

Why the minimum cut is the wrong objective

the cut between two groupscut(A, B) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ B} w_ij

The cheapest way to sever a graph is to lop off one node: the cut is then that node's degree, which for a boundary pixel is small. Minimum cut therefore returns a single pixel and the rest of the image, every time, and it does so optimally. The objective has no notion of a balanced partition, so it buys cheapness by making one side tiny.

The fix: normalise by how much each side is attached to everything

association: all the weight touching a groupassoc(A, V) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ V} w_ij = ∑_{i ∈ A} dᵢ assoc(A, A) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ A} w_ij the normalised cutNcut(A, B) = cut(A, B)/assoc(A, V) + cut(A, B)/assoc(B, V) and the normalised association, which is the same statement upside downNassoc(A, B) = assoc(A, A)/assoc(A, V) + assoc(B, B)/assoc(B, V) Ncut(A, B) + Nassoc(A, B) = 2 exactly, for every partition

The identity in the last line is worth checking rather than remembering: assoc(A,A) + cut(A,B) = assoc(A,V) by definition, so the two ratios for group A sum to 1, and likewise for B. So minimising the disassociation between the groups is exactly the same as maximising the association within them. The criterion does not have to be told to prefer balanced groups — cutting off a single node makes assoc(A,V) tiny, the first term goes to 1, and the total is close to its maximum instead of its minimum. Both failure modes are excluded by one normalisation.

The bad news, stated first. Minimising Ncut exactly over all partitions of a general graph is NP-complete. Everything below is a relaxation, not a solution, and the page will keep saying so.

Rewriting Ncut as a Rayleigh quotient

Let x be the ±1 indicator of the partition, d = W·1, D = diag(d) and L = D − W. Define

the two-valued vector the criterion turns out to be abouty = ((1 + x) − b(1 − x)) / 2 so yᵢ = 1 for i ∈ A and yᵢ = −b for i ∈ B with b = k/(1 − k), k = (∑_{i ∈ A} dᵢ)/(∑_i dᵢ) chosen so that yᵀd = 0 then, and this is an identity rather than an approximationNcut(A, B) = yᵀ(D − W)y / (yᵀDy)

That equality is exact for every partition. It was checked here by brute force: on the eight-node graph in the figure, over all 254 non-trivial bipartitions, the largest disagreement between Ncut(A,B) and the Rayleigh quotient of its own y is 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ — floating point, and nothing else.

So the discrete problem is:

the exact problem — hard because of the last lineminimise yᵀLy / yᵀDy subject to yᵀd = 0 and yᵢ ∈ {1, −b} ← this constraint is what makes it NP-complete

The relaxation

Drop the last line. Let y be any real vector. The minimum of a Rayleigh quotient over a subspace is an eigenvalue, so the relaxed problem is solved by the generalised eigenvector belonging to the second-smallest generalised eigenvalue:

the relaxed problem is a generalised eigenproblem(D − W)·y = λ·D·y which becomes an ordinary symmetric eigenproblem by the substitution z = D^{1/2}y(I − N)·z = λ·z, N = D^{−1/2}·W·D^{−1/2} the normalised affinity matrix and the answer is read off the second-smallest eigenvalueλ₁ = 0 always, with y = 1 — the useless solution "everything in one group" λ₂ and its eigenvector y₂ — the relaxed optimum, automatically satisfying y₂ᵀd = 0

Then round: split the nodes by the sign of y₂, or better, sort the nodes by y₂ and try all n−1 splits of that ordering, keeping the one with the best actual Ncut.

Exactly what is and is not guaranteed. Three separate claims, only two of which are true.
· True. λ₂ is a lower bound on the minimum discrete Ncut, because the relaxed feasible set contains the discrete one. On the figure's default graph λ₂ = 0.028708 and the true discrete minimum, found by brute force over all 254 bipartitions, is 0.029557. The gap is small here and is not small in general.
· True. The eigenvector is the exact solution of the relaxed problem, computed to machine precision by a standard eigensolver.
· False, and often stated. "Normalised cuts solves the normalised cut problem." It does not. The rounding step has no approximation guarantee — there is no theorem bounding the Ncut of the thresholded eigenvector by any constant times the optimum, and examples exist where it is far off. What the eigenvector supplies is a one-dimensional ordering of the nodes that is usually excellent, and the sweep over that ordering supplies a partition that is usually excellent. "Usually excellent" is the honest claim, and it is worth a great deal — but it is not optimality, and the figure lets you hunt for a setting where the sign cut and the brute-force optimum disagree.

More than two regions

  • Recursive bisection. Cut, then recurse on each half, stopping when the Ncut value exceeds a threshold. Cheap, and it can only produce partitions that are nested — a genuine limitation, as §02's ladder argument predicts.
  • Use k eigenvectors at once. Take the eigenvectors for λ₂ … λ_{k+1}, treat each node's coordinates in that k-dimensional space as a feature vector, and run k-means on them. This is spectral clustering proper, and it is what the general-purpose implementations do. It replaces the ill-posed "where do I threshold this eigenvector" with the ill-posed "what is k", which is §02 again.
  • Cost. The matrices are sparse and only the bottom few eigenvectors are needed, so a Lanczos-type solver is used rather than a dense decomposition. It is still the expensive part of the pipeline, and it is the reason normalised cuts is almost always run on superpixels (§25) rather than pixels.

22 Why that eigenvector — three ways to see it

"Take the second-smallest eigenvector" is the kind of instruction that gets memorised rather than understood. Here are three independent reasons it is the right one, each of which makes a different part of the behaviour obvious.

1 · Algebraically: the first one is trivial, so the second is the first useful one

Since every row of L = D − W sums to zero, L·1 = 0: the all-ones vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue zero, always, for every graph. In the generalised problem it is the same vector. It is a perfectly valid minimiser of yᵀLy/yᵀDy — it just says "put everything in one group", which achieves a cut of zero by not cutting.

The constraint yᵀd = 0 is precisely the statement "be D-orthogonal to that trivial solution". Under that constraint the minimum of the Rayleigh quotient is the next eigenvalue, by the standard variational characterisation of eigenvalues — see Eigendecomposition & the SVD for the Courant–Fischer statement and Quadratic Forms for the Rayleigh quotient itself. So λ₂ is not a choice; it is what "the best non-trivial answer" means.

2 · Analytically: it is the quietest way to disagree

Recall yᵀLy = ½∑_{ij} w_ij(yᵢ − yⱼ)². Minimising it wants y constant; the constraint forbids that. So the eigenvector is the least-varying non-constant function on the graph — it must change somewhere, and the cheapest place to change is across the weakest edges. The eigenvector's steepest transition is the cut. That is why thresholding it works at all.

3 · Physically: it is the fundamental mode of vibration

Read L as a stiffness matrix and D as a mass matrix, so Ly = λDy is the free-vibration equation of a network of masses joined by springs, with spring constants w_ij and masses dᵢ. Then:

  • λ₁ = 0 with y = 1 is the rigid-body mode: everything moves together, nothing stretches, no energy.
  • λ₂ is the fundamental mode — the lowest-frequency real vibration. Its eigenvector has exactly one nodal line, where the displacement passes through zero, and the parts on either side move in opposite directions. Two heavy sub-networks joined by weak springs will oscillate against each other about the weak springs, so the nodal line falls on the weak connection. That nodal line is the cut, and the sign of the eigenvector is which side you are on.
  • Higher eigenvectors are higher harmonics with more nodal lines, which is exactly why using k of them gives k groups.
One theorem worth knowing, because it is checkable. The number of eigenvalues of L equal to zero is exactly the number of connected components of the graph, and the corresponding eigenvectors are the indicators of those components. So λ₂ = 0 if and only if the graph is disconnected — which is why λ₂ is called the algebraic connectivity, and why a small λ₂ means "nearly disconnected", which is exactly what a good cut is looking for. 23 Graph cuts: the one place on this page where the answer is provably optimal

Everything so far has been a heuristic, a relaxation or a local minimum. This section is different, and the difference is worth stating up front: for a two-label problem with the right kind of smoothness term, the global minimum of the energy is found exactly, in polynomial time. The conditions on "the right kind" are narrow and precise, and the rest of the section is mostly about them.

The energy

a binary labelling energy, f_p ∈ {0, 1}E(f) = ∑_p D_p(f_p) + ∑_{(p,q) ∈ N} V_pq(f_p, f_q) the data term — how badly pixel p fits the model of the region it was assigned toD_p(1) = −log Pr( I_p | foreground ) D_p(0) = −log Pr( I_p | background ) simplest useful choice: D_p(k) = (I_p − μ_k)² richer: a colour histogram, or a Gaussian mixture the smoothness term — pay to place a boundary, and pay less where the image already has oneV_pq(a, b) = λ · exp( −(I_p − I_q)² / (2σ²) ) · [a ≠ b]

The contrast-sensitive weight is the important detail. A constant penalty λ·[a ≠ b] minimises boundary length, which shrinks objects and rounds off their corners for the same reason a snake shrinks (§14). Multiplying by exp(−Δ²/2σ²) makes the boundary cheap exactly where the image has a step, so the energy prefers to cut along edges it did not have to invent.

The construction

Build a directed graph with one node per pixel plus two terminals — a source S standing for label 1 and a sink T standing for label 0 — and these capacities:

terminal (t-link) capacitiescap(S → p) = D_p(0) cutting it means p ends up on the sink side, labelled 0 cap(p → T) = D_p(1) cutting it means p ends up on the source side, labelled 1 neighbour (n-link) capacities, both directionscap(p → q) = cap(q → p) = V_pq(0, 1) then, for any S–T cut, the capacity severed is exactly the energy of the corresponding labellingcapacity of the cut = E(f) + const

Every S–T cut corresponds to a labelling and vice versa, and their costs agree. So the minimum cut is the minimum-energy labelling, and the minimum cut is computable because of the theorem below.

The max-flow min-cut theorem. In any flow network, the value of a maximum flow from S to T equals the capacity of a minimum S–T cut. This is a genuine theorem, not a heuristic. It also hands you the cut for free: at maximum flow, the source side of the minimum cut is exactly the set of nodes still reachable from S along edges with residual capacity left. So one max-flow computation both produces the optimal labelling and certifies it, and the certificate is the flow itself — any labelling with a lower energy would be a cut with capacity below the flow value, which is impossible.

The condition, stated exactly

Not every energy can be built this way. A pairwise term is representable as a set of non-negative capacities if and only if it is submodular:

the submodularity condition for a binary pairwise termV_pq(0, 0) + V_pq(1, 1) ≤ V_pq(0, 1) + V_pq(1, 0) for the contrast-sensitive term above, with any λ ≥ 00 + 0 ≤ λ·w_pq + λ·w_pq which holds for every λ ≥ 0 and every w_pq ≥ 0 ✓

In words: agreeing must never cost more than disagreeing. Any smoothness term that prefers neighbours to be the same is submodular; a term that rewarded neighbours for differing would not be, and no non-negative capacity assignment can represent it. In practice this condition is satisfied by essentially every smoothness term anyone actually wants, which is why the restriction is easy to forget — but it is the hypothesis the whole guarantee rests on.

The overclaim to avoid. "Graph cuts finds the global optimum of segmentation energies." It finds the global optimum of a two-label energy with submodular pairwise terms. Change either condition and the guarantee is gone:
· More than two labels is NP-hard in general. The standard algorithms, α-expansion and α-swap, are iterated binary graph cuts and are approximations. α-expansion carries a factor-of-two bound on the energy, and only when the pairwise term is a metric; α-swap carries no bound at all.
· Non-submodular binary terms cannot be represented. Roof duality (QPBO) handles them by returning a partial labelling — provably optimal on the pixels it does label, and silent on the rest.
· The energy is still a model. A global optimum of the wrong energy is a wrong answer computed exactly. This is a real and common outcome, and it is the reason the data term — the region model — is where the engineering effort actually goes.
Nine pixels, one flow network — the flow saturates, the cut appears, and brute force agrees every one of the 512 labellings is evaluated beside it, so "global optimum" is checked rather than asserted
  • Four numbers that are always equal. The maximum flow, the capacity of the minimum cut, the energy of the labelling the cut induces, and the brute-force minimum over all 512 labellings. At the default they are all 5.692173. Move any slider and they stay equal — the panel would turn red if they ever did not, and it never does, because the theorem is a theorem.

That is worth seeing rather than assuming: λ is not a granularity dial that always works. Whether it bites depends entirely on the scale of the data term, and the two are in different units unless somebody normalises them, which is a large part of what tuning a segmentation energy actually consists of.
  • The region models decide this, not the smoothness. Drag the foreground mean down to 3: every pixel is now closer to the foreground model than to the background one, the optimum flips to all nine foreground, and there is no boundary at all.   • Set the smoothness to zero. Every pixel independently takes whichever label fits it better, and the energy becomes ∑_p min(D_p(0), D_p(1)) = 4.25. 24 Interactive segmentation: letting the user supply the missing question

§02 argued that no algorithm can know which partition is wanted. The most honest response to that argument is not a cleverer algorithm; it is an interface. A few strokes from a user carry exactly the information the pixels do not, and the methods built around that idea are the ones that actually get deployed.

Seeds into an energy

A user paints a few strokes marked object and a few marked background. Those pixels do two jobs at once, and the second is the important one:

  1. Hard constraints. Seeded pixels are tied to their terminal with infinite capacity, so no cut can ever separate them from it. The optimum is then the best labelling consistent with what the user said.
  2. Training data. The seeded pixels are a labelled sample of the two region models. Estimate a colour histogram or a Gaussian mixture from each, and the data term D_p in §23 stops being a guess.

Iterating the estimate: the bounding-box trick

The best-known refinement removes even the strokes. The user drags one rectangle; everything outside it is definitely background, everything inside is probably foreground. Then:

  1. Fit a background colour mixture to the pixels outside the box and a foreground mixture to those inside.
  2. Solve the binary graph cut with those models — a global optimum, by §23.
  3. Re-estimate both mixtures from the labelling just produced.
  4. Repeat until the labelling stops changing. Optionally accept a few corrective strokes and re-solve.

Each individual cut is globally optimal; the loop is not. It is coordinate descent alternating between the labelling and the model parameters — the same structure as expectation–maximisation and with the same guarantee, which is that the energy decreases monotonically and converges to a local minimum. Starting from a bounding box rather than from nothing is what makes that local minimum a good one, and the initialisation is doing more work than the optimiser.

The continuous relaxation, and what it buys

Replace the binary labels by real numbers in [0, 1] and the discontinuity penalty by a quadratic one. The energy becomes a quadratic form, its minimum is the solution of a sparse linear system, and thresholding that solution at 0.5 gives a segmentation.

the relaxed problem — a weighted Laplacian system with the seeds as boundary conditionsminimise ∑_{(p,q)} w_pq · (u_p − u_q)² subject to u = 1 on the object seeds, u = 0 on the background seeds ⇔ L_U · u_U = −B · u_seeds one sparse symmetric positive-definite solve

The solution has three readings that are all the same computation, and each is illuminating:

  • A random walk. u_p is the probability that a random walker starting at pixel p, stepping to neighbours with probability proportional to w, reaches an object seed before a background seed.
  • An electrical network. Treat each w_pq as a conductance, tie the object seeds to 1 volt and the background seeds to 0. Then u is the potential at every node. Boundaries are resistive, interiors are conductive, and the voltage drop happens across the boundary.
  • A diffusion. The solution is the steady state of heat spreading from the seeds through a medium whose conductivity is the affinity.

Compared with graph cuts, the relaxation gives a soft answer — useful directly for matting, where a pixel really is part object — and it extends to k labels by solving one system per label and taking the largest, which graph cuts cannot do exactly. It gives up the exactness: thresholding at 0.5 has no optimality guarantee for the original binary energy, and the quadratic penalty smooths boundaries that the discontinuity penalty would have kept sharp.

The other family: geodesic distance. Faster still, and worth knowing because it is a completely different trade. Define a length for a path through the image that is large where the image changes, and label each pixel by whichever seed set is geodesically nearer. One pass of a fast-marching or raster-scan distance transform per label, and it is effectively instantaneous. It is far more sensitive to seed placement than a cut is — the answer is decided by the single cheapest path, so one weak spot in a boundary loses the whole region — but at interactive rates that sensitivity is arguably a feature, because the user sees it immediately and adds a stroke.

25 Superpixels: deliberately over-segmenting, on purpose, as a first move

§02 concluded that if you cannot know the right level of granularity, the safe move is to produce something finer than any level you might want, and let a later stage group it. That is the whole idea. A superpixel is not meant to be an object; it is meant to be an atom that no object boundary passes through.

What superpixels are for

  • They collapse the problem size. The figure's image has 96 × 72 = 6 912 pixels and comes out as 54 superpixels at the default settings — a factor of 128. Normalised cuts on 6 912 nodes is a large sparse eigenproblem; on 60 nodes it is a dense 60 × 60 decomposition that finishes instantly. This is why every graph method in §20 to §23 is run on superpixels in practice.
  • They give you a region to compute statistics over. A single pixel has one value; a superpixel has a colour histogram, a texture descriptor, a shape and a size. Features that are meaningless per-pixel become usable, which is exactly what the affinities of §20 want.
  • They are conservative. A merging algorithm can undo a superpixel boundary that should not have been there. Nothing can undo a superpixel that straddled a real boundary — the error is permanent. So the design target is boundary recall: never miss a true boundary, and do not much care how many false ones you introduce.

SLIC, and the two changes that turn k-means into a superpixel algorithm

SLIC is k-means in the five-dimensional joint space of §19 — (L*, a*, b*, x, y) — with two modifications, and everything that page says about k-means applies unchanged apart from these.

  1. Initialise on a regular grid of spacing S = √(N/k), then nudge each centre to the lowest-gradient pixel in its 3 × 3 neighbourhood, so no centre begins its life sitting on an edge or on a noise spike.
  2. Restrict the search to a 2S × 2S window around each centre. This is the change that matters computationally: standard k-means compares every pixel to every centre, at cost O(NkI); SLIC compares each pixel to the handful of centres whose windows contain it, at cost O(NI) independent of k.
  3. Use a distance that puts the two halves on a common scale. Spatial distance is divided by S so it is in units of "grid cells", and the compactness constant m says how many units of colour one grid cell is worth.
  4. Enforce connectivity afterwards. Nothing in the k-means step guarantees a cluster is connected, so a final pass splits each label into its connected pieces and absorbs the small ones into a neighbour. This pass is not cosmetic — it changes the superpixel count, sometimes substantially.

26 Evaluating a segmentation, and why the benchmarks are contested

§02 said there is no single correct partition. That makes evaluation genuinely hard rather than merely fiddly, and it is worth being precise about which metric penalises which mistake — because every one of them is degenerate in some direction, and every one can be gamed.

The two region-overlap measures, and their exact relationship

given a predicted set P and a ground-truth set GIoU = Jaccard = J = |P ∩ G| / |P ∪ G| Dice = F₁ on pixels = D = 2|P ∩ G| / (|P| + |G|) they are two views of one number — write i = |P∩G|, and note |P∪G| = |P| + |G| − iD = 2i/(|P|+|G|) J = i/(|P|+|G|−i) ⇒ D = 2J/(1 + J) and J = D/(2 − D) consequences, all exactD ≥ J always, with equality only at J = 0 and J = 1 the map J ↦ D is strictly increasing, so for ONE pair of masks they always rank the same way

That relation was checked here on 20 000 random mask pairs; the largest deviation from D = 2J/(1+J) and from J = D/(2−D) was 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶. So if someone reports Dice and you want IoU, you can convert, and vice versa, with no loss.

And yet they disagree, in the way that matters. A monotone relation between two numbers does not survive averaging. Mean IoU and mean Dice over a set of images can rank two methods in opposite orders, and here is a concrete instance with ground truth of exactly 100 pixels on each of two images:

Why a region measure is not enough

Both measures count pixels, so they are dominated by region area and nearly blind to boundary position. Shift the boundary of a large blob by one pixel all the way round and the IoU barely moves; do the same to a thin structure and it collapses. For anything where the boundary is the answer — medical contours, matting, editing — the region measure is measuring the wrong thing.

boundary precision, recall and F-measure at a matching tolerance τprecision = |{ p ∈ ∂P : dist(p, ∂G) ≤ τ }| / |∂P| are the boundaries I drew real? recall = |{ g ∈ ∂G : dist(g, ∂P) ≤ τ }| / |∂G| did I find all the real boundaries? F = 2·precision·recall / (precision + recall)

The tolerance τ is not a detail: it is the only thing that makes the measure usable at all, since exact boundary agreement is unattainable, and it is also the easiest knob to tune a result with. A boundary F-measure without its tolerance quoted is not a number.

Comparing partitions when there is no foreground

Unsupervised segmentation produces a partition, not a foreground mask, so overlap measures do not directly apply. Three standard choices:

  • Rand index and its adjusted form. Count the pixel pairs the two partitions agree about — same-region in both, or different-region in both — over all pairs. The adjusted version rescales so that a random partition scores 0 and an identical one scores 1. This is the measure §02's figure uses, and it is the reason that figure shows numbers around 0.8 rather than around 0.98.
  • Variation of information. VI(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − 2I(X; Y), in nats: the information you lose about one partition given the other, plus the information you gain. It is a genuine metric — it satisfies the triangle inequality — which the Rand index is not, and it is much less prone to saturation.
  • Segmentation covering. For each ground-truth region, take the best-matching predicted region's IoU, and average weighted by region size. Intuitive, and it forgives over-segmentation far more than under-segmentation, which may or may not be what you want.
Why the benchmarks are contested, in four sentences. One: the ground truth is a set of mutually inconsistent human answers, so the ceiling is not 1 and nobody agrees on what it is (§02 measured inter-annotator agreement at 0.79 to 0.94 on a synthetic scene with only three plausible readings; on real photographs it is lower). Two: comparing a method that returns 8 regions against one that returns 80 requires a metric that is fair across granularities, and every such metric encodes an opinion about whether over-segmentation is worse than under-segmentation. Three: every metric above has a tolerance, a matching rule or a normalisation that changes rankings, and papers do not always quote them. Four: the metric that a method is tuned against is the metric it wins on, and §13's figure already showed a machine outscoring a human on a metric while getting the picture wrong. None of this makes benchmarks useless; it makes a single benchmark number uninformative, which is a different and more manageable problem.

  • Set the size ratio to 0.9 with the offset at 0: concentric discs whose boundaries are about 2.6 pixels apart. IoU 0.818, boundary F 0.942 — both mildly unhappy.   • Set the tolerance to 0. Both boundary numbers drop to nearly nothing even for an excellent prediction, because exact boundary coincidence essentially never happens on a discrete grid. That is why the tolerance exists, and why quoting an F-measure without it is meaningless.

What learned segmentation changed, and what it did not

Semantic and instance segmentation with neural networks — Convolutional Networks §10 — replaced most of this page for most applications. It is worth being precise about which parts.

what changed
The features, and the supervision
Every method here works on a hand-designed feature: intensity, a colour distance, a gradient magnitude, a filter-bank histogram. A network learns the feature from labelled examples. That is the whole difference, and it is enormous, because §20's warning — that a graph method cannot recover a boundary its affinity did not encode — is answered by learning the affinity.
what changed
The question being answered
A network trained on labelled data does not partition the image by similarity; it labels each pixel with a category. That dissolves §02's ill-posedness by supplying the missing question in the training labels — the granularity is whatever the annotators used. It does not solve the problem; it moves it into the dataset, where it belongs.
what survived
The energies, as post-processing
Fully-connected conditional random fields — §23's energy with a Gaussian pairwise term over all pairs — were for several years the standard refinement on top of a network's coarse output, sharpening boundaries the downsampling had blurred. The data term became the network's per-pixel scores; the smoothness term stayed exactly as written in §23.
what survived
The evaluation, and the ill-posedness
§26 is unchanged: mean IoU is the reported number for semantic segmentation, with every degeneracy this page listed. And §02's argument is untouched — annotators still disagree, granularity is still a choice, and the ceiling on any benchmark is still not 1.
what survived
Watershed, and superpixels
Marker-controlled watershed is still the standard way to split touching instances once a network has produced a mask and a set of centres — the network supplies the markers, §13 does the splitting. Superpixels persist wherever labelled data does not exist.
the honest caveat
Learned methods need labels
Every method on this page runs on one image with no training data. That is not a small thing when the domain is a new microscope, a new sensor, or a task nobody has annotated. The classical methods are what you use before there is a dataset, and what you use to build one.

Drills

  3. An object covers 5% of the frame and its intensity is 4σ above the background's. Predict, before checking §06's figure, whether Otsu's threshold lands near the Bayes-optimal one. Then explain the answer using the factor P₁P₂.
  4. An image is ink at 90 on paper at 210 under a shading field varying by a factor of 3 across the frame. Show that no global threshold can separate them, and state the exact condition in general.
  5. Draw the 11 × 11 diagonal diamond ring of §08 and count foreground and background components under all four combinations of 4- and 8-connectivity. You should get (16, 2), (16, 1), (1, 2), (1, 1). Say which two are self-consistent, and why.
  6. A region-growing front is at a boundary where the weakest crossing pixel differs from the region mean by δ. Explain why the fixed-reference and running-mean predicates leak at different tolerances, and which leaks first.
  7. Give a homogeneity predicate for which top-down splitting and bottom-up merging return different answers on the same image, and say which condition of §03 is doing the damage.
  8. Two regions have internal differences Int(R₁) = 4 and Int(R₂) = 60, sizes 400 and 900, and the weakest edge between them is 22. With k = 400, do they merge? Compute MInt and say which region's term is binding.
  9. A one-dimensional landscape has minima at heights 15, 25, 31, 36, 40 with saddles between consecutive pairs at 54.5, 53.9, 53.5 and 48.3. List the persistences and say how many basins survive a filter of h = 20.
  10. A snake of N points sits on a circle of radius r with no image force. Show that both internal energy terms are proportional to r², and deduce the two standard remedies.
  11. Write the mean-shift update for the Epanechnikov kernel in one sentence with no formula, and say why it terminates in finitely many steps while the Gaussian version does not.
  12. On a graph with degree vector d, verify that Ncut(A,B) + Nassoc(A,B) = 2 from the definitions in three lines.
  13. Explain why λ₂ of the generalised eigenproblem is a lower bound on the minimum normalised cut, and construct the two-sentence argument that no rounding of the eigenvector can be guaranteed to attain it.
  14. Check that V(a,b) = λ·w·[a ≠ b] is submodular for λ, w ≥ 0, and construct a pairwise term that is not. What does the min-cut construction do with yours?
