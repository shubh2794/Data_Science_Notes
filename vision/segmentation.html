Segmentation

An image is a rectangle of numbers with no seams in it. Segmentation is the act of drawing seams anyway — cutting the pixel grid into groups that "go together" — and the uncomfortable fact that organises this entire part is that there is no single correct answer to cut towards. Two people shown the same photograph will not draw the same regions, and neither will they be wrong. So every algorithm below is not a solver for a well-posed problem; it is a criterion — a statement of what "going together" is going to mean today — plus an optimiser for it. Read each method twice: once for the criterion, which is the honest content, and once for the optimiser, which is the engineering. When a segmentation looks bad, it is almost always the criterion that was wrong.

01 What segmentation is, and what this page does not cover

Every method on this page takes the same input — one array of pixels — and produces the same kind of output: a label per pixel. What separates them is entirely what they are willing to call similar, and how far apart in the image two similar pixels are allowed to be.

pixels→a similarity criterion→an objective→an optimiser→a label map→connected components→regions

The subject organises into four families, and the order below is roughly the order in which they were invented, which is also roughly the order of how much of the image each one looks at before deciding anything.

family 1 · §04–§08
Thresholding
Decide each pixel on its own value alone. The whole image contributes only through the histogram, and spatial arrangement enters afterwards, through connected components. Cheapest thing that works, and it works far more often than its reputation suggests.
family 2 · §09–§13
Region-based
Grow, split or merge spatially connected sets under a homogeneity predicate. Regions are connected by construction, so you never have to clean up afterwards — but the answer depends on the order you visited things in.
family 3 · §14–§19
Contour & density
Evolve a boundary downhill in an energy (snakes, level sets), or climb uphill to the modes of a density fitted to the pixels (mean shift). Both are continuous optimisation, and both land in local optima.
family 4 · §20–§25
Graph-based
Make the image a weighted graph and cut it. Two very different cuts appear: the normalised cut, which is NP-hard and gets relaxed into an eigenvector problem, and the minimum cut of a binary energy, which is solved exactly in polynomial time by max-flow.

· Edges, gradients and boundary detection are Part 4. Segmentation splits into a boundary view and a region view of the same problem; Part 4 owns the boundary view, this page owns the region view, and §12 is where they meet, because the watershed is run on a gradient image that Part 4 computes.

02 There is no correct segmentation, and saying so early saves a lot of grief

Almost every other problem in this series has a ground truth that exists whether or not anyone measured it. A 3D point really is somewhere; a homography really does or does not relate two images. A segmentation is not like that. The partition is not a property of the scene; it is a property of the question being asked about the scene.

Consider a photograph of a person in a striped shirt. How many regions is that? One — the person. Two — person and background. Three — head, shirt, trousers. Forty — every stripe. All four answers are defensible, and none of them is recoverable from the pixels, because the pixels are identical in all four cases. The information that decides between them lives outside the image: in what the segmentation is for.

This has three consequences that shape everything below.

  • Every algorithm has a granularity knob, and it is never optional. The number of clusters k, the mean-shift bandwidth h, the merge threshold τ, the number of recursive normalised cuts, the superpixel count — these look like nuisance hyperparameters and they are not. They are the place where you supply the missing question. An algorithm that claims not to have one has hidden it in a constant.
  • Human annotators disagree with each other, substantially. On the standard hand-labelled benchmarks, the different people who segmented the same photograph produce partitions that differ as much from one another as good algorithms differ from any one of them. This is not annotator sloppiness; it is the ill-posedness showing up as data.
  • Therefore a benchmark number is a comparison against one particular reading of the image. A score of 0.7 against annotator A and 0.5 against annotator B does not mean the algorithm got worse; it means B drew different seams. Any evaluation that averages over annotators is averaging over incompatible answers — §26 is where this gets uncomfortable.

The honest framing, then, is not "find the segmentation" but: the algorithm proposes a nested family of partitions, and something outside the algorithm picks a level. Most of the good methods below are best understood that way — a hierarchy, plus a cut through it.

One scene, a whole ladder of defensible partitions — and three people who each stopped at a different rung drag the granularity slider; no setting agrees with all three annotators, and none is wrong
  • The default has the right number of regions and the wrong regions. Annotator A drew five; the slider starts at five; the adjusted Rand index between them is 0.445. Matching the count is not remotely the same as matching the partition, and any method that is tuned by counting regions is being tuned on the wrong quantity.
  • Slide to the granularity that best matches annotator A, then switch to annotator B. At K = 10 the agreement with A is 0.941; the same partition scores 0.803 against B, whose own optimum is K = 8. There is no single setting that is simultaneously optimal against all three. The annotators do not agree with each other either — 0.789, 0.936 and 0.812 pairwise — and that residual is irreducible: it is what "the answer depends on the question" looks like as a number.
  • The machine can outscore a human, and that is a fact about the metric. At K = 8 the intensity merge agrees with annotator B at 0.858, which is higher than B's agreement with either of the other two people. Nobody should conclude that the algorithm reads the scene better than a person does. It means the metric rewards a particular kind of similarity, and that a machine tuned to that metric can win on it while still being wrong about what the picture contains. §26 returns to this.
  • The machine's ladder is nested; the annotators' answers are not on it. Agglomerative merging by intensity produces a strictly nested family of partitions, so at every rung each region is a union of finer regions. Annotator B groups the door with the tree trunk, because both are wood, while the wall that lies between them is masonry. {door, trunk} is never a region at any rung of the intensity ladder — those two atoms only ever end up together when everything does, at K = 1 — so no choice of granularity produces B's answer. A better criterion might; a better granularity cannot.
  • This is the argument for over-segmenting deliberately. If you cannot know the right level, produce a partition that is safely finer than any level you might want — one that does not merge across any boundary the reader might care about — and let a later stage group them. That idea has a name and it is superpixels, §25.

03 The formal statement, and exactly where it stops determining anything

There is a standard four-condition definition of a segmentation. It is worth writing down precisely, because seeing what it does not pin down is the fastest route to understanding why there are so many algorithms.

Let R be the whole image domain — the set of all pixel positions — and let P be a homogeneity predicate, a function that takes a set of pixels and returns true when that set is "uniform enough". A segmentation is a finite collection of subsets R₁, …, R_n satisfying four conditions.

the four conditions on a segmentation1. ⋃ᵢ Rᵢ = R complete — every pixel is labelled 2. Rᵢ ∩ Rⱼ = ∅ for i ≠ j disjoint — no pixel is labelled twice 3. P(Rᵢ) = true for every i each region is internally homogeneous 4. P(Rᵢ ∪ Rⱼ) = false for Rᵢ adjacent Rⱼ no two neighbours could be merged

Conditions 1 and 2 say the output is a partition. Condition 3 says the regions are good. Condition 4 — the one that is usually skipped over — says the segmentation is maximal: it forbids the trivial answer of one region per pixel, which satisfies 1, 2 and 3 for any sane P. Most implementations also require each Rᵢ to be connected, which is a fifth condition and is not implied by the other four.

The three things this definition leaves completely open

gap 1
P is not given
"Homogeneous enough" is exactly the modelling decision. Range below a threshold? Variance below a threshold? Every pixel within 2σ of the region mean? A unimodal histogram? Each is a different P, each satisfies the definition, and each gives a different answer on the same image.
gap 2
The solution is not unique
For almost any interesting P, many partitions satisfy all four conditions at once. The definition is a feasibility condition, not an optimisation problem — it has a solution set, not a solution. Which one you get is decided by the algorithm's visiting order, not by the definition.
gap 3
P is usually not hereditary
A predicate is hereditary if P(A) = true implies P(B) = true for every B ⊆ A. "Range ≤ τ" is hereditary; "the histogram is unimodal" is not, and neither is "variance ≤ τ" restricted to sub-regions of unequal size. Without heredity, splitting a good region can produce a bad one, and the recursion in §10 stops behaving.
Merging and splitting are not dual. Run the same predicate top-down and bottom-up and you get different answers. The cleanest counterexample is a chessboard. Take P = "the mean grey level of the region is within tolerance of every pixel's" — no: take P = "the region's mean lies in the middle band". Applied top-down, the whole chessboard has mean grey exactly 0.5, the predicate passes immediately, and the algorithm returns one region and stops. Applied bottom-up from single pixels, no black pixel will ever merge with a white one, and the algorithm returns the 64 squares. Same predicate, same image, opposite answers, and the reason is that condition 3 is checked at different scales. Any time a paper reports "region merging" or "region splitting" results, that choice is doing real work.

Three ways to turn a feasibility condition into an algorithm

  1. Fix a rule and a visiting order, and accept the answer it lands on. Region growing (§09), split-and-merge (§10) and the watershed (§12) do this. Fast, deterministic given the order, and the order matters.
  2. Define a global cost and minimise it. Replace conditions 3 and 4 with a number — a sum of a data term measuring how well pixels fit their region and a boundary term penalising the seams. Snakes (§14), level sets (§16), normalised cuts (§21) and graph cuts (§23) all do this. The criterion becomes explicit and arguable, which is progress; the optimisation becomes the hard part.
  3. Model the pixels as samples from a density and find its structure. Mean shift (§18) and mixture models do this. The criterion moves entirely into feature space, and spatial connectivity has to be put back by hand afterwards.
The trap. Reading condition 4 as "the algorithm cannot be improved". It says only that no two adjacent regions can be merged under this predicate. A segmentation can satisfy all four conditions and still be visibly, badly wrong — every region internally uniform, no mergeable pair, and the seams in all the wrong places. Satisfying the definition is necessary and nowhere near sufficient, which is precisely why §26 exists.

26 Evaluating a segmentation, and why the benchmarks are contested

§02 said there is no single correct partition. That makes evaluation genuinely hard rather than merely fiddly, and it is worth being precise about which metric penalises which mistake — because every one of them is degenerate in some direction, and every one can be gamed.

The two region-overlap measures, and their exact relationship

given a predicted set P and a ground-truth set GIoU = Jaccard = J = |P ∩ G| / |P ∪ G| Dice = F₁ on pixels = D = 2|P ∩ G| / (|P| + |G|) they are two views of one number — write i = |P∩G|, and note |P∪G| = |P| + |G| − iD = 2i/(|P|+|G|) J = i/(|P|+|G|−i) ⇒ D = 2J/(1 + J) and J = D/(2 − D) consequences, all exactD ≥ J always, with equality only at J = 0 and J = 1 the map J ↦ D is strictly increasing, so for ONE pair of masks they always rank the same way

That relation was checked here on 20 000 random mask pairs; the largest deviation from D = 2J/(1+J) and from J = D/(2−D) was 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶. So if someone reports Dice and you want IoU, you can convert, and vice versa, with no loss.

And yet they disagree, in the way that matters. A monotone relation between two numbers does not survive averaging. Mean IoU and mean Dice over a set of images can rank two methods in opposite orders, and here is a concrete instance with ground truth of exactly 100 pixels on each of two images:

IoU punishes the total failure on image 1 more harshly than Dice does, because D = 2J/(1+J) is concave and stretches the low end. Which is "correct" depends entirely on whether one catastrophic image should outweigh two mediocre ones, and that is a decision about the application, not about the metric.

Why a region measure is not enough

Both measures count pixels, so they are dominated by region area and nearly blind to boundary position. Shift the boundary of a large blob by one pixel all the way round and the IoU barely moves; do the same to a thin structure and it collapses. For anything where the boundary is the answer — medical contours, matting, editing — the region measure is measuring the wrong thing.

boundary precision, recall and F-measure at a matching tolerance τprecision = |{ p ∈ ∂P : dist(p, ∂G) ≤ τ }| / |∂P| are the boundaries I drew real? recall = |{ g ∈ ∂G : dist(g, ∂P) ≤ τ }| / |∂G| did I find all the real boundaries? F = 2·precision·recall / (precision + recall)

The tolerance τ is not a detail: it is the only thing that makes the measure usable at all, since exact boundary agreement is unattainable, and it is also the easiest knob to tune a result with. A boundary F-measure without its tolerance quoted is not a number.

Comparing partitions when there is no foreground

Unsupervised segmentation produces a partition, not a foreground mask, so overlap measures do not directly apply. Three standard choices:

  • Rand index and its adjusted form. Count the pixel pairs the two partitions agree about — same-region in both, or different-region in both — over all pairs. The adjusted version rescales so that a random partition scores 0 and an identical one scores 1. This is the measure §02's figure uses, and it is the reason that figure shows numbers around 0.8 rather than around 0.98.
  • Variation of information. VI(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − 2I(X; Y), in nats: the information you lose about one partition given the other, plus the information you gain. It is a genuine metric — it satisfies the triangle inequality — which the Rand index is not, and it is much less prone to saturation.
  • Segmentation covering. For each ground-truth region, take the best-matching predicted region's IoU, and average weighted by region size. Intuitive, and it forgives over-segmentation far more than under-segmentation, which may or may not be what you want.
Why the benchmarks are contested, in four sentences. One: the ground truth is a set of mutually inconsistent human answers, so the ceiling is not 1 and nobody agrees on what it is (§02 measured inter-annotator agreement at 0.79 to 0.94 on a synthetic scene with only three plausible readings; on real photographs it is lower). Two: comparing a method that returns 8 regions against one that returns 80 requires a metric that is fair across granularities, and every such metric encodes an opinion about whether over-segmentation is worse than under-segmentation. Three: every metric above has a tolerance, a matching rule or a normalisation that changes rankings, and papers do not always quote them. Four: the metric that a method is tuned against is the metric it wins on, and §13's figure already showed a machine outscoring a human on a metric while getting the picture wrong. None of this makes benchmarks useless; it makes a single benchmark number uninformative, which is a different and more manageable problem.
Two masks, four metrics, and the exact curve that relates two of them drag the prediction off the truth and watch which metric complains first
  • Drag the offset from 0 upward. Dice stays above IoU the whole way, and the gap is an inverted U: 0.080 at an offset of 4, a maximum of 0.167 at 14, and back to 0.126 at 30. At the default, IoU reads 0.494 and Dice reads 0.662 on the same two masks. A paper reporting Dice and a paper reporting IoU on the same result differ by up to seventeen points and both are correct.
  • Switch to the thin ring and set the offset to 2. IoU falls from 1.000 to 0.439 and Dice to 0.610, while the boundary F stays at exactly 1.000 — the whole shift is inside the tolerance. On the disc, the same two-pixel shift only takes IoU to 0.908. On thin structures a region measure is a boundary measure with an unreasonably harsh tolerance, and it is usually not the measure anybody meant.
  • Set the size ratio to 0.9 with the offset at 0: concentric discs whose boundaries are about 2.6 pixels apart. IoU 0.818, boundary F 0.942 — both mildly unhappy. Now set the ratio to 1.2, where the boundaries are five pixels apart. IoU is still a respectable 0.689, and the boundary F is exactly 0.000, because not one boundary pixel of either shape is within the tolerance of the other. The two families do not degrade together; they degrade on completely different schedules, and reporting one of them conceals the other's complaint entirely.
  • Set the tolerance to 0. Both boundary numbers drop to nearly nothing even for an excellent prediction, because exact boundary coincidence essentially never happens on a discrete grid. That is why the tolerance exists, and why quoting an F-measure without it is meaningless.

What learned segmentation changed, and what it did not

Semantic and instance segmentation with neural networks — Convolutional Networks §10 — replaced most of this page for most applications. It is worth being precise about which parts.

what changed
The features, and the supervision
Every method here works on a hand-designed feature: intensity, a colour distance, a gradient magnitude, a filter-bank histogram. A network learns the feature from labelled examples. That is the whole difference, and it is enormous, because §20's warning — that a graph method cannot recover a boundary its affinity did not encode — is answered by learning the affinity.
what changed
The question being answered
A network trained on labelled data does not partition the image by similarity; it labels each pixel with a category. That dissolves §02's ill-posedness by supplying the missing question in the training labels — the granularity is whatever the annotators used. It does not solve the problem; it moves it into the dataset, where it belongs.
what survived
The energies, as post-processing
Fully-connected conditional random fields — §23's energy with a Gaussian pairwise term over all pairs — were for several years the standard refinement on top of a network's coarse output, sharpening boundaries the downsampling had blurred. The data term became the network's per-pixel scores; the smoothness term stayed exactly as written in §23.
what survived
The evaluation, and the ill-posedness
§26 is unchanged: mean IoU is the reported number for semantic segmentation, with every degeneracy this page listed. And §02's argument is untouched — annotators still disagree, granularity is still a choice, and the ceiling on any benchmark is still not 1.
what survived
Watershed, and superpixels
Marker-controlled watershed is still the standard way to split touching instances once a network has produced a mask and a set of centres — the network supplies the markers, §13 does the splitting. Superpixels persist wherever labelled data does not exist.
the honest caveat
Learned methods need labels
Every method on this page runs on one image with no training data. That is not a small thing when the domain is a new microscope, a new sensor, or a task nobody has annotated. The classical methods are what you use before there is a dataset, and what you use to build one.

28 Practice

The drills below are all checkable with the figures on this page or with ten lines of code. The derivations underneath them are the ones worth being able to reproduce on a blank page, because every one of them is a place where the standard summary of a method is subtly wrong.

Drills

  1. Take the sixteen-level histogram of §05, n = (2, 5, 12, 20, 14, 6, 3, 2, 2, 4, 9, 18, 22, 13, 5, 3). Compute μ_G and σ_T² by hand, then σ_B²(7) from P₁P₂(μ₁−μ₂)², and check that σ_W²(7) = σ_T² − σ_B²(7). You should get 7.821429, 19.989541, 17.718788 and 2.270753.
  2. Prove that the histogram of two equal-weight, equal-variance Gaussians is bimodal exactly when their means are more than 2σ apart. Then explain, without computing anything, why an unbalanced mixture needs a larger separation.
  3. An object covers 5% of the frame and its intensity is 4σ above the background's. Predict, before checking §06's figure, whether Otsu's threshold lands near the Bayes-optimal one. Then explain the answer using the factor P₁P₂.
  4. An image is ink at 90 on paper at 210 under a shading field varying by a factor of 3 across the frame. Show that no global threshold can separate them, and state the exact condition in general.
  5. Draw the 11 × 11 diagonal diamond ring of §08 and count foreground and background components under all four combinations of 4- and 8-connectivity. You should get (16, 2), (16, 1), (1, 2), (1, 1). Say which two are self-consistent, and why.
  6. A region-growing front is at a boundary where the weakest crossing pixel differs from the region mean by δ. Explain why the fixed-reference and running-mean predicates leak at different tolerances, and which leaks first.
  7. Give a homogeneity predicate for which top-down splitting and bottom-up merging return different answers on the same image, and say which condition of §03 is doing the damage.
  8. Two regions have internal differences Int(R₁) = 4 and Int(R₂) = 60, sizes 400 and 900, and the weakest edge between them is 22. With k = 400, do they merge? Compute MInt and say which region's term is binding.
  9. A one-dimensional landscape has minima at heights 15, 25, 31, 36, 40 with saddles between consecutive pairs at 54.5, 53.9, 53.5 and 48.3. List the persistences and say how many basins survive a filter of h = 20.
  10. A snake of N points sits on a circle of radius r with no image force. Show that both internal energy terms are proportional to r², and deduce the two standard remedies.
  11. Write the mean-shift update for the Epanechnikov kernel in one sentence with no formula, and say why it terminates in finitely many steps while the Gaussian version does not.
  12. On a graph with degree vector d, verify that Ncut(A,B) + Nassoc(A,B) = 2 from the definitions in three lines.
  13. Explain why λ₂ of the generalised eigenproblem is a lower bound on the minimum normalised cut, and construct the two-sentence argument that no rounding of the eigenvector can be guaranteed to attain it.
  14. Check that V(a,b) = λ·w·[a ≠ b] is submodular for λ, w ≥ 0, and construct a pairwise term that is not. What does the min-cut construction do with yours?
  15. A prediction has IoU 0.6. What is its Dice? A different prediction has Dice 0.6 — what is its IoU? Then construct, from scratch, a two-image example where mean IoU and mean Dice disagree about which of two methods is better.

Things to be able to derive from a blank page

Where this goes next. Part 6, Alignment & Motion adds a second frame, which supplies the one cue this page never had: common fate. Pixels that move together belong together, and a motion field turns the affinity of §20 into something far more discriminative than colour ever was — at the cost of having to estimate the motion first. Part 7, Stereo & Structure from Motion adds a second viewpoint and therefore depth, which segments by the one property that actually corresponds to objecthood: two surfaces at different distances are different things, whatever colour they are. Almost every failure on this page — the two bright bars of §19, the touching cells of §13, the texture of §10 — is trivially resolved by a depth map, which is a fair summary of why single-image segmentation is as hard as it is.
