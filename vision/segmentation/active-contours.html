14 Active contours: a curve that falls downhill into an edge

Everything so far has grouped pixels. A snake does the opposite: it represents the answer as a curve and moves the curve until it is happy. That change of representation buys sub-pixel boundaries and built-in smoothness, and costs you topology, initialisation and any hope of a global optimum.

The energy

Let the contour be f(s) = (x(s), y(s)), parameterised by arc length s. Its energy is a sum of an internal term that depends only on the curve's shape and an external term that depends only on the image.

internal energy — the curve's own opinion about its shapeE_int = ∫ [ α(s)·‖f_s(s)‖² + β(s)·‖f_ss(s)‖² ] ds the first-derivative term is elasticity: it resists stretching the second-derivative term is rigidity: it resists bending discretised on N samples with spacing h, which is what an implementation minimisesE_int = ∑ᵢ α(i)·‖f(i+1) − f(i)‖² / h² + ∑ᵢ β(i)·‖f(i+1) − 2f(i) + f(i−1)‖² / h⁴ external energy — the image's opinion, usually just the edge termE_edge = ∑ᵢ −‖∇I(f(i))‖² or E_edge = ∑ᵢ −|(G_σ * ∇²I)(f(i))|² optional user constraintsE_spring = ∑ᵢ kᵢ·‖f(i) − d(i)‖² attract point i to an anchor d(i) E_balloon = −p · (area enclosed) a constant outward pressure along the normal

The α term makes the curve behave like a rubber band and the β term like a thin metal strip. Setting β = 0 at one control point lets a corner form there, which is how a snake is allowed to have corners at all; leaving β > 0 everywhere guarantees a smooth curve and guarantees it will round off every genuine corner in the object.

Why a snake shrinks, derived

This is worth doing because it is the source of the single most common complaint about snakes, and because the answer is three lines.

Take a closed snake of N equally spaced points on a circle of radius r, with no image force at all. Consecutive points are 2πr/N apart, so:

the elasticity term on a circle of radius r∑ᵢ ‖f(i+1) − f(i)‖² = N · (2πr/N)² = 4π²r² / N ∝ r² the rigidity term on the same circle — second difference ≈ curvature × spacing²‖f(i+1) − 2f(i) + f(i−1)‖ ≈ (1/r)·(2πr/N)² = 4π²r / N² ∑ᵢ ‖·‖² = N · (4π²r/N²)² = 16π⁴r² / N³ ∝ r²

Both internal terms are proportional to r², so both are minimised at r = 0. A snake with no image force collapses to a point, and a snake whose image force is weak collapses past the boundary it was supposed to find. There are exactly two remedies: initialise outside the object so the shrinkage is doing useful work, or add the balloon term with the pressure pointing outward, which is a constant force with no image justification whatsoever and has to be turned off once the curve arrives.

Minimising it

Taking the variation of E_int gives a fourth-order term, which discretises into a pentadiagonal circulant matrix A = αD₂ + βD₄ — the second- and fourth-difference operators. The standard update is semi-implicit: treat the internal energy implicitly, which is unconditionally stable, and the image force explicitly, which is cheap.

one semi-implicit step, γ the step size(I + γA)·f^(t+1) = f^(t) + γ·F_ext(f^(t)) ⇒ f^(t+1) = (I + γA)⁻¹ ( f^(t) + γ·F_ext(f^(t)) ) and the image force is the downhill direction of the external energyF_ext = −∇E_ext = w_edge · ∇‖∇(G_σ * I)‖²

(I + γA) is banded and circulant, so it is factored once and reused at every step, and a step costs O(N). The whole evolution of a hundred-point snake for a hundred iterations is microseconds; the expensive part was never the optimisation.

  • Set the edge force to zero and run. The contour shrinks monotonically, regardless of where it started and regardless of α and β: from an initial 5542 px² at radius 42 it is down to 204 px² after 300 iterations and still falling, and from radius 10 it reaches 11 px². There is no equilibrium to find — both internal terms go as r², so the only stationary point is the origin. That is the derivation above, animated, and it is why "the snake vanished" is the most common first result.
  • Set the start radius to 10 — well inside the object — with no balloon. The snake sits in the flat interior where the external energy is constant, feels no image force at all, and shrinks to an area of 11 px². Now nudge the balloon pressure to 0.15: it inflates until the image force at the boundary balances the pressure and lands at IoU 0.960. Push the pressure to 0.5 and it bursts straight through the boundary to 9393 px². The balloon is not an image term; it is an admission that the image term has no reach, and it has to be turned off by hand once the curve has arrived.
  • Push the rigidity up and watch the notch. The deepest point of the object's notch is 21.09 pixels from the centre. At β = 0 the snake gets to 23.80 — already 2.7 pixels short, because the elasticity term alone resists the detour. At β = 0.6 it gets only to 24.95, nearly four pixels short, and the region score falls from 0.974 to 0.967. Stiffness does not merely smooth the answer; it decides which answers are reachable, and the cost is paid exactly at the concavities, which are usually the informative part of a shape.
  • Look at the middle panel. The external energy is a narrow trench along the boundary and flat everywhere else. Outside that trench the gradient of the external energy is zero, so the snake is not being attracted from a distance — it is only ever attracted once it is already close. That is the capture range problem, and it is §15.

15 The four things a parametric snake cannot do

Snakes are worth understanding not because they are the state of the art — they have not been for decades — but because their four failure modes are the four problems every later contour method was built to solve, and each solution is named after the failure it fixes.

FailureWhy it happensWhat was built to fix it
Local minimaThe energy is not convex and the update is gradient descent, so the answer is whatever basin the initialisation was in. Two initialisations five pixels apart can converge to different boundaries.Dynamic programming over a discretised band around the current curve gives the global optimum within that band in O(N·m²) for m candidate positions per point — genuinely global, but only over the band. Simulated annealing and multi-start are the alternatives, and both are slow.
Capture rangeE_edge = −‖∇I‖² is essentially zero everywhere except within a couple of pixels of an edge, so its gradient is zero too. A snake more than a few pixels away feels nothing.Blur the edge map harder — which moves the edges. Or use the distance transform to the detected edges as the potential, which has a non-zero gradient everywhere. Or gradient vector flow: diffuse the edge gradient field outward into the homogeneous regions by solving a vector diffusion equation, so a far-away snake still gets a direction to move in.
Fixed topologyA closed polyline of N points is one curve. It cannot split into two, merge two into one, or grow a hole, because none of those is expressible as moving its points.Level sets (§16), which represent the curve implicitly and get topology changes for nothing. Explicit surgery on the polyline also exists and is fiddly.
ReparameterisationNothing keeps the control points evenly spaced. They bunch up in concave parts and spread out in convex ones, so the discrete energy stops approximating the continuous one and can even self-intersect.Resample by arc length after every few steps. Or use a B-spline snake, f(s) = ∑ₖ Bₖ(s)·xₖ, whose small number of control points removes both this problem and much of the local-minimum problem — at the cost of not being able to represent fine detail.

Shape priors: the most effective fix, and the most restrictive

If the object being found is always roughly the same shape — a hand, a face profile, a vertebra, a resistor — the strongest constraint available is not smoothness but a model of what shapes are possible. Stack the control points of many training outlines into one long vector, take the mean and covariance, and diagonalise:

a point distribution modelx = (x₁, y₁, x₂, y₂, …, x_K, y_K)ᵀ one training shape, as a 2K-vector C = (1/P) ∑_p (x_p − x̄)(x_p − x̄)ᵀ the shape covariance over P examples C = Φ · diag(λ₀ … λ_{K−1}) · Φᵀ eigendecomposition keep the first m eigenvectors and parameterise shape by m numbers instead of 2Kx = x̄ + Φ̂·b, Φ̂ = the first m columns of Φ the prior energy, and the usual hard box on top of itE_shape = ½ bᵀ·diag(1/λ₀ … 1/λ_{m−1})·b = ∑_m b_m²/(2λ_m) and |b_m| ≤ 3√λ_m

This is principal component analysis applied to outlines, and the fitting loop is the active shape model: at each control point search along the contour normal for the strongest edge, propose the displaced points, and then project that proposal back onto the shape subspace by solving for b and clipping it. The projection is what makes it robust: a few control points that latched onto the wrong edge are outvoted by the model. The price is total: the model cannot represent a shape it has never seen, and a genuinely unusual instance is silently pulled back to something ordinary.

The interactive cousin. If a user is present, the global-optimum problem becomes easy. Precompute a cost for every edge between neighbouring pixels — low where a boundary is likely, from gradient magnitude, orientation agreement and zero crossings — and then, as the mouse moves, run Dijkstra's algorithm from the last anchor point to the cursor and draw the minimum-cost path. It snaps to the boundary in real time, it is globally optimal on that graph rather than locally optimal, and it costs one shortest-path computation per mouse move. The two refinements that make it usable are freezing the path behind the cursor after a pause, so it stops jumping, and learning the intensity profile under the accepted segment so the wire prefers to continue along a similar-looking boundary rather than defecting to a stronger nearby one. The graph machinery is the graph-algorithm notes'; the cost function is Part 4's.

16 Level sets: stop moving the curve, move a surface underneath it

The trick is to stop representing the contour at all. Keep instead a scalar function φ(x, y) over the whole image, and define the contour to be wherever it crosses zero. Moving the contour is then a matter of changing φ, and the contour can split, merge, vanish and appear without anything special happening, because none of those events is a change to the representation.

the implicit representationC(t) = { (x, y) : φ(x, y, t) = 0 } φ < 0 inside, φ > 0 outside (or the reverse; pick one and keep it) the usual choice for φ: the signed distance to the contour, for which‖∇φ‖ = 1 everywhere the outward normal is n̂ = ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ the curvature is κ = div( ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ ) moving the contour with normal speed F becomes a PDE on φ∂φ/∂t = F · ‖∇φ‖

That last line is the whole method. Any evolution you can express as "move every point of the contour along its normal at speed F" becomes a partial differential equation on a fixed grid, and grids do not care about topology.

The edge-driven version: geodesic active contours

g is an edge-stopping function — near 1 in flat areas, near 0 on an edgeg(I) = 1 / (1 + ‖∇(G_σ * I)‖²) the evolution, in the two equivalent forms∂φ/∂t = ‖∇φ‖ · div( g(I) · ∇φ/‖∇φ‖ ) = g(I)·‖∇φ‖·κ + ∇g(I) · ∇φ the first term smooths — curvature flow, switched off on an edge because g is small there the second term attracts — it slides the contour down the gradient of g, onto the edge

Read the second form. The first term moves the contour along its curvature — it shortens and straightens the curve — but it is multiplied by g, so it switches itself off on an edge and leaves the curve where it is. The second term slides the contour down the gradient of g, that is, toward the places where g is small, that is, onto the edges. It is the level-set analogue of the snake's E_edge and it inherits the snake's capture-range problem exactly: ∇g is zero wherever the image is flat.

The region-driven version, which does not need edges at all

The important step is to stop using the gradient. Instead of asking the contour to sit on an edge, ask it to make the statistics inside and outside as different as possible:

the two-phase piecewise-constant energyE(C, c₁, c₂) = λ₁·∫_inside (I − c₁)² dx + λ₂·∫_outside (I − c₂)² dx + μ·Length(C) for a fixed contour the optimal constants are just the two means, in closed formc₁ = mean of I inside C c₂ = mean of I outside C and the level-set gradient flow, with a smoothed delta so the whole grid can move∂φ/∂t = δ_ε(φ) · [ μ·κ − λ₁(I − c₁)² + λ₂(I − c₂)² ] δ_ε(φ) = (1/π) · ε / (ε² + φ²)

This is the two-phase case of a much older piecewise-smooth segmentation model, and it has three properties the edge version lacks. It has global reach — every pixel contributes to c₁ and c₂, so the contour is driven from anywhere in the image, not only from within a few pixels of an edge. It works on boundaries that have no gradient at all, such as a slow ramp between two regions with different means. And it is far less sensitive to noise, because it integrates over regions rather than differentiating.

What it costs

  • You now solve a PDE on the whole grid. A snake moved 72 points; a level set updates every pixel of φ at every step. The standard fix is the narrow band: only update φ within a few pixels of the zero set, and rebuild the band when the contour leaves it. Cost falls from O(N) per step to O(√N)-ish, at the price of real bookkeeping.
  • φ does not stay a distance function. The evolution destroys ‖∇φ‖ = 1, and once φ becomes very flat or very steep near the zero set the numerics degrade. The remedy is periodic reinitialisation — recompute the signed distance to the current zero set, typically with a fast marching method — which is an extra step with no physical meaning that nonetheless has to be tuned.
  • The topology freedom is not always wanted. If you know there is exactly one object, a method that will happily return three is a liability, and constraining a level set to stay connected is awkward. This is a real reason parametric contours survive.
  • It is still a local minimum. The energy is non-convex in φ and the flow is gradient descent. Region-based versions are much less sensitive to initialisation than edge-based ones, but "much less sensitive" is not "insensitive".
One contour, two objects — the split happens because nothing in the representation forbids it watch the component count in the right-hand panel step from 1 to 2 without any special case being triggered
  • Step the iteration slider through iteration 54. That is where the component count steps from one to two at the default settings — and nothing else happens there at all. The energy curve does not kink, the surface does not jump, and no branch of the update was entered. The zero set of a smooth function simply stopped being connected. That non-event is the entire argument for the implicit representation.
  • Start from a grid of small circles. 31 separate contours at iteration zero; 2 within a handful of iterations, at IoU 0.996 — essentially the same answer the single big circle reaches, from an initialisation that shares no shape with it. They grow, merge where they are inside the same disc, and die in the background. Merging costs the representation exactly as little as splitting, and this initialisation is the standard defence against a bad local minimum: seed everywhere and let the energy sort it out.
  • Start from a circle inside the left object only. The contour grows out to fill the left disc, scores exactly 0.500 against the pair — half the truth, found perfectly — and stops there for ever. It never crosses the gap. This is the distinction worth being precise about: the region energy is globally informed — c₁ and c₂ are averages over every pixel in the image — but the motion is local, because the smoothed delta δ_ε(φ) only lets a band a few pixels wide move at each step. A global criterion does not give you a global flow.
  • Raise the length penalty. The region score falls from 0.999 at μ = 1 to 0.953 at μ = 5, the split arrives at iteration 6 instead of 54, and the early evolution becomes visibly unstable: the component count flickers as high as 6 over the first sixty iterations before settling at two. μ trades boundary fidelity against boundary length, and the curvature term it multiplies is also the stiffest, least stable part of the numerical scheme — which is why practical implementations keep it small and lean on the data term.
