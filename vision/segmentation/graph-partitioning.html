20 The image as a weighted graph

Every remaining method on this page starts by throwing away the grid and keeping a graph. One node per pixel — or, much more often, one node per superpixel — an edge between pairs that are close enough to compare, and a weight on each edge saying how much they belong together. Segmentation is then a question about cutting a graph, and graph theory has answers.

the affinity, in the form the normalised-cuts literature usesw_ij = exp( −‖Fᵢ − Fⱼ‖² / σ_F² − ‖xᵢ − xⱼ‖² / σ_s² ) if ‖xᵢ − xⱼ‖ < r, and 0 otherwise F = a feature vector: intensity, colour, or a vector of oriented filter responses x = pixel position r = the connection radius, in pixels

Three details in that line carry all the weight.

  • It is the negative exponential of the joint-domain distance of §19. Same two bandwidths, same product structure, same trade-off between spatial and feature proximity — written as a similarity in [0, 1] rather than as a kernel. If §19's h_s and h_r made sense to you, σ_s and σ_F are the same two numbers.
  • The radius r makes W sparse, and sparsity is the entire reason this is computable. A dense affinity matrix for a one-megapixel image has 10¹² entries. With r = 5 each node has about eighty neighbours, so W has 8 × 10⁷ non-zeros — large but ordinary, and every algorithm below works on sparse matrices.
  • The exponential is not innocent. Because w decays as a Gaussian in the feature difference, σ_F sets a hard scale: differences much larger than σ_F are all equally "zero", and differences much smaller than it are all equally "one". Set σ_F too small and the graph disconnects into pixels; too large and it becomes a uniform blob with no structure. The interesting range is narrow and it is image-dependent.

Better weights than a colour difference

The affinity is where all the domain knowledge goes, and improving it usually beats improving the algorithm.

texture
Filter-bank histograms
Replace F by a histogram of oriented filter responses over a small window and use a histogram distance. Two patches of the same fabric then have high affinity even though their pixels differ wildly — which is the fix for §10's checkerboard.
intervening contour
Look at what is between
Set w_ij = 1 − max_{x on the segment ij} p_boundary(x): two pixels are dissimilar if a strong boundary lies between them, however similar they are themselves. This is the single most effective affinity improvement in the literature, and it needs a boundary detector — Part 4's.
motion
Common fate
Two pixels that move together over a video belong together. The affinity becomes a distance between motion vectors — Part 6's.
the honest caveat
Everything downstream inherits the affinity
A spectral method cannot recover a boundary the affinity did not encode. When a graph-based segmentation looks wrong, the graph is nearly always the reason and the eigenvector nearly never is.

The three matrices everything is written in

from the weights, two derived matrices and one combinationW = [w_ij] the affinity matrix, symmetric, non-negative, zero diagonal by convention d = W·1, D = diag(d) the degree matrix; dᵢ is how strongly node i is attached to everything L = D − W the graph Laplacian and the identity that makes the Laplacian useful — for any vector yyᵀLy = ½ ∑_{i,j} w_ij (yᵢ − yⱼ)² ≥ 0

That last identity is the reason the Laplacian appears in every spectral method. It says yᵀLy is a weighted measure of how much y disagrees across edges: it is small when y is nearly constant on strongly connected groups and large when y changes across heavy edges. Minimising it, subject to not being constant, is asking for a labelling that respects the graph — which is exactly what §21 does.

A small image, the graph it becomes, and the affinity matrix that graph is turn σ_F down until the matrix goes black outside the diagonal blocks — that is the graph falling apart
  • The block structure is the segmentation, before any algorithm has run. With the rows ordered by region the matrix is three bright diagonal blocks and almost nothing off them. Every method in §21 to §23 is a way of finding that ordering without being told it.
  • Turn off the ordering. The same matrix, in raster order, looks like a banded mess with no visible structure — and it is the identical matrix. Spectral clustering's real job is to recover the permutation, and the second eigenvector of §21 is a continuous relaxation of exactly that.
  • Watch the degrees on a boundary. A pixel in the interior of a region has a high degree; one sitting on a boundary has a low degree, because half its neighbours disagree with it. Normalising by degree — which is what "normalised" means in normalised cuts — is what stops the algorithm from simply lopping off the low-degree pixels.
  • Push the feature bandwidth to 60. The within-to-across ratio collapses from 35.6 to 2.5. At that scale a step of 45 grey levels is a small difference, the block structure fades out of the matrix, and no spectral method downstream can recover a boundary the affinity has stopped encoding.
  • Push the radius to 5 and the spatial bandwidth to 6 instead. The matrix fills from 23.4% dense to 65.3% and the ratio actually improves slightly, to 50.2 — but every eigenvector computation in §21 now costs proportionally more, and on a real image with a boundary that is not a clean straight line the long-range edges do start to carry affinity across it. Bigger neighbourhoods buy robustness and cost time; they are not free either way.

21 Normalised cuts: the right criterion, an NP-hard problem, and an honest relaxation

Cutting a graph into two pieces has an obvious cost — the total weight of the edges you severed. Minimising that cost is a classical, polynomial-time problem. It is also completely useless for segmentation, and understanding why produces the correct criterion in one step.

Why the minimum cut is the wrong objective

the cut between two groupscut(A, B) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ B} w_ij

The cheapest way to sever a graph is to lop off one node: the cut is then that node's degree, which for a boundary pixel is small. Minimum cut therefore returns a single pixel and the rest of the image, every time, and it does so optimally. The objective has no notion of a balanced partition, so it buys cheapness by making one side tiny.

The fix: normalise by how much each side is attached to everything

association: all the weight touching a groupassoc(A, V) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ V} w_ij = ∑_{i ∈ A} dᵢ assoc(A, A) = ∑_{i ∈ A, j ∈ A} w_ij the normalised cutNcut(A, B) = cut(A, B)/assoc(A, V) + cut(A, B)/assoc(B, V) and the normalised association, which is the same statement upside downNassoc(A, B) = assoc(A, A)/assoc(A, V) + assoc(B, B)/assoc(B, V) Ncut(A, B) + Nassoc(A, B) = 2 exactly, for every partition

The identity in the last line is worth checking rather than remembering: assoc(A,A) + cut(A,B) = assoc(A,V) by definition, so the two ratios for group A sum to 1, and likewise for B. So minimising the disassociation between the groups is exactly the same as maximising the association within them. The criterion does not have to be told to prefer balanced groups — cutting off a single node makes assoc(A,V) tiny, the first term goes to 1, and the total is close to its maximum instead of its minimum. Both failure modes are excluded by one normalisation.

The bad news, stated first. Minimising Ncut exactly over all partitions of a general graph is NP-complete. Everything below is a relaxation, not a solution, and the page will keep saying so.

Rewriting Ncut as a Rayleigh quotient

Let x be the ±1 indicator of the partition, d = W·1, D = diag(d) and L = D − W. Define

the two-valued vector the criterion turns out to be abouty = ((1 + x) − b(1 − x)) / 2 so yᵢ = 1 for i ∈ A and yᵢ = −b for i ∈ B with b = k/(1 − k), k = (∑_{i ∈ A} dᵢ)/(∑_i dᵢ) chosen so that yᵀd = 0 then, and this is an identity rather than an approximationNcut(A, B) = yᵀ(D − W)y / (yᵀDy)

That equality is exact for every partition. It was checked here by brute force: on the eight-node graph in the figure, over all 254 non-trivial bipartitions, the largest disagreement between Ncut(A,B) and the Rayleigh quotient of its own y is 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ — floating point, and nothing else.

So the discrete problem is:

the exact problem — hard because of the last lineminimise yᵀLy / yᵀDy subject to yᵀd = 0 and yᵢ ∈ {1, −b} ← this constraint is what makes it NP-complete

The relaxation

Drop the last line. Let y be any real vector. The minimum of a Rayleigh quotient over a subspace is an eigenvalue, so the relaxed problem is solved by the generalised eigenvector belonging to the second-smallest generalised eigenvalue:

the relaxed problem is a generalised eigenproblem(D − W)·y = λ·D·y which becomes an ordinary symmetric eigenproblem by the substitution z = D^{1/2}y(I − N)·z = λ·z, N = D^{−1/2}·W·D^{−1/2} the normalised affinity matrix and the answer is read off the second-smallest eigenvalueλ₁ = 0 always, with y = 1 — the useless solution "everything in one group" λ₂ and its eigenvector y₂ — the relaxed optimum, automatically satisfying y₂ᵀd = 0

Then round: split the nodes by the sign of y₂, or better, sort the nodes by y₂ and try all n−1 splits of that ordering, keeping the one with the best actual Ncut.

Exactly what is and is not guaranteed. Three separate claims, only two of which are true.
· True. λ₂ is a lower bound on the minimum discrete Ncut, because the relaxed feasible set contains the discrete one. On the figure's default graph λ₂ = 0.028708 and the true discrete minimum, found by brute force over all 254 bipartitions, is 0.029557. The gap is small here and is not small in general.
· True. The eigenvector is the exact solution of the relaxed problem, computed to machine precision by a standard eigensolver.
· False, and often stated. "Normalised cuts solves the normalised cut problem." It does not. The rounding step has no approximation guarantee — there is no theorem bounding the Ncut of the thresholded eigenvector by any constant times the optimum, and examples exist where it is far off. What the eigenvector supplies is a one-dimensional ordering of the nodes that is usually excellent, and the sweep over that ordering supplies a partition that is usually excellent. "Usually excellent" is the honest claim, and it is worth a great deal — but it is not optimality, and the figure lets you hunt for a setting where the sign cut and the brute-force optimum disagree.

More than two regions

  • Recursive bisection. Cut, then recurse on each half, stopping when the Ncut value exceeds a threshold. Cheap, and it can only produce partitions that are nested — a genuine limitation, as §02's ladder argument predicts.
  • Use k eigenvectors at once. Take the eigenvectors for λ₂ … λ_{k+1}, treat each node's coordinates in that k-dimensional space as a feature vector, and run k-means on them. This is spectral clustering proper, and it is what the general-purpose implementations do. It replaces the ill-posed "where do I threshold this eigenvector" with the ill-posed "what is k", which is §02 again.
  • Cost. The matrices are sparse and only the bottom few eigenvectors are needed, so a Lanczos-type solver is used rather than a dense decomposition. It is still the expensive part of the pipeline, and it is the reason normalised cuts is almost always run on superpixels (§25) rather than pixels.

22 Why that eigenvector — three ways to see it

"Take the second-smallest eigenvector" is the kind of instruction that gets memorised rather than understood. Here are three independent reasons it is the right one, each of which makes a different part of the behaviour obvious.

1 · Algebraically: the first one is trivial, so the second is the first useful one

Since every row of L = D − W sums to zero, L·1 = 0: the all-ones vector is an eigenvector with eigenvalue zero, always, for every graph. In the generalised problem it is the same vector. It is a perfectly valid minimiser of yᵀLy/yᵀDy — it just says "put everything in one group", which achieves a cut of zero by not cutting.

The constraint yᵀd = 0 is precisely the statement "be D-orthogonal to that trivial solution". Under that constraint the minimum of the Rayleigh quotient is the next eigenvalue, by the standard variational characterisation of eigenvalues — see Eigendecomposition & the SVD for the Courant–Fischer statement and Quadratic Forms for the Rayleigh quotient itself. So λ₂ is not a choice; it is what "the best non-trivial answer" means.

2 · Analytically: it is the quietest way to disagree

Recall yᵀLy = ½∑_{ij} w_ij(yᵢ − yⱼ)². Minimising it wants y constant; the constraint forbids that. So the eigenvector is the least-varying non-constant function on the graph — it must change somewhere, and the cheapest place to change is across the weakest edges. The eigenvector's steepest transition is the cut. That is why thresholding it works at all.

3 · Physically: it is the fundamental mode of vibration

Read L as a stiffness matrix and D as a mass matrix, so Ly = λDy is the free-vibration equation of a network of masses joined by springs, with spring constants w_ij and masses dᵢ. Then:

  • λ₁ = 0 with y = 1 is the rigid-body mode: everything moves together, nothing stretches, no energy.
  • λ₂ is the fundamental mode — the lowest-frequency real vibration. Its eigenvector has exactly one nodal line, where the displacement passes through zero, and the parts on either side move in opposite directions. Two heavy sub-networks joined by weak springs will oscillate against each other about the weak springs, so the nodal line falls on the weak connection. That nodal line is the cut, and the sign of the eigenvector is which side you are on.
  • Higher eigenvectors are higher harmonics with more nodal lines, which is exactly why using k of them gives k groups.
One theorem worth knowing, because it is checkable. The number of eigenvalues of L equal to zero is exactly the number of connected components of the graph, and the corresponding eigenvectors are the indicators of those components. So λ₂ = 0 if and only if the graph is disconnected — which is why λ₂ is called the algebraic connectivity, and why a small λ₂ means "nearly disconnected", which is exactly what a good cut is looking for. The relaxation cannot be an equality, because its feasible set strictly contains the discrete one — so the number the eigensolver returns is a certificate that no partition can do better than λ₂, and nothing more.
  • Watch the minimum unnormalised cut. At the default it is 0.1500 and it happens to split the two groups four against four. Now set the bridge to 0.02, the second bridge to 0.60 and the asymmetry to 0.20. 23 Graph cuts: the one place on this page where the answer is provably optimal

Everything so far has been a heuristic, a relaxation or a local minimum. This section is different, and the difference is worth stating up front: for a two-label problem with the right kind of smoothness term, the global minimum of the energy is found exactly, in polynomial time. The conditions on "the right kind" are narrow and precise, and the rest of the section is mostly about them.

The energy

a binary labelling energy, f_p ∈ {0, 1}E(f) = ∑_p D_p(f_p) + ∑_{(p,q) ∈ N} V_pq(f_p, f_q) the data term — how badly pixel p fits the model of the region it was assigned toD_p(1) = −log Pr( I_p | foreground ) D_p(0) = −log Pr( I_p | background ) simplest useful choice: D_p(k) = (I_p − μ_k)² richer: a colour histogram, or a Gaussian mixture the smoothness term — pay to place a boundary, and pay less where the image already has oneV_pq(a, b) = λ · exp( −(I_p − I_q)² / (2σ²) ) · [a ≠ b]

The contrast-sensitive weight is the important detail. A constant penalty λ·[a ≠ b] minimises boundary length, which shrinks objects and rounds off their corners for the same reason a snake shrinks (§14). Multiplying by exp(−Δ²/2σ²) makes the boundary cheap exactly where the image has a step, so the energy prefers to cut along edges it did not have to invent.

The construction

Build a directed graph with one node per pixel plus two terminals — a source S standing for label 1 and a sink T standing for label 0 — and these capacities:

terminal (t-link) capacitiescap(S → p) = D_p(0) cutting it means p ends up on the sink side, labelled 0 cap(p → T) = D_p(1) cutting it means p ends up on the source side, labelled 1 neighbour (n-link) capacities, both directionscap(p → q) = cap(q → p) = V_pq(0, 1) then, for any S–T cut, the capacity severed is exactly the energy of the corresponding labellingcapacity of the cut = E(f) + const

Every S–T cut corresponds to a labelling and vice versa, and their costs agree. So the minimum cut is the minimum-energy labelling, and the minimum cut is computable because of the theorem below.

The max-flow min-cut theorem. In any flow network, the value of a maximum flow from S to T equals the capacity of a minimum S–T cut. This is a genuine theorem, not a heuristic. It also hands you the cut for free: at maximum flow, the source side of the minimum cut is exactly the set of nodes still reachable from S along edges with residual capacity left. So one max-flow computation both produces the optimal labelling and certifies it, and the certificate is the flow itself — any labelling with a lower energy would be a cut with capacity below the flow value, which is impossible.

The condition, stated exactly

Not every energy can be built this way. A pairwise term is representable as a set of non-negative capacities if and only if it is submodular:

the submodularity condition for a binary pairwise termV_pq(0, 0) + V_pq(1, 1) ≤ V_pq(0, 1) + V_pq(1, 0) for the contrast-sensitive term above, with any λ ≥ 00 + 0 ≤ λ·w_pq + λ·w_pq which holds for every λ ≥ 0 and every w_pq ≥ 0 ✓

In words: agreeing must never cost more than disagreeing. Any smoothness term that prefers neighbours to be the same is submodular; a term that rewarded neighbours for differing would not be, and no non-negative capacity assignment can represent it. In practice this condition is satisfied by essentially every smoothness term anyone actually wants, which is why the restriction is easy to forget — but it is the hypothesis the whole guarantee rests on.

The overclaim to avoid. "Graph cuts finds the global optimum of segmentation energies." It finds the global optimum of a two-label energy with submodular pairwise terms. Change either condition and the guarantee is gone:
· More than two labels is NP-hard in general. The standard algorithms, α-expansion and α-swap, are iterated binary graph cuts and are approximations. α-expansion carries a factor-of-two bound on the energy, and only when the pairwise term is a metric; α-swap carries no bound at all.
· Non-submodular binary terms cannot be represented. Roof duality (QPBO) handles them by returning a partial labelling — provably optimal on the pixels it does label, and silent on the rest.
· The energy is still a model. A global optimum of the wrong energy is a wrong answer computed exactly. This is a real and common outcome, and it is the reason the data term — the region model — is where the engineering effort actually goes.
Nine pixels, one flow network — the flow saturates, the cut appears, and brute force agrees every one of the 512 labellings is evaluated beside it, so "global optimum" is checked rather than asserted
  • Four numbers that are always equal. The maximum flow, the capacity of the minimum cut, the energy of the labelling the cut induces, and the brute-force minimum over all 512 labellings. At the default they are all 5.692173. Move any slider and they stay equal — the panel would turn red if they ever did not, and it never does, because the theorem is a theorem.
  • Look at the middle pixel at the default. Its value is 3, so the foreground model fits it terribly — D(1) = 30.25 against D(0) = 0.25 — and it stays background even though it is surrounded on three sides by foreground. A greedy or region-growing method would have swallowed it. The global optimum keeps it, because the arithmetic says keeping it is cheaper, and there is nothing to argue with.
  • Turn the smoothness all the way up to 12, and watch nothing happen. The labelling is unchanged at every λ up to 80. On this image the data terms are enormous — the middle pixel pays 30.25 to be called foreground — while the boundary cost is at most 2λ per edge, so the smoothness never becomes decisive. That is worth seeing rather than assuming: λ is not a granularity dial that always works. Whether it bites depends entirely on the scale of the data term, and the two are in different units unless somebody normalises them, which is a large part of what tuning a segmentation energy actually consists of.
  • The region models decide this, not the smoothness. Drag the foreground mean down to 3: every pixel is now closer to the foreground model than to the background one, the optimum flips to all nine foreground, and there is no boundary at all. Drag the background mean up to 7 and two pixels defect the other way. This is the general case, and it is why the whole contribution of the best-known interactive graph-cut system is a better way of estimating the two models rather than a better way of cutting.
  • Set the smoothness to zero. Every pixel independently takes whichever label fits it better, and the energy becomes ∑_p min(D_p(0), D_p(1)) = 4.25. The pairwise term is the only thing making this a segmentation rather than nine separate decisions, which is the same observation §04 made about thresholding.
  • Watch the saturated edges. At maximum flow they form a complete barrier between source and sink: you cannot get from S to T without crossing one. That barrier is the cut, and finding it is why max-flow returns the segmentation rather than just a number.

24 Interactive segmentation: letting the user supply the missing question

§02 argued that no algorithm can know which partition is wanted. The most honest response to that argument is not a cleverer algorithm; it is an interface. A few strokes from a user carry exactly the information the pixels do not, and the methods built around that idea are the ones that actually get deployed.

Seeds into an energy

A user paints a few strokes marked object and a few marked background. Those pixels do two jobs at once, and the second is the important one:

  1. Hard constraints. Seeded pixels are tied to their terminal with infinite capacity, so no cut can ever separate them from it. The optimum is then the best labelling consistent with what the user said.
  2. Training data. The seeded pixels are a labelled sample of the two region models. Estimate a colour histogram or a Gaussian mixture from each, and the data term D_p in §23 stops being a guess.

Iterating the estimate: the bounding-box trick

The best-known refinement removes even the strokes. The user drags one rectangle; everything outside it is definitely background, everything inside is probably foreground. Then:

  1. Fit a background colour mixture to the pixels outside the box and a foreground mixture to those inside.
  2. Solve the binary graph cut with those models — a global optimum, by §23.
  3. Re-estimate both mixtures from the labelling just produced.
  4. Repeat until the labelling stops changing. Optionally accept a few corrective strokes and re-solve.

Each individual cut is globally optimal; the loop is not. It is coordinate descent alternating between the labelling and the model parameters — the same structure as expectation–maximisation and with the same guarantee, which is that the energy decreases monotonically and converges to a local minimum. Starting from a bounding box rather than from nothing is what makes that local minimum a good one, and the initialisation is doing more work than the optimiser.

The continuous relaxation, and what it buys

Replace the binary labels by real numbers in [0, 1] and the discontinuity penalty by a quadratic one. The energy becomes a quadratic form, its minimum is the solution of a sparse linear system, and thresholding that solution at 0.5 gives a segmentation.

the relaxed problem — a weighted Laplacian system with the seeds as boundary conditionsminimise ∑_{(p,q)} w_pq · (u_p − u_q)² subject to u = 1 on the object seeds, u = 0 on the background seeds ⇔ L_U · u_U = −B · u_seeds one sparse symmetric positive-definite solve

The solution has three readings that are all the same computation, and each is illuminating:

  • A random walk. u_p is the probability that a random walker starting at pixel p, stepping to neighbours with probability proportional to w, reaches an object seed before a background seed.
  • An electrical network. Treat each w_pq as a conductance, tie the object seeds to 1 volt and the background seeds to 0. Then u is the potential at every node. Boundaries are resistive, interiors are conductive, and the voltage drop happens across the boundary.
  • A diffusion. The solution is the steady state of heat spreading from the seeds through a medium whose conductivity is the affinity.

Compared with graph cuts, the relaxation gives a soft answer — useful directly for matting, where a pixel really is part object — and it extends to k labels by solving one system per label and taking the largest, which graph cuts cannot do exactly. It gives up the exactness: thresholding at 0.5 has no optimality guarantee for the original binary energy, and the quadratic penalty smooths boundaries that the discontinuity penalty would have kept sharp.

The other family: geodesic distance. Faster still, and worth knowing because it is a completely different trade. Define a length for a path through the image that is large where the image changes, and label each pixel by whichever seed set is geodesically nearer. One pass of a fast-marching or raster-scan distance transform per label, and it is effectively instantaneous. It is far more sensitive to seed placement than a cut is — the answer is decided by the single cheapest path, so one weak spot in a boundary loses the whole region — but at interactive rates that sensitivity is arguably a feature, because the user sees it immediately and adds a stroke.

25 Superpixels: deliberately over-segmenting, on purpose, as a first move

§02 concluded that if you cannot know the right level of granularity, the safe move is to produce something finer than any level you might want, and let a later stage group it. That is the whole idea. A superpixel is not meant to be an object; it is meant to be an atom that no object boundary passes through.

What superpixels are for

  • They collapse the problem size. The figure's image has 96 × 72 = 6 912 pixels and comes out as 54 superpixels at the default settings — a factor of 128. Normalised cuts on 6 912 nodes is a large sparse eigenproblem; on 60 nodes it is a dense 60 × 60 decomposition that finishes instantly. This is why every graph method in §20 to §23 is run on superpixels in practice.
  • They give you a region to compute statistics over. A single pixel has one value; a superpixel has a colour histogram, a texture descriptor, a shape and a size. Features that are meaningless per-pixel become usable, which is exactly what the affinities of §20 want.
  • They are conservative. A merging algorithm can undo a superpixel boundary that should not have been there. Nothing can undo a superpixel that straddled a real boundary — the error is permanent. So the design target is boundary recall: never miss a true boundary, and do not much care how many false ones you introduce.

SLIC, and the two changes that turn k-means into a superpixel algorithm

SLIC is k-means in the five-dimensional joint space of §19 — (L*, a*, b*, x, y) — with two modifications, and everything that page says about k-means applies unchanged apart from these.

  1. Initialise on a regular grid of spacing S = √(N/k), then nudge each centre to the lowest-gradient pixel in its 3 × 3 neighbourhood, so no centre begins its life sitting on an edge or on a noise spike.
  2. Restrict the search to a 2S × 2S window around each centre. This is the change that matters computationally: standard k-means compares every pixel to every centre, at cost O(NkI); SLIC compares each pixel to the handful of centres whose windows contain it, at cost O(NI) independent of k.
  3. Use a distance that puts the two halves on a common scale. Spatial distance is divided by S so it is in units of "grid cells", and the compactness constant m says how many units of colour one grid cell is worth.
  4. Enforce connectivity afterwards. Nothing in the k-means step guarantees a cluster is connected, so a final pass splits each label into its connected pieces and absorbs the small ones into a neighbour. This pass is not cosmetic — it changes the superpixel count, sometimes substantially.

What the three metrics mean, and what they measure here

MetricDefinitionReading
Boundary recallThe fraction of true boundary pixels that have a superpixel boundary within one pixel. Higher is better; this is the metric that matters most.A recall below 1 means some real boundary has been crossed by a superpixel, and no downstream stage can recover it.
Under-segmentation errorFor each superpixel, the fraction of it that falls outside its own majority region, averaged over the image. Lower is better.Measures how badly the boundaries are crossed, not merely whether. A superpixel that pokes one pixel over a boundary is nearly free; one that straddles it evenly is a disaster.
CompactnessThe area-weighted mean of 4πA/P², which is 1 for a disc and small for a stringy shape.Not a quality measure on its own — it is what you are spending. Compact superpixels are easier to build features on and easier to draw a regular graph over; they are not more correct.
  • The trade is not monotone, and the figure measures it. At k = 60, boundary recall is 0.884 at m = 2, rises to 0.998 at m = 20, and falls back to 0.951 at m = 40. The textbook expectation — that a small m hugs boundaries best — is wrong here, and the picture shows why: with colour dominating, the superpixels become long stringy things, the connectivity pass then absorbs the fragments, and only 39 superpixels survive out of a nominal 63. Larger superpixels have less boundary available to place. Good boundary adherence needs cells that are both small and image-shaped; a colour-only distance buys the second by giving up the first.
  • The actual count is not the requested count. Ask for 60 and you get between 39 and 64 depending on m, because the connectivity pass merges and splits. Any pipeline that assumes a fixed superpixel count is making an assumption the algorithm does not honour.
  • Watch the thin bright wire. It is two pixels wide, and it is the structure superpixels lose first. At small k and large m the rigid cells drive straight across it and it disappears into their means — visible immediately in the mean-painted panel. Thin structures are the standard failure of every superpixel method, and the reason detectors that need them do not use superpixels.
  • Turn the iteration count to 1. The result is already recognisable: SLIC converges in a handful of iterations because the initialisation is a grid and the search window keeps every point near its own centre. Ten iterations is the usual recommendation and four is usually enough, which is the other half of why it is fast.
