17 Pixels as samples: the density view of segmentation

Forget the image grid for a moment. Take every pixel's feature vector — its colour, or its colour and position — and throw the points into feature space. Segmentation becomes: find the clusters in this cloud, then map the cluster labels back onto the grid.

That reframing is what connects this part of vision to the whole of cluster analysis. It is also where all the leverage is, because it separates two decisions that are usually tangled together: what makes two pixels similar (the feature vector and its metric) and what makes a group a group (the clustering criterion).

This page does not re-teach clustering. Clustering & k-Means owns k-means, its initialisation, choosing k, hierarchical linkage, DBSCAN and cluster validity. Descriptive Statistics owns the kernel density estimate itself. What is specific to images, and therefore lives here, is: which feature vector, how spatial position is folded in, and what the answer looks like when it is projected back onto a grid — which is §18 and §19.

Two things about that formula are worth noticing. First, the normalising constant has been dropped. For finding modes it is irrelevant — a positive constant does not move a maximum — and dropping it means the "density" here does not integrate to one, which is fine and is why the source literature is casual about it. Second, the kernel is written as a profile of the squared distance, k(r) with r = ‖·‖²/h². That is not decoration: the mean-shift derivation in §18 needs the derivative with respect to r, and writing it this way makes the derivative kernel g(r) = −k′(r) fall out in one line.

The bandwidth is the model

h plays exactly the role that k plays in k-means, τ plays in region growing and h plays in the persistence filter: it is the place where the granularity of §02 gets supplied. Small h resolves every sample as its own bump; large h fuses everything into one. Neither the data nor the algorithm can choose it.

The one genuinely useful heuristic is stability: sweep h, count the modes, and look for a plateau — a wide interval of bandwidths over which the answer does not change. A structure that survives a factor of four in bandwidth is a structure; one that appears and disappears within ten percent is noise. §18's figure plots that staircase directly, and on its data the three-mode answer holds from h = 1.92 all the way to h = 9.08 — a factor of 4.73 — while the seven-mode answer holds only from 1.32 to 1.52, a factor of 1.15.

18 Mean shift: gradient ascent that needs no gradient and no step size

Once the density is defined, the clusters are its modes and the cluster memberships are the basins of attraction. Actually computing f everywhere is out of the question in more than two dimensions. Mean shift finds the modes without ever evaluating f at all — and the reason is a two-line rearrangement that turns the gradient into a weighted average.

The derivation

Differentiate the estimate. Writing k′ for the derivative of the profile and g = −k′:

the gradient of the density estimate∇f(x) = ∇ ∑ᵢ k( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) = ∑ᵢ k′(·) · 2(x − xᵢ)/h² ∝ ∑ᵢ (xᵢ − x) · g( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) now factor out the total weight — this is the whole trick∇f(x) ∝ [ ∑ᵢ gᵢ ] · ( ∑ᵢ xᵢ gᵢ / ∑ᵢ gᵢ − x ) where gᵢ = g( ‖x − xᵢ‖²/h² ) the bracketed vector is the mean shiftm(x) = ∑ᵢ xᵢ·gᵢ / ∑ᵢ gᵢ − x = (weighted mean of the neighbours) − (where you are) so the update is: move to the weighted mean, and repeaty_{k+1} = y_k + m(y_k) = ∑ᵢ xᵢ·g(‖y_k − xᵢ‖²/h²) / ∑ᵢ g(‖y_k − xᵢ‖²/h²)

Three consequences follow, and each of them is a reason the method is used.

  • The mean shift vector points along the density gradient. Not approximately — exactly, because the only thing separating them is the positive scalar ∑gᵢ. So the iteration is gradient ascent, and it never needs the gradient computed.
  • The step size is automatic and correct. The scalar that was divided out, ∑gᵢ, is large where the density is high. Dividing by it means the step is short in dense regions — near a mode, where you want to slow down — and long in sparse ones. This is why mean shift converges without a line search, and why naive gradient ascent on the same f does not: with a fixed step size it overshoots the peaks and crawls across the flats.
  • It converges, and the proof is short. For a monotonically decreasing convex profile k, the density is non-decreasing along the iteration, f(y_{k+1}) ≥ f(y_k), and the sequence is bounded, so it converges to a stationary point. With the Epanechnikov kernel the derivative kernel is a uniform ball, the update becomes "move to the mean of the points inside the ball", and it terminates in a finite number of steps. With the Gaussian kernel the trajectory is smoother and the results are usually better, but convergence near a mode is asymptotic and slow.
Mean shift is the watershed run backwards. The watershed of §12 starts at every minimum and floods upward, assigning each pixel to the minimum it drains into. Mean shift starts at every sample and climbs upward, assigning each sample to the maximum it ascends to. Same construction, same basins-of-attraction definition of a region, opposite sign — and the same failure mode, over-segmentation from spurious extrema, controlled by the same kind of smoothing parameter. If you understand one, you have understood the other.

The procedure, and what it costs

  1. Start a mode estimate at every sample xᵢ.
  2. Iterate the weighted-mean update until the shift falls below a tolerance.
  3. Merge converged points that end up within a small distance of one another; those groups are the modes.
  4. Label each original sample by the mode its trajectory reached. That labelling is the segmentation.

Cost is the problem: each iteration of each trajectory touches every sample, so a full run is O(n²) per iteration with n = the number of pixels. The standard accelerations are to subsample the starting points and assign the rest by nearest trajectory; to use a spatial data structure so only samples within the kernel's support are visited (immediate, and exact, for the Epanechnikov kernel because its support is finite); or to evaluate a coarse version of f on a low-resolution grid and locate its stationary points there.

Bandwidths are not transferable between kernels.

19 The joint domain: putting position back into the feature vector

Clustering pixels by colour alone throws away the one thing that makes an image an image. Two objects on opposite sides of the frame that happen to be the same colour become one cluster, and a cluster that is scattered across the picture is not a region.

The fix is not to post-process. It is to put position into the feature vector, so that spatial proximity is part of what "similar" means:

the joint spatial–range feature vectorx_j = ( x_s , x_r ) x_s = (x, y) the spatial domain x_r = colour the range domain and a product kernel, because the two halves are in different units and need different bandwidthsK(x_j) = (C / (h_s²·h_r^p)) · k( ‖x_s‖² / h_s² ) · k( ‖x_r‖² / h_r² ) so the three parameters of a mean-shift segmentation areh_s spatial bandwidth — how far a pixel may look for company, in pixels h_r range bandwidth — how different a colour may be and still be company M minimum region size — regions below this are absorbed afterwards

With colour the feature vector is five-dimensional: (x, y, L*, u*, v*). The perceptual colour space matters here — Euclidean distance in it is meant to approximate perceived difference, so one h_r is meaningful across the whole gamut, which it is not in raw RGB. That is Part 1's subject.

The relationship to the bilateral filter, stated precisely. The kernel above is the bilateral filter's kernel — a spatial term times a range term, exactly. The difference is what moves. The bilateral filter computes the same weighted average and writes it into the pixel's colour, leaving the pixel where it is; it is an edge-preserving smoother. Mean shift writes the weighted average back into both the colour and the position, so the sample migrates through the joint space toward its mode and can be clustered when it gets there. One is a filter, the other is a segmentation, and the only difference in the code is which coordinates you update. The filter belongs to Part 2.

What each bandwidth does

  • h_r alone behaves exactly like §18: it decides how many colour clusters exist, and a cluster can be spread anywhere in the frame.
  • h_s decides the maximum size of a region that can be found on its own, and — much more importantly — makes clusters spatially coherent, because two pixels more than a few h_s apart contribute nothing to each other's trajectory whatever their colours.
  • M is not part of the model at all. It is the same post-hoc minimum-size pass as §11's and §13's, and it exists because mean shift, like every mode-finding method, produces a long tail of tiny regions along boundaries.
