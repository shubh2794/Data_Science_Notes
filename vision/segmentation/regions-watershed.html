09 Region growing: the oldest idea, and the one failure that defines it

Start from a seed pixel, repeatedly annex any neighbour that is similar enough, stop when nothing else qualifies. Three lines of pseudocode, no parameters except a tolerance, and regions that are connected by construction. It is the first thing anyone writes and it fails in a characteristic, instructive way.

the algorithmR ← {s} s is the seed while ∃ p ∉ R adjacent to R with pred(p, R): R ← R ∪ {p} two predicates that look almost identical and behave completely differentlyfixed reference: pred(p, R) = |I(p) − I(s)| ≤ τ running mean: pred(p, R) = |I(p) − μ_R| ≤ τ with μ_R updated after every annexation

Fixed reference against running mean

Fixed reference I(s)Running mean μ_R
What it assumesThe region is a plateau at one value, and the seed sits on it.Only that neighbouring pixels resemble what has been accepted so far.
ShadingFails. A slow ramp across the object eventually exceeds τ and the region stops halfway.Copes. The mean drifts with the ramp, so the region walks up the gradient.

The visiting order is irrelevant.Total. μ_R depends on the accepted set, so a different order gives a different region. Breadth-first, depth-first and best-first give three different answers.

The one honest sentence about region growing. The fixed-reference version is not really an algorithm at all — it is thresholding at [I(s) − τ, I(s) + τ] followed by connected components (§08), and everything §04 to §07 says applies to it unchanged. Only the running-mean version is genuinely a different method, and the thing that makes it different — that the criterion adapts to what has already been accepted — is exactly the thing that makes it leak.

Leakage, and why it is not fixable by tuning

Two regions that ought to be separate almost always touch somewhere: a few pixels where the boundary is blurred, or noise happened to bridge the gap, or the objects genuinely abut. A region-growing front only needs one pixel of the boundary to be within tolerance. It goes through, and on the other side it is inside a large uniform region where every neighbour qualifies, so it fills the whole thing.

The failure is a cliff, not a slope: the region is exactly right, exactly right, exactly right, and then it is twice the size it should be. On the figure below the running-mean variant first admits a pixel of the neighbour at a tolerance of 23.50; at a tolerance of 28 it has taken 22 of the neighbour's pixels, and at 30 — a nudge of two grey levels — it has taken 633 of them. The parameter has no safe setting because the safe setting is a knife edge whose position depends on the noise in a handful of pixels.

The four repairs, and what each costs

  1. Grow on the gradient, not on the intensity. Require the step between the candidate and its already-accepted neighbour to be small, rather than the candidate's distance to the region mean. A blurred boundary is still a run of small steps, so this helps less than it sounds, but it makes the criterion local and removes the runaway feedback.
  2. Order the front by cost, not by arrival. Keep the boundary pixels in a priority queue ordered by |I(p) − μ_R| and always take the cheapest. The region then grows outward in order of similarity rather than in order of geometry, so the weak boundary is crossed last instead of first. This is seeded region growing, and it is one line of difference from the naive version and much better behaved.
  3. Make the seeds compete. Grow all regions at once and let each pixel go to whichever front reaches it first under its own predicate. A front arriving at the neck from one side meets a front arriving from the other, and neither crosses. Measured on the figure's image, two competing seeds never let a single pixel cross the neck, at any tolerance the figure can search — where a single seed has swallowed the whole neighbour by a tolerance of 40. (Above about 88 both regions do leak into the background, which is a different failure and one that competition cannot fix, because there is no third seed to race.) This is the single most effective fix, and taken to its limit with one seed per local minimum it is the watershed of §12.
  4. Constrain the result afterwards. Cap the region area, require compactness, or reject a region whose growth curve shows a sudden jump. Crude, and sometimes the only thing that works.

  • Tick the competing seed. The leak disappears entirely, and stays gone as you raise the tolerance far past the point where the single-seed version has swallowed everything. Nothing about the image changed; what changed is that the boundary is now decided by a race rather than by a threshold.
  • Push the tolerance past 88. Now the background comes in too, and the region is the whole frame. The three regimes — under-grown, correct, leaked into the neighbour, leaked into everything — are separated by tolerance values that are properties of this image and cannot be transferred to another one.

10 Splitting, merging, and the quadtree that sits between them

Region growing works upward from single pixels. The opposite strategy starts with the whole image and cuts. Neither alone is any good; the combination is, and understanding why is understanding what each half contributes.

Splitting: recursive subdivision under a predicate

  1. Start with the whole image as one region.
  2. For each region, test the homogeneity predicate P. If it holds, stop; the region is a leaf.
  3. If it fails, cut the region into four equal quadrants and recurse on each.
  4. Stop also at a minimum block size, typically 1×1 or 2×2.

The bookkeeping is a quadtree: the root is the image, every internal node has four children, and the leaves are the regions. On a 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ image the tree has depth at most n, the total work is O(N) if the predicate is evaluated from summed-area tables, and the result is a partition into axis-aligned dyadic squares.

Which is precisely the problem. Every region boundary is a horizontal or vertical line at a power-of-two coordinate. A diagonal edge comes out as a staircase; a circle comes out as a pixelated approximation; and two halves of one smooth object that happen to straddle the image's centre line are never in the same region, no matter how similar they are, because splitting never puts anything back together.

The predicates, and the trap in each

Predicate P(R)Hereditary?Behaviour, and where it misleads
max(R) − min(R) ≤ τyesThe safest choice, because heredity guarantees that splitting a good region gives good children, so the recursion is monotone. Ruinously sensitive to a single outlier pixel — one hot pixel splits a block all the way down.
σ²(R) ≤ τnoRobust to outliers, which is why it is popular. But a checkerboard of two values has a large variance and splits forever, while a smooth ramp of the same range has a smaller variance and does not — variance cannot distinguish texture from a gradient.
|z − μ_R| ≤ 2σ_R ∀znoSelf-referential: the tolerance is estimated from the very data it is testing. A perfectly uniform block has σ_R ≈ 0 and fails its own test on noise alone.
H(R) is unimodalnoThe most semantically sensible and the least stable — the answer depends on histogram binning and on how much the histogram was smoothed, exactly as in §04.
Heredity is not a technicality. If P is hereditary — true on a set implies true on every subset — the recursion terminates cleanly, the leaves are the maximal dyadic blocks that satisfy P, and the answer does not depend on the order of the recursion. If it is not, a block can fail the test while all four of its children fail it too and their children pass, so the depth reached is not a monotone function of the data, and two implementations that visit the tree in different orders can produce different leaves. Range is hereditary. Variance is not.

Merging: repairing what splitting broke

After splitting, run a pass that fuses adjacent leaves whose union still satisfies the predicate:

the merge conditionmerge Rᵢ and Rⱼ if Rᵢ adjacent Rⱼ and P(Rᵢ ∪ Rⱼ) = true stop when condition 4 of §03 holds — no adjacent pair can still be merged

Merging repairs three of splitting's four sins. It reunites the halves of an object that straddled a cut line; it eliminates the artificial boundary between two identical adjacent blocks; and it lets a region take a non-rectangular shape, because a union of squares need not be a square. What it does not repair is the staircase: the leaf boundaries are still on dyadic lines, so a diagonal edge remains a diagonal edge quantised to blocks, and the finest step is the smallest leaf. That is the permanent cost of the representation, and the reason quadtree segmentation lost to the graph and energy methods later on this page.

Merging also introduces the order-dependence that splitting did not have. Merging R₁ into R₂ changes R₂'s statistics, which may now prevent a merge with R₃ that would have succeeded a moment earlier. Greedy merging in increasing order of dissimilarity — the same idea as single-link agglomerative clustering — is the usual compromise, and §11 turns it into something with an actual guarantee.

  • Look at where the 76 regions actually are. 64 of them are inside the 16 × 16 checkerboard patch — one per cell — so the entire rest of the image, disc, rectangle, ellipse and shaded background, comes out in 12 regions. The predicate recurses until the blocks are as small as the checker cells and then stops, and the merge pass cannot fuse any two of them, because the union of two adjacent cells has a range of about 70 and the tolerance is 22. A texture is not a tolerance that needs adjusting; it is a region that is uniform in a statistic these predicates do not compute. Handling it needs a texture feature, which is Part 4's.
  • Where do the single-pixel leaves go? At the default there are 264 of them and not one is inside the checkerboard — they lie along the boundaries of the disc, the rectangle and the ellipse. A quadtree spends its resolution almost entirely on edges, which is exactly what makes it a good compression structure and a poor segmentation: the finest detail is stored where the boundary is, and the boundary is still quantised to a dyadic staircase. Raise the smallest-block slider and both the leaf count and the fidelity fall together.
  • Switch to the variance predicate at the same tolerance. The checkerboard behaves differently from the shaded background even though both have similar ranges, and neither predicate handles both well. That is the non-heredity in the table above, visible: variance measures spread without regard to arrangement, so it cannot tell a ramp from a rattle.

11 The region adjacency graph, and a merge rule that adapts to each region

Once regions exist, the natural object is not the image any more but a graph: one node per region, an edge between every pair that touches, and a weight on each edge saying how different they are. Merging becomes edge contraction, and the whole of §10's merge pass becomes a question about which edges to contract.

the region adjacency graphG = (V, E) V = {R₁, …, R_n} E = { (i, j) : Rᵢ touches Rⱼ } w(i, j) = a dissimilarity — |μᵢ − μⱼ|, a colour distance, a histogram distance, an edge strength along the shared boundary

The RAG is a genuine compression: an image with a million pixels might have a few thousand regions and a few tens of thousands of edges, so an algorithm that would be hopeless on pixels becomes cheap. That is the reason superpixels (§25) exist, and the reason the graph methods of §20 to §23 are usually run on regions rather than raw pixels.

Why a single global threshold on w cannot work

The obvious rule is "contract every edge with w < T". It fails for a reason that is worth measuring rather than asserting. Consider an image with two smooth halves separated by a small step, and a patch of high-frequency texture sitting in one of them. Two edge populations:

Edge populationcountminmedianmax
Across the boundary between the two smooth halves — the boundary that must survive409.3217.0721.60

The second interval strictly contains the first. There is no value of T that cuts every edge of the first population and no edge of the second, and this is not a tuning failure — the populations are not separable by any threshold on w because they overlap in every part of their range. Measured: 20.4% of the texture's internal edges are weaker than the weakest boundary edge, and 52.9% are stronger than the strongest one.

What the picture shows and the threshold cannot use is that the two populations differ in context: a weight of 20 is enormous compared with the surroundings of a smooth region and unremarkable inside a texture. The fix is to compare each candidate edge not against a constant but against the region's own internal variability.

The graph-based merging predicate

Define, for a region R and a pair of adjacent regions:

internal difference — the largest edge in the region's minimum spanning treeInt(R) = max_{e ∈ MST(R)} w(e) difference between two regions — the weakest edge joining themDif(R₁, R₂) = min_{e = (v₁, v₂), v₁ ∈ R₁, v₂ ∈ R₂} w(e) the comparison, with a size-dependent slack τ(R) = k/|R|MInt(R₁, R₂) = min( Int(R₁) + k/|R₁| , Int(R₂) + k/|R₂| ) merge exactly when there is no evidence of a boundarymerge R₁, R₂ ⟺ Dif(R₁, R₂) ≤ MInt(R₁, R₂)

Read what this says. Two regions stay apart only if the weakest link between them is stronger than the strongest link inside either of them (plus slack). A texture patch has a large Int, so it tolerates a strong external edge and stays whole; a smooth half has a tiny Int, so even a modest step at its border counts as evidence and survives. The threshold is per-region and is read off the data.

The slack τ(R) = k/|R| exists because Int(R) is meaningless for a small region — a single pixel has no internal edges at all, so Int = 0 and it would never merge with anything. The slack is large for small regions and vanishes for large ones, so k is not a threshold and not a region count: it is a preferred region size. Doubling k makes regions bigger; it does not make the criterion stricter.

The algorithm, and what is actually guaranteed

  1. Sort all edges by weight, ascending. On a pixel grid with 4- or 8-neighbours this is O(N log N), or O(N) with a counting sort on integer weights.
  2. Process edges in order. For edge (a, b) with weight w, find the current regions R_a, R_b. If they are already the same, skip.
  3. Because edges arrive in ascending order, this edge is Dif(R_a, R_b) — no other edge between them has yet been seen, so none is smaller. Merge if w ≤ MInt, and set the new region's Int to w, which is also correct because w is the largest edge in the merged MST.
  4. Optionally, a final pass absorbing every region below a minimum area into its most similar neighbour.
What the guarantee is, stated exactly — and what it is not. Call a segmentation too fine if some pair of adjacent regions in it satisfies the merge condition, so there is no evidence of a boundary between them. Call it too coarse if some proper refinement of it is not too fine. The theorem is that the greedy algorithm above produces a segmentation that is neither too fine nor too coarse, and that such a segmentation exists and can be found in near-linear time.

This is not a claim that the result minimises any global objective, and it is not a uniqueness claim. Both properties are defined with respect to the very predicate the algorithm uses, so the theorem says the greedy procedure is self-consistent, not that the answer is the best partition of the image by some independent standard. Change w, or change k, and you get a different segmentation which is equally "neither too fine nor too coarse". The guarantee is real and it is much stronger than most merging heuristics offer; it is simply a guarantee about the algorithm's relationship to its own criterion, which is the pattern this whole page keeps running into.

Every practical implementation has this post-pass, and it is not part of the theorem.

12 The watershed: read the image as a landscape and flood it

Treat the grey value as an elevation. The image becomes a terrain: dark regions are valleys, bright ridges are mountain chains. Punch a hole at the bottom of every valley and let water rise uniformly from below. Where two rising lakes are about to meet, build a dam. When the whole terrain is submerged, the dams are the segmentation.

The three kinds of point

  • Regional minima — connected sets of pixels from which every path leads strictly upward. Every one of them seeds a lake.
  • Catchment basin points — a raindrop landing here runs down into one particular minimum. These are the region interiors.
  • Watershed points — a raindrop landing here could run into two or more different minima. These are the dams, and they form the boundaries.

The definition is entirely determined by the terrain: no thresholds, no seeds, no parameters at all. That is the watershed's great virtue and, in the same breath, its defect — §13.

You almost never flood the image itself

Images are not landscapes of the right shape. Objects are usually not dark blobs separated by bright ridges — they are regions of roughly constant value separated by edges. So the transform is run on the gradient magnitude, where uniform interiors become flat valleys and object boundaries become the ridges the dams will be built on.

what actually gets floodedg(x, y) = ‖∇(G_σ * I)(x, y)‖ smooth first, then take the gradient magnitude and for colour, one common choiceg = max over channels, or the norm of the per-channel gradients, or a structure-tensor magnitude

The smoothing is not optional and its scale σ is the single most consequential parameter in the whole pipeline, because every spurious local minimum of g becomes a region. The gradient operators live in Part 4 and the smoothing in Part 2; this section takes g as given.

The flooding algorithm

The classic implementation sorts pixels by value once, then sweeps levels upward, which is what makes it near-linear.

  1. Sort all pixels by g. With integer values this is a counting sort in O(N + L).
  2. At level n, take the set of pixels with g = n and find its connected components.
  3. For each such component q, look at what it touches in the already-flooded set C[n−1]:
    · nothing — a new minimum has been reached; open a new basin;
    · exactly one basin — q is part of that basin's rise; absorb it;
    · two or more basins — a ridge has been crested; build a dam inside q and do not let the basins merge.
  4. Increment the level and repeat to the maximum.

An equivalent and more common implementation replaces the level sweep with a priority queue: initialise it with the minima, repeatedly pop the lowest-valued boundary pixel, assign it to its neighbour's basin if all labelled neighbours agree, and mark it as a dam if they do not. Written that way, the watershed is exactly §09's competitive region growing with the global rule "always take the cheapest pixel next" — and that is why it does not leak: the front cannot cross a ridge until every pixel below the ridge has already been claimed.

Persistence: how deep is a basin, really? Every minimum except the global one eventually merges into a deeper neighbour, at the level of the lowest saddle joining them. The difference between that saddle level and the minimum's own depth is the minimum's persistence — the depth of water it can hold before spilling over. It is the honest measure of how significant a basin is, it is invariant to how wide the basin is, and it is the quantity every over-segmentation fix is secretly thresholding. On the landscape below, the five minima have persistences of 8.20, 17.31, 23.02, 29.85 and infinity — and reading those five numbers tells you in advance exactly how the region count will fall as you filter.

13 Over-segmentation, and the three ways out of it

The watershed has no parameters, which sounds like a virtue until you notice what it implies: the number of regions is decided entirely by the number of local minima in the gradient image, and a noisy gradient image has thousands of them. The transform does exactly what it promises and the answer is useless.

On the figure below — six discs on a flat background, moderate noise, a moderate pre-smooth — the gradient magnitude has 264 regional minima, so the raw watershed returns 264 regions for an image with seven. Its adjusted Rand index against the truth is 0.019: statistically indistinguishable from shredding the image at random. This is not an edge case; it is what the raw watershed does on every real image.

Where the spurious minima come from

  • Noise. Independent noise in the image becomes noise in the gradient, and every local dip is a minimum. Halving the noise roughly halves the count, and never removes it.
  • Texture. Real surfaces have gradient structure at every scale. A patch of fabric or foliage is a mountain range of minima, all of them real features of the image and none of them a region boundary.
  • Quantisation and compression. Eight-bit values and block-transform artefacts create flat plateaus and small steps that are genuine minima of the digitised signal and have no counterpart in the scene.
  • Interiors that are not flat. Shading across an object makes its interior a gentle slope with several tiny dips rather than one basin.

Fix 1 — smooth harder

Increasing the pre-smoothing σ destroys shallow minima and reduces the count. It is the cheapest option and the least controllable: at σ = 1.0 the figure's gradient has 637 minima, at σ = 1.6 it has 264, and at σ = 2.4 it has 88 — still more than twelve times too many, and by then the boundaries have moved. Smoothing removes small minima and large ones alike, and it displaces every edge it survives. Nobody has ever found the right σ this way; it is a first pass, not a fix.

Fix 2 — filter the minima by persistence

§12's persistence is the principled version. Suppress every minimum whose persistence is below h, and flood from what remains. In morphology this is the h-minima transform, implemented by grey-scale reconstruction of g + h under g; on the merge tree it is simply deleting the subtrees that never held more than h of water.

It works, and it is sharply sensitive. On the figure: h = 5 gives 11 regions, h = 8 gives 10, h = 12 gives 8, and h = 16 gives exactly 7 at an adjusted Rand index of 0.928. Four values, four different answers, and only the reader knowing there are six discs can tell which was right.

Fix 3 — impose the minima

The practical answer abandons the pretence that the region count can be discovered. Supply a set of markers and modify the terrain so that its regional minima are exactly the markers and nothing else — that is minima imposition, and it guarantees the region count in advance.

minima imposition, in the morphological formset f(x) = 0 on the marker set M set f(x) = g_max everywhere else then flood the grey-scale reconstruction of min(g + 1, f) under f in practice, and equivalently for the partition, simply seed the flooding at M and nowhere else

Two kinds of marker are used together, and both are necessary:

internal markers
One per object
A click, a scribble, a stroke — or automatic: the regional maxima of a distance transform, the output of a crude detector, the connected components of a coarse threshold. Each one becomes exactly one region.
external markers
One or more for the background
Without them the background has no seed, and the object basins expand until they collide with each other rather than stopping at the objects' edges. The image border, or the watershed lines of a coarse first pass, are the usual choices.

With six internal markers and the border as the external marker, the figure's marker-controlled watershed returns 7 regions at an adjusted Rand index of 0.928 — the same accuracy the best possible h achieves, without having to find it. What changed is not the algorithm but who supplies the region count, and that is the honest characterisation of every interactive segmentation method on this page.

Why the score is 0.928 and not 1. Even a perfect marker set does not give a perfect segmentation, because the watershed line sits on the ridge of the smoothed gradient, and smoothing widened that ridge and pulled it slightly off the true edge. A one-pixel boundary displacement over a few thousand boundary pixels is worth a few percent of any region-overlap score. This is the boundary-localisation cost of pre-smoothing, and it is the reason §26 insists on reporting a boundary measure alongside a region measure — they penalise different mistakes.
