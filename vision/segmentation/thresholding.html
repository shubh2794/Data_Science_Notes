04 Global thresholding: the whole image, through a one-dimensional keyhole

The simplest possible segmenter compares each pixel against one number. It has no idea where the pixel is, what is next to it, or what shape anything has. Everything it knows about the image is the histogram — and the histogram is invariant to shuffling the pixels.

global thresholdB(x, y) = 1 if I(x, y) > t, 0 otherwise t ∈ {0, 1, …, L − 1}

That single line is doing a lot of assuming, and it is worth making the assumptions explicit before choosing t, because when thresholding fails it is always one of these that broke.

AssumptionWhat it means in the imageWhat breaks it, and what to do
Two classesEvery pixel is either object or background — no third material, no "partly".Three materials need two thresholds (multi-level Otsu, §05). Anti-aliased and blurred edges genuinely are "partly", and no threshold handles them; that is what soft segmentation and matting are for.
Separable in valueThe two classes occupy different, barely overlapping ranges of intensity.If the ranges overlap, no threshold is good — the best one still misclassifies, and §06 quantifies exactly how much. Change the feature, not the threshold: texture energy, colour distance, gradient magnitude.
Uniform illuminationThe same material has the same value everywhere in the frame.The most common failure by far. A shading gradient shifts one class's range across the image until it crosses the other's. §07 is the fix.
Spatial structure is irrelevantA pixel's own value is sufficient evidence.Noise breaks this instantly: an isolated bright speck in the background is indistinguishable from an object pixel. The repair is not a better threshold but a post-pass — smooth before, or filter components by size after (§08).

Choosing t: five families, in rough order of how much they assume

  • Fixed / manual. Somebody typed a number. Perfectly respectable when the imaging is controlled — a backlit part on a conveyor, a document scanner — and indefensible otherwise.
  • Percentile. "The object is 12% of the frame", so put t at the 88th percentile of the histogram. Uses prior knowledge of size rather than of brightness, which is sometimes the thing you actually know.
  • Valley seeking. Smooth the histogram and put t at the minimum between the two largest peaks. Intuitive, and fragile: the answer depends entirely on how much you smoothed, and a histogram with no valley returns nonsense without complaining.
  • Variance / discriminant criteria. Score every candidate t by how well it separates the two induced classes, and take the best. This is Otsu's method, §05, and it is the default for good reasons.
  • Entropy and model-fitting criteria. Maximise the sum of the entropies of the two classes, or fit a two-Gaussian mixture and put t where the posteriors cross. The mixture route is the only one that is Bayes-optimal when its model is right — and §06 shows Otsu landing very close to it anyway.
Thresholding is one-dimensional clustering, and it is worth saying so out loud. The histogram is a weighted set of points on a line; a threshold is a partition of that line into two intervals. Everything Clustering & k-Means says about choosing k, about clusters of unequal size, and about criteria that reward compactness applies verbatim — with the enormous simplification that in one dimension with two clusters you can afford to enumerate every candidate and take the exact optimum. §05 shows that Otsu is precisely that exhaustive enumeration, and that what it finds is the exact global optimum of 2-means on the grey levels.

05 Otsu's method, derived — and why two opposite-sounding criteria are the same criterion

Otsu's method is usually quoted as "maximise the between-class variance" and sometimes as "minimise the within-class variance". Both are correct, they are not two methods, and the reason is a one-line identity worth deriving rather than believing.

Setup and notation

Let the image have L grey levels and let p(l) = n_l / N be the normalised histogram, so ∑ p(l) = 1. A threshold t splits the levels into class C₁ = {0, …, t} and class C₂ = {t+1, …, L−1}. Everything below is a function of t and of the histogram alone — no pixel positions are ever consulted.

class weights, means and variancesP₁(t) = ∑_{l=0}^{t} p(l) P₂(t) = ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} p(l) = 1 − P₁(t) μ₁(t) = (1/P₁) ∑_{l=0}^{t} l·p(l) μ₂(t) = (1/P₂) ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} l·p(l) σ₁²(t) = (1/P₁) ∑_{l=0}^{t} (l − μ₁)² p(l) σ₂²(t) = (1/P₂) ∑_{l=t+1}^{L−1} (l − μ₂)² p(l) μ_G = ∑_{l} l·p(l) σ_T² = ∑_{l} (l − μ_G)² p(l)

μ_G and σ_T² are the global mean and variance of the whole histogram. Neither depends on t. Hold on to that; it is the entire argument.

The two candidate criteria

within-class (intra-class) variance — the weighted average of the two spreadsσ_W²(t) = P₁(t)·σ₁²(t) + P₂(t)·σ₂²(t) between-class (inter-class) variance — the spread of the two means about the global meanσ_B²(t) = P₁(t)·(μ₁(t) − μ_G)² + P₂(t)·(μ₂(t) − μ_G)²

A good threshold should make each class tight — small σ_W² — and the two classes far apart — large σ_B². Those sound like two different demands. They are one demand.

The decomposition, in full

Start from the total variance and split the sum at t, adding and subtracting the relevant class mean inside each piece.

step 1 — split and insert the class meansσ_T² = ∑_{l≤t} (l − μ₁ + μ₁ − μ_G)² p(l) + ∑_{l>t} (l − μ₂ + μ₂ − μ_G)² p(l) step 2 — expand each square into three terms = ∑_{l≤t} [ (l−μ₁)² + 2(l−μ₁)(μ₁−μ_G) + (μ₁−μ_G)² ] p(l) + ∑_{l>t} [ … same with μ₂ … ] step 3 — the cross terms are exactly zero, because μ₁ is the mean of class 1∑_{l≤t} (l − μ₁) p(l) = P₁μ₁ − μ₁P₁ = 0 and likewise for class 2 step 4 — what is leftσ_T² = [ P₁σ₁² + P₂σ₂² ] + [ P₁(μ₁−μ_G)² + P₂(μ₂−μ_G)² ] = σ_W²(t) + σ_B²(t)
The identity, and the conclusion. σ_W²(t) + σ_B²(t) = σ_T² for every t, and σ_T² does not depend on t. So as t sweeps the grey levels, whatever σ_B² gains, σ_W² loses, exactly. Maximising the between-class variance and minimising the within-class variance are the same optimisation, and so is maximising the ratio σ_B²/σ_W² = σ_B²/(σ_T² − σ_B²), which is monotone increasing in σ_B². Three criteria, one argmax. This is the analysis-of-variance decomposition, and it is the same identity that underlies Fisher's discriminant.

Otsu maximises σ_B² rather than minimising σ_W² for a purely computational reason: σ_B² can be evaluated in constant time per candidate from two running sums, whereas σ_W² naively needs the class variances. Simplify σ_B² using μ_G = P₁μ₁ + P₂μ₂ and P₂ = 1 − P₁:

the two useful closed formsμ₁ − μ_G = P₂(μ₁ − μ₂) μ₂ − μ_G = P₁(μ₂ − μ₁) ⇒ σ_B²(t) = P₁P₂²(μ₁−μ₂)² + P₂P₁²(μ₁−μ₂)² = P₁P₂(P₁+P₂)(μ₁−μ₂)² ⇒ σ_B²(t) = P₁(t)·P₂(t)·(μ₁(t) − μ₂(t))² ← the interpretable form σ_B²(t) = (μ_G·ω(t) − m(t))² / (ω(t)·(1 − ω(t))) ← the computable form where ω(t) = ∑_{l≤t} p(l) and m(t) = ∑_{l≤t} l·p(l) (the unnormalised first moment)

The second form is what an implementation runs: sweep t from 0 upward, keep the running sums ω and m, evaluate one expression, remember the best. Cost: one pass to build the histogram, one pass of length L over it. For 8-bit images that is 256 evaluations regardless of image size, which is why Otsu is essentially free.

The interpretable form σ_B² = P₁P₂(μ₁−μ₂)² says something the "between-class variance" name hides: the criterion is the squared gap between the class means, weighted by how balanced the split is. The factor P₁P₂ is maximised at a 50/50 split, so Otsu quietly prefers balanced classes — which is exactly the assumption that breaks when the object occupies 2% of the frame. §06 has the counterexample.

Otsu is exact 2-means on the grey levels — not an approximation to it. The quantity σ_W² = P₁σ₁² + P₂σ₂² is, up to the factor N, the k-means objective: the total within-cluster sum of squares. Minimising it over thresholds only ever considers partitions into two intervals. That loses nothing, because in one dimension a nearest-centroid assignment always is an interval partition — the boundary is the midpoint of the two centroids. Enumerating the L−1 thresholds therefore enumerates every partition k-means could possibly converge to. On the worked histogram, brute force over all 65 534 non-trivial two-way partitions of the sixteen levels gives a minimum within-cluster sum of squares of 2.270753, attained by the mask 11111111 00000000 — precisely the interval split at t = 7, and precisely σ_W²(7). So on a one-dimensional histogram, unlike everywhere else, k-means has no local-minimum problem and no initialisation to get wrong. See Clustering & k-Means for what happens the moment you add a second dimension.

Multi-level Otsu

Nothing in the derivation is specific to two classes. With K classes cut by thresholds t₁ < … < t_{K−1}, the between-class variance generalises to σ_B² = ∑_k P_k(μ_k − μ_G)², the identity σ_T² = σ_W² + σ_B² still holds, and the optimum is still the argmax. What does not generalise is the cost: exhaustive search is O(L^{K−1}), so 8-bit three-class Otsu is 32 640 evaluations — fine — and five-class is 8.6 billion, which is not. Dynamic programming over the cumulative moments reduces this to O(KL²), which is the standard implementation. A cheaper and more common hack is recursive Otsu: threshold, then re-threshold the larger class. It is not the same answer as joint multi-level Otsu, and it is not optimal, but it is one line.

Whether it means anything is §06.

06 When is a histogram bimodal enough to bother — and what Otsu does when it is not

Otsu's method always returns a threshold. It returns one for a picture of a blank wall. The criterion has a maximum for any histogram whatsoever, because it is zero at both ends and positive in between, so "it found a threshold" carries no information at all about whether a threshold was the right idea.

The exact condition, for the model everyone has in mind

Take the standard mental picture: object and background are both Gaussian in intensity, with the same spread σ, means separated by d, and equal areas. Is the resulting histogram bimodal? There is a clean answer.

Put the two means at ±a with a = d/2, and set σ = 1 without loss of generality. The mixture, up to a constant, is f(x) = e^{−(x−a)²/2} + e^{−(x+a)²/2}. By symmetry x = 0 is always a critical point; the whole question is whether it is a peak or a valley.

second derivative of the symmetric two-Gaussian mixturef″(x) = [(x−a)² − 1]·e^{−(x−a)²/2} + [(x+a)² − 1]·e^{−(x+a)²/2} f″(0) = (a² − 1)e^{−a²/2} + (a² − 1)e^{−a²/2} = 2(a² − 1)·e^{−a²/2} so the sign of f″(0) is the sign of a² − 1a < 1 ⇒ f″(0) < 0 ⇒ x = 0 is a maximum ⇒ unimodal a > 1 ⇒ f″(0) > 0 ⇒ x = 0 is a minimum ⇒ bimodal, with two peaks either side in the original units, with a = d/2σthe histogram is bimodal ⟺ d > 2σ
Two standard deviations, exactly. Equal-weight, equal-variance Gaussian classes produce a visibly two-humped histogram if and only if their means are more than 2σ apart. Below that the two humps have already fused into one, even though the classes are perfectly well defined and a threshold still separates them tolerably. So "the histogram has no valley" is not the same statement as "thresholding will not work" — the valley disappears long before the threshold becomes useless. At d = 2σ exactly, the best achievable error is already down to about 16%, and at d = 1.5σ, where the histogram is a single smooth hump with no hint of structure, the best possible threshold still gets 77% of pixels right.

Unequal class sizes make it much worse. The minority class has to be pushed considerably further out before it raises a bump of its own on the flank of the majority hump. Measured on the figure's own grid:

The failure mode that matters: P₁P₂ wants a balanced split

Recall §05's interpretable form, σ_B² = P₁P₂(μ₁ − μ₂)². The factor P₁P₂ peaks at P₁ = P₂ = ½, so among thresholds with similar mean separation, Otsu prefers the one that halves the image. When the object really is about half the frame this bias costs nothing. When the object is 5% of the frame it is catastrophic: Otsu drags the threshold deep into the background hump to fabricate a more balanced split.

The claim to be careful about. "Otsu is optimal." It is optimal for its own criterion, and its criterion is not the error rate. With a 5% object separated by a generous 4σ — a histogram that is genuinely bimodal — Otsu picks a threshold with a 26.8% misclassification rate where the Bayes-optimal threshold achieves 0.8%. That is a factor of 33, on an easy image, with the histogram doing exactly what the textbook says it should. The fix is not a better search; the search was already exact. The fix is a different criterion — fit a two-component mixture and threshold where the posteriors cross, or use a method that knows the class prior.

What to do when the histogram is unimodal

  • Check whether it should be. A unimodal histogram from a scene that visibly contains two things means the feature is wrong, not the threshold. Intensity is often the wrong feature: try local gradient magnitude, a texture energy, a colour-opponent channel, or the distance from a fitted background model.
  • Use a unimodal-specific rule. The best known takes the line from the histogram's peak to its far tail end and puts the threshold at the point of the histogram furthest from that line — a corner-finding rule rather than a valley-finding one. It has no valley to depend on and degrades gracefully.
  • Use the prior. If you know the object covers roughly π of the frame, a percentile threshold at 1 − π is often dramatically better than Otsu, precisely because it supplies the information P₁P₂ is guessing at.
  • Do not trust the separability number as a bimodality test. The normalised criterion η = σ_B²(t*)/σ_T² ∈ [0, 1] is often quoted as a quality score. It measures how much of the variance the split explains, which is not the same thing. The disastrous 5%-object case above scores η = 0.548; a balanced pair separated by only 1.5σ, where Otsu is exactly Bayes-optimal and cannot be improved, scores a higher 0.656. η ranks them backwards on the question you care about.
Two Gaussians, a slider for how far apart and a slider for how big the object is the default histogram has one hump — and Otsu answers anyway, 3× worse than Bayes

  • Set the object fraction to 0.05 and the separation to 4. The histogram is bimodal, the little object hump is clearly visible, and Otsu's line sits nowhere near it. This is the case worth remembering: a visibly bimodal histogram is not a guarantee that Otsu will find the valley, because Otsu is not looking for the valley.
  • Watch the "fraction labelled object" row. Otsu's answer stays stubbornly near a half of the frame long after the true fraction has dropped to a few percent. That row is the P₁P₂ factor made visible, and it is the single best diagnostic for "Otsu is the wrong tool here".
  • Slide the separation below 2 with balanced classes. Otsu and Bayes coincide exactly, and both still have a large error. That is the honest picture of a hard problem: the method is optimal and the answer is still bad, because the classes genuinely overlap. No threshold on this feature will do better, and looking for one is wasted effort.

07 Adaptive thresholding: when one number cannot cover the frame

The most common reason a global threshold fails has nothing to do with the algorithm that chose it. It is that the same material is not the same brightness in different parts of the picture, so no single number can separate the classes anywhere except locally.

Why illumination is fatal, in one equation

A pixel's value is, to a first approximation, the product of what the surface is and how much light fell on it:

the multiplicative image modelI(x, y) = R(x, y) · S(x, y) R = reflectance (the thing you want) S = shading (the thing you do not) taking logs turns the product into a sum, which is why this is called the homomorphic viewlog I = log R + log S and log S is low-frequency — it varies slowly across the frame

Segmenting means recovering a property of R. A global threshold on I asks whether R·S > t, which is R > t/S(x, y) — a threshold on reflectance that silently moves as you cross the image. If S varies by more than the contrast ratio between the two materials, the dark material in the bright corner becomes brighter than the light material in the dark corner, and the two classes are no longer separable by any constant.

The exact condition for a global threshold to survive. Let the two materials have reflectances R_o < R_b and let shading range over [S_min, S_max]. A constant threshold exists if and only if the two value ranges do not overlap: R_o·S_max < R_b·S_min, that is S_max/S_min < R_b/R_o. The permissible illumination variation is exactly the material contrast ratio. Ink at 90 on paper at 210 gives a ratio of 2.33, so a shading field that swings by more than 2.33× has already destroyed global thresholding, whatever criterion picked the threshold. Printed text under a desk lamp routinely swings by more than that across one page.

Three local rules, and what each is trying to fix

All of them replace the constant t with a field t(x, y) estimated from a window W of side 2w+1 centred on the pixel. Write μ_W and σ_W for the local mean and standard deviation.

mean minus offset — the simplest, and often enought(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) − C Niblack — scale the offset by the local contrastt(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) + k·σ_W(x, y), k ≈ −0.2 for dark objects Sauvola — shrink the offset to nothing where the window is flatt(x, y) = μ_W(x, y) · [ 1 + k·( σ_W(x, y)/R − 1 ) ], k ≈ 0.2, R ≈ 128

Read the third one carefully, because the algebra is the whole idea. When the window has strong contrast, σ_W → R, the bracket → 1, and t → μ_W: it behaves like the plain mean rule. When the window is flat — nothing in it but background and noise — σ_W → 0, the bracket → 1 − k, and t → 0.8·μ_W, a threshold well below everything present, so nothing fires. Niblack does the opposite: with σ_W → 0 its threshold collapses onto μ_W itself, and half the noise in an empty region falls below it.

The failure everyone hits first. Running a local rule and getting a snowstorm of speckle in every blank area. It is not a bug and no amount of tuning C removes it. A local rule has to invent a boundary inside every window, because it has no way to know the window contains only one class. Measured on the figure's own blank band with an 8-pixel half-window: the mean-minus-offset rule fires on 13.67% of blank pixels and Niblack on 25.69%, while Sauvola fires on 0.00% — because Sauvola is the one rule of the three that was designed to notice that the window is flat.

Choosing the window size: a two-sided constraint, and both sides bite

  • Too small and the window fits inside an object. Then μ_W is the object's own mean, the threshold tracks the object, and the interior of every thick stroke is labelled background — objects come out hollow. The window must be larger than the widest object feature.
  • Too large and the window spans the illumination change it was supposed to be immune to. In the limit w → ∞ the local rule is the global rule and inherits all its failures. The window must be smaller than the scale on which the shading varies.
  • If those two constraints do not leave a gap, no window size works, and that is a real possibility — a thick object under fast-varying light. The answer then is not a better window but the next paragraph.

The alternative that is often better: estimate the shading and divide it out

Rather than making the threshold vary, make the image stop varying. Estimate S — by a morphological opening with a structuring element larger than every object (the "rolling ball" background), by a heavy low-pass filter, or by fitting a low-order polynomial or plane to the image — then form I/Ŝ, or equivalently log I − log Ŝ, and threshold that globally. The advantage is that after correction you get a single, interpretable, image-wide threshold back, and every diagnostic from §05 and §06 becomes usable again. The morphology this needs belongs to Part 2.

08 From a binary map to regions: connected components, and the paradox in the grid

A threshold produces a binary image, not a segmentation. Nothing so far has grouped anything. The step that turns "these pixels are bright" into "these are three objects" is connected-component labelling, and it is where spatial information finally enters a thresholding pipeline.

The definition, and the choice hiding inside it

Two foreground pixels are in the same component when a path of foreground pixels joins them, each consecutive pair being neighbours. Everything turns on what "neighbour" means, and there are two conventions on a square grid.

4-connectivity — the von Neumann neighbourhoodN₄(x, y) = { (x±1, y), (x, y±1) } distance ‖·‖₁ = 1 8-connectivity — the Moore neighbourhoodN₈(x, y) = N₄(x, y) ∪ { (x±1, y±1) } distance ‖·‖_∞ = 1

This looks like a taste question. It is not: the two conventions give different numbers of objects, different numbers of holes, and — if you choose the same one for foreground and background — a topological contradiction.

The Jordan curve paradox, on eleven by eleven pixels

The Jordan curve theorem says a simple closed curve in the plane divides it into exactly two pieces, an inside and an outside, and the curve itself is connected. Draw a diagonal diamond ring of 16 pixels on an 11 × 11 grid and count.

ConventionForeground piecesBackground piecesVerdict
4 for both162The "curve" is 16 isolated dots and is not connected at all — yet it still separates inside from outside. Not a curve, but it does divide the plane.
8 for both11The contradiction. The ring is a single connected closed curve, and the inside and the outside are also connected — to each other, through the diagonal gaps. Jordan fails.
8 foreground, 4 background12Connected curve, two background pieces. Jordan holds. This is the standard choice, and the reason libraries default to 8-connectivity for objects.
4 foreground, 8 background161Also consistent, and appropriate when the objects are the light regions rather than the dark ones. The rule is simply that the two must differ.
The rule to remember. Foreground and background must use opposite connectivities. Using 8 for both makes thin diagonal structures both connect and fail to separate; using 4 for both shatters every diagonal line into dust. Which of the two mixed conventions you pick decides whether diagonal contact counts as touching, and that is a modelling decision about your objects, not a mathematical one. The alternative fix, used in some morphology systems, is to work on a hexagonal grid, where every neighbour is at the same distance and the question never arises.

The algorithm

Labelling is one pass with a union–find structure, and it is worth knowing because the same structure reappears in the graph-based merging of §11 and in the watershed of §12.

  1. Raster scan. For each foreground pixel, look at the already-visited neighbours — for 8-connectivity that is the pixel to the left and the three above. If none is labelled, mint a new label. If one is, copy it.
  2. Record collisions. If two visited neighbours carry different labels, both are correct and the labels are equivalent: union(a, b). This is a U-shaped object being met from both arms before the join is seen.
  3. Second pass. Replace every label by find(label), and renumber consecutively.

Cost is O(N·α(N)) where α is the inverse Ackermann function — constant for any conceivable image. The flood-fill alternative (one depth- or breadth-first search per component) is O(N) and simpler to write, but touches the image in scattered order and is slower in practice on large images for cache reasons.

What you get once you have components

  • Area — the pixel count. The single most useful filter there is: drop everything below a minimum area and most threshold noise disappears.
  • Perimeter — the count of boundary edges, or a chain-code length. Beware: the naive count over-estimates diagonal boundaries by up to √2, so a "circularity" computed from it is biased.
  • Bounding box, centroid, second moments — the centroid (x̄, ȳ) and the covariance of the pixel coordinates give an equivalent ellipse, its orientation and its elongation, all in one pass.
  • Euler number E = C − H, components minus holes. Cheap, and a genuinely topological descriptor — but it depends on the connectivity convention exactly as the counts above do, so it must be quoted together with the convention.
  • Compactness 4πA/P², which is 1 for a circle and small for a ragged shape. Useful for rejecting noise clusters, and sensitive to the perimeter bias just mentioned.
Two connectivities, one diamond, and a topological contradiction you can count set both menus to 8 and read the background count — the inside and the outside are the same region
  • Set both menus to 4. Sixteen foreground components — the ring is dust — but still two background components. A set can separate the plane without being connected.
  • Set both menus to 8. One foreground component and one background component. The interior and the exterior have merged through the diagonal gaps between ring pixels. There is now no meaningful notion of "inside this object", and any hole-filling or Euler-number code built on this convention pair returns nonsense.
  • Set foreground 8, background 4. One curve, two background pieces, one hole, Euler number 1 − 1 = 0 — an annulus, which is what a ring is. This is the only pair of the four that gets the topology of a ring right while keeping the ring connected.
  • On the right, push the minimum-area slider. The component count falls off a cliff: the salt noise is nearly all components of one to three pixels, and the blobs are hundreds. That gap is why area filtering works, and the width of the gap is a decent measure of how well the threshold did.
