Stereo & Structure from Motion

Part 1 wrote down a camera and threw depth away. The six parts since have been about pixels, filters, frequencies, features, regions and 2-D motion — all of them living inside a single image plane. This part gets the third dimension back. Two views of one rigid scene are not independent: the two rays through a matched pair must meet, and that single geometric fact — coplanarity — collapses a two-dimensional correspondence search into a one-dimensional one, produces a 3 × 3 matrix that encodes the entire relative pose of the pair, and lets a point be placed in space. Everything else here is a consequence: the essential and fundamental matrices, triangulation, rectification, dense stereo, and finally the joint refinement of thousands of cameras and millions of points that is bundle adjustment. Part 1 invented the numbers. This part recovers them.

01 Two views, one rigid world

A single image is consistent with infinitely many scenes. A second image, taken from a different place, is not — provided you know, or can work out, where that second place was. This part is the whole of that "provided".

two images→features & matches→fundamental matrix F→essential matrix E→relative pose (R, t)→triangulate→rectify→dense disparity→depth→more views→bundle adjustment→a metric 3-D model

The chain has one hinge in it, and everything turns on that hinge. It is §02: a point in one image does not correspond to a point in the other, it corresponds to a line. The line is the image of the ray that produced the point, and because a ray is one-dimensional its image is one-dimensional. That is the difference between searching a 640 × 480 image for a match — 307 200 candidates — and searching a single row of 640 pixels, or in practice a 64-pixel window of one row. It is a reduction of about four orders of magnitude, it is exact rather than heuristic, and it is the reason stereo works at all.

what two views give you
Shape, up to a scale
Relative pose and 3-D structure, but only up to one global scale factor. No amount of imagery fixes the size of the world — a doll's house photographed close up and a real house photographed far away produce identical image sequences. A known baseline, a known object, or a metric sensor is the only fix. §29.
what they cost you
A correspondence problem
All of this rests on knowing which pixel in image 1 is which pixel in image 0. That is Part 4's problem for sparse points and §19–§24's problem for every pixel, and it is by a wide margin the least reliable link in the chain.
what breaks it
Degenerate motion
If the camera only rotates, or the scene is a single plane, or the baseline is tiny, the geometry below is not merely inaccurate — it is undetermined, with a whole family of matrices fitting the data exactly. §11 measures the collapse.
the honest optimum
Reprojection error
Every linear method on this page is a trick for getting close enough to start. The quantity that actually deserves minimising is the sum of squared distances between predicted and measured image points, and minimising it is §27.

The notes, in 5 parts

The hub sets up two views of one rigid world and closes with what learning changed and the practice that ends the series. The parts follow the geometry from constraint to reconstruction. Part 1 derives the epipolar constraint and its matrices. Part 2 estimates them from noisy, contaminated correspondences. Part 3 recovers the relative pose and triangulates points. Part 4 rectifies the pair and matches densely for disparity. Part 5 scales up to many views, with factorization, bundle adjustment and drift.

32 What learning changed, and what it left exactly as it was

The short version: the matching became learned and the geometry did not. Every equation on this page is still true, still used, and still the thing the learned components are plugged into.

Unchanged, and not by accident

still exactly true
The epipolar constraint
x̂₁ᵀ[t]ₓRx̂₀ = 0 is a statement about three coplanar vectors. No amount of data changes it, and every learned matcher is evaluated against it.
still exactly true
Z = fB/d and its Z² error law
§18 is arithmetic. A learned stereo network that produces disparity inherits the law unchanged, including the range limit it implies.
still exactly true
Gauge freedom and the degeneracies
Scale is still unrecoverable from images. Pure rotation still determines no baseline. A planar scene still leaves a three-parameter family of fundamental matrices. These are properties of the problem, not of the estimator.
still the final step
Bundle adjustment
Every serious modern pipeline, learned front end or not, ends with §27. Nothing has replaced minimising reprojection error, because nothing else is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Replaced, and by how much

StageWhat replaced itWhat actually changed
Sparse matching (§11's input)Learned detectors and descriptors, and — the bigger change — learned matchers that reason jointly about all the correspondences at once with attention, rather than comparing descriptors independently and then applying a ratio test.The largest practical improvement in this whole pipeline. Wide-baseline and low-texture pairs that produced 20% inliers now produce 70%, and by §10's formula that turns 21 000 RANSAC iterations into 54. Part 4 §29 owns this; the consequence for geometry is that RANSAC matters less than it did.
Dense stereo (§19–§23)Networks that build a cost volume from learned features and aggregate it with learned 3-D convolutions, then regress a sub-pixel disparity by a soft argmin.The cost volume survived. It is still C(x, y, d), still built by shifting one feature map against another, still aggregated. What was replaced is the hand-designed matching cost of §19 and the hand-designed aggregation of §21 and §22. Textureless and repeated regions improved most, because the network's prior is stronger than a smoothness term.
Monocular depthA network that predicts depth from one image.This is not stereo and it is not geometry. A single image does not determine depth (Part 1's first loss), so the network is not inverting a projection — it is applying a learned prior about object sizes, perspective cues and texture gradients. Consequences: the output is ambiguous up to scale, and often up to an affine transform of inverse depth; it does not obey the Z² law because nothing is being triangulated; and it fails on scenes unlike its training data in ways that have no geometric signature. It is enormously useful and it is a different quantity.
Relative pose (§07–§13)Networks that regress (R, t) directly from an image pair.Mostly a dead end, and instructively so. Direct pose regression is consistently beaten by a learned matcher followed by the classical five-point algorithm and bundle adjustment. The geometry is a better inductive bias than anything a network learns from scratch, so the winning architecture is learned correspondence plus exact geometry, not learned geometry.
Multi-view stereo & surface reconstructionLearned plane-sweep volumes; then neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting for the representation.These replace the representation of the scene and the way it is rendered. They still consume camera poses, and those poses still come from §25–§28. When a radiance-field paper says "we assume known camera poses", the assumption is this page.
The pattern is worth naming, because it recurs. Learning replaced the parts of the pipeline that were heuristic approximations to an unknown function — what does a corner look like, how similar are two patches, how should cost be smoothed — and left untouched the parts that were exact consequences of known physics and geometry. That is not a coincidence and it is not temporary. A network can learn to recognise a correspondence far better than a hand-written descriptor can, because "these two patches show the same surface" is a statement about the world that has no closed form. It cannot learn the epipolar constraint better than the epipolar constraint, because the epipolar constraint is already exactly right.

33 Practice — and the end of the series

First the things worth being able to do from memory, then the honest accounting of where seven parts have got to and where they stop.

Derivations to be able to reproduce, unaided

  • The epipolar constraint twice: once as the coplanarity of three vectors, once by eliminating the two unknown depths from d₁x̂₁ = R(d₀x̂₀) + t.
  • Why E = [t]ₓR maps a point to a line, and why that forces it to be singular.
  • F = K₁⁻ᵀEK₀⁻¹, with the transposes in the right places, and the inverse E = K₁ᵀFK₀.
  • Rank 2 from rank[t]ₓ = 2; and the two equal singular values of E from EEᵀ = ‖t‖²I − ttᵀ.
  • The degree-of-freedom counts: 5 for E, 7 for F, and hence the minimum sample sizes.
  • The design row of the eight-point algorithm, written out, and why its columns span five decades in pixel coordinates.
  • Why "solve Af = 0 subject to ‖f‖ = 1" means "smallest right singular vector", and where else on this page that same step appears — five other places.
  • The rank-2 projection U diag(σ₁, σ₂, 0)Vᵀ, why it is optimal, and why the essential-matrix version averages the first two instead.
  • SR = UZUᵀ·UWVᵀ = U(ZW)Vᵀ, and why t̂ is the last column of U.
  • The mid-point normal equations from rⱼ² = ‖(I − v̂ⱼv̂ⱼᵀ)(p − cⱼ)‖².
  • The two DLT rows per view, u p₂ᵀ − p₀ᵀ, and why the residual they minimise is depth-weighted.
  • Z = fB/d from two similar triangles, and σ_Z = Z²σ_d/(fB) by differentiating it — and the more useful form σ_Z/Z = σ_d/d.
  • The parabola vertex δ = (C₋₁ − C₊₁)/(2(C₋₁ − 2C₀ + C₊₁)), and Var(δ) = 2σ_I²/∑I_x².
  • Why the affine measurement matrix has rank 3, and what the metric constraints on L = QQᵀ are.
  • The Schur complement A′_cc = A_cc − A_pcᵀA_pp⁻¹A_pc, and why A_pp⁻¹ is free.
  • Where the seven in gauge freedom comes from, and why it is seven and not six.

Ten claims that are nearly right

"The fundamental matrix has rank 2." A true one does. An estimated one has rank 3 and the constraint must be imposed, which changes it. §06, §09.

"Lots of RANSAC inliers means the geometry is right." On a plane, every match is an inlier of a three-parameter family of wrong answers. §11.

"Optimal triangulation gives the most accurate 3-D point." It minimises reprojection error, which at long range is not 3-D error — measured, a factor of seventeen the other way. §16.

"Enforcing rank 2 improves the estimate." It makes the fit about 5% worse. It is still right, because only then is the estimate a fundamental matrix at all. §09.

"Rectification is an approximation." It is exact to 10⁻¹³ px. What is approximate is the resampling. And it does not exist at all for forward motion. §17.

"A bigger baseline is better." Better for depth precision, worse for matching — more occlusion, more foreshortening, a longer search. §18, §24.

"Census is the robust matching cost, so use it." It is the worst of the four on clean textured data, at 29% against SAD's 15%, and much the worst in low-contrast regions, because it thresholds noise. Use it when the images differ radiometrically. §19.

"More SGM directions is always better." Measured: 2 directions beat 8 on one small scene. What eight buys is isotropy, which a bad-pixel count does not see. §22.

"Sub-pixel refinement is unbiased." On near-Nyquist texture the bias swings 0.087 px peak to peak towards the nearest integer, and at high noise the clamp adds a bias of −0.17 px. §23.

"Monocular depth networks solve stereo." They estimate a different quantity by a different mechanism — a prior, not a triangulation — and do not obey the Z² law because nothing is being triangulated. §32.

When a two-view reconstruction is wrong, check these in order

  1. Are you feeding pixels to E, or rays to F? The residual will be off by about f². §04.
  2. Did you normalise before the eight-point solve? §08.
  3. Did you enforce rank 2? Are the epipolar lines actually concurrent? §09.
  4. Is the scene planar, or the motion a pure rotation, or the baseline tiny? Check σ₇/σ₈ of the design matrix and fit a homography for comparison. §11.
  5. Did you pick the pose by cheirality over all the points, or over one? And are your points far enough away for the vote to be noise? §13.
  6. Is lens distortion removed? A distorted image has curved epipolar lines and nothing above applies. Part 1 §17.
  7. Is the principal point where you think it is? A cropped image has moved it, and self-calibration assumes it has not. §30.
  8. Did you finish with bundle adjustment? Is its converged cost near σ²(m − p)? Far above means outliers or a wrong camera model; far below means over-parameterisation. §27.

Seven parts, one arrow, reversed

This series had a single shape, and it is worth stating now that it is finished. Part 1 wrote down a forward model: a point in a room, a rigid transformation, a division by depth, a lens, a surface, a sensor, three eight-bit numbers. It estimated nothing. Its whole purpose was to be precise about what a camera does, so that the rest of the series had something exact to invert.

1 a camera, defined→2 operations on pixels→3 the same operations, in frequency→4 points worth matching→5 regions worth grouping→6 motion in the plane→7 structure in space

Each part after the first inverted one more stage of that model, and the inversions got harder in a specific way — they became less local. Part 2 and Part 3 operate on a neighbourhood: a filter output depends on a few dozen pixels, and the Fourier view of Part 3 is the same operation seen through a change of basis. Part 4 widens the neighbourhood to a patch and asks a question that a single pixel cannot answer — is this location distinctive? Part 5 widens it to a region and asks a question that no patch can answer — where does this object end? Part 6 couples two entire images through a handful of shared parameters. And this part couples every image to every other through a shared 3-D world, which is why its final act is one optimisation over every camera and every point at once.

The through-line, then: Part 1 threw depth away in one line of algebra, and Part 7 spent thirty-three sections getting it back — and could only do it by adding a second viewpoint, because the information genuinely was not there. Everything between was about making the correspondences reliable enough for that second viewpoint to be useful. The series is, in the end, one long argument that geometry is easy and matching is hard.

What the series does not cover, said plainly

  • Learned depth estimation as a field. §32 says what monocular and learned stereo networks change and what they do not, in two paragraphs. It is not a survey. The architectures, the training objectives, the scale-and-shift-invariant losses, the synthetic-data pipelines and the benchmarks are a subject of their own and this series does not enter it.
  • SLAM as a systems problem. §31 covers drift, pose graphs and loop closure as geometry. Real simultaneous localisation and mapping is an engineering discipline: keyframe selection, map management, relocalisation after tracking loss, inertial fusion, real-time budgets, marginalisation and the sliding window. None of that is here.
  • Multi-view stereo and surface reconstruction. This page stops at a sparse point cloud and a two-view disparity map. Turning many views into a dense, watertight, textured surface — patch-based MVS, depth-map fusion, volumetric integration, Poisson reconstruction, meshing, texture blending — is a whole chapter that begins where §28 ends.
  • Non-rigid structure from motion. Every line of this page assumed the scene was rigid. Deformable objects, articulated bodies and dynamic scenes break the assumption at its root: the rank-3 argument of §26 becomes a rank-3K argument for a K-basis deformation model, the epipolar constraint stops holding for moving points, and the whole problem becomes under-determined in a way that only strong priors resolve.
  • Radiance fields and modern scene representations. They consume the poses this page produces and replace what happens next. Out of scope, and pointed at rather than covered.

Where to go from here

  • The learned half of vision. Convolutional Networks — whose first-layer filters are Part 2's oriented derivatives and whose stride-and-pool hierarchy is Part 4's scale space with the scale axis quantised by architecture; its dense-prediction section is where §19–§23's cost volume reappears as a learned one. Then Vision Transformers, whose patchification is a stride-16 convolution and whose attention is the learned matcher that §32 says changed correspondence more than anything else in twenty years.
  • The linear algebra this page stands on. Singular Value Decomposition is the single most load-bearing page in the whole series for this part: the eight-point algorithm, the rank-2 projection, linear triangulation, factorization and the epipoles are all one theorem applied five times. Eigendecomposition is behind it and behind Part 4's structure tensor. Projections is §14's (I − v̂v̂ᵀ) exactly. Quadratic forms & PCA is §26's factorization with a different name on it, and Systems, Inverses & Determinants is the Schur complement of §28 in its natural home.
  • The optimisation. Optimization §08 for Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt and why curvature is worth its cost — §27 is that page's methods applied to a problem with a hundred thousand variables and a very particular sparsity.
  • The statistics. Statistics for what a covariance means, why σ²(m − p) is the expected residual, and what a confidence interval on a reconstructed point would have to assume.
Where this goes sideways. Into convolutional networks, where §20's cost volume is rebuilt from learned features and aggregated by learned 3-D convolutions, and where §19's four hand-designed costs become one learned one; into vision transformers, whose attention over two images is precisely the joint matcher that §11's RANSAC was invented to survive the absence of; into the singular value decomposition, which is not a tool this page uses but the single theorem it is mostly made of; into optimization, where §27 is a large sparse non-linear least-squares problem and §28 is what makes it tractable; into calculus, for the Jacobian chain of §27 and the differentiation of Z = fB/d that produced the entire range limit of stereo; into model evaluation, because §19 to §24 are an extended argument that an aggregate benchmark number hides the phenomenon and the per-region breakdown is the real result; into Part 5, whose segments are the support regions of §21's best local aggregation; and into Part 4's RANSAC, which this page consumed twice and could not have been written without.
One last thing, and it is the thing this part is really about. Every method here has been a way of choosing between explanations of the same measurements — four poses that all satisfy the same essential matrix, three families of fundamental matrices that all fit a planar scene, two triangulated points that both explain the rays, a disparity that a flat wall cannot decide. In every case the resolution came from outside the algebra: light travels forwards, so points are in front of cameras. Surfaces are mostly smooth, so disparity is mostly smooth. The world is rigid, so a bundle of rays must meet. Geometry tells you what is possible; it never tells you which possibility is real. Something else always has to, and knowing exactly what that something is — and what it costs you when it is false — is the whole of doing this well.
