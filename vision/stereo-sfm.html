Stereo & Structure from Motion

Part 1 wrote down a camera and threw depth away. The six parts since have been about pixels, filters, frequencies, features, regions and 2-D motion — all of them living inside a single image plane. This part gets the third dimension back. Two views of one rigid scene are not independent: the two rays through a matched pair must meet, and that single geometric fact — coplanarity — collapses a two-dimensional correspondence search into a one-dimensional one, produces a 3 × 3 matrix that encodes the entire relative pose of the pair, and lets a point be placed in space. Everything else here is a consequence: the essential and fundamental matrices, triangulation, rectification, dense stereo, and finally the joint refinement of thousands of cameras and millions of points that is bundle adjustment. Part 1 invented the numbers. This part recovers them.

01 Two views, one rigid world

A single image is consistent with infinitely many scenes. A second image, taken from a different place, is not — provided you know, or can work out, where that second place was. This part is the whole of that "provided".

two images→features & matches→fundamental matrix F→essential matrix E→relative pose (R, t)→triangulate→rectify→dense disparity→depth→more views→bundle adjustment→a metric 3-D model

The chain has one hinge in it, and everything turns on that hinge. It is §02: a point in one image does not correspond to a point in the other, it corresponds to a line. The line is the image of the ray that produced the point, and because a ray is one-dimensional its image is one-dimensional. That is the difference between searching a 640 × 480 image for a match — 307 200 candidates — and searching a single row of 640 pixels, or in practice a 64-pixel window of one row. It is a reduction of about four orders of magnitude, it is exact rather than heuristic, and it is the reason stereo works at all.

02 Epipolar geometry: why the search is one-dimensional

Three points — the two camera centres and the world point — define a plane. Both images of the world point lie on that plane's intersection with their own image plane. Nothing else on this page is as important as that sentence.

The construction, in order

Fix two cameras with centres c₀ and c₁, and a scene point p visible in both. Five objects follow immediately, and each has a name because each does work later.

ObjectDefinitionWhat it is for
BaselineThe line through c₀ and c₁. Its direction is t up to sign and scale.The axis the whole construction rotates about. If its length is zero there is no epipolar geometry at all (§11).
Epipolar plane πThe plane through c₀, c₁ and p.One per scene point. As p moves, π rotates about the baseline — the planes form a pencil hinged on the baseline.
Epipolar lines l₀, l₁π ∩ image plane 0 and π ∩ image plane 1.x₀ lies on l₀ and x₁ on l₁, by construction. This is the 2-D → 1-D collapse.
Epipoles e₀, e₁The image of the other camera centre: e₀ is where c₁ projects in image 0, e₁ is where c₀ projects in image 1.Every epipolar line in image j passes through e_j, because the baseline lies in every epipolar plane. §03.
Epipolar constraintThe algebraic statement that c₀, c₁, p are coplanar.One scalar equation per correspondence — the equation that E and F encode. §04, §05.

The coplanarity, written out

Put camera 0 at the origin in canonical orientation, so the world frame is camera 0's frame; camera 1 is then at c₁ = −Rᵀt. Three vectors lie in the epipolar plane: the baseline c₁ − c₀, the ray from c₀ to p, and the ray from c₁ to p. Three coplanar vectors have zero scalar triple product, and that is the whole derivation.

the epipolar constraint as a triple productthe three vectors, all expressed in camera 0's frame: baseline b = c₁ − c₀ = −Rᵀt ray to p from c₀ r₀ = x̂₀ (any positive multiple of it) ray to p from c₁ r₁ = Rᵀx̂₁ (rotated into camera 0's frame) coplanar ⟺ det[b, r₀, r₁] = 0 ⟺ (Rᵀx̂₁) · (Rᵀt × x̂₀) = 0 and since Rᵀ is orthogonal it preserves both the dot and the cross product, (Rᵀx̂₁) · (Rᵀt × x̂₀) = (Rᵀx̂₁) · Rᵀ(t × Rx̂₀) = x̂₁ · (t × Rx̂₀) = x̂₁ᵀ[t]ₓRx̂₀ ⇒ x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0 with E = [t]ₓR

There is a second derivation, algebraically shorter and geometrically emptier, which is worth having because it shows where the cross product comes from rather than being pulled out of a hat. Write the rigid motion of the 3-D point between the two camera frames and then annihilate the translation.

the same thing, by eliminating the unknown depthsthe point, seen twice, at unknown depths d₀ and d₁ along its normalised rays: d₁ x̂₁ = R (d₀ x̂₀) + t the rigid motion cross both sides with t, which annihilates the t on the right: d₁ [t]ₓ x̂₁ = d₀ [t]ₓ R x̂₀ now dot both sides with x̂₁. The left side becomes x̂₁ᵀ[t]ₓx̂₁ = x̂₁·(t × x̂₁) = 0, because a triple product with a repeated entry vanishes. Hence d₀ x̂₁ᵀ ([t]ₓ R) x̂₀ = 0 and, for any point with d₀ ≠ 0, x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0.
Read the two derivations against each other. The first says the constraint is coplanarity, and would still be true if you had never heard of a matrix. The second says the constraint is what survives after the two unknown depths have been eliminated — which is why it is a necessary condition on a correspondence but not a sufficient one. A pair (x₀, x₁) satisfying x̂₁ᵀEx̂₀ = 0 is only guaranteed to have intersecting rays; whether they intersect in front of both cameras, or behind, or at infinity, is a further question and it is §13's.

The collapse, counted

Without the constraint, a match for x₀ could be any of the W × H pixels of image 1. With it, the candidates are the pixels of image 1 that lie on the line l₁ — at most √(W² + H²) of them, and in a rectified pair (§17) exactly the pixels of one row within the disparity range. For the running rig's 640 × 480 images:

the size of the search, before and afterunconstrained 2-D search: 640 × 480 = 307 200 candidates constrained to an epipolar line: ≤ √(640² + 480²) = 800 candidates rectified, one row: 640 candidates rectified, disparity range 0–63: 64 candidates reduction factor: 307 200 / 64 = 4 800 × and the reduction is EXACT, not a heuristic prune: for a rigid scene and correct calibration, the true match cannot lie off the line. Everything gained here is gained for free — but it is bought with the assumption of rigidity, which §24 and §32 will both spend time attacking.
The epipolar plane sweeping about the baseline, and the line it draws in the second image drag the point in image 0, or move it in depth — and watch what does not change
The one thing to take from that figure. Slide the depth. The point in image 1 moves; the line it moves along does not. That is the entire content of epipolar geometry: the line is a function of the point in image 0 and the relative pose, and of nothing else. Depth chooses a position on the line, and depth is exactly the unknown you are trying to recover. Slide the lateral position instead and the line rotates about the epipole — the epipole is the one point common to every epipolar line, because the baseline lies in every epipolar plane.

03 Epipoles, the pencil of lines, and where the epipole hides

Every epipolar plane contains the baseline. Therefore every epipolar line contains the image of the baseline's far end. That single point organises the whole family.

Why every epipolar line passes through the epipole

The baseline joins c₀ to c₁, and by definition it lies in every epipolar plane — the plane is spanned by the baseline and one scene ray. So the baseline's intersection with image plane 1 lies on every epipolar line of image 1. That intersection is the projection of c₀ into camera 1, which is the epipole e₁. The lines therefore form a pencil: a one-parameter family of lines through a common point, parameterised by the rotation angle of the epipolar plane about the baseline.

the epipoles, three equivalent definitionsgeometric e₀ = the image of c₁ in camera 0, e₁ = the image of c₀ in camera 1 projective e₀ = P₀ c̃₁ e₁ = P₁ c̃₀ algebraic F e₀ = 0 Fᵀ e₁ = 0 i.e. e₀ is the RIGHT null vector of F and e₁ is the LEFT null vector. The algebraic form is the one that gets used, because it needs no cameras — only F. It is also the reason F must be rank-deficient: it has to have a null vector at all.

The algebraic definition falls straight out of the pencil. Every line l₁ = Fx̄₀ passes through e₁, so e₁ᵀFx̄₀ = 0 for every x̄₀, so the row vector e₁ᵀF annihilates everything, so e₁ᵀF = 0ᵀ. The same argument transposed gives Fe₀ = 0. Two null vectors, one on each side — and a 3 × 3 matrix with a null vector on each side is exactly a matrix of rank at most 2. §06 makes that precise.

Where the epipole sits, and what that tells you about the motion

The epipole is the image of the direction of travel. That makes its position a direct read-out of what the camera did, and it is worth being able to recognise the three regimes on sight.

MotionEpipoleWhat the image looks like
Forward (translation along the optical axis)At or near the principal point, well inside the frame.Epipolar lines radiate from a point in the middle of the picture, and the apparent motion of every feature is outward from it. This point is the focus of expansion. It is what forward motion looks like, and it is why a hallway seen from a moving car appears to explode outward from the vanishing point ahead.
Sideways (translation in the image plane)At infinity, in the direction of travel.Epipolar lines are parallel. This is the configuration a rectified stereo pair is deliberately warped into (§17), because parallel horizontal epipolar lines are image rows and image rows are what memory is laid out in.
ObliqueOutside the frame, at a finite point.The general case, and the running rig's case: e₀ = (5320, 490) and e₁ = (2420, 414) pixels for a 640 × 480 image. The lines converge, but only well off-picture, so they look almost parallel inside the frame. This is the usual situation and it is why "nearly parallel" is not the same as "parallel".
Pure rotationUndefined.t = 0 makes E = [0]ₓR = 0 and F = 0. There is no epipolar geometry, because there is no baseline and no epipolar plane. The two images are related by a homography instead, and that is Part 6's object, not this page's. §11 shows the estimator collapsing when you ask it for an F anyway.
A distinction worth being pedantic about. The epipole is where the other camera centre projects. The focus of expansion is where the apparent motion field radiates from. They coincide when the rotation is zero, and only then. Under simultaneous rotation and translation the motion field is the sum of a rotational component — which depends only on the rotation and not at all on the scene — and a translational component which radiates from the epipole. Cancel the rotation and the epipole becomes the focus of expansion again; this is exactly the trick used when the rotation is known independently, and it reduces the estimation problem to finding one 2-D point rather than a whole matrix.

04 The essential matrix

One 3 × 3 matrix, five degrees of freedom, and it contains the entire relative pose of a calibrated pair — the rotation exactly and the translation up to length.

definitionE = [t]ₓ R and the constraint it enforces: x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0 where x̂ⱼ = Kⱼ⁻¹ x̄ⱼ is the NORMALISED ray, and (R, t) is the pose of camera 1 relative to camera 0: p₁ = R p₀ + t. E is defined only up to a non-zero scale, because the constraint is homogeneous — and the missing scale is exactly ‖t‖, the length of the baseline, which no amount of imagery can supply.

Reading the definition as a composition of two maps

It is worth not treating E as an opaque product. It is two operations applied in order, and the order is right-to-left as always.

what E does to a ray, one factor at a timex̂₀ a ray direction, in camera 0's frame R x̂₀ the same direction, re-expressed in camera 1's frame [t]ₓ (R x̂₀) = t × (R x̂₀) the NORMAL of the plane spanned by t and that ray — which is precisely the epipolar plane so l₁ = E x̂₀ is the epipolar plane's normal, written in camera 1's frame; and "x̂₁ lies in that plane" is the statement x̂₁ · l₁ = 0, i.e. x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0. The line l₁ = E x̂₀ is a LINE, not a point. E maps points to lines. That is why it cannot be a homography, cannot be inverted, and must be singular.

05 The fundamental matrix, and how to measure a violation of it

The same constraint, written in pixels instead of rays. It asks for no calibration, which is why it is the one you can actually estimate from two photographs of unknown provenance.

definition, by substitutionstart from the calibrated constraint and substitute x̂ⱼ = Kⱼ⁻¹x̄ⱼ: x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = (K₁⁻¹x̄₁)ᵀ E (K₀⁻¹x̄₀) = x̄₁ᵀ (K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹) x̄₀ = x̄₁ᵀ F x̄₀ = 0 F = K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹ = K₁⁻ᵀ [t]ₓ R K₀⁻¹ and, going the other way, if the calibrations ARE known: E = K₁ᵀ F K₀. Note the transposes. K₁ appears TRANSPOSED and INVERTED on the left, K₀ merely inverted on the right, and getting this wrong produces a matrix that is almost right — which is worse than one that is obviously wrong.

The constraint is x̄₁ᵀFx̄₀ = 0, so the obvious residual is the left-hand side itself. It is also, on its own, close to useless, because it is not measured in any unit anyone cares about — scale F by 10 and every residual scales by 10. Three progressively better answers:

06 Rank 2, and how many numbers there really are

Both matrices are singular, and that is not a numerical detail — it is the algebraic shadow of the fact that all the epipolar lines meet at a point.

The rank is exactly 2, and here is why

a three-line proof, for a true E and a true F[t]ₓ is skew-symmetric and 3 × 3. Its null space is spanned by t itself, because t × t = 0, and it contains nothing else when t ≠ 0. So rank[t]ₓ = 2 exactly. R is orthogonal, hence invertible, so multiplying by it changes no rank: rank(E) = rank([t]ₓR) = rank([t]ₓ) = 2. K₀ and K₁ are upper triangular with non-zero diagonal, hence invertible, so rank(F) = rank(K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹) = rank(E) = 2. A 3 × 3 matrix of rank 2 has a one-dimensional null space on each side. Those two null directions are the two epipoles, which is the algebra of §03 arriving from the other end. And rank 2 is equivalent to det F = 0, which is ONE polynomial equation in the entries — the constraint that costs F its eighth degree of freedom.

Stated as a theorem: "the fundamental matrix has rank 2."

Stated correctly: a true fundamental matrix — one that arises from an actual pair of cameras with a non-zero baseline — has rank exactly 2. A matrix estimated from noisy correspondences by the eight-point algorithm has rank 3 with probability 1, and its smallest singular value is a small non-zero number. Rank 2 must then be imposed, and imposing it changes the matrix. §09 is entirely about how much, and about the sense in which the change is optimal.

The extra property that E has and F does not

F's only constraint is det F = 0. E has a stronger one, and it is worth deriving because it is what makes the five-point algorithm possible and what a decomposition of E silently assumes.

AND THE CONVERSE HOLDS: any 3 × 3 matrix whose singular values are (σ, σ, 0) with σ > 0 IS an essential matrix, i.e. it factors as [t]ₓR for some rotation R and some t. That converse is what makes §12's decomposition well posed.

What rank 2 buys: the epipolar lines meet at a point, and only if the rank is 2 the perturbation slider starts at zero — the lines are exactly concurrent — then break it
The practical consequence. If you skip the rank-2 enforcement of §09, your epipolar lines do not meet. Every downstream step then quietly degrades: the epipoles are ill-defined, so rectification (§17) has no well-posed target; triangulated rays never intersect, so triangulation falls back on a least-squares fudge; and the decomposition of §12 has no null direction to read the translation off. The enforcement is not cosmetic tidying, it is putting the estimate back inside the set of things that can be a fundamental matrix at all.

07 The eight-point algorithm

Each correspondence contributes one linear equation in the nine unknown entries. Stack them, take the smallest right singular vector, and you have a fundamental matrix. It is the simplest estimator on this page and, unmodified, the worst.

One correspondence, one equation

09 Enforcing rank 2, and what it costs

The eight-point solution is a nine-vector. Reshaped, it is a matrix of rank 3, because nothing in the linear system asked for anything else. Putting it back on the rank-2 manifold is one SVD, and the projection is optimal in a sense that can be stated exactly.

the projectiongiven the raw estimate F̂ = U diag(σ₁, σ₂, σ₃) Vᵀ with σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ σ₃ > 0, F = U diag(σ₁, σ₂, 0) Vᵀ. EXACT STATEMENT (Eckart–Young–Mirsky). Among all matrices of rank ≤ 2, this F is the UNIQUE closest to F̂ in the Frobenius norm, and also a closest one in the spectral norm. The distance it moves is ‖F − F̂‖_F = σ₃. The theorem is proved on the singular value decomposition page; it is used here, not re-derived. WHAT IT IS NOT. It is not the rank-2 matrix that best fits the CORRESPONDENCES. It is the rank-2 matrix nearest to a particular nine-vector, and that nine-vector was itself chosen by minimising an algebraic residual under an arbitrary normalisation. Two approximations, composed. The estimator that actually minimises reprojection error is non-linear, and is §27.
The corresponding projection for E. An essential matrix needs (σ, σ, 0), not merely (σ₁, σ₂, 0). The nearest essential matrix in the Frobenius norm therefore replaces both leading singular values by their mean: minimising (σ₁ − a)² + (σ₂ − a)² + σ₃² over a gives a = (σ₁ + σ₂)/2, so E = U·diag(a, a, 0)·Vᵀ. Doing this before the decomposition of §12 matters, because that decomposition assumes exactly this structure — feed it a matrix with unequal leading singular values and the two candidate rotations it produces are not rotations.

10 Seven points, five points, and why fewer is worth so much

Eight points make the problem linear. Seven make it cubic. Five make it a tenth-degree polynomial and took twenty years to solve properly. Every one of those steps down was worth taking, and the reason is a formula from Part 4.

The seven-point algorithm

use the rank constraint instead of an eighth pointWith N = 7, A is 7 × 9 and generically has a TWO-dimensional null space. Take its two smallest right singular vectors f₀ and f₁, reshape them into F₀ and F₁; every matrix F(α) = α F₀ + (1 − α) F₁ satisfies all seven correspondences exactly. Now impose the constraint the linear system ignored: det F(α) = 0 which is a CUBIC in α, because the determinant of a 3 × 3 matrix is cubic in its entries and the entries are affine in α. A cubic with real coefficients has one or three real roots, so this returns ONE or THREE candidate fundamental matrices. Disambiguating: score each candidate against correspondences NOT used in the sample. Inside RANSAC this is free — the consensus step already scores every candidate against every match, so a 7-point sample simply generates up to three hypotheses per draw.

The five-point algorithm, in outline

With calibration known, the unknown is E, which has only 5 degrees of freedom. Five correspondences give five linear equations, leaving a four-dimensional null space, so E = xE₀ + yE₁ + zE₂ + wE₃ up to scale — three unknowns. The remaining equations are the essential-matrix conditions of §06: det E = 0, plus the nine scalar equations packed into 2EEᵀE − tr(EEᵀ)E = 0. Eliminating variables from that polynomial system yields a single tenth-degree univariate polynomial, so there are up to ten candidate essential matrices, again disambiguated by scoring. It is genuinely intricate; what matters here is only why anyone bothered.

why the sample size dominates everything — the RANSAC iteration count from part 4N = log(1 − p) / log(1 − wˢ) p = target success probability, w = inlier rate, s = sample size at p = 0.99: s = 4 (homography) s = 5 (E) s = 7 (F) s = 8 (F, linear) w = 0.70 17 26 54 78 w = 0.50 72 146 588 1177 w = 0.30 567 1893 21 055 70 188 Read the bottom row. And the five-point algorithm buys something the seven-point cannot. Because it estimates E rather than F, it works on a planar scene and under pure rotation without collapsing in the way §11 is about to show — the calibration constraints supply the information the geometry does not. That robustness, not the smaller sample alone, is why calibrated pipelines prefer it.

11 Robust estimation, and the configurations where nothing helps

Correspondences from Part 4 contain outliers, so the estimator has to be robust. But there is a second, nastier failure that robustness does not touch: configurations in which the data are all perfect inliers and the answer is still not determined.

The robust loop, in one paragraph

Sample 7 correspondences (or 8, or 5 in the calibrated case); fit; score every match by its Sampson distance (§05) against a threshold set from the expected localisation noise, typically 1–3 px; keep the largest consensus set; update the inlier rate w and hence N adaptively; finally refit on all inliers and refine non-linearly. Part 4 §27 owns the sampling theory, the adaptive-N update and the breakdown analysis; nothing about it changes here except the model and the residual. What does change is the failure mode, and that is the rest of this section.

13 Cheirality: the constraint that light travels forwards

The projective camera of Part 1 is happy to image points behind itself. Real cameras are not. That asymmetry — the only piece of physics in this whole section — is what picks one pose out of four.

the testfor each candidate (R, t): triangulate every correspondence → a 3-D point p depth in camera 0: z₀ = p_z (camera 0 is at the origin, depth in camera 1: z₁ = (R p + t)_z canonically oriented) count the points with z₀ > 0 AND z₁ > 0 choose the candidate with the largest count. In the noise-free case ONE point decides it, and the counts are 40/40 for the correct candidate and 0/40 for each of the other three — measured, in the figure below. With noise, and especially with distant points, individual points do flip, so the majority vote is what you use.

Where the test degrades, and why

A point at depth Z has a triangulated depth uncertainty growing like Z² (§18 derives and measures this). As Z grows, the uncertainty eventually exceeds Z itself, and at that moment the sign of the estimated depth becomes a coin flip. Points near the plane at infinity therefore vote randomly. Two consequences, both practical:

  • Never decide on one point. The noise-free argument that one point suffices is correct and useless. Vote with all of them, or with all of them weighted by 1/Z.
  • Down-weight or discard distant points in the vote. A far-field point contributes almost nothing to the pose anyway — its rays are nearly parallel — but it contributes a full vote to a decision it has no information about.
  • Points behind the cameras are not always errors. A reconstruction can legitimately contain points whose rays diverge, which are best re-interpreted as points at infinity by setting the homogeneous fourth coordinate to zero rather than forced to a large finite depth.
Cheirality is also a global tool. Beyond picking one of four poses, the same "in front of the camera" constraint is what upgrades a projective reconstruction (§05) to a quasi-affine one — it identifies which side of the plane at infinity the scene is on, and that is genuine information about the projective ambiguity that no amount of point matching supplies. It is also worth adding to the RANSAC scoring of §11: a hypothesis that places a third of its inliers behind a camera should not be scored as though it explained them.

14 Triangulation I: the mid-point method

Two rays that should meet, and do not. The most obvious repair is to take the point that is closest to both of them — obvious, cheap, and not what you want.

With the pose known, a correspondence gives two rays: from c₀ in direction v̂₀ = N(R₀⁻¹K₀⁻¹x̄₀), and from c₁ in direction v̂₁, where N(·) normalises to unit length. Because the image points carry noise, the rays are skew — in three dimensions two general lines do not intersect, and the probability that these two do is zero.

the derivation, in four steps1 · The nearest point to a candidate p on ray j is qⱼ = cⱼ + dⱼ v̂ⱼ where dⱼ minimises ‖cⱼ + dⱼ v̂ⱼ − p‖². Note also that the sum runs over ANY number of views: mid-point generalises to N views for free, which is one of its two genuine virtues. The other is that it needs no SVD.
Why it is nevertheless the wrong objective. It minimises a distance in the world, in metres. But the noise is in the image, in pixels, and one pixel of image noise corresponds to a world distance proportional to depth. Mid-point therefore silently weights near points more heavily than far ones and — worse — is not invariant to the projective frame. Re-express the same cameras and points in a different projective coordinate system and mid-point returns a different point; the reprojection-error minimiser returns the same one. For an uncalibrated reconstruction, where the projective frame is arbitrary anyway, that is close to disqualifying.

A common defence: "the rays almost meet, so it hardly matters which repair you use."

Measured, in the figure of §16: at a metre of baseline, five metres of depth and a pixel of noise, the three methods differ by less than a percent in 3-D error — the defence holds. Shrink the baseline to 15 cm and push the scene to 25 metres and the same three methods differ by a factor of seventeen, and in the direction nobody expects: the mid-point estimate becomes the most accurate in 3-D while remaining the worst by reprojection error. The defence is a statement about a configuration, not about the methods.

15 Triangulation II: the linear method

Work in homogeneous coordinates, clear the perspective division, and the whole thing becomes one more homogeneous linear system — the same step as §07, §09 and §26.

Keeping X homogeneous, and never dividing, is the numerically sound choice when the scene contains far-away points, and it is also what §27's bundle adjustment should use for its structure parameters.

What it actually minimises, and why that is not what you want

Multiplying out the denominator is not free. The residual that the linear system drives to zero is u(p₂ᵀX) − p₀ᵀX, and p₂ᵀX is the depth of the point in that camera. So the residual is the reprojection error multiplied by the depth. Distant points, which are the least reliable, get the largest weight. This is the same defect as the algebraic residual of §05, in a new costume, and it has the same fix — either weight each row by 1/(p₂ᵀX) and iterate (which is exactly Sampson-style iterative re-weighting), or stop pretending and minimise the real thing.

16 Triangulation III: the optimal two-view method

Ask the right question and the answer is a polynomial. The right question is: what is the nearest pair of image points that could have come from a single 3-D point?

the objective, and the reduction that makes it solvableminimise d(x₀, x̂₀)² + d(x₁, x̂₁)² over all pairs (x̂₀, x̂₁) subject to x̂₁ᵀ F x̂₀ = 0. Once such a pair is found, its rays meet EXACTLY, so any triangulation method returns the same point, and that point is the global minimiser of reprojection error. The reduction. The constraint says x̂₀ lies on some epipolar line l₀ and x̂₁ on its partner l₁. Given the line, the optimal x̂₀ is the FOOT OF THE PERPENDICULAR from x₀ to l₀, and likewise for x̂₁. So the two-dimensional search collapses to a search over the ONE-PARAMETER pencil of epipolar planes: minimise s(θ) = d(x₀, l₀(θ))² + d(x₁, l₁(θ))² θ ∈ [0, π) After translating each image so its point is at the origin and rotating so the epipole is on the x-axis, s(θ) becomes a ratio of quadratics in t = tan θ, and setting its derivative to zero clears to a polynomial of DEGREE SIX. 17 Rectification: making the epipolar lines into scanlines

The epipolar constraint already reduced the search to a line. Rectification makes that line the same row of memory in both images, which turns a geometric fact into an engineering one.

Searching along an arbitrary line in an image means interpolating: the line passes between pixel centres, so every sample is a weighted average of four neighbours, and the cost aggregation of §21 has to be done along a slanted window. Searching along a row means incrementing a pointer. That is the entire motivation, and it is worth a resampling of both images.

the construction — one new orientation, shared by both camerasboth rectified cameras get the SAME rotation R_rect and the SAME intrinsics K_new, and keep their own centres. Build R_rect from its three rows: r₁ = (c₁ − c₀) / ‖c₁ − c₀‖ the new x-axis IS the baseline r₂ = (k × r₁) / ‖k × r₁‖ k = the old optical axis of camera 0; this makes the new y-axis ⟂ the baseline and as close to the old "up" as possible r₃ = r₁ × r₂ the new optical axis, ⟂ to the baseline P′ⱼ = K_new [ R_rect | −R_rect cⱼ ] and the warp that takes the old image j to the new one is the homography Hⱼ = K_new R_rect (Kⱼ Rⱼ)⁻¹ (a pure 3 × 3 homography, because the camera CENTRE has not moved — only the orientation and the intrinsics have) The choice of K_new is free and matters. Taking the mean of K₀ and K₁ with the skew zeroed is the simple choice; minimising the distortion the warp introduces, subject to the same constraints, is the refinement production code uses, and it is worth the extra work whenever the vergence is large.

18 Disparity and depth: the inverse law and its consequences

In a rectified pair, matching reduces to one number per pixel — how far left or right its partner is. That number is not depth. It is inverse depth, and everything unpleasant about stereo follows from the "inverse".

the derivation, three lines, in the rectified frametwo identical cameras, focal length f px, centres at (0,0,0) and (B,0,0), both looking along +z with no rotation. A world point (X, Y, Z) projects to u₀ = f·X/Z + cₓ u₁ = f·(X − B)/Z + cₓ v₀ = v₁ so the horizontal difference — the DISPARITY — is d ≡ u₀ − u₁ = f·B/Z and therefore Z = f·B / d Read off four facts immediately: · d > 0 for any point in front of the cameras, and d = 0 exactly at infinity; · d is INDEPENDENT of X and Y — every point at depth Z has the same disparity; · the map Z ↦ d is a hyperbola, so equal steps in d are NOT equal steps in Z; · d scales with both f and B, which is why they are the two design knobs of a rig.

The Z² law, derived

propagating the matching error into depthdifferentiate Z = fB/d with respect to d: dZ/dd = −fB/d² = −(fB)/(fB/Z)² = −Z²/(fB) so for small disparity error σ_d, σ_Z ≈ Z² σ_d / (fB) absolute depth error grows as Z² and, dividing by Z, σ_Z / Z ≈ Z σ_d / (fB) = σ_d / d relative depth error grows as Z¹ The second form is the useful one and is worth memorising: RELATIVE depth accuracy equals RELATIVE disparity accuracy. A quarter-pixel matcher on a 12-pixel disparity gives 2% depth; the same matcher on a 2.5-pixel disparity gives 10%. The matcher did not get worse. The geometry did.

19 Matching costs: what "these two patches look alike" means

Everything from here to §24 is dense stereo: a disparity for every pixel, not just for the corners of Part 4. The first decision is the smallest one and it decides more than it looks.

After rectification, a candidate match for the left pixel (x, y) at disparity d is the right pixel (x − d, y). A matching cost C(x, y, d) scores that candidate, lower being better. Comparing single pixels is hopeless — one intensity carries almost no information — so every cost compares a window around the two pixels.

the four costs this page measures, on a (2r+1)² windowSAD C = (1/n) ∑ | I_L(x+i, y+j) − I_R(x−d+i, y+j) | SSD C = (1/n) ∑ ( I_L(x+i, y+j) − I_R(x−d+i, y+j) )² NCC C = 1 − ∑(I_L − Ī_L)(I_R − Ī_R) / √( ∑(I_L − Ī_L)² ∑(I_R − Ī_R)² ) — invariant to I → aI + b for any a > 0, b, by construction CENSUS at each pixel, write the bit string "is each neighbour darker than the centre?"; C = the HAMMING DISTANCE between the two strings. — invariant to ANY strictly increasing intensity mapping, not just affine ones, because it only records ORDER. Also naturally robust at depth discontinuities, because a few outlying pixels flip a few bits and no more, whereas one outlier can dominate an SSD.

The two cameras of a stereo rig have different sensors, different lenses, different vignetting and — if auto-exposure is on — different exposures. A surface is also not Lambertian, so it does not reflect the same radiance towards both cameras. The result is that I_R ≈ aI_L + b with a and b unknown and slowly varying. The rest of the taxonomy, in one place. The four above are the ones this page measures; several others matter in practice. Rank transform replaces each pixel by the rank of its intensity in its window and then uses SAD — cheaper than census, weaker. Mutual information estimates the joint intensity histogram between the images and is invariant to any monotone or even non-monotone relation, at the cost of needing a global iteration to estimate it. Gradient-based costs difference the derivatives rather than the intensities, killing the bias but not the gain. Sampling-insensitive costs compare each pixel against a linearly interpolated interval in the other image rather than a single value, which removes a systematic bias at high frequencies. And bilateral-subtraction costs remove a locally weighted mean first. The empirical finding that keeps recurring is that census and mutual information are the two that survive real illumination change.

20 The cost volume, and winner-take-all

Compute the cost at every pixel for every candidate disparity and you have a three-dimensional array. Almost every dense stereo algorithm ever written is a different way of collapsing it to two dimensions.

the object, and the four stages that operate on itC(x, y, d) x ∈ [0, W), y ∈ [0, H), d ∈ [0, d_max] the DISPARITY SPACE IMAGE — one scalar per pixel per hypothesis. For a 640 × 480 image and 64 disparities that is 19.7 M entries. 1 · matching cost computation fill C §19 2 · cost aggregation smooth C over a spatial support §21 3 · disparity computation collapse C to d(x, y) §20, §22 4 · disparity refinement sub-pixel, cross-check, hole fill §23 Every stereo algorithm is a choice at each of the four. LOCAL methods put their effort into stages 1 and 2 and make stage 3 trivial — WINNER-TAKE-ALL, d(x, y) = argmin_d C(x, y, d) independently at every pixel. GLOBAL methods skip stage 2 and make stage 3 an optimisation over the whole image, with an explicit smoothness term (§22).

Reading the slices

Two slices are worth knowing on sight, because everything a stereo algorithm does is visible in one of them.

  • A constant-d slice, C(·, ·, d₀), is an image that is dark exactly where the scene is at the depth corresponding to d₀. Sweeping d₀ is exactly the plane sweep of §17 in the rectified case: each slice is the photo-consistency of one hypothesised depth plane.
  • A constant-y slice, C(·, y₀, ·), is the cost matrix for one pair of scanlines, with x across and d down. The correct answer is a path through this matrix, and every fronto-parallel surface is a horizontal segment of it. This slice is where dynamic programming and semi-global matching live, and it is the left panel of the figure below.

21 Aggregation: the window that cannot be the right size

A single pixel carries no information; a window carries some. Every extra pixel of window buys signal in flat regions and pays for it at depth edges, and there is no radius at which both bills are zero.

aggregation is a filter applied to each disparity slice independentlyC(x, y, d) = w(x, y) ∗ C₀(x, y, d) for each d separately with a box w this is a moving average, computable in O(1) per pixel per disparity by a summed-area table REGARDLESS of the window size — the same integral-image trick as part 2. So a bigger window costs nothing at run time; it costs accuracy. The hidden assumption. Averaging the cost over a window says "the disparity is the same everywhere in this window". That is exactly the FRONTO-PARALLEL assumption. It is wrong at every depth discontinuity, and it is wrong on every slanted surface, where the true disparity varies linearly across the window and the aggregated cost is a blur of several correct answers.

What replaced it

  • Adaptive-weight aggregation. Instead of choosing a window shape, weight every pixel in a large window by how similar its colour is to the centre and how far away it is — a joint bilateral filter of the cost volume, guided by the reference image.   • Segmentation-based aggregation. Segment the reference image (Part 5), assume disparity is smooth within a segment, and fit a plane per segment. Better again, and it inherits Part 5's failure modes wholesale.
  • Three-dimensional support. Aggregate over a region of (x, y, d) space that is slanted rather than fronto-parallel, so that a sloping surface is supported by a sloping window. Equivalent to a limited-disparity-gradient constraint.
  • Nothing at all. Global methods (§22) skip aggregation entirely and put the smoothness into an explicit energy where it can be reasoned about, which is generally the better trade once you can afford the optimisation.

22 Global methods: putting the smoothness where you can argue with it

A local method's prior is hidden inside the shape of a window. A global method writes it down as a term in an objective, which makes it inspectable, tunable, and — the real prize — able to propagate information into regions that have none.

the energyE(d) = E_data(d) + λ E_smooth(d) E_data(d) = ∑_(x,y) C( x, y, d(x,y) ) the cost volume of §20 E_smooth(d) = ∑_(x,y) ρ( d(x,y) − d(x+1, y) ) + ρ( d(x,y) − d(x, y+1) ) The whole design is the choice of ρ: ρ(t) = t² standard regularisation. Smooth EVERYWHERE, including across depth discontinuities, which it therefore blurs into ramps. Convex and easy to minimise, and wrong at exactly the places that matter. ρ(t) = |t| total variation. Allows jumps, still penalises them linearly. ρ(t) = min(|t|, T) TRUNCATED — discontinuity-preserving. Once two neighbours differ by more than T there is no further penalty for differing more, so an edge costs a fixed amount rather than an unbounded one. Not convex, which is why the optimisation gets hard. ρ_d(Δd) · ρ_I(ΔI) modulated by the IMAGE gradient: make the smoothness penalty small where the reference image has a strong edge. This encodes "depth discontinuities tend to coincide with intensity edges", it is cheap, and it accounts for a large part of the practical advantage of global methods. It is also a prior that a glass door violates.
The complexity wall. Minimising this energy exactly over a 2-D grid is NP-hard for the interesting choices of ρ. Everything practical is therefore an approximation: graph cuts (α-expansion gives a solution within a known factor of the optimum for metric ρ), loopy belief propagation (no guarantee, works well), simulated annealing (asymptotic guarantee, useless run times). One special case is exactly solvable in polynomial time, and it is the one that took over the field.

One dimension is easy: scanline dynamic programming

Drop the vertical smoothness term and the energy decouples into independent rows. Each row is then a shortest-path problem through the (x, d) slice of §20, solvable exactly by dynamic programming in O(W · d_max) per row with the recursion

the scanline recursionL(x, d) = C(x, d) + min_{d′} [ L(x−1, d′) + ρ(d − d′) ] which is exact for the 1-D energy, and cheap. Its two defects are structural: · STREAKING. Rows are optimised independently, so a wrong decision propagates along a row and nothing above or below contradicts it. The result is unmistakable: horizontal stripes of wrong disparity. · The ORDERING CONSTRAINT. Classic DP formulations also assume that the left-to-right order of matched points is the same in both images. That is false whenever a thin object stands in front of a distant background — the background can be seen on both sides of it in one view and not the other — and enforcing it then forbids the correct answer.

Semi-global matching: the fix that made this practical

The insight is embarrassingly simple. If one scanline direction gives a good but streaky result, run the same recursion along eight directions — left, right, up, down and the four diagonals — and sum the resulting cost arrays before taking the winner. No direction is privileged, so no direction's streaks survive.

the SGM recursion, per direction rL_r(p, d) = C(p, d) + min ⎧ L_r(p − r, d) ⎪ L_r(p − r, d−1) + P₁ ⎨ L_r(p − r, d+1) + P₁ ⎪ min_k L_r(p − r, k) + P₂ ⎩ − min_k L_r(p − r, k) ← keeps L bounded S(p, d) = ∑_r L_r(p, d) d(p) = argmin_d S(p, d) Two penalties, not one, and the difference is the whole idea: P₁ is the SMALL penalty for a ±1 change of disparity — it lets SLANTED surfaces bend gradually without being punished as discontinuities; P₂ ≫ P₁ is the penalty for any larger jump — it is what preserves real edges. Standard practice reduces P₂ where the image gradient is large, which is the intensity-modulated smoothness of the energy above, applied locally. The subtraction of min_k L_r(p − r, k) does not change the argmin at any pixel; it only stops L growing without bound along the path, so that L fits in 16 bits — which is why SGM runs in real time on modest hardware.

An integer disparity map quantises the world into d_max planes, and §18 showed how far apart those planes are at range. A parabola through three cost values fixes most of it, for almost nothing, and introduces one systematic error worth knowing about.

Confidence, for free

The same denominator that can be dangerous is also the most useful by-product of the fit. a = (C₋₁ − 2C₀ + C₊₁)/2 is the curvature of the cost at its minimum, and Var(d) = 2σ_I²/(2a) turns it directly into an error bar. 25 Structure from motion: the pipeline

Two views give relative pose up to scale. Many views give a whole reconstruction — but only if the pairwise answers can be glued together without the seams accumulating, and that gluing is the entire engineering problem.

images→features→pairwise matching→geometric verification (§11)→tracks→pick an initial pair→E, pose, triangulate→add a camera by pose from 3-D points→triangulate new points→bundle adjust→repeat→final global bundle adjustment

The four steps that are not obvious

  • Track building. A track is the set of 2-D features across many images that are all projections of one 3-D point. It is built by taking the transitive closure of pairwise matches, and it goes wrong constantly: two different 3-D points on a repeating façade get matched into a single track, and that track then pulls two unrelated parts of the scene together. Tracks that are internally inconsistent — the same image contributing two features to one track — must be split or discarded.
  • Choosing the initial pair. Not the pair with the most matches. The pair with many matches and substantial out-of-plane parallax, because §11's planar degeneracy is fatal here. The standard test is to fit a homography to the matches as well as an essential matrix and to prefer the pair where the homography fails — a low homography inlier ratio means real 3-D structure.
  • Adding a camera. Once some 3-D points exist, a new image that observes enough of them can be posed by perspective-n-point: solve for (R, t) from 2-D–3-D correspondences, inside RANSAC. This is the step that lets the reconstruction grow, and it is also the step through which a bad track injects a bad camera.
  • When to bundle adjust. After every camera is too slow; after all of them is too late, because the initialisation for the last cameras will be hopeless. In practice: after every few cameras, plus a full pass at the end, plus a full pass whenever the reconstruction grows by some fraction.

Its two real limitations, and what they became

  • It needs a complete measurement matrix. Every point must be visible in every frame. Real tracks are riddled with holes — points leave the field of view, get occluded, or fail to match. The classical repair is to factor a dense sub-block first and use it to hallucinate the missing entries, then grow; the modern framing is low-rank matrix completion, and the same problem appears wherever principal component analysis meets missing data.
  • It needs an affine camera. The fix is to iterate: reconstruct affinely, estimate the per-frame perspective correction ηⱼ = 1/t_zⱼ from the reconstruction, divide it out of the measurements, and factor again. Three to five rounds usually converge. A fully projective factorization instead scales each measurement by its projective depth and iterates on those, which needs no calibration at all.
  • What it is actually for now. Not as a final answer — bundle adjustment (§27) is strictly better whenever it can be initialised. Factorization's value is that it is a closed form: it cannot get stuck in a local minimum, so it is an excellent initialiser for the thing that can.

27 Bundle adjustment: the objective everything else approximates

Every estimator on this page has been a way of getting close enough. This is the thing they were getting close to: minimise, over all cameras and all points at once, the distance between where the features were seen and where the model says they should be.

Why it is not a linear problem, and how it is solved anyway

It is non-linear twice over: π divides by depth, and the rotations live on a curved manifold. So it is solved by iterative linearisation — Gauss–Newton, damped into Levenberg–Marquardt:

one stepstack every residual into r(θ) and let J = ∂r/∂θ. The standard schedule: try a step; if the cost fell, accept it and divide λ; if it rose, reject and multiply λ. Two lines of bookkeeping and it is what makes the method usable from a mediocre initialisation. AND THE DAMPING IS NOT OPTIONAL HERE. JᵀJ for a bundle adjustment problem is EXACTLY SINGULAR — it has a seven-dimensional null space, always, no matter how good the data is. Gauss–Newton cannot take a step at all; the λdiag term is what makes the matrix invertible. §29 explains where the seven comes from.
Three parameterisation choices that are not cosmetic.
· Rotations: never as a 3 × 3 matrix with nine free entries — that is six redundant parameters and an orthogonality constraint the solver will violate. Use a local three-vector increment, R ← R·exp([δ]ₓ), re-linearised each iteration, or a unit quaternion with a normalisation step. Part 1 §10 and §11 own both.
· Points: homogeneous four-vectors rather than (X, Y, Z). A distant point has an enormous Z and a terrible condition number in Euclidean coordinates, and no condition number at all in homogeneous ones — it simply approaches W = 0. This is the same argument as §15's.
· The Jacobian: computed by differentiating the chain of transforms one link at a time and composing, which is exactly reverse-mode automatic differentiation. A camera rig with several fixed cameras on a moving platform is then handled by inserting two more links in the chain rather than by rewriting anything.

28 Sparsity, the arrowhead, and the Schur complement

A real reconstruction has thousands of cameras and millions of points. Solving a dense linear system in that many unknowns is impossible, and it is also unnecessary, because the system is not dense — and the shape of its sparsity is dictated by one fact about the problem.

the factEvery observation xᵢⱼ depends on EXACTLY TWO things: the 3-D point pᵢ and the camera j. It does not depend on any other point or on any other camera. So the two rows of J belonging to that observation are zero everywhere except in ONE 6-column camera block and ONE 3-column point block. Structure from motion is a BIPARTITE problem — points on one side, cameras on the other, observations as edges — and every sparsity property below is a restatement of that.

The Hessian, block by block

Order the parameters with the structure first and the motion second — which is the right order when there are more points than cameras, as there almost always are. Then A = JᵀJ has four blocks:

the arrowhead ⎡ A_pp A_pc ⎤ A_pp 3N × 3N, BLOCK DIAGONAL with 3 × 3 blocks A = ⎢ ⎥ A_cc 6M × 6M, BLOCK DIAGONAL with 6 × 6 blocks ⎣ A_pcᵀ A_cc ⎦ A_pc 3N × 6M, one 3 × 6 block per OBSERVATION A_pp is block diagonal because two different points share no residual — no residual depends on two points. A_cc is block diagonal for the same reason about cameras. All the coupling lives in A_pc, and it is as sparse as the visibility pattern. The shape is an ARROWHEAD: a diagonal spine with one dense border.
eliminating the points — the Schur complementsolve ⎡ A_pp A_pc ⎤ ⎡ Δp ⎤ = ⎡ b_p ⎤ by eliminating Δp from the first row: ⎣ A_pcᵀ A_cc ⎦ ⎣ Δc ⎦ ⎣ b_c ⎦ Δp = A_pp⁻¹ ( b_p − A_pc Δc ) substitute into the second row: ( A_cc − A_pcᵀ A_pp⁻¹ A_pc ) Δc = b_c − A_pcᵀ A_pp⁻¹ b_p └────────── A′_cc ──────────┘ the REDUCED CAMERA SYSTEM, 6M × 6M And A_pp⁻¹ is free: A_pp is block diagonal, so inverting it is N independent 3 × 3 inversions, not one 3N × 3N inversion. That is the whole trick, and it turns a system of size 6M + 3N into one of size 6M — for a thousand cameras and a million points, from 3 006 000 unknowns to 6 000.

The fill-in, stated exactly

Loose statement: "eliminating the points makes the camera block dense."

Exact statement, and it is verified in the figure below: the reduced camera matrix A′_cc has a non-zero 6 × 6 block in position (j, k) if and only if cameras j and k observe at least one point in common. Nothing more and nothing less. So A′_cc is dense only when the camera graph is a clique — every camera seeing something every other camera sees — which happens for a city square photographed from all round and does not happen for a vehicle driving down a road. The sparsity of the reduced system is the sparsity of the co-visibility graph, and that is a fact about the capture, not about the algebra.

Three consequences that follow directly.
· Order global parameters last. A shared intrinsic calibration, or a rig's camera-to-rig transforms, couples every observation. Put those parameters at the end of the ordering, along the outer edge of the arrowhead, so their fill-in is confined to one border rather than smeared through the whole matrix.
· The reverse ordering is sometimes right. Eliminating cameras instead of points is better when there are more cameras than points — tracking a handful of markers through thousands of video frames. The structure is symmetric; only the counts decide.
· Beyond a certain size, stop factorising. Once A′_cc is itself too large or too dense, solve it by conjugate gradients with a preconditioner, which never forms the matrix at all — only its product with a vector, which the sparsity makes cheap.

29 Gauge freedom, and the ambiguities that are not gauge freedom

A reconstruction has seven directions in which you can move every camera and every point at once without changing a single pixel of prediction. Recognising them is the difference between a solver that works and one that reports nonsense with confidence.

the sevenlet S be any similarity transformation of space — a rotation, a translation and a uniform scaling. Replace every point and every camera by its transformed version: pᵢ → S pᵢ Pⱼ → Pⱼ S⁻¹ then every predicted image point is unchanged, because Pⱼ S⁻¹ S pᵢ = Pⱼ pᵢ. So the objective is EXACTLY FLAT along the seven-parameter family of similarities: 3 rotation + 3 translation + 1 scale = 7 MEASURED. Three things that look like gauge freedom and are not

  • The bas-relief ambiguity. A shallow object undergoing a large rotation and a deep object undergoing a small one produce almost the same images. This is a near-degeneracy, not an exact one: the objective is not flat along it, only very nearly flat, so it shows up as a small-but-non-zero eigenvalue rather than a zero. It is the direction along which the covariance is largest in almost every small-baseline reconstruction, and it is why depth-versus-motion is the hardest thing to pin down. The same ambiguity appears in factorization (§26) as the reflection, and in shading as the depth-versus-illuminant-angle trade.
  • The projective ambiguity. With uncalibrated cameras the freedom is not seven-dimensional but fifteen-dimensional — the whole projective group of 3-space — because Pⱼ → PⱼH⁻¹, pᵢ → Hpᵢ preserves every image for any invertible 4 × 4 H. Removing it down to the similarity's seven is exactly what self-calibration (§30) does, and the eight extra dimensions are why it is hard.
  • Individual parameter uncertainties. A per-parameter error bar taken from the diagonal of the covariance hides the correlations, and in structure from motion the correlations are the whole story: point depths and camera baselines move together. Look at the leading eigenvectors of the covariance, not at its diagonal.

30 Calibration: buying back the metric

Everything metric on this page — angles, parallelism, ratios of lengths — depends on knowing K. There are two ways to know it: measure it once against a known object, or infer it from the images themselves and accept the conditions under which that is possible.

What a calibration target buys, and how

A planar target with a known pattern gives, per view, a set of 2-D–3-D correspondences whose 3-D points all lie on one plane. Put that plane at Z = 0; the projection then collapses to a homography:

from a planar target to constraints on Kfor a point (X, Y, 0) on the target, x̃ ∼ K [ r₁ r₂ r₃ | t ] (X, Y, 0, 1)ᵀ = K [ r₁ r₂ t ] (X, Y, 1)ᵀ = H (X, Y, 1)ᵀ so H = λ K [r₁ r₂ t], estimated from ≥ 4 correspondences by the direct linear transform (part 6). Now use the fact that r₁ and r₂ are ORTHONORMAL columns of a rotation: r₁ = λ⁻¹K⁻¹h₁ r₂ = λ⁻¹K⁻¹h₂ with h₁, h₂ the first two columns of H r₁ᵀr₂ = 0 ⟹ h₁ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₂ = 0 ‖r₁‖=‖r₂‖ ⟹ h₁ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₁ = h₂ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₂ TWO linear constraints per view on the symmetric matrix B = K⁻ᵀK⁻¹, which has 6 entries and 5 degrees of freedom up to scale. So THREE views of a planar target determine K completely; two views suffice if the skew is assumed zero; one view suffices if the aspect ratio and principal point are also assumed. Recover K from B by Cholesky. Note the structure: this is EXACTLY the metric-upgrade step of factorization (§26) — solve a homogeneous linear system for a symmetric matrix, then take its square root. B is the image of the ABSOLUTE CONIC, and it is the single object that all of calibration and self-calibration is about.
What the target really buys is non-degeneracy. Every view of the target must be at a genuinely different orientation: two views of a plane at the same orientation give the same two constraints twice. It also buys distortion: the target's straight lines are known to be straight, so the radial coefficients of Part 1 §17 can be estimated at the same time, and they must be, because a distorted image of a plane is not related to the plane by a homography at all.

Self-calibration, in outline

With no target, the calibration has to come out of the correspondences. It cannot come out of them entirely: post-multiplying every camera by H̃⁻¹ and pre-multiplying every point by H̃ leaves every image unchanged (§29), and an RQ decomposition of the transformed cameras gives a completely different, equally valid, set of calibration matrices. Every self-calibration method therefore assumes something, and the assumption is what makes it work:

  • Constant intrinsics across views. The most common assumption. The same camera took all the pictures, so K is one unknown rather than n. Three or more views then generally suffice.
  • Zero skew, unit aspect ratio, principal point at the image centre. Nearly always true of a modern sensor, and it reduces K to a single unknown focal length. It fails for a cropped image — the principal point is no longer central — and for a photograph of a photograph.
  • Two views and the Kruppa equations. With the above assumptions, both focal lengths can be recovered from a single fundamental matrix. Write D_j = K_jK_jᵀ = diag(f_j², f_j², 1) and take the singular vectors u₀, u₁, v₀, v₁ of F and its singular values σ₀, σ₁; the three ratios u₁ᵀD₀u₁ / σ₀²v₀ᵀD₁v₀ = −u₀ᵀD₀u₁ / σ₀σ₁v₀ᵀD₁v₁ = u₀ᵀD₀u₀ / σ₁²v₁ᵀD₁v₁ are each affine in f₀² over affine in f₁², so cross-multiplying gives quadratics that solve directly.
  • Vanishing points. Three mutually orthogonal vanishing points in one image determine K outright, because they are the images of three points on the plane at infinity that are conjugate with respect to the absolute conic. Architectural scenes hand this over for free.

Stated as a theorem: "two views with known principal point and zero skew determine both focal lengths."

Stated correctly: they determine them generically, and there are configurations in which they determine nothing at all — notably when there is no relative rotation between the two views, and when the two optical axes intersect. In those cases the focal lengths trade off directly against the depth of the reconstruction, which is the bas-relief ambiguity of §29 again: a deeper scene photographed with a longer lens looks the same. The practical advice that follows is unromantic and correct: take the focal length from the image metadata as an initial value, and let bundle adjustment refine it along with everything else.

31 Drift, and the one measurement that removes it

An incremental reconstruction estimates each pose relative to the last. Errors therefore compound, and they compound at a rate that depends on which quantity is wrong — a fact worth knowing before deciding what to fix.

Three growth laws, measured

A camera walking in a straight line, one unit per step, with per-step errors added to its odometry. The end-point position error after n steps, fitted on a log–log axis over n = 10 to 320, 300 trials each — the same measurement the figure below runs live:

Per-step errorMeasured exponentPredictedWhy
Translation noise only, σ_t = 0.02 per step0.503½The position is a sum of n independent errors: an ordinary random walk, so the standard deviation grows like √n. Benign.
Rotation noise, σ_θ = 0.005 rad per step1.4413⁄2Much worse, and this is the one that matters. The heading is itself a random walk, and the position integrates the heading. Lateral error ≈ ∑ₖ θₖ, whose variance is σ_θ² ∑ₖ (n − k)² ≈ σ_θ² n³/3 — so the error grows like n^1.5. A rotation error is not a rotation error; it is a lever arm.

At n = 320 the bias produces an end-point error of about 102 units against about 17 from the same amount of unbiased rotation noise. Bias is worse than noise by a whole power of n.
The design consequence. Since rotation errors dominate, the effort belongs there: use wide-field-of-view cameras, which pin down rotation far better than narrow ones; use as many well-spread features as possible rather than many clustered ones; and prefer estimators that get the rotation right even at the cost of the translation, which is exactly why global methods solve rotation averaging first and separately (§25). And since a bias is worse than noise, hunt for systematic errors — an uncorrected radial distortion, a wrong principal point, a rolling shutter — before adding more features.

Loop closure

The cure for accumulated drift is not a better incremental estimator. It is one measurement that is not incremental: recognising that the camera has returned somewhere it has been before, and adding a constraint directly between two poses that are far apart in time. That single edge converts an open chain into a cycle, and a cycle has a consistency condition that the chain did not.

pose-graph optimisationnodes: the poses x₀ … x_{n−1}. edges: relative-pose measurements zᵢⱼ with weights. minimise ∑ ‖ ⊖xᵢ ⊕ xⱼ ⊖ zᵢⱼ ‖²_Ωᵢⱼ (i,j) where ⊕ and ⊖ compose and invert poses. This is bundle adjustment with the 3-D points already eliminated — the structure has been marginalised into the relative-pose constraints — so it is small, sparse and fast, and it is the standard back end of any system that has to run in real time. MEASURED, on a 40-pose closed loop with 0.02 translation noise, 0.01 rad rotation noise and a rotation bias of 0.004 rad per step: open-loop RMS position error 1.2574 gap between the last pose and the first 1.4144 after one loop-closure edge and 8 iterations 0.1883 a factor of 6.7 The optimiser does not "close the gap" by moving the last pose. It DISTRIBUTES the inconsistency backwards over every pose in the loop, in proportion to how uncertain each edge was, which is why the whole trajectory improves and not just its end.

The hard part is the optimisation.

The hard part is the recognition. Pose-graph optimisation is a well-conditioned sparse least-squares problem and is essentially solved. Deciding that this image shows the same place as that one, several thousand frames ago, is a retrieval problem — and it has an asymmetry that makes it dangerous: a missed loop closure costs you the drift you already had, while a false one welds two unrelated parts of the map together and destroys the reconstruction outright. So the verification is geometric and strict: retrieve candidates by appearance, then require a consistent relative pose supported by many inliers, then require that the closure is consistent with other closures. Repeated architecture is the adversary throughout, exactly as in §11 and §24.

Drift accumulating around a loop, and one closure edge redistributing it the closure starts off — turn it on and watch the correction spread backwards over the whole trajectory

A network can learn to recognise a correspondence far better than a hand-written descriptor can, because "these two patches show the same surface" is a statement about the world that has no closed form. It cannot learn the epipolar constraint better than the epipolar constraint, because the epipolar constraint is already exactly right.

Ten claims that are nearly right

"The fundamental matrix has rank 2." A true one does. An estimated one has rank 3 and the constraint must be imposed, which changes it. §06, §09.

"Lots of RANSAC inliers means the geometry is right." On a plane, every match is an inlier of a three-parameter family of wrong answers. §11.

"Optimal triangulation gives the most accurate 3-D point." It minimises reprojection error, which at long range is not 3-D error — measured, a factor of seventeen the other way. §16.

"Enforcing rank 2 improves the estimate." It makes the fit about 5% worse. It is still right, because only then is the estimate a fundamental matrix at all. §09.

"Rectification is an approximation." It is exact to 10⁻¹³ px. What is approximate is the resampling. And it does not exist at all for forward motion. §17.

"A bigger baseline is better." Better for depth precision, worse for matching — more occlusion, more foreshortening, a longer search. §18, §24.

"Census is the robust matching cost, so use it." It is the worst of the four on clean textured data, at 29% against SAD's 15%, and much the worst in low-contrast regions, because it thresholds noise. Use it when the images differ radiometrically. §19.

"More SGM directions is always better." Measured: 2 directions beat 8 on one small scene. What eight buys is isotropy, which a bad-pixel count does not see. §22.

"Sub-pixel refinement is unbiased." On near-Nyquist texture the bias swings 0.087 px peak to peak towards the nearest integer, and at high noise the clamp adds a bias of −0.17 px. §23.

"Monocular depth networks solve stereo." They estimate a different quantity by a different mechanism — a prior, not a triangulation — and do not obey the Z² law because nothing is being triangulated. §32.

When a two-view reconstruction is wrong, check these in order

  1. Are you feeding pixels to E, or rays to F? The residual will be off by about f². §04.
  2. Did you normalise before the eight-point solve? §08.
  3. Did you enforce rank 2? Are the epipolar lines actually concurrent? §09.
  4. Is the scene planar, or the motion a pure rotation, or the baseline tiny? Check σ₇/σ₈ of the design matrix and fit a homography for comparison. §11.
  5. Did you pick the pose by cheirality over all the points, or over one? And are your points far enough away for the vote to be noise? §13.
  6. Is lens distortion removed? A distorted image has curved epipolar lines and nothing above applies. Part 1 §17.
  7. Is the principal point where you think it is? A cropped image has moved it, and self-calibration assumes it has not. §30.
  8. Did you finish with bundle adjustment? Is its converged cost near σ²(m − p)? Far above means outliers or a wrong camera model; far below means over-parameterisation. §27.

Seven parts, one arrow, reversed

This series had a single shape, and it is worth stating now that it is finished. Part 1 wrote down a forward model: a point in a room, a rigid transformation, a division by depth, a lens, a surface, a sensor, three eight-bit numbers. It estimated nothing. Its whole purpose was to be precise about what a camera does, so that the rest of the series had something exact to invert.

1 a camera, defined→2 operations on pixels→3 the same operations, in frequency→4 points worth matching→5 regions worth grouping→6 motion in the plane→7 structure in space

Each part after the first inverted one more stage of that model, and the inversions got harder in a specific way — they became less local. Part 2 and Part 3 operate on a neighbourhood: a filter output depends on a few dozen pixels, and the Fourier view of Part 3 is the same operation seen through a change of basis. Part 4 widens the neighbourhood to a patch and asks a question that a single pixel cannot answer — is this location distinctive? Part 5 widens it to a region and asks a question that no patch can answer — where does this object end? Part 6 couples two entire images through a handful of shared parameters. And this part couples every image to every other through a shared 3-D world, which is why its final act is one optimisation over every camera and every point at once.

The through-line, then: Part 1 threw depth away in one line of algebra, and Part 7 spent thirty-three sections getting it back — and could only do it by adding a second viewpoint, because the information genuinely was not there. Everything between was about making the correspondences reliable enough for that second viewpoint to be useful. The series is, in the end, one long argument that geometry is easy and matching is hard.

What the series does not cover, said plainly

  • Learned depth estimation as a field. §32 says what monocular and learned stereo networks change and what they do not, in two paragraphs. It is not a survey. The architectures, the training objectives, the scale-and-shift-invariant losses, the synthetic-data pipelines and the benchmarks are a subject of their own and this series does not enter it.
  • SLAM as a systems problem. §31 covers drift, pose graphs and loop closure as geometry. Real simultaneous localisation and mapping is an engineering discipline: keyframe selection, map management, relocalisation after tracking loss, inertial fusion, real-time budgets, marginalisation and the sliding window. None of that is here.
  • Multi-view stereo and surface reconstruction. This page stops at a sparse point cloud and a two-view disparity map. Turning many views into a dense, watertight, textured surface — patch-based MVS, depth-map fusion, volumetric integration, Poisson reconstruction, meshing, texture blending — is a whole chapter that begins where §28 ends.
  • Non-rigid structure from motion. Every line of this page assumed the scene was rigid. Deformable objects, articulated bodies and dynamic scenes break the assumption at its root: the rank-3 argument of §26 becomes a rank-3K argument for a K-basis deformation model, the epipolar constraint stops holding for moving points, and the whole problem becomes under-determined in a way that only strong priors resolve.
  • Radiance fields and modern scene representations. They consume the poses this page produces and replace what happens next. Out of scope, and pointed at rather than covered.

Where this goes sideways. Into convolutional networks, where §20's cost volume is rebuilt from learned features and aggregated by learned 3-D convolutions, and where §19's four hand-designed costs become one learned one; into vision transformers, whose attention over two images is precisely the joint matcher that §11's RANSAC was invented to survive the absence of; into the singular value decomposition, which is not a tool this page uses but the single theorem it is mostly made of; into optimization, where §27 is a large sparse non-linear least-squares problem and §28 is what makes it tractable; into calculus, for the Jacobian chain of §27 and the differentiation of Z = fB/d that produced the entire range limit of stereo; into model evaluation, because §19 to §24 are an extended argument that an aggregate benchmark number hides the phenomenon and the per-region breakdown is the real result; into Part 5, whose segments are the support regions of §21's best local aggregation; and into Part 4's RANSAC, which this page consumed twice and could not have been written without.
One last thing, and it is the thing this part is really about. Every method here has been a way of choosing between explanations of the same measurements — four poses that all satisfy the same essential matrix, three families of fundamental matrices that all fit a planar scene, two triangulated points that both explain the rays, a disparity that a flat wall cannot decide. In every case the resolution came from outside the algebra: light travels forwards, so points are in front of cameras. Surfaces are mostly smooth, so disparity is mostly smooth. The world is rigid, so a bundle of rays must meet. Geometry tells you what is possible; it never tells you which possibility is real. Something else always has to, and knowing exactly what that something is — and what it costs you when it is false — is the whole of doing this well.
