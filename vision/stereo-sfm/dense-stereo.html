17 Rectification: making the epipolar lines into scanlines

The epipolar constraint already reduced the search to a line. Rectification makes that line the same row of memory in both images, which turns a geometric fact into an engineering one.

Searching along an arbitrary line in an image means interpolating: the line passes between pixel centres, so every sample is a weighted average of four neighbours, and the cost aggregation of §21 has to be done along a slanted window. Searching along a row means incrementing a pointer. That is the entire motivation, and it is worth a resampling of both images.

the construction — one new orientation, shared by both camerasboth rectified cameras get the SAME rotation R_rect and the SAME intrinsics K_new, and keep their own centres. Build R_rect from its three rows: r₁ = (c₁ − c₀) / ‖c₁ − c₀‖ the new x-axis IS the baseline r₂ = (k × r₁) / ‖k × r₁‖ k = the old optical axis of camera 0; this makes the new y-axis ⟂ the baseline and as close to the old "up" as possible r₃ = r₁ × r₂ the new optical axis, ⟂ to the baseline P′ⱼ = K_new [ R_rect | −R_rect cⱼ ] and the warp that takes the old image j to the new one is the homography Hⱼ = K_new R_rect (Kⱼ Rⱼ)⁻¹ (a pure 3 × 3 homography, because the camera CENTRE has not moved — only the orientation and the intrinsics have) The choice of K_new is free and matters. Taking the mean of K₀ and K₁ with the skew zeroed is the simple choice; minimising the distortion the warp introduces, subject to the same constraints, is the refinement production code uses, and it is worth the extra work whenever the vergence is large.

What is true afterwards, verified

Applying this to the running rig — baseline 1.006 m, verged 8°, different intrinsics in the two cameras — and checking every one of the 40 correspondences:

Rectification is exact, not approximate. Every number above is at the level of double-precision rounding. That is worth stressing because rectified stereo is often described as an approximation or a convenience; it is neither. It is a change of coordinates that leaves the geometry alone. What is approximate is the resampling: the warp is a homography, the images are sampled, and interpolating them costs a little sharpness. That cost is Part 2's subject, not this page's.

When it fails, and what to do instead

SituationWhat happens
Epipole inside, or near, the image — i.e. mostly forward motionThe fatal case. Rectification sends the epipole to infinity, and a homography that sends an interior point to infinity tears the image apart: the required output canvas grows without bound as the epipole approaches the frame. There is no fix within rectification. The standard alternative is the plane sweep: hypothesise a family of planes at increasing depth, warp every image onto each plane by the homography it induces, and measure photo-consistency there. It needs no rectification, works for any camera arrangement, and generalises to many views at once.
Baseline nearly parallel to the optical axisSame problem in different words — k × r₁ → 0 and the second row of R_rect is undefined.
Very different focal lengths or strong vergenceWorks, but one image must be magnified to match the other, and magnifying resamples above its own resolution. Magnify the smaller image rather than shrinking the larger, so nothing is aliased away.
More than two viewsSimultaneous rectification of n views is possible only if the n camera centres are collinear, because there must be a single baseline direction. Otherwise, rectify pairs, or sweep planes.
Significant lens distortionMust be removed first. Distortion is not a homography, so it does not compose into H; a rectified image built from distorted pixels has curved epipolar "lines". Part 1 §17 owns the model and its inverse.

18 Disparity and depth: the inverse law and its consequences

In a rectified pair, matching reduces to one number per pixel — how far left or right its partner is. That number is not depth. It is inverse depth, and everything unpleasant about stereo follows from the "inverse".

the derivation, three lines, in the rectified frametwo identical cameras, focal length f px, centres at (0,0,0) and (B,0,0), both looking along +z with no rotation. A world point (X, Y, Z) projects to u₀ = f·X/Z + cₓ u₁ = f·(X − B)/Z + cₓ v₀ = v₁ so the horizontal difference — the DISPARITY — is d ≡ u₀ − u₁ = f·B/Z and therefore Z = f·B / d Read off four facts immediately: · d > 0 for any point in front of the cameras, and d = 0 exactly at infinity; · d is INDEPENDENT of X and Y — every point at depth Z has the same disparity; · the map Z ↦ d is a hyperbola, so equal steps in d are NOT equal steps in Z; · d scales with both f and B, which is why they are the two design knobs of a rig.

The Z² law, derived

propagating the matching error into depthdifferentiate Z = fB/d with respect to d: dZ/dd = −fB/d² = −(fB)/(fB/Z)² = −Z²/(fB) so for small disparity error σ_d, σ_Z ≈ Z² σ_d / (fB) absolute depth error grows as Z² and, dividing by Z, σ_Z / Z ≈ Z σ_d / (fB) = σ_d / d relative depth error grows as Z¹ The second form is the useful one and is worth memorising: RELATIVE depth accuracy equals RELATIVE disparity accuracy. A quarter-pixel matcher on a 12-pixel disparity gives 2% depth; the same matcher on a 2.5-pixel disparity gives 10%. The matcher did not get worse. The geometry did.

What this means for building a rig. Fix a tolerable relative error, say 5%. Then d ≥ σ_d/0.05 = 20σ_d, so with a quarter-pixel matcher you need d ≥ 5 px, so Z ≤ fB/5. With f = 500 and B = 0.12 m that is 12 m, full stop. To double the range you must double f — which halves the field of view — or double B — which increases occlusion, foreshortening and the disparity search range, all of which make the matching harder and so increase σ_d. There is no free parameter. Every stereo rig is a fixed point of this argument, and it is why long-range depth is not a stereo problem.

19 Matching costs: what "these two patches look alike" means

Everything from here to §24 is dense stereo: a disparity for every pixel, not just for the corners of Part 4. The first decision is the smallest one and it decides more than it looks.

After rectification, a candidate match for the left pixel (x, y) at disparity d is the right pixel (x − d, y). A matching cost C(x, y, d) scores that candidate, lower being better. Comparing single pixels is hopeless — one intensity carries almost no information — so every cost compares a window around the two pixels.

the four costs this page measures, on a (2r+1)² windowSAD C = (1/n) ∑ | I_L(x+i, y+j) − I_R(x−d+i, y+j) | SSD C = (1/n) ∑ ( I_L(x+i, y+j) − I_R(x−d+i, y+j) )² NCC C = 1 − ∑(I_L − Ī_L)(I_R − Ī_R) / √( ∑(I_L − Ī_L)² ∑(I_R − Ī_R)² ) — invariant to I → aI + b for any a > 0, b, by construction CENSUS at each pixel, write the bit string "is each neighbour darker than the centre?"; C = the HAMMING DISTANCE between the two strings. — invariant to ANY strictly increasing intensity mapping, not just affine ones, because it only records ORDER. Also naturally robust at depth discontinuities, because a few outlying pixels flip a few bits and no more, whereas one outlier can dominate an SSD.

Why radiometric invariance is not a luxury

The two cameras of a stereo rig have different sensors, different lenses, different vignetting and — if auto-exposure is on — different exposures. A surface is also not Lambertian, so it does not reflect the same radiance towards both cameras. The result is that I_R ≈ aI_L + b with a and b unknown and slowly varying. Read the table twice. Read left-to-right and the story is that SAD and SSD collapse completely — from one bad pixel in seven to five in six — under a change a human eye would barely notice, while NCC and census do not move by a tenth of a percentage point. Read top-to-bottom and the story is the opposite: on clean data SAD is the best of the four and census is much the worst, at 29.2% against 14.5%. There is no free invariance.

The census result is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it is the clearest case of an invariance being paid for. Census records only the sign of each neighbour's difference from the centre pixel. In a region of strong texture that sign is decided by the texture and is stable. In a region of weak texture it is decided by whatever is left — here, 1.5 grey levels of sensor noise — and roughly half the bits become coin flips. The synthetic pair below contains a deliberately flat patch, and census is wrong on 79.5% of it against SAD's 46.9%. Census is the right choice when the two images differ radiometrically and the scene is textured. It is the wrong choice on a white wall.

The rest of the taxonomy, in one place. The four above are the ones this page measures; several others matter in practice. Rank transform replaces each pixel by the rank of its intensity in its window and then uses SAD — cheaper than census, weaker. Mutual information estimates the joint intensity histogram between the images and is invariant to any monotone or even non-monotone relation, at the cost of needing a global iteration to estimate it. Gradient-based costs difference the derivatives rather than the intensities, killing the bias but not the gain. Sampling-insensitive costs compare each pixel against a linearly interpolated interval in the other image rather than a single value, which removes a systematic bias at high frequencies. And bilateral-subtraction costs remove a locally weighted mean first. The empirical finding that keeps recurring is that census and mutual information are the two that survive real illumination change.

20 The cost volume, and winner-take-all

Compute the cost at every pixel for every candidate disparity and you have a three-dimensional array. Almost every dense stereo algorithm ever written is a different way of collapsing it to two dimensions.

the object, and the four stages that operate on itC(x, y, d) x ∈ [0, W), y ∈ [0, H), d ∈ [0, d_max] the DISPARITY SPACE IMAGE — one scalar per pixel per hypothesis. For a 640 × 480 image and 64 disparities that is 19.7 M entries. 1 · matching cost computation fill C §19 2 · cost aggregation smooth C over a spatial support §21 3 · disparity computation collapse C to d(x, y) §20, §22 4 · disparity refinement sub-pixel, cross-check, hole fill §23 Every stereo algorithm is a choice at each of the four. LOCAL methods put their effort into stages 1 and 2 and make stage 3 trivial — WINNER-TAKE-ALL, d(x, y) = argmin_d C(x, y, d) independently at every pixel. GLOBAL methods skip stage 2 and make stage 3 an optimisation over the whole image, with an explicit smoothness term (§22).

Reading the slices

Two slices are worth knowing on sight, because everything a stereo algorithm does is visible in one of them.

  • A constant-d slice, C(·, ·, d₀), is an image that is dark exactly where the scene is at the depth corresponding to d₀. Sweeping d₀ is exactly the plane sweep of §17 in the rectified case: each slice is the photo-consistency of one hypothesised depth plane.
  • A constant-y slice, C(·, y₀, ·), is the cost matrix for one pair of scanlines, with x across and d down. The correct answer is a path through this matrix, and every fronto-parallel surface is a horizontal segment of it. This slice is where dynamic programming and semi-global matching live, and it is the left panel of the figure below.

What winner-take-all does and does not do

PropertyConsequence
No spatial couplingNeighbouring pixels are decided independently. All the smoothness a local method has comes from the overlap of the aggregation windows, which is an implicit and rather crude prior — it assumes the surface is fronto-parallel across the window, which is exactly what §21 measures the cost of.
Uniqueness only one wayEvery left pixel gets exactly one disparity, but nothing stops two left pixels claiming the same right pixel. Real geometry forbids that except at occlusions. The fix is left–right cross-checking: compute the map both ways and discard any pixel where d_L(x) ≠ d_R(x − d_L(x)). It detects occlusions almost for free, and it is the single cheapest quality improvement available.
Integer outputThe scene is quantised into d_max + 1 depth planes, which by §18 are far apart at range. Sub-pixel interpolation, §23.
No notion of confidenceA pixel whose cost curve is a sharp V and a pixel whose curve is dead flat both produce one integer with no qualifier. §23 recovers a confidence from the curvature.

21 Aggregation: the window that cannot be the right size

A single pixel carries no information; a window carries some. Every extra pixel of window buys signal in flat regions and pays for it at depth edges, and there is no radius at which both bills are zero.

aggregation is a filter applied to each disparity slice independentlyC(x, y, d) = w(x, y) ∗ C₀(x, y, d) for each d separately with a box w this is a moving average, computable in O(1) per pixel per disparity by a summed-area table REGARDLESS of the window size — the same integral-image trick as part 2. So a bigger window costs nothing at run time; it costs accuracy. The hidden assumption. Averaging the cost over a window says "the disparity is the same everywhere in this window". That is exactly the FRONTO-PARALLEL assumption. It is wrong at every depth discontinuity, and it is wrong on every slanted surface, where the true disparity varies linearly across the window and the aggregated cost is a blur of several correct answers.

Shiftable windows: getting most of it back for almost nothing

A pixel next to a depth edge is badly served by a window centred on it, and well served by a window that lies entirely on its own side of the edge. Trying all shifted windows and taking the best is equivalent to a much cheaper operation.

What replaced it

  • Adaptive-weight aggregation. Instead of choosing a window shape, weight every pixel in a large window by how similar its colour is to the centre and how far away it is — a joint bilateral filter of the cost volume, guided by the reference image. It assumes colour edges coincide with depth edges, which is usually right and occasionally catastrophic. This is the strongest local method there is, and it is a bilateral filter applied to a cost volume rather than to an image.
  • Segmentation-based aggregation. Segment the reference image (Part 5), assume disparity is smooth within a segment, and fit a plane per segment. Better again, and it inherits Part 5's failure modes wholesale.
  • Three-dimensional support. Aggregate over a region of (x, y, d) space that is slanted rather than fronto-parallel, so that a sloping surface is supported by a sloping window. Equivalent to a limited-disparity-gradient constraint.
  • Nothing at all. Global methods (§22) skip aggregation entirely and put the smoothness into an explicit energy where it can be reasoned about, which is generally the better trade once you can afford the optimisation.

22 Global methods: putting the smoothness where you can argue with it

A local method's prior is hidden inside the shape of a window. A global method writes it down as a term in an objective, which makes it inspectable, tunable, and — the real prize — able to propagate information into regions that have none.

the energyE(d) = E_data(d) + λ E_smooth(d) E_data(d) = ∑_(x,y) C( x, y, d(x,y) ) the cost volume of §20 E_smooth(d) = ∑_(x,y) ρ( d(x,y) − d(x+1, y) ) + ρ( d(x,y) − d(x, y+1) ) The whole design is the choice of ρ: ρ(t) = t² standard regularisation. Smooth EVERYWHERE, including across depth discontinuities, which it therefore blurs into ramps. Convex and easy to minimise, and wrong at exactly the places that matter. ρ(t) = |t| total variation. Allows jumps, still penalises them linearly. ρ(t) = min(|t|, T) TRUNCATED — discontinuity-preserving. Once two neighbours differ by more than T there is no further penalty for differing more, so an edge costs a fixed amount rather than an unbounded one. Not convex, which is why the optimisation gets hard. ρ_d(Δd) · ρ_I(ΔI) modulated by the IMAGE gradient: make the smoothness penalty small where the reference image has a strong edge. This encodes "depth discontinuities tend to coincide with intensity edges", it is cheap, and it accounts for a large part of the practical advantage of global methods. It is also a prior that a glass door violates.
The complexity wall. Minimising this energy exactly over a 2-D grid is NP-hard for the interesting choices of ρ. Everything practical is therefore an approximation: graph cuts (α-expansion gives a solution within a known factor of the optimum for metric ρ), loopy belief propagation (no guarantee, works well), simulated annealing (asymptotic guarantee, useless run times). One special case is exactly solvable in polynomial time, and it is the one that took over the field.

One dimension is easy: scanline dynamic programming

Drop the vertical smoothness term and the energy decouples into independent rows. Each row is then a shortest-path problem through the (x, d) slice of §20, solvable exactly by dynamic programming in O(W · d_max) per row with the recursion

the scanline recursionL(x, d) = C(x, d) + min_{d′} [ L(x−1, d′) + ρ(d − d′) ] which is exact for the 1-D energy, and cheap. Its two defects are structural: · STREAKING. Rows are optimised independently, so a wrong decision propagates along a row and nothing above or below contradicts it. The result is unmistakable: horizontal stripes of wrong disparity. · The ORDERING CONSTRAINT. Classic DP formulations also assume that the left-to-right order of matched points is the same in both images. That is false whenever a thin object stands in front of a distant background — the background can be seen on both sides of it in one view and not the other — and enforcing it then forbids the correct answer.

Semi-global matching: the fix that made this practical

The insight is embarrassingly simple. If one scanline direction gives a good but streaky result, run the same recursion along eight directions — left, right, up, down and the four diagonals — and sum the resulting cost arrays before taking the winner. No direction is privileged, so no direction's streaks survive.

the SGM recursion, per direction rL_r(p, d) = C(p, d) + min ⎧ L_r(p − r, d) ⎪ L_r(p − r, d−1) + P₁ ⎨ L_r(p − r, d+1) + P₁ ⎪ min_k L_r(p − r, k) + P₂ ⎩ − min_k L_r(p − r, k) ← keeps L bounded S(p, d) = ∑_r L_r(p, d) d(p) = argmin_d S(p, d) Two penalties, not one, and the difference is the whole idea: P₁ is the SMALL penalty for a ±1 change of disparity — it lets SLANTED surfaces bend gradually without being punished as discontinuities; P₂ ≫ P₁ is the penalty for any larger jump — it is what preserves real edges. Standard practice reduces P₂ where the image gradient is large, which is the intensity-modulated smoothness of the energy above, applied locally. The subtraction of min_k L_r(p − r, k) does not change the argmin at any pixel; it only stops L growing without bound along the path, so that L fits in 16 bits — which is why SGM runs in real time on modest hardware.

23 Sub-pixel refinement, its variance, and its bias

An integer disparity map quantises the world into d_max planes, and §18 showed how far apart those planes are at range. A parabola through three cost values fixes most of it, for almost nothing, and introduces one systematic error worth knowing about.

the parabola fitlet d̂ be the integer winner and C₋₁, C₀, C₊₁ the costs at d̂ − 1, d̂, d̂ + 1. Fit C(d̂ + t) ≈ at² + bt + c through the three points: c = C₀ b = (C₊₁ − C₋₁)/2 a = (C₋₁ − 2C₀ + C₊₁)/2 the vertex is at t = −b/(2a), i.e. C₋₁ − C₊₁ δ = ─────────────────────── and d = d̂ + δ 2 (C₋₁ − 2C₀ + C₊₁) ALWAYS CLAMP δ to [−½, +½]. The denominator is the discrete curvature; when it is near zero — a flat cost curve, which is exactly a textureless region — the vertex flies off to infinity. Every real implementation clamps, and the clamp changes the estimator's statistics, which the measurement below has to account for rather than ignore.

The variance, derived

propagating image noise into the sub-pixel estimatewrite e(x, d) = I_L(x) − I_R(x − d) over the window, and let both images carry independent Gaussian noise of variance σ_I². Near the true disparity d*, C(d) = ∑ e² the SSD dC/dd = −2 ∑ e · I′_R chain rule d²C/dd² ≈ 2 ∑ (I′_R)² = 2G where G ≡ ∑ I_x² drop the e·I″ term, which vanishes at d* at d* the residual e is pure noise of variance 2σ_I² (one σ_I² from each image), so Var(dC/dd)|_{d*} = 4 · 2σ_I² · G = 8σ_I² G and one Newton step Δd = −(dC/dd)/(d²C/dd²) has Var(Δd) = 8σ_I² G / (2G)² = 2σ_I² / G σ_d = σ_I √(2/G) Read it as a statement about texture: G = ∑ I_x² is the SQUARED GRADIENT ENERGY of the window, which is the (1,1) entry of part 4's structure tensor summed over the window. A flat window has G ≈ 0 and infinite variance; a strongly textured one has large G and small variance. This is the same quantity that decides whether a corner is localisable in part 4 §05, arriving here as a depth uncertainty.
The version usually quoted, and the difference. The classic result is written Var(d) = σ_I²/a with a the curvature of the cost as a function of d. That is the same formula with the noise charged to one image rather than two: if only one view is noisy the residual variance is σ_I² rather than 2σ_I² and the factor of two disappears. Pixel locking

The parabola is fitted to a cost function that is not a parabola, and the mismatch is systematic rather than random: the estimate is pulled towards the nearest integer disparity. The size of the effect depends entirely on how much energy the texture has near the sampling limit.

On texture with energy near the sampling limit it is an unmistakable S-curve, 0.087 px peak to peak and reaching −0.067 px, negative below the half-pixel point and positive above it: every estimate is pulled towards the NEAREST INTEGER. A disparity histogram of a whole image then shows visible spikes at the integers, which is where the name comes from. The cause is aliasing, which is part 3's subject: a sampled signal with content above half the sampling rate has a cost function that is not even approximately parabolic near its minimum. The fixes are to low-pass before matching, which costs resolution; to fit a model that respects the sampling, rather than a parabola; or to use more than three samples. Note that the swing is 24 times larger at 4 px than at 34 px, while the NOISE-driven error at these settings is the same either way — so the bias, not the variance, is what limits sub-pixel accuracy on fine texture.

Confidence, for free

The same denominator that can be dangerous is also the most useful by-product of the fit. a = (C₋₁ − 2C₀ + C₊₁)/2 is the curvature of the cost at its minimum, and Var(d) = 2σ_I²/(2a) turns it directly into an error bar. A dense stereo output should carry it. Related cheap confidence measures: the ratio of the best cost to the second-best distinct minimum (which catches the periodic-pattern failure of §24 specifically), and agreement between the left-to-right and right-to-left maps (§20). None of them is expensive and all of them are usually thrown away.

24 The failure modes, and which of them are fixable

Dense stereo fails in a small number of characteristic ways, and they are worth knowing individually because they have different remedies — and because two of them are not remediable at all, only detectable.

FailureWhy it happensMeasured, on the synthetic pairRemedy
Textureless regionsThe cost curve is flat: many disparities explain the data equally well. There is no information in the data — this is not an estimation problem but an under-determination.Winner-take-all, SAD 5 × 5: 46.9% bad in the flat patch, against 3.9% on textured backgroundA prior. Repeated structureThe cost curve is multi-modal, with one minimum per period of the pattern, and they are of nearly equal depth. Here the data actively lies: the wrong answer fits as well as the right one.SAD 5 × 5: 46.7% bad in the striped region; the single probe of §20 chose d = 2 against a truth of 8.6Widen the aggregation window past the period; use a wider baseline so the disparity range covers fewer periods; use a global method with a smoothness term that prefers a consistent alias (SGM: 0.0% here); or detect it from the ratio of best to second-best minimum and refuse to answer.

Slanted surfacesA window assumes constant disparity within it, so a sloping surface is reconstructed as a staircase (§21).SAD 5 × 5 across the slanted plane: a staircase of treads several pixels wide instead of a rampSlanted support windows, plane-fitting per segment, or a smoothness term that penalises the second difference of disparity rather than the first.
Depth discontinuitiesA window straddling an edge votes for a compromise, so the foreground fattens by roughly the window radius.Within 2 px of an edge the error rises with window size, 12.7% at r = 1 to 17.7% at r = 7, while it falls everywhere elseShiftable windows (§21) cut it by a factor of five or six; adaptive-weight aggregation; intensity-modulated smoothness in a global method.
Thin structuresA foreground object narrower than the window is swallowed by it. It also violates the ordering constraint, so dynamic-programming formulations that enforce ordering cannot represent the right answer at all.—Small windows plus a global method; do not enforce ordering; or accept that a chair leg will not appear.
Transparency and reflectionA pixel genuinely observes two surfaces at two depths. The model — one disparity per pixel — cannot express the answer.—None, within this formulation. Layered or multi-hypothesis models are required.
The two categories, and why the distinction matters. Textureless and occluded regions are cases where the data does not contain the answer. The honest responses are to interpolate from a prior and say so, or to return "unknown". Repeated structure and specular highlights are worse: the data contains a confident wrong answer, and any confidence measure derived from the cost curve alone will endorse it. That is why the useful confidence measures (§23) look at the second-best minimum and at left–right agreement rather than at the depth of the winner.
