02 Epipolar geometry: why the search is one-dimensional

Three points — the two camera centres and the world point — define a plane. Both images of the world point lie on that plane's intersection with their own image plane. Nothing else on this page is as important as that sentence.

The construction, in order

Fix two cameras with centres c₀ and c₁, and a scene point p visible in both. Five objects follow immediately, and each has a name because each does work later.

ObjectDefinitionWhat it is for
BaselineThe line through c₀ and c₁. Its direction is t up to sign and scale.The axis the whole construction rotates about. If its length is zero there is no epipolar geometry at all (§11).
Epipolar plane πThe plane through c₀, c₁ and p.One per scene point. As p moves, π rotates about the baseline — the planes form a pencil hinged on the baseline.
Epipolar lines l₀, l₁π ∩ image plane 0 and π ∩ image plane 1.x₀ lies on l₀ and x₁ on l₁, by construction. This is the 2-D → 1-D collapse.
Epipoles e₀, e₁The image of the other camera centre: e₀ is where c₁ projects in image 0, e₁ is where c₀ projects in image 1.Every epipolar line in image j passes through e_j, because the baseline lies in every epipolar plane. §03.
Epipolar constraintThe algebraic statement that c₀, c₁, p are coplanar.One scalar equation per correspondence — the equation that E and F encode. §04, §05.

The coplanarity, written out

Put camera 0 at the origin in canonical orientation, so the world frame is camera 0's frame; camera 1 is then at c₁ = −Rᵀt. Three vectors lie in the epipolar plane: the baseline c₁ − c₀, the ray from c₀ to p, and the ray from c₁ to p. Three coplanar vectors have zero scalar triple product, and that is the whole derivation.

the epipolar constraint as a triple productthe three vectors, all expressed in camera 0's frame: baseline b = c₁ − c₀ = −Rᵀt ray to p from c₀ r₀ = x̂₀ (any positive multiple of it) ray to p from c₁ r₁ = Rᵀx̂₁ (rotated into camera 0's frame) coplanar ⟺ det[b, r₀, r₁] = 0 ⟺ (Rᵀx̂₁) · (Rᵀt × x̂₀) = 0 and since Rᵀ is orthogonal it preserves both the dot and the cross product, (Rᵀx̂₁) · (Rᵀt × x̂₀) = (Rᵀx̂₁) · Rᵀ(t × Rx̂₀) = x̂₁ · (t × Rx̂₀) = x̂₁ᵀ[t]ₓRx̂₀ ⇒ x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0 with E = [t]ₓR

There is a second derivation, algebraically shorter and geometrically emptier, which is worth having because it shows where the cross product comes from rather than being pulled out of a hat. Write the rigid motion of the 3-D point between the two camera frames and then annihilate the translation.

the same thing, by eliminating the unknown depthsthe point, seen twice, at unknown depths d₀ and d₁ along its normalised rays: d₁ x̂₁ = R (d₀ x̂₀) + t the rigid motion cross both sides with t, which annihilates the t on the right: d₁ [t]ₓ x̂₁ = d₀ [t]ₓ R x̂₀ now dot both sides with x̂₁. The left side becomes x̂₁ᵀ[t]ₓx̂₁ = x̂₁·(t × x̂₁) = 0, because a triple product with a repeated entry vanishes. Hence d₀ x̂₁ᵀ ([t]ₓ R) x̂₀ = 0 and, for any point with d₀ ≠ 0, x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0.
Read the two derivations against each other. The first says the constraint is coplanarity, and would still be true if you had never heard of a matrix. The second says the constraint is what survives after the two unknown depths have been eliminated — which is why it is a necessary condition on a correspondence but not a sufficient one. A pair (x₀, x₁) satisfying x̂₁ᵀEx̂₀ = 0 is only guaranteed to have intersecting rays; whether they intersect in front of both cameras, or behind, or at infinity, is a further question and it is §13's.

The collapse, counted

Without the constraint, a match for x₀ could be any of the W × H pixels of image 1. With it, the candidates are the pixels of image 1 that lie on the line l₁ — at most √(W² + H²) of them, and in a rectified pair (§17) exactly the pixels of one row within the disparity range. For the running rig's 640 × 480 images:

the size of the search, before and afterunconstrained 2-D search: 640 × 480 = 307 200 candidates constrained to an epipolar line: ≤ √(640² + 480²) = 800 candidates rectified, one row: 640 candidates rectified, disparity range 0–63: 64 candidates reduction factor: 307 200 / 64 = 4 800 × and the reduction is EXACT, not a heuristic prune: for a rigid scene and correct calibration, the true match cannot lie off the line. Everything gained here is gained for free — but it is bought with the assumption of rigidity, which §24 and §32 will both spend time attacking.
The epipolar plane sweeping about the baseline, and the line it draws in the second image drag the point in image 0, or move it in depth — and watch what does not change
The one thing to take from that figure. Slide the depth. The point in image 1 moves; the line it moves along does not. That is the entire content of epipolar geometry: the line is a function of the point in image 0 and the relative pose, and of nothing else. Depth chooses a position on the line, and depth is exactly the unknown you are trying to recover. Slide the lateral position instead and the line rotates about the epipole — the epipole is the one point common to every epipolar line, because the baseline lies in every epipolar plane.

03 Epipoles, the pencil of lines, and where the epipole hides

Every epipolar plane contains the baseline. Therefore every epipolar line contains the image of the baseline's far end. That single point organises the whole family.

Why every epipolar line passes through the epipole

The baseline joins c₀ to c₁, and by definition it lies in every epipolar plane — the plane is spanned by the baseline and one scene ray. So the baseline's intersection with image plane 1 lies on every epipolar line of image 1. That intersection is the projection of c₀ into camera 1, which is the epipole e₁. The lines therefore form a pencil: a one-parameter family of lines through a common point, parameterised by the rotation angle of the epipolar plane about the baseline.

the epipoles, three equivalent definitionsgeometric e₀ = the image of c₁ in camera 0, e₁ = the image of c₀ in camera 1 projective e₀ = P₀ c̃₁ e₁ = P₁ c̃₀ algebraic F e₀ = 0 Fᵀ e₁ = 0 i.e. e₀ is the RIGHT null vector of F and e₁ is the LEFT null vector. The algebraic form is the one that gets used, because it needs no cameras — only F. It is also the reason F must be rank-deficient: it has to have a null vector at all.

The algebraic definition falls straight out of the pencil. Every line l₁ = Fx̄₀ passes through e₁, so e₁ᵀFx̄₀ = 0 for every x̄₀, so the row vector e₁ᵀF annihilates everything, so e₁ᵀF = 0ᵀ. The same argument transposed gives Fe₀ = 0. Two null vectors, one on each side — and a 3 × 3 matrix with a null vector on each side is exactly a matrix of rank at most 2. §06 makes that precise.

Where the epipole sits, and what that tells you about the motion

The epipole is the image of the direction of travel. That makes its position a direct read-out of what the camera did, and it is worth being able to recognise the three regimes on sight.

MotionEpipoleWhat the image looks like
Forward (translation along the optical axis)At or near the principal point, well inside the frame.Epipolar lines radiate from a point in the middle of the picture, and the apparent motion of every feature is outward from it. This point is the focus of expansion. It is what forward motion looks like, and it is why a hallway seen from a moving car appears to explode outward from the vanishing point ahead.
Sideways (translation in the image plane)At infinity, in the direction of travel.Epipolar lines are parallel. This is the configuration a rectified stereo pair is deliberately warped into (§17), because parallel horizontal epipolar lines are image rows and image rows are what memory is laid out in.
ObliqueOutside the frame, at a finite point.The general case, and the running rig's case: e₀ = (5320, 490) and e₁ = (2420, 414) pixels for a 640 × 480 image. The lines converge, but only well off-picture, so they look almost parallel inside the frame. This is the usual situation and it is why "nearly parallel" is not the same as "parallel".
Pure rotationUndefined.t = 0 makes E = [0]ₓR = 0 and F = 0. There is no epipolar geometry, because there is no baseline and no epipolar plane. The two images are related by a homography instead, and that is Part 6's object, not this page's. §11 shows the estimator collapsing when you ask it for an F anyway.
A distinction worth being pedantic about. The epipole is where the other camera centre projects. The focus of expansion is where the apparent motion field radiates from. They coincide when the rotation is zero, and only then. Under simultaneous rotation and translation the motion field is the sum of a rotational component — which depends only on the rotation and not at all on the scene — and a translational component which radiates from the epipole. Cancel the rotation and the epipole becomes the focus of expansion again; this is exactly the trick used when the rotation is known independently, and it reduces the estimation problem to finding one 2-D point rather than a whole matrix.

04 The essential matrix

One 3 × 3 matrix, five degrees of freedom, and it contains the entire relative pose of a calibrated pair — the rotation exactly and the translation up to length.

definitionE = [t]ₓ R and the constraint it enforces: x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0 where x̂ⱼ = Kⱼ⁻¹ x̄ⱼ is the NORMALISED ray, and (R, t) is the pose of camera 1 relative to camera 0: p₁ = R p₀ + t. E is defined only up to a non-zero scale, because the constraint is homogeneous — and the missing scale is exactly ‖t‖, the length of the baseline, which no amount of imagery can supply.

Reading the definition as a composition of two maps

It is worth not treating E as an opaque product. It is two operations applied in order, and the order is right-to-left as always.

what E does to a ray, one factor at a timex̂₀ a ray direction, in camera 0's frame R x̂₀ the same direction, re-expressed in camera 1's frame [t]ₓ (R x̂₀) = t × (R x̂₀) the NORMAL of the plane spanned by t and that ray — which is precisely the epipolar plane so l₁ = E x̂₀ is the epipolar plane's normal, written in camera 1's frame; and "x̂₁ lies in that plane" is the statement x̂₁ · l₁ = 0, i.e. x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = 0. The line l₁ = E x̂₀ is a LINE, not a point. E maps points to lines. That is why it cannot be a homography, cannot be inverted, and must be singular.

The worked example, in full

From the running rig: R = R_z(2°)R_y(−8°)R_x(3°) and t = (−0.973851, −0.078733, −0.240656). Multiplying out:

the three matrices, to six decimals ⎡ 0.000000 0.240656 -0.078733 ⎤ ⎡ 0.989665 -0.042131 -0.137071 ⎤ [t]ₓ = ⎢ -0.240656 0.000000 0.973851 ⎥ R = ⎢ 0.034560 0.997767 -0.057154 ⎥ ⎣ 0.078733 -0.973851 0.000000 ⎦ ⎣ 0.139173 0.051827 0.988911 ⎦ ⎡ -0.002640 0.236038 -0.091614 ⎤ E = ⎢ -0.102634 0.060610 0.996039 ⎥ singular values of E: ⎣ 0.044263 -0.974994 0.044868 ⎦ (1.006231, 1.006231, 0.000000) The two equal non-zero singular values are not a coincidence and not an accident of these numbers. They are the DEFINING algebraic property of an essential matrix, and §06 proves it. Their common value is ‖t‖ = 1.006231 m, which is the baseline — which is why an E recovered from images alone has an arbitrary common value there instead.
The trap that catches everyone once. E operates on normalised rays x̂ = K⁻¹x̄, not on pixels. Feed it pixel coordinates and the constraint is not approximately violated, it is violated by a factor of roughly f² — for f = 500 that is a quarter of a million. The figure below puts both residuals on the same logarithmic axis so the size of the mistake is unmissable. The matrix that does take pixels is F, and that is §05.

05 The fundamental matrix, and how to measure a violation of it

The same constraint, written in pixels instead of rays. It asks for no calibration, which is why it is the one you can actually estimate from two photographs of unknown provenance.

definition, by substitutionstart from the calibrated constraint and substitute x̂ⱼ = Kⱼ⁻¹x̄ⱼ: x̂₁ᵀ E x̂₀ = (K₁⁻¹x̄₁)ᵀ E (K₀⁻¹x̄₀) = x̄₁ᵀ (K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹) x̄₀ = x̄₁ᵀ F x̄₀ = 0 F = K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹ = K₁⁻ᵀ [t]ₓ R K₀⁻¹ and, going the other way, if the calibrations ARE known: E = K₁ᵀ F K₀. Note the transposes. K₁ appears TRANSPOSED and INVERTED on the left, K₀ merely inverted on the right, and getting this wrong produces a matrix that is almost right — which is worse than one that is obviously wrong.

Why the gap between the two matters more than it looks. The extra two degrees of freedom in F are not free information — they are ignorance. An F is consistent with a whole family of (K₀, K₁, R, t) that differ by a projective transformation of space. The reconstruction you get from F alone has the right incidence structure — points on lines stay on lines — but a cube can come out as a general parallelepiped and a right angle as any angle at all. Turning that into a metric model is self-calibration, §30, and it is the hardest thing on this page.

Three ways to measure "how badly is this constraint violated"

The constraint is x̄₁ᵀFx̄₀ = 0, so the obvious residual is the left-hand side itself. It is also, on its own, close to useless, because it is not measured in any unit anyone cares about — scale F by 10 and every residual scales by 10. Three progressively better answers:

algebraic, geometric, and the one estimators actually usewrite l₁ = F x̄₀ = (a₁, b₁, c₁) and l₀ = Fᵀx̄₁ = (a₀, b₀, c₀) r = x̄₁ᵀ F x̄₀ (the same scalar appears in both dot products) 1 · ALGEBRAIC residual r Cheap, linear in the entries of F, and what the eight-point algorithm actually minimises. Depends on the scale of F and on where the points are in the image. 2 · SYMMETRIC EPIPOLAR distance d₀ = |r| / √(a₀² + b₀²) in image 0 d₁ = |r| / √(a₁² + b₁²) in image 1 d_sym = √((d₀² + d₁²)/2) Real pixels, scale-free, symmetric in the two views. This is what every error on this page is reported in. 3 · SAMPSON distance d_S² = r² / (a₀² + b₀² + a₁² + b₁²) The first-order approximation to the true reprojection error — the distance you would have to move BOTH points to make the constraint hold exactly. It costs one extra division over the algebraic residual and it is the right inlier test inside RANSAC (§11) and the right cost inside a two-view refinement.

All three vanish together on an exact correspondence, so they agree about what is right and disagree about how wrong everything else is. That disagreement is the whole subject of §08: an estimator that minimises the wrong one gets a systematically wrong answer, and the wrongness is measurable.

06 Rank 2, and how many numbers there really are

Both matrices are singular, and that is not a numerical detail — it is the algebraic shadow of the fact that all the epipolar lines meet at a point.

The rank is exactly 2, and here is why

a three-line proof, for a true E and a true F[t]ₓ is skew-symmetric and 3 × 3. Its null space is spanned by t itself, because t × t = 0, and it contains nothing else when t ≠ 0. So rank[t]ₓ = 2 exactly. R is orthogonal, hence invertible, so multiplying by it changes no rank: rank(E) = rank([t]ₓR) = rank([t]ₓ) = 2. K₀ and K₁ are upper triangular with non-zero diagonal, hence invertible, so rank(F) = rank(K₁⁻ᵀ E K₀⁻¹) = rank(E) = 2. A 3 × 3 matrix of rank 2 has a one-dimensional null space on each side. Those two null directions are the two epipoles, which is the algebra of §03 arriving from the other end. And rank 2 is equivalent to det F = 0, which is ONE polynomial equation in the entries — the constraint that costs F its eighth degree of freedom.

Stated as a theorem: "the fundamental matrix has rank 2."

Stated correctly: a true fundamental matrix — one that arises from an actual pair of cameras with a non-zero baseline — has rank exactly 2. A matrix estimated from noisy correspondences by the eight-point algorithm has rank 3 with probability 1, and its smallest singular value is a small non-zero number. Rank 2 must then be imposed, and imposing it changes the matrix. §09 is entirely about how much, and about the sense in which the change is optimal.

The extra property that E has and F does not

F's only constraint is det F = 0. E has a stronger one, and it is worth deriving because it is what makes the five-point algorithm possible and what a decomposition of E silently assumes.

why an essential matrix has two equal singular valuesE Eᵀ = [t]ₓ R Rᵀ [t]ₓᵀ = [t]ₓ[t]ₓᵀ = −[t]ₓ² and the standard identity [t]ₓ² = t tᵀ − ‖t‖² I gives E Eᵀ = ‖t‖² I − t tᵀ. Now read off the eigenvalues of that by inspection: for any v ⊥ t : (‖t‖²I − ttᵀ)v = ‖t‖² v eigenvalue ‖t‖², twice for v = t : (‖t‖²I − ttᵀ)t = ‖t‖²t − ‖t‖²t = 0 eigenvalue 0, once The singular values of E are the square roots: (‖t‖, ‖t‖, 0). AND THE CONVERSE HOLDS: any 3 × 3 matrix whose singular values are (σ, σ, 0) with σ > 0 IS an essential matrix, i.e. it factors as [t]ₓR for some rotation R and some t. That converse is what makes §12's decomposition well posed.

Counting the degrees of freedom, twice

two independent counts, and they agreeE — count the parameters: R : 3 t : 3 overall scale : −1 → 5 E — count the constraints: 9 entries − 1 (scale) − 3 (the essential conditions) → 5 [the essential matrices form a 6-dimensional cone inside the 9-dimensional space of 3 × 3 matrices, i.e. codimension 3: one constraint for det E = 0 and two more for σ₁ = σ₂. All three are packaged in the single matrix identity 2EEᵀE = tr(EEᵀ)E, which holds if and only if the matrix is essential.] F — count the parameters: E : 5 K₀ : ≤ 5 K₁ : ≤ 5 but F only sees the combination K₁⁻ᵀ[t]ₓRK₀⁻¹, and the count that always works is the second one: F — count the constraints: 9 entries − 1 (scale) − 1 (det F = 0) → 7 Hence the minimum number of point correspondences: each gives ONE linear equation, so you need at least 7 for F and 5 for E. Eight is used for F anyway because eight makes the system linear (§07); seven makes it cubic (§10); five makes it a tenth- degree polynomial and was a genuinely hard result.
What rank 2 buys: the epipolar lines meet at a point, and only if the rank is 2 the perturbation slider starts at zero — the lines are exactly concurrent — then break it
The practical consequence. If you skip the rank-2 enforcement of §09, your epipolar lines do not meet. Every downstream step then quietly degrades: the epipoles are ill-defined, so rectification (§17) has no well-posed target; triangulated rays never intersect, so triangulation falls back on a least-squares fudge; and the decomposition of §12 has no null direction to read the translation off. The enforcement is not cosmetic tidying, it is putting the estimate back inside the set of things that can be a fundamental matrix at all.
