07 The eight-point algorithm

Each correspondence contributes one linear equation in the nine unknown entries. Stack them, take the smallest right singular vector, and you have a fundamental matrix. It is the simplest estimator on this page and, unmodified, the worst.

One correspondence, one equation

Write x̄₀ = (u₀, v₀, 1) and x̄₁ = (u₁, v₁, 1) and expand the quadratic form. Every term is one entry of F multiplied by a known product of measurements, so the equation is linear in the entries of F even though it is bilinear in the measurements.

the expansion, in full ⎡ f₀₀ f₀₁ f₀₂ ⎤ ⎡ u₀ ⎤ x̄₁ᵀ F x̄₀ = (u₁ v₁ 1) ⎢ f₁₀ f₁₁ f₁₂ ⎥ ⎢ v₀ ⎥ = 0 ⎣ f₂₀ f₂₁ f₂₂ ⎦ ⎣ 1 ⎦ = u₁u₀ f₀₀ + u₁v₀ f₀₁ + u₁ f₀₂ + v₁u₀ f₁₀ + v₁v₀ f₁₁ + v₁ f₁₂ + u₀ f₂₀ + v₀ f₂₁ + f₂₂ so with f = (f₀₀, f₀₁, f₀₂, f₁₀, f₁₁, f₁₂, f₂₀, f₂₁, f₂₂)ᵀ the i-th row of the design matrix A is aᵢ = ( u₁u₀ , u₁v₀ , u₁ , v₁u₀ , v₁v₀ , v₁ , u₀ , v₀ , 1 ) and the whole problem is A f = 0, A of size N × 9.
solving a homogeneous system, which is the same step five times on this pageminimise ‖A f‖² subject to ‖f‖ = 1 (the constraint is needed: f = 0 is a solution of Af = 0 and is useless) The minimiser is the RIGHT SINGULAR VECTOR of A belonging to its SMALLEST singular value, and the achieved minimum is that singular value. This is a theorem about the SVD, proved on the singular value decomposition page, and it is used here for F, in §09 for the rank-2 projection, in §15 for triangulation, in §26 for factorization, and in §10 for the seven-point null space. Learn it once.

Why "eight" and not "nine". Nine unknowns, but the equation is homogeneous, so scaling F by any non-zero constant changes nothing. Eight independent equations therefore suffice to determine the one-dimensional solution space. And the rank constraint det F = 0 is not used at this stage: the eight-point algorithm deliberately ignores it, gets a rank-3 answer, and repairs it afterwards. That two-step structure — solve in the larger, easier space, then project back onto the constraint set — recurs throughout this page and is worth naming.

08 Normalisation: the two lines of code that change everything

Look again at that design row. Some entries are products of two pixel coordinates and are of order 10⁵. Others are single coordinates, of order 10². One is exactly 1. A least-squares solver treats them as equally trustworthy, and they are not.

the design row for one real correspondence of the running rigx₀ = (360, 210) px, x₁ = (178.91, 172.75) px: aᵢ = ( 64 407 , 37 571 , 178.9 , 62 190 , 36 278 , 172.8 , 360 , 210 , 1 ) └──────── ~10⁵ ────────┘ └──── ~10⁵ ────┘ └─ 10² ─┘ └1┘ Five orders of magnitude between the largest and smallest column. A perturbation of one pixel in u₀ moves the first column by ~180 and the seventh by 1 — so the solver weights the first correspondence's contribution to f₀₀ about 180 times as heavily as its contribution to f₂₀, for no reason whatsoever except where the image origin is. Move the principal point and the answer changes. That is not an estimator, it is an accident.

The fix, in three steps

isotropic normalisation, applied independently to each image1 · translate so the centroid of the points is at the origin 2 · scale so the MEAN distance from the origin is √2 (so the "average" point is (1, 1) and every column of A is O(1)) 3 · solve for F̃ in the normalised coordinates, then undo the transforms: ⎡ s 0 −s·μᵤ ⎤ s = √2 / mean‖xᵢ − μ‖ Tⱼ = ⎢ 0 s −s·μᵥ ⎥ and F = T₁ᵀ F̃ T₀ ⎣ 0 0 1 ⎦ The undoing is exact, not approximate: if x̃ = Tx̄ then x̄₁ᵀF x̄₀ = x̃₁ᵀ(T₁⁻ᵀFT₀⁻¹)x̃₀, so the F̃ that satisfies the constraint in normalised coordinates corresponds to F = T₁ᵀF̃T₀ in pixels. Two lines of code and one line of undo. Why √2 and not 1: with mean distance √2 in two dimensions, the "typical" point has both coordinates of order 1, so the products in the design row are also of order 1 and no column dominates. A cheaper variant sets the coordinate VARIANCE to 1, which avoids a per-point square root and behaves the same.
What normalisation is and is not. It is a change of basis that makes the numerical problem well conditioned, and it costs nothing. It is not a change to the model, and it is not a fix for a bad configuration: normalising a planar scene still leaves the null space three-dimensional (§11), because that degeneracy is geometric, not numerical. Normalisation cures conditioning. Nothing cures degeneracy except a different scene or a different model.

09 Enforcing rank 2, and what it costs

The eight-point solution is a nine-vector. Reshaped, it is a matrix of rank 3, because nothing in the linear system asked for anything else. Putting it back on the rank-2 manifold is one SVD, and the projection is optimal in a sense that can be stated exactly.

the projectiongiven the raw estimate F̂ = U diag(σ₁, σ₂, σ₃) Vᵀ with σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ σ₃ > 0, F = U diag(σ₁, σ₂, 0) Vᵀ. EXACT STATEMENT (Eckart–Young–Mirsky). Among all matrices of rank ≤ 2, this F is the UNIQUE closest to F̂ in the Frobenius norm, and also a closest one in the spectral norm. The distance it moves is ‖F − F̂‖_F = σ₃. The theorem is proved on the singular value decomposition page; it is used here, not re-derived. WHAT IT IS NOT. It is not the rank-2 matrix that best fits the CORRESPONDENCES. It is the rank-2 matrix nearest to a particular nine-vector, and that nine-vector was itself chosen by minimising an algebraic residual under an arbitrary normalisation. Two approximations, composed. The estimator that actually minimises reprojection error is non-linear, and is §27.
The corresponding projection for E. An essential matrix needs (σ, σ, 0), not merely (σ₁, σ₂, 0). The nearest essential matrix in the Frobenius norm therefore replaces both leading singular values by their mean: minimising (σ₁ − a)² + (σ₂ − a)² + σ₃² over a gives a = (σ₁ + σ₂)/2, so E = U·diag(a, a, 0)·Vᵀ. Doing this before the decomposition of §12 matters, because that decomposition assumes exactly this structure — feed it a matrix with unequal leading singular values and the two candidate rotations it produces are not rotations.

The tempting summary: "enforce rank 2 because it improves the estimate."

The accurate one: enforce rank 2 because an estimate that is not rank 2 is not a fundamental matrix, and every consumer of it assumes it is one. You pay a few per cent of fitting accuracy for that. If you want both — validity and the best geometric fit — you do not choose between them, you run §27's non-linear refinement with the rank constraint built into the parameterisation.

10 Seven points, five points, and why fewer is worth so much

Eight points make the problem linear. Seven make it cubic. Five make it a tenth-degree polynomial and took twenty years to solve properly. Every one of those steps down was worth taking, and the reason is a formula from Part 4.

The seven-point algorithm

use the rank constraint instead of an eighth pointWith N = 7, A is 7 × 9 and generically has a TWO-dimensional null space. Take its two smallest right singular vectors f₀ and f₁, reshape them into F₀ and F₁; every matrix F(α) = α F₀ + (1 − α) F₁ satisfies all seven correspondences exactly. Now impose the constraint the linear system ignored: det F(α) = 0 which is a CUBIC in α, because the determinant of a 3 × 3 matrix is cubic in its entries and the entries are affine in α. A cubic with real coefficients has one or three real roots, so this returns ONE or THREE candidate fundamental matrices. Disambiguating: score each candidate against correspondences NOT used in the sample. Inside RANSAC this is free — the consensus step already scores every candidate against every match, so a 7-point sample simply generates up to three hypotheses per draw.

The five-point algorithm, in outline

With calibration known, the unknown is E, which has only 5 degrees of freedom. Five correspondences give five linear equations, leaving a four-dimensional null space, so E = xE₀ + yE₁ + zE₂ + wE₃ up to scale — three unknowns. The remaining equations are the essential-matrix conditions of §06: det E = 0, plus the nine scalar equations packed into 2EEᵀE − tr(EEᵀ)E = 0. Eliminating variables from that polynomial system yields a single tenth-degree univariate polynomial, so there are up to ten candidate essential matrices, again disambiguated by scoring. It is genuinely intricate; what matters here is only why anyone bothered.

why the sample size dominates everything — the RANSAC iteration count from part 4N = log(1 − p) / log(1 − wˢ) p = target success probability, w = inlier rate, s = sample size at p = 0.99: s = 4 (homography) s = 5 (E) s = 7 (F) s = 8 (F, linear) w = 0.70 17 26 54 78 w = 0.50 72 146 588 1177 w = 0.30 567 1893 21 055 70 188 Read the bottom row. At a 30% inlier rate — which a wide-baseline pair with repeated architecture will hand you without embarrassment — the five-point algorithm needs under two thousand samples and the linear eight-point needs seventy thousand. That is the entire justification for the algebraic pain: N grows EXPONENTIALLY in s, so each point removed from the minimal sample divides the work by roughly 1/w.
And the five-point algorithm buys something the seven-point cannot. Because it estimates E rather than F, it works on a planar scene and under pure rotation without collapsing in the way §11 is about to show — the calibration constraints supply the information the geometry does not. That robustness, not the smaller sample alone, is why calibrated pipelines prefer it.

11 Robust estimation, and the configurations where nothing helps

Correspondences from Part 4 contain outliers, so the estimator has to be robust. But there is a second, nastier failure that robustness does not touch: configurations in which the data are all perfect inliers and the answer is still not determined.

The robust loop, in one paragraph

Sample 7 correspondences (or 8, or 5 in the calibrated case); fit; score every match by its Sampson distance (§05) against a threshold set from the expected localisation noise, typically 1–3 px; keep the largest consensus set; update the inlier rate w and hence N adaptively; finally refit on all inliers and refine non-linearly. Part 4 §27 owns the sampling theory, the adaptive-N update and the breakdown analysis; nothing about it changes here except the model and the residual. What does change is the failure mode, and that is the rest of this section.

The three degenerate configurations, with the collapse measured

Recall that the eight-point system is Af = 0 with A of size N × 9. A well-posed problem has rank A = 8: a one-dimensional null space, one answer up to scale. Degeneracy means the rank drops further, and every vector in the enlarged null space is an F that fits every correspondence exactly. And note WHICH F you get: whichever one the SVD happens to return, which is the null vector of smallest norm-weighted residual, which is determined by rounding error. The epipole it implies is arbitrary. The reconstruction it implies is nonsense. Nothing in the residuals says so, because the residuals are all zero.

The dangerous belief: "RANSAC found 340 inliers out of 380, so the geometry is good."

What actually happened, possibly: the scene was a building façade, i.e. a plane. Every one of those 380 matches is consistent with a three-parameter family of fundamental matrices, RANSAC returned one of them, and it is wrong. A large consensus set is evidence about the correspondences, not about the configuration. The standard defences are: fit a homography as well and compare model-selection scores, so a plane is detected rather than ignored; test the last two singular values of A for a rank gap; and, in the calibrated case, use the five-point algorithm, which does not have this degeneracy at all.
