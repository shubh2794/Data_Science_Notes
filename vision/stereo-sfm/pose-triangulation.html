12 Decomposing E into four candidate poses

An essential matrix contains a rotation and a translation direction. Extracting them is one SVD and a fixed pair of orthogonal matrices — but it produces four answers, and only geometry, not algebra, can choose between them.

the decompositionlet E = U diag(1, 1, 0) Vᵀ, with det U = det V = +1 (flip the sign of a column of each if not — this is legal, because E = U Σ Vᵀ = (−U) Σ (−V)ᵀ). define two fixed orthogonal matrices ⎡ 0 −1 0 ⎤ ⎡ 0 1 0 ⎤ W = ⎢ 1 0 0 ⎥ Z = ⎢ −1 0 0 ⎥ note Z W = diag(1, 1, 0) ⎣ 0 0 1 ⎦ ⎣ 0 0 0 ⎦ then, with S = U Z Uᵀ (skew, with null vector u₃) and R = U W Vᵀ, S R = U Z Uᵀ U W Vᵀ = U (Z W) Vᵀ = U diag(1,1,0) Vᵀ = E. ✔ So E = [t]ₓR with t ∝ u₃, the LAST COLUMN OF U — which is exactly the left null vector of E, i.e. the epipole. The four candidates are (R_a, +t̂) (R_a, −t̂) (R_b, +t̂) (R_b, −t̂) R_a = U W Vᵀ R_b = U Wᵀ Vᵀ t̂ = u₃ = third column of U and both R_a and R_b have determinant +1 once the signs of U and V have been fixed, so both are genuine rotations. Nothing algebraic distinguishes them.

What the four candidates actually are, geometrically

candidate 1
The right one
All scene points reconstruct in front of both cameras.
candidate 2
Translation reversed
Same rotation, camera 1 moved to the opposite side. Every point now reconstructs behind one of the cameras. The two views are still "consistent" — the rays still meet — but they meet on the wrong side of the optical centres.
candidate 3
The twisted pair
R_b = R_a composed with a rotation of 180° about the baseline. Camera 1 has been flipped upside down about the line joining the centres. The epipolar geometry is identical — same epipoles, same lines — which is precisely why E cannot tell them apart.
candidate 4
Both
Twisted and reversed.
the twisted pair, verifiedR_b R_aᵀ = U Wᵀ Vᵀ V Wᵀ Uᵀ = U (Wᵀ)² Uᵀ = U diag(−1, −1, 1) Uᵀ and diag(−1, −1, 1) is a rotation by 180° about the third axis, which U maps to u₃ = t̂. So R_b = [rotation by π about t̂] · R_a, exactly. This is why the two rotations produce the same epipolar lines: rotating camera 1 by 180° about the baseline maps every epipolar plane to itself. The pencil is unchanged. The RECONSTRUCTION is not — half the points end up behind the camera — and that is the hook §13 pulls on.
The one that catches people out. The decomposition assumes the singular values are (1, 1, 0). An E computed as K₁ᵀFK₀ from a noisy F will not have them, and if you feed it to the formulas above, U and V are still orthogonal so R_a and R_b are still rotations — but they are the rotations belonging to the nearest essential matrix, not to yours, and no warning is issued. Project onto diag(a, a, 0) with a = (σ₁ + σ₂)/2 first, as §09 said, and check the ratio σ₂/σ₁: if it is far from 1, the calibration or the correspondences are wrong and the pose you are about to extract is fiction.

13 Cheirality: the constraint that light travels forwards

The projective camera of Part 1 is happy to image points behind itself. Real cameras are not. That asymmetry — the only piece of physics in this whole section — is what picks one pose out of four.

the testfor each candidate (R, t): triangulate every correspondence → a 3-D point p depth in camera 0: z₀ = p_z (camera 0 is at the origin, depth in camera 1: z₁ = (R p + t)_z canonically oriented) count the points with z₀ > 0 AND z₁ > 0 choose the candidate with the largest count. In the noise-free case ONE point decides it, and the counts are 40/40 for the correct candidate and 0/40 for each of the other three — measured, in the figure below. With noise, and especially with distant points, individual points do flip, so the majority vote is what you use.

Where the test degrades, and why

A point at depth Z has a triangulated depth uncertainty growing like Z² (§18 derives and measures this). As Z grows, the uncertainty eventually exceeds Z itself, and at that moment the sign of the estimated depth becomes a coin flip. Points near the plane at infinity therefore vote randomly. Two consequences, both practical:

  • Never decide on one point. The noise-free argument that one point suffices is correct and useless. Vote with all of them, or with all of them weighted by 1/Z.
  • Down-weight or discard distant points in the vote. A far-field point contributes almost nothing to the pose anyway — its rays are nearly parallel — but it contributes a full vote to a decision it has no information about.
  • Points behind the cameras are not always errors. A reconstruction can legitimately contain points whose rays diverge, which are best re-interpreted as points at infinity by setting the homogeneous fourth coordinate to zero rather than forced to a large finite depth.
Cheirality is also a global tool. Beyond picking one of four poses, the same "in front of the camera" constraint is what upgrades a projective reconstruction (§05) to a quasi-affine one — it identifies which side of the plane at infinity the scene is on, and that is genuine information about the projective ambiguity that no amount of point matching supplies. It is also worth adding to the RANSAC scoring of §11: a hypothesis that places a third of its inliers behind a camera should not be scored as though it explained them.

14 Triangulation I: the mid-point method

Two rays that should meet, and do not. The most obvious repair is to take the point that is closest to both of them — obvious, cheap, and not what you want.

With the pose known, a correspondence gives two rays: from c₀ in direction v̂₀ = N(R₀⁻¹K₀⁻¹x̄₀), and from c₁ in direction v̂₁, where N(·) normalises to unit length. Because the image points carry noise, the rays are skew — in three dimensions two general lines do not intersect, and the probability that these two do is zero.

the derivation, in four steps1 · The nearest point to a candidate p on ray j is qⱼ = cⱼ + dⱼ v̂ⱼ where dⱼ minimises ‖cⱼ + dⱼ v̂ⱼ − p‖². Differentiating: dⱼ = v̂ⱼ · (p − cⱼ). 2 · Substituting back, the squared distance from p to ray j is rⱼ² = ‖(I − v̂ⱼ v̂ⱼᵀ)(p − cⱼ)‖² (I − v̂v̂ᵀ) projects OUT the ray direction 3 · Sum over the views and set the gradient to zero: ∇ ∑ⱼ rⱼ² = 2 ∑ⱼ (I − v̂ⱼ v̂ⱼᵀ)(p − cⱼ) = 0 4 · which is a 3 × 3 linear system, solvable in closed form: ⎡ ⎤⁻¹ ⎡ ⎤ p = ⎢ ∑ⱼ (I − v̂ⱼv̂ⱼᵀ) ⎥ ⎢ ∑ⱼ (I − v̂ⱼv̂ⱼᵀ)cⱼ ⎥ ⎣ ⎦ ⎣ ⎦ Note (I − v̂v̂ᵀ) is idempotent and symmetric — it is the orthogonal projector onto the plane perpendicular to v̂, which is why it appears twice with no square. Note also that the sum runs over ANY number of views: mid-point generalises to N views for free, which is one of its two genuine virtues. The other is that it needs no SVD.
Why it is nevertheless the wrong objective. It minimises a distance in the world, in metres. But the noise is in the image, in pixels, and one pixel of image noise corresponds to a world distance proportional to depth. Mid-point therefore silently weights near points more heavily than far ones and — worse — is not invariant to the projective frame. Re-express the same cameras and points in a different projective coordinate system and mid-point returns a different point; the reprojection-error minimiser returns the same one. For an uncalibrated reconstruction, where the projective frame is arbitrary anyway, that is close to disqualifying.

A common defence: "the rays almost meet, so it hardly matters which repair you use."

Measured, in the figure of §16: at a metre of baseline, five metres of depth and a pixel of noise, the three methods differ by less than a percent in 3-D error — the defence holds. Shrink the baseline to 15 cm and push the scene to 25 metres and the same three methods differ by a factor of seventeen, and in the direction nobody expects: the mid-point estimate becomes the most accurate in 3-D while remaining the worst by reprojection error. The defence is a statement about a configuration, not about the methods.

15 Triangulation II: the linear method

Work in homogeneous coordinates, clear the perspective division, and the whole thing becomes one more homogeneous linear system — the same step as §07, §09 and §26.

clearing the divisionthe projection of a homogeneous 3-D point X = (X, Y, Z, W) into camera j, with rows p₀ᵀ, p₁ᵀ, p₂ᵀ of the 3 × 4 matrix Pⱼ, is u = (p₀ᵀX) / (p₂ᵀX) v = (p₁ᵀX) / (p₂ᵀX) multiply out the denominators: u (p₂ᵀX) − (p₀ᵀX) = 0 → row: u p₂ᵀ − p₀ᵀ v (p₂ᵀX) − (p₁ᵀX) = 0 → row: v p₂ᵀ − p₁ᵀ Two rows per view. Two views give a 4 × 4 homogeneous system A X = 0; solve it as always, by the smallest right singular vector. Then dehomogenise by dividing by W — UNLESS W ≈ 0, in which case the point is at (or near) infinity and dividing is exactly the wrong thing to do. Keeping X homogeneous, and never dividing, is the numerically sound choice when the scene contains far-away points, and it is also what §27's bundle adjustment should use for its structure parameters.

What it actually minimises, and why that is not what you want

Multiplying out the denominator is not free. The residual that the linear system drives to zero is u(p₂ᵀX) − p₀ᵀX, and p₂ᵀX is the depth of the point in that camera. So the residual is the reprojection error multiplied by the depth. Distant points, which are the least reliable, get the largest weight. This is the same defect as the algebraic residual of §05, in a new costume, and it has the same fix — either weight each row by 1/(p₂ᵀX) and iterate (which is exactly Sampson-style iterative re-weighting), or stop pretending and minimise the real thing.

mid-point
Closest to both rays
3 × 3 solve, no SVD, any number of views. Minimises metres, not pixels. Not projective-invariant.
linear DLT
Smallest singular vector
4 × 4 SVD, any number of views, handles points at infinity if you keep W. Minimises a depth-weighted algebraic residual. Is projective-invariant when the point is far from infinity, which is its main advantage over mid-point.
optimal, two views
Minimum reprojection error
Closed form via a degree-six polynomial, or by minimising over the one-parameter pencil directly. The only one of the three that answers the question actually asked. §16.
n views
Non-linear least squares
No closed form for more than two views. Initialise with DLT and run Levenberg–Marquardt on the reprojection error — which is bundle adjustment with the cameras held fixed, and is §27 in miniature.

16 Triangulation III: the optimal two-view method

Ask the right question and the answer is a polynomial. The right question is: what is the nearest pair of image points that could have come from a single 3-D point?

the objective, and the reduction that makes it solvableminimise d(x₀, x̂₀)² + d(x₁, x̂₁)² over all pairs (x̂₀, x̂₁) subject to x̂₁ᵀ F x̂₀ = 0. Once such a pair is found, its rays meet EXACTLY, so any triangulation method returns the same point, and that point is the global minimiser of reprojection error. The reduction. The constraint says x̂₀ lies on some epipolar line l₀ and x̂₁ on its partner l₁. Given the line, the optimal x̂₀ is the FOOT OF THE PERPENDICULAR from x₀ to l₀, and likewise for x̂₁. So the two-dimensional search collapses to a search over the ONE-PARAMETER pencil of epipolar planes: minimise s(θ) = d(x₀, l₀(θ))² + d(x₁, l₁(θ))² θ ∈ [0, π) After translating each image so its point is at the origin and rotating so the epipole is on the x-axis, s(θ) becomes a ratio of quadratics in t = tan θ, and setting its derivative to zero clears to a polynomial of DEGREE SIX. Six roots, up to six real critical points, evaluate s at each and take the smallest — plus the limit t → ∞, which corresponds to the epipolar line through the point being the one parallel to the image axis, and which the polynomial cannot represent.

The measurement, and the result nobody expects

Three methods, one ground truth, two error measures. The reprojection-cost ordering is exactly as advertised — the "optimal" method is optimal at the thing it optimises, always, by construction. The 3-D ordering is not.

Minimising reprojection error is not minimising 3-D error, and at long range the difference is enormous: a handful of near-parallel-ray points get thrown to huge depths by the reprojection-optimal estimate, which is entirely happy to move a point from 25 m to 2 km for a hundredth of a pixel. The mid-point method cannot do this: its answer is bounded by the geometry of the two rays, and here that crude bound is worth more than optimality.

Stated as a theorem: "the optimal triangulation method gives the most accurate 3-D point."

Stated correctly: it gives the 3-D point that minimises reprojection error, which is the maximum-likelihood estimate under isotropic Gaussian image noise. That is the right estimator for the measurement model, and it is still not the minimiser of 3-D error, because the map from image error to 3-D error is wildly non-linear and blows up as the rays become parallel. If what you need is a 3-D point cloud with bounded error, the reprojection-optimal estimate must be paired with a cut on the ray angle or with homogeneous coordinates that let a point sit at infinity honestly.
