25 Structure from motion: the pipeline

Two views give relative pose up to scale. Many views give a whole reconstruction — but only if the pairwise answers can be glued together without the seams accumulating, and that gluing is the entire engineering problem.

images→features→pairwise matching→geometric verification (§11)→tracks→pick an initial pair→E, pose, triangulate→add a camera by pose from 3-D points→triangulate new points→bundle adjust→repeat→final global bundle adjustment

The four steps that are not obvious

  • Track building. A track is the set of 2-D features across many images that are all projections of one 3-D point. It is built by taking the transitive closure of pairwise matches, and it goes wrong constantly: two different 3-D points on a repeating façade get matched into a single track, and that track then pulls two unrelated parts of the scene together. Tracks that are internally inconsistent — the same image contributing two features to one track — must be split or discarded.
  • Choosing the initial pair. Not the pair with the most matches. The pair with many matches and substantial out-of-plane parallax, because §11's planar degeneracy is fatal here. The standard test is to fit a homography to the matches as well as an essential matrix and to prefer the pair where the homography fails — a low homography inlier ratio means real 3-D structure.
  • Adding a camera. Once some 3-D points exist, a new image that observes enough of them can be posed by perspective-n-point: solve for (R, t) from 2-D–3-D correspondences, inside RANSAC. This is the step that lets the reconstruction grow, and it is also the step through which a bad track injects a bad camera.
  • When to bundle adjust. After every camera is too slow; after all of them is too late, because the initialisation for the last cameras will be hopeless. In practice: after every few cameras, plus a full pass at the end, plus a full pass whenever the reconstruction grows by some fraction.

A third option that is not a compromise. Hierarchical reconstruction builds reliable three-view sub-models first — a triplet is the smallest configuration in which a reconstruction can be checked against a constraint it was not fitted to — and then merges sub-models pairwise up a tree. It has the robustness of incremental and the O(N log N) shape of global, at the cost of a merging step that has its own failure modes.

26 Factorization: the whole reconstruction in one SVD

If the camera is affine rather than perspective, structure and motion come out of a single matrix decomposition — no initialisation, no iteration, no local minima. The catch is in the "if".

the measurement matrix, and why it has rank 3under orthographic or weak-perspective projection there is no division by depth, so xⱼᵢ = Mⱼ pᵢ + tⱼ Mⱼ = the top 2 × 3 block of camera j Put the world origin at the CENTROID of the points. Then the centroid of the observations in frame j is x̄ⱼ = Mⱼ·0 + tⱼ = tⱼ, so subtracting the per-frame centroid removes the translations exactly: x̃ⱼᵢ = xⱼᵢ − x̄ⱼ = Mⱼ pᵢ Stack every frame and every point into one 2M × N matrix: ⎡ x̃₁₁ ⋯ x̃₁N ⎤ ⎡ M₁ ⎤ Ŵ = ⎢ ⋮ ⋮ ⎥ = ⎢ ⋮ ⎥ [ p₁ ⋯ p_N ] = M̂ Ŝ ⎣ x̃_M1 ⋯ x̃_MN ⎦ ⎣ M_M ⎦ (2M × N) (2M × 3) (3 × N) A product of a 2M × 3 and a 3 × N matrix has rank AT MOST 3, whatever M and N are. That is the theorem, and it is the whole method: the measurement matrix of a rigid scene under affine projection is rank 3, and its rank-3 factors ARE the motion and the structure.
recovering the factors, and the ambiguity that survivesSVD: Ŵ = U Σ Vᵀ. Keep the leading three: M̂ = U₃, Ŝ = Σ₃V₃ᵀ. With noise, the rank-3 truncation is the CLOSEST rank-3 matrix in the Frobenius norm (Eckart–Young again, §09) — so the SVD also does the denoising for free. But the factorisation is not unique: for ANY invertible 3 × 3 matrix Q, Ŵ = (M̂ Q)(Q⁻¹ Ŝ) so M̂ and Ŝ are recovered only up to a linear transformation of space. Fixing Q is the "metric upgrade", and it uses what we know about the CAMERAS, not the scene: the two rows of every Mⱼ are orthogonal and of equal length (scaled orthography). Writing L = QQᵀ and uⱼ, vⱼ for the two rows of M̂ belonging to frame j: uⱼᵀ L uⱼ − vⱼᵀ L vⱼ = 0 2M linear equations uⱼᵀ L vⱼ = 0 in the SIX entries of the symmetric L Solve that homogeneous system by — of course — the smallest right singular vector, then take Q as a matrix square root of L via its eigen-decomposition. WHAT SURVIVES. L determines Q only up to Q → QO with O orthogonal, so the shape is recovered up to a global rotation — expected, since no world frame was ever given — AND up to a REFLECTION, because O may have determinant −1. That reflection is the classic DEPTH REVERSAL: a convex object rotating left and its mirror image rotating right produce identical orthographic image sequences. It is the same thing as the Necker cube illusion, and no amount of affine data resolves it.

Its two real limitations, and what they became

  • It needs a complete measurement matrix. Every point must be visible in every frame. Real tracks are riddled with holes — points leave the field of view, get occluded, or fail to match. The classical repair is to factor a dense sub-block first and use it to hallucinate the missing entries, then grow; the modern framing is low-rank matrix completion, and the same problem appears wherever principal component analysis meets missing data.
  • It needs an affine camera. The fix is to iterate: reconstruct affinely, estimate the per-frame perspective correction ηⱼ = 1/t_zⱼ from the reconstruction, divide it out of the measurements, and factor again. Three to five rounds usually converge. A fully projective factorization instead scales each measurement by its projective depth and iterates on those, which needs no calibration at all.
  • What it is actually for now. Not as a final answer — bundle adjustment (§27) is strictly better whenever it can be initialised. Factorization's value is that it is a closed form: it cannot get stuck in a local minimum, so it is an excellent initialiser for the thing that can.

27 Bundle adjustment: the objective everything else approximates

Every estimator on this page has been a way of getting close enough. This is the thing they were getting close to: minimise, over all cameras and all points at once, the distance between where the features were seen and where the model says they should be.

the objective ⎧ ⎫ minimise over {Rⱼ, cⱼ, Kⱼ} and {pᵢ}: ∑ ⎨ ρ( ‖ xᵢⱼ − π(pᵢ ; Rⱼ, cⱼ, Kⱼ) ‖²_Σᵢⱼ ) ⎬ i,j ⎩ ⎭ π is the full projection chain of part 1 §16: subtract the camera centre, rotate, divide by z, apply radial distortion, apply K Σᵢⱼ is the measurement covariance — a feature detected at scale σ is localised to about σ pixels, so the weight should depend on the scale it was found at ρ is a robust loss (Huber, Cauchy, Tukey) so that one surviving mismatch does not drag ten cameras with it. With ρ(s) = s it is plain least squares. The sum runs ONLY over observations that actually exist — the visibility pattern. "Bundle" refers to the bundles of rays joining each camera centre to the points it sees; "adjustment" is the photogrammetric word for iterative refinement. The name is a century old and the method is still the one that ends every pipeline.

Why it is not a linear problem, and how it is solved anyway

It is non-linear twice over: π divides by depth, and the rotations live on a curved manifold. So it is solved by iterative linearisation — Gauss–Newton, damped into Levenberg–Marquardt:

one stepstack every residual into r(θ) and let J = ∂r/∂θ. Then Gauss–Newton: (JᵀJ) Δθ = −Jᵀ r Levenberg–Marquardt: (JᵀJ + λ diag(JᵀJ)) Δθ = −Jᵀ r λ small → a Gauss–Newton step, fast near the optimum. λ large → a small step down the gradient, safe far from it. The standard schedule: try a step; if the cost fell, accept it and divide λ; if it rose, reject and multiply λ. Two lines of bookkeeping and it is what makes the method usable from a mediocre initialisation. AND THE DAMPING IS NOT OPTIONAL HERE. JᵀJ for a bundle adjustment problem is EXACTLY SINGULAR — it has a seven-dimensional null space, always, no matter how good the data is. Gauss–Newton cannot take a step at all; the λdiag term is what makes the matrix invertible. §29 explains where the seven comes from.
Three parameterisation choices that are not cosmetic.
· Rotations: never as a 3 × 3 matrix with nine free entries — that is six redundant parameters and an orthogonality constraint the solver will violate. Use a local three-vector increment, R ← R·exp([δ]ₓ), re-linearised each iteration, or a unit quaternion with a normalisation step. Part 1 §10 and §11 own both.
· Points: homogeneous four-vectors rather than (X, Y, Z). A distant point has an enormous Z and a terrible condition number in Euclidean coordinates, and no condition number at all in homogeneous ones — it simply approaches W = 0. This is the same argument as §15's.
· The Jacobian: computed by differentiating the chain of transforms one link at a time and composing, which is exactly reverse-mode automatic differentiation. A camera rig with several fixed cameras on a moving platform is then handled by inserting two more links in the chain rather than by rewriting anything.

28 Sparsity, the arrowhead, and the Schur complement

A real reconstruction has thousands of cameras and millions of points. Solving a dense linear system in that many unknowns is impossible, and it is also unnecessary, because the system is not dense — and the shape of its sparsity is dictated by one fact about the problem.

the factEvery observation xᵢⱼ depends on EXACTLY TWO things: the 3-D point pᵢ and the camera j. It does not depend on any other point or on any other camera. So the two rows of J belonging to that observation are zero everywhere except in ONE 6-column camera block and ONE 3-column point block. Structure from motion is a BIPARTITE problem — points on one side, cameras on the other, observations as edges — and every sparsity property below is a restatement of that.

The Hessian, block by block

Order the parameters with the structure first and the motion second — which is the right order when there are more points than cameras, as there almost always are. Then A = JᵀJ has four blocks:

the arrowhead ⎡ A_pp A_pc ⎤ A_pp 3N × 3N, BLOCK DIAGONAL with 3 × 3 blocks A = ⎢ ⎥ A_cc 6M × 6M, BLOCK DIAGONAL with 6 × 6 blocks ⎣ A_pcᵀ A_cc ⎦ A_pc 3N × 6M, one 3 × 6 block per OBSERVATION A_pp is block diagonal because two different points share no residual — no residual depends on two points. A_cc is block diagonal for the same reason about cameras. All the coupling lives in A_pc, and it is as sparse as the visibility pattern. The shape is an ARROWHEAD: a diagonal spine with one dense border.
eliminating the points — the Schur complementsolve ⎡ A_pp A_pc ⎤ ⎡ Δp ⎤ = ⎡ b_p ⎤ by eliminating Δp from the first row: ⎣ A_pcᵀ A_cc ⎦ ⎣ Δc ⎦ ⎣ b_c ⎦ Δp = A_pp⁻¹ ( b_p − A_pc Δc ) substitute into the second row: ( A_cc − A_pcᵀ A_pp⁻¹ A_pc ) Δc = b_c − A_pcᵀ A_pp⁻¹ b_p └────────── A′_cc ──────────┘ the REDUCED CAMERA SYSTEM, 6M × 6M And A_pp⁻¹ is free: A_pp is block diagonal, so inverting it is N independent 3 × 3 inversions, not one 3N × 3N inversion. That is the whole trick, and it turns a system of size 6M + 3N into one of size 6M — for a thousand cameras and a million points, from 3 006 000 unknowns to 6 000.

The fill-in, stated exactly

Loose statement: "eliminating the points makes the camera block dense."

Exact statement, and it is verified in the figure below: the reduced camera matrix A′_cc has a non-zero 6 × 6 block in position (j, k) if and only if cameras j and k observe at least one point in common. Nothing more and nothing less. So A′_cc is dense only when the camera graph is a clique — every camera seeing something every other camera sees — which happens for a city square photographed from all round and does not happen for a vehicle driving down a road. The sparsity of the reduced system is the sparsity of the co-visibility graph, and that is a fact about the capture, not about the algebra.

Three consequences that follow directly.
· Order global parameters last. A shared intrinsic calibration, or a rig's camera-to-rig transforms, couples every observation. Put those parameters at the end of the ordering, along the outer edge of the arrowhead, so their fill-in is confined to one border rather than smeared through the whole matrix.
· The reverse ordering is sometimes right. Eliminating cameras instead of points is better when there are more cameras than points — tracking a handful of markers through thousands of video frames. The structure is symmetric; only the counts decide.
· Beyond a certain size, stop factorising. Once A′_cc is itself too large or too dense, solve it by conjugate gradients with a preconditioner, which never forms the matrix at all — only its product with a vector, which the sparsity makes cheap.
The bipartite graph, the Jacobian it implies, and the fill-in the Schur complement creates the fill-in pattern is checked against "these two cameras share a point" — the readout says whether it matches exactly

29 Gauge freedom, and the ambiguities that are not gauge freedom

A reconstruction has seven directions in which you can move every camera and every point at once without changing a single pixel of prediction. Recognising them is the difference between a solver that works and one that reports nonsense with confidence.

the sevenlet S be any similarity transformation of space — a rotation, a translation and a uniform scaling. Replace every point and every camera by its transformed version: pᵢ → S pᵢ Pⱼ → Pⱼ S⁻¹ then every predicted image point is unchanged, because Pⱼ S⁻¹ S pᵢ = Pⱼ pᵢ. So the objective is EXACTLY FLAT along the seven-parameter family of similarities: 3 rotation + 3 translation + 1 scale = 7 MEASURED. For the bundle problem of §27 — 6 cameras, 25 points, 300 residuals, 111 parameters — the singular values of J at the true parameters are …, 75.0, 61.0, 28.4, | 5.5e-8, 4.8e-8, 4.5e-8, 4.3e-8, 3.9e-8, 3.3e-8, 2.7e-8 Three healthy small values, then a gap of nine orders of magnitude, then EXACTLY SEVEN values at the level of rounding. Not six, not eight. The rank of J is 111 − 7 = 104.

Three things that look like gauge freedom and are not

  • The bas-relief ambiguity. A shallow object undergoing a large rotation and a deep object undergoing a small one produce almost the same images. This is a near-degeneracy, not an exact one: the objective is not flat along it, only very nearly flat, so it shows up as a small-but-non-zero eigenvalue rather than a zero. It is the direction along which the covariance is largest in almost every small-baseline reconstruction, and it is why depth-versus-motion is the hardest thing to pin down. The same ambiguity appears in factorization (§26) as the reflection, and in shading as the depth-versus-illuminant-angle trade.
  • The projective ambiguity. With uncalibrated cameras the freedom is not seven-dimensional but fifteen-dimensional — the whole projective group of 3-space — because Pⱼ → PⱼH⁻¹, pᵢ → Hpᵢ preserves every image for any invertible 4 × 4 H. Removing it down to the similarity's seven is exactly what self-calibration (§30) does, and the eight extra dimensions are why it is hard.
  • Individual parameter uncertainties. A per-parameter error bar taken from the diagonal of the covariance hides the correlations, and in structure from motion the correlations are the whole story: point depths and camera baselines move together. Look at the leading eigenvectors of the covariance, not at its diagonal.

30 Calibration: buying back the metric

Everything metric on this page — angles, parallelism, ratios of lengths — depends on knowing K. There are two ways to know it: measure it once against a known object, or infer it from the images themselves and accept the conditions under which that is possible.

What a calibration target buys, and how

A planar target with a known pattern gives, per view, a set of 2-D–3-D correspondences whose 3-D points all lie on one plane. Put that plane at Z = 0; the projection then collapses to a homography:

from a planar target to constraints on Kfor a point (X, Y, 0) on the target, x̃ ∼ K [ r₁ r₂ r₃ | t ] (X, Y, 0, 1)ᵀ = K [ r₁ r₂ t ] (X, Y, 1)ᵀ = H (X, Y, 1)ᵀ so H = λ K [r₁ r₂ t], estimated from ≥ 4 correspondences by the direct linear transform (part 6). Now use the fact that r₁ and r₂ are ORTHONORMAL columns of a rotation: r₁ = λ⁻¹K⁻¹h₁ r₂ = λ⁻¹K⁻¹h₂ with h₁, h₂ the first two columns of H r₁ᵀr₂ = 0 ⟹ h₁ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₂ = 0 ‖r₁‖=‖r₂‖ ⟹ h₁ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₁ = h₂ᵀ (K⁻ᵀK⁻¹) h₂ TWO linear constraints per view on the symmetric matrix B = K⁻ᵀK⁻¹, which has 6 entries and 5 degrees of freedom up to scale. So THREE views of a planar target determine K completely; two views suffice if the skew is assumed zero; one view suffices if the aspect ratio and principal point are also assumed. Recover K from B by Cholesky. Note the structure: this is EXACTLY the metric-upgrade step of factorization (§26) — solve a homogeneous linear system for a symmetric matrix, then take its square root. B is the image of the ABSOLUTE CONIC, and it is the single object that all of calibration and self-calibration is about.
What the target really buys is non-degeneracy. Every view of the target must be at a genuinely different orientation: two views of a plane at the same orientation give the same two constraints twice. It also buys distortion: the target's straight lines are known to be straight, so the radial coefficients of Part 1 §17 can be estimated at the same time, and they must be, because a distorted image of a plane is not related to the plane by a homography at all.

Self-calibration, in outline

With no target, the calibration has to come out of the correspondences. It cannot come out of them entirely: post-multiplying every camera by H̃⁻¹ and pre-multiplying every point by H̃ leaves every image unchanged (§29), and an RQ decomposition of the transformed cameras gives a completely different, equally valid, set of calibration matrices. Every self-calibration method therefore assumes something, and the assumption is what makes it work:

  • Constant intrinsics across views. The most common assumption. The same camera took all the pictures, so K is one unknown rather than n. Three or more views then generally suffice.
  • Zero skew, unit aspect ratio, principal point at the image centre. Nearly always true of a modern sensor, and it reduces K to a single unknown focal length. It fails for a cropped image — the principal point is no longer central — and for a photograph of a photograph.
  • Two views and the Kruppa equations. With the above assumptions, both focal lengths can be recovered from a single fundamental matrix. Write D_j = K_jK_jᵀ = diag(f_j², f_j², 1) and take the singular vectors u₀, u₁, v₀, v₁ of F and its singular values σ₀, σ₁; the three ratios u₁ᵀD₀u₁ / σ₀²v₀ᵀD₁v₀ = −u₀ᵀD₀u₁ / σ₀σ₁v₀ᵀD₁v₁ = u₀ᵀD₀u₀ / σ₁²v₁ᵀD₁v₁ are each affine in f₀² over affine in f₁², so cross-multiplying gives quadratics that solve directly.
  • Vanishing points. Three mutually orthogonal vanishing points in one image determine K outright, because they are the images of three points on the plane at infinity that are conjugate with respect to the absolute conic. Architectural scenes hand this over for free.

Stated as a theorem: "two views with known principal point and zero skew determine both focal lengths."

Stated correctly: they determine them generically, and there are configurations in which they determine nothing at all — notably when there is no relative rotation between the two views, and when the two optical axes intersect. In those cases the focal lengths trade off directly against the depth of the reconstruction, which is the bas-relief ambiguity of §29 again: a deeper scene photographed with a longer lens looks the same. The practical advice that follows is unromantic and correct: take the focal length from the image metadata as an initial value, and let bundle adjustment refine it along with everything else.

31 Drift, and the one measurement that removes it

An incremental reconstruction estimates each pose relative to the last. Errors therefore compound, and they compound at a rate that depends on which quantity is wrong — a fact worth knowing before deciding what to fix.

Three growth laws, measured

A camera walking in a straight line, one unit per step, with per-step errors added to its odometry. The end-point position error after n steps, fitted on a log–log axis over n = 10 to 320, 300 trials each — the same measurement the figure below runs live:

The design consequence. Since rotation errors dominate, the effort belongs there: use wide-field-of-view cameras, which pin down rotation far better than narrow ones; use as many well-spread features as possible rather than many clustered ones; and prefer estimators that get the rotation right even at the cost of the translation, which is exactly why global methods solve rotation averaging first and separately (§25). And since a bias is worse than noise, hunt for systematic errors — an uncorrected radial distortion, a wrong principal point, a rolling shutter — before adding more features.

Loop closure

The cure for accumulated drift is not a better incremental estimator. It is one measurement that is not incremental: recognising that the camera has returned somewhere it has been before, and adding a constraint directly between two poses that are far apart in time. That single edge converts an open chain into a cycle, and a cycle has a consistency condition that the chain did not.

pose-graph optimisationnodes: the poses x₀ … x_{n−1}. edges: relative-pose measurements zᵢⱼ with weights. minimise ∑ ‖ ⊖xᵢ ⊕ xⱼ ⊖ zᵢⱼ ‖²_Ωᵢⱼ (i,j) where ⊕ and ⊖ compose and invert poses. This is bundle adjustment with the 3-D points already eliminated — the structure has been marginalised into the relative-pose constraints — so it is small, sparse and fast, and it is the standard back end of any system that has to run in real time. MEASURED, on a 40-pose closed loop with 0.02 translation noise, 0.01 rad rotation noise and a rotation bias of 0.004 rad per step: open-loop RMS position error 1.2574 gap between the last pose and the first 1.4144 after one loop-closure edge and 8 iterations 0.1883 a factor of 6.7 The optimiser does not "close the gap" by moving the last pose. It DISTRIBUTES the inconsistency backwards over every pose in the loop, in proportion to how uncertain each edge was, which is why the whole trajectory improves and not just its end.

The hard part is the optimisation.

The hard part is the recognition. Pose-graph optimisation is a well-conditioned sparse least-squares problem and is essentially solved. Deciding that this image shows the same place as that one, several thousand frames ago, is a retrieval problem — and it has an asymmetry that makes it dangerous: a missed loop closure costs you the drift you already had, while a false one welds two unrelated parts of the map together and destroys the reconstruction outright. So the verification is geometric and strict: retrieve candidates by appearance, then require a consistent relative pose supported by many inliers, then require that the closure is consistent with other closures. Repeated architecture is the adversary throughout, exactly as in §11 and §24.
