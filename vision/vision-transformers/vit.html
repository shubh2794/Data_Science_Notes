ViT: Scale Beats Inductive Bias

The paper that applied a standard Transformer encoder, almost unchanged, to a sequence of 16×16 image patches. The hub page covers how patch tokens, the class token and the encoder work. This page reads the paper as a paper. It covers the exact equations, what the paper says about inductive bias, the three model sizes and their training recipe, the results table and its compute column, the data-size experiments that show where ViT starts to win, the scaling study, what the trained model's embeddings and attention look like, and the appendix ablations.

01 The claim, and what it was arguing against

In 2020, attention in vision was mostly used together with convolutions, or to replace some parts of a convolutional network while keeping its overall structure. The paper's claim is that this reliance on CNNs is not necessary. A pure Transformer applied directly to a sequence of image patches does very well on image classification. When it is pre-trained on enough data and then transferred to mid-sized or small benchmarks, it matches or beats the best convolutional networks while using substantially less pre-training compute.

  • The design rule. Follow the original Transformer as closely as possible, so that scalable NLP Transformer architectures and their efficient implementations can be used almost out of the box. The only image-specific steps are cutting the image into patches and, at fine-tuning time, resizing the position embeddings (§03–§04).
  • The catch, stated up front. Trained on mid-sized datasets such as ImageNet without strong regularisation, these models land a few points below ResNets of comparable size. The paper attributes this to missing inductive biases (translation equivariance and locality) and argues that large-scale training "trumps inductive bias" (§10).
  • The background. Convolutional priors are on CNNs. The encoder itself is on Transformers, and the paper it copies is on The Original Transformer (2017).

02 The exact formulation (Eq. 1–4)

The whole model fits in four lines. The hub explains patch counting and the class token, so this section gives only the equations as the paper prints them, plus the two details people usually miss: what the head looks like and how it is swapped.

  • Pre-norm, not post-norm. Equations 2–3 put LN inside the residual branch. That is the reverse of the 2017 paper's LayerNorm(x + f(x)). The difference is explained on Residual Stream, Norms & FFN.
  • Learnable 1-D position embeddings. E_pos is a plain table with one learned vector per sequence slot, in raster order. The paper does not use sinusoids or a 2-D scheme by default. §14 shows why this choice barely matters.
  • The head changes between stages. During pre-training the classification head on y is an MLP with one hidden layer, which uses a tanh non-linearity according to the appendix. During fine-tuning that head is removed and replaced by a single zero-initialised D × K linear layer, where K is the number of downstream classes.
  • Patch embedding. Because E is shared across patches with stride equal to patch size, it is a convolution with kernel P and stride P. The hub covers this in its patchify section (Vision Transformers).

03 Inductive bias, and the hybrid architecture

The paper is precise about how little image structure the model gets for free. It is not "no bias". The bias is confined to two moments, and everything else must be learned.

in a CNN
Built into every layer
Locality, two-dimensional neighbourhood structure and translation equivariance hold in each layer, throughout the whole model.
in ViT
Only the MLP is local
The per-token MLP layers are local and translation-equivariant. Self-attention is global. Nothing in the attention layers knows which patches are neighbours.
where 2-D structure enters
Twice, and sparingly
(1) When the image is cut into patches. (2) At fine-tuning, when position embeddings are resized for a new resolution (§04). At initialisation the position embeddings carry no 2-D information. All spatial relations are learned from scratch.

The hybrid: CNN features as the token sequence

  • The idea. Instead of raw pixels, form the input sequence from a CNN feature map. The projection E of Eq. 1 is applied to patches of that map. In the special case of 1×1 patches, the sequence is just the feature map with its spatial dimensions flattened, projected to width D.
  • What the paper built. A ResNet50 stem ("R50+ViT"), fed into ViT with a patch size of one "pixel". The sequence length is varied in one of two ways. (i) Take the output of stage 4 of a regular ResNet50. (ii) Remove stage 4, put the same number of layers into stage 3, and take the output of this extended stage 3. Option (ii) gives a 4× longer sequence and a more expensive ViT.
  • The ResNet modifications. In both the baselines and the hybrid stems, Batch Normalisation is replaced with Group Normalisation, and the convolutions are standardised. This is the large-transfer ResNet recipe that the paper also uses as its main baseline.
  • How hybrids compare. They are slightly better than pure ViT at small compute budgets, and the gap vanishes for larger models (§11). Their early-layer attention is also less local (§12), because the ResNet has already done the local work.

04 Fine-tuning at higher resolution: 2-D interpolation of position embeddings

Pre-training is done at 224×224. Fine-tuning is usually done higher, at 384 by default and 512 or 518 for the headline ImageNet numbers. The paper notes that a higher fine-tuning resolution is often beneficial. The patch size stays fixed, so a larger image gives a longer sequence. The Transformer can process any length, but the pre-trained E_pos has one row per old slot, so it no longer fits.

the grids involved (our arithmetic from the paper's resolutions)pre-train 224 px, P = 16 → 14 × 14 = 196 patches (+1 class token) fine-tune 384 px, P = 16 → 24 × 24 = 576 patches default for transfer (Table 4) 512 px, P = 16 → 32 × 32 = 1024 patches ViT-L/16 on ImageNet, Table 2 518 px, P = 14 → 37 × 37 = 1369 patches ViT-H/14 on ImageNet, Table 2 (pre-trained grid 16 × 16) the fix the paper usesreshape the N patch rows of E_pos into a g × g × D grid (the class-token row is set aside) 2-D interpolate to g′ × g′ × D "according to their location in the original image" flatten back to g′² rows and re-attach the class-token row

The paper says only "2D interpolation". It does not name the kernel. The figure below uses bilinear interpolation with patch centres aligned, which is one common choice. Keeping the class-token row unchanged is how public implementations handle it. That is general knowledge, not a statement in the paper.

Resizing a 14 × 14 position-embedding grid for fine-tuning drag the resolution and pick a target patch · left: the pre-trained grid, coloured by similarity to the chosen interpolated embedding, with its four source neighbours outlined · right: the new grid · embeddings are a synthetic stand-in with smooth row/column structure, not the paper's learned weights

This interpolation is the point where the model is given 2-D structure by hand. The other point is patch cutting. Without it, the model would have to learn new position vectors for 380 slots it never saw (our arithmetic: 576 − 196). With it, each new slot starts from a weighted mix of its spatial neighbours.

  • De-duplication. Each pre-training set is de-duplicated against the test sets of the downstream tasks, following the large-transfer ResNet work it compares against. Without this, a model pre-trained on hundreds of millions of web images could have seen the evaluation images.
  • Transfer benchmarks. ImageNet, scored on both the original validation labels and the cleaned-up ReaL labels. Also CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, Oxford-IIIT Pets and Oxford Flowers-102. These use the same pre-processing as the ResNet baseline.
  • VTAB. 19 tasks with 1 000 training examples each, in three groups. Natural covers ordinary photos. Specialized covers medical and satellite imagery. Structured covers tasks that need geometric understanding, such as localisation and counting (§09).

Fine-tuning accuracy vs linear few-shot accuracy

Most results are fine-tuning accuracy, measured after fine-tuning the whole model on each downstream set. Where fine-tuning every checkpoint would be too costly, the paper uses few-shot linear accuracy. The representations are frozen, and a regularised least-squares regression maps the representations of a small training subset to {−1, 1}^K target vectors.

the few-shot probe (the closed form is the standard ridge solution, general knowledge)X ∈ ℝ^(n × D) frozen y-features of n labelled images Y ∈ {−1, 1}^(n × K) one-hot targets scaled to ±1 W* = argmin_W ‖ X W − Y ‖² + λ ‖ W ‖² = ( XᵀX + λ I )⁻¹ Xᵀ Y predict argmax_k ( x W* )_k

The closed form takes seconds to compute, which is what makes the data-subset sweep (§10) and the ablations (§14) affordable. "5-shot ImageNet" means five labelled images per class go into X.

10 How much pre-training data does it need?

Two experiments, one conclusion. The first varies the dataset (ImageNet → ImageNet-21k → JFT-300M) and fine-tunes. The second varies the amount of data within JFT and uses only a linear probe. In both, convolutional inductive bias is worth a lot at small data and is overtaken as data grows.

Experiment 1: three datasets, fine-tuned to ImageNet (Figure 3 and Table 5)

To give the small datasets a fair chance, the paper tunes three regularisers there: weight decay, dropout and label smoothing. The values in Table 5 are ImageNet top-1 after fine-tuning at 384, without the Polyak averaging and 512-pixel resolution of Table 2.

  • On ImageNet alone, bigger is worse. ViT-L/16 scores 76.53 against 77.91 for ViT-B/16, even with heavy regularisation.
  • On ImageNet-21k they draw level. ViT-L/16 reaches 85.15 and ViT-H/14 85.13.
  • Only on JFT-300M does size pay. ViT-H/14 reaches 88.04, ViT-L/16 87.12 and ViT-B/16 84.15. The paper's figure also shows a shaded band for large-transfer ResNets of several sizes. Those ResNets beat ViT when pre-trained on ImageNet, and ViT overtakes them on the larger sets.
  • Patch 32 needs even more data. ViT-B/32 drops slightly from 21k to JFT (81.28 → 80.73), while ViT-L/32 gains 3.4 points (our arithmetic).

Experiment 2: random JFT subsets of 9M, 30M, 90M and 300M (Figure 4)

  • Designed to remove regularisation as a confound. No extra regularisation is used, the hyperparameters are the same at every size, and the paper uses early stopping, reporting the best validation accuracy reached during training. The paper evaluates with linear few-shot accuracy on ImageNet (§06), so the subsets measure the representation, not the fine-tuning recipe.
  • The crossover. ViT-B/32 is slightly faster than ResNet50. It is much worse on the 9M subset and better from 90M images upward. The same holds for ResNet152x2 against ViT-L/16. The paper gives this result only as a plot, so this page does not quote values from it.
  • The paper's reading. Convolutional inductive bias is useful for small datasets. For larger ones, learning the relevant patterns directly from data is sufficient, and even beneficial. Its caption summarises it as ResNets doing better on small pre-training sets but plateauing sooner.

Later work showed that the small-data gap is partly a training-recipe problem. With strong augmentation, regularisation and distillation, a ViT trains competitively on ImageNet alone. That work is beyond this paper and is summarised on the hub's DeiT section (Vision Transformers) and on Knowledge Distillation.

On Flowers, by contrast, even the 55-exaFLOP ViT-B/32 is ahead.

12 Inspecting the trained model

These are three probes of what ViT learns in place of the priors it lacks, plus one way to read its attention. They are qualitative figures, illustrated on chosen models and images.

patch embedding E (ViT-L/32)
Filters look like a CNN's first layer
The top principal components of the learned embedding filters look like plausible basis functions for a low-dimensional representation of the fine structure within each patch. Our reading of the figure is that they resemble the oriented edges and colour blobs of early CNN filters.
position embeddings (ViT-L/32)
2-D topology is learned
Cosine similarity between position embeddings shows that closer patches have more similar embeddings. Row and column structure appears, so patches in the same row or column are similar. For larger grids a sinusoidal pattern sometimes shows. This is why hand-built 2-D schemes add nothing (§14).
mean attention distance
Local and global heads from layer 1
This is the average image-space distance across which a head integrates information, weighted by attention, over 128 example images. It is analogous to receptive-field size in a CNN. In lower layers it varies widely across heads: some already attend over most of the image, others stay near the query. Distance grows with depth, and in the second half of the network most heads attend widely.
mean attention distance of one head (the paper's definition, written out)d̄(head) = mean over images and query positions q of ∑ₖ A[q, k] · ‖ pos(q) − pos(k) ‖ (in pixels) attention rollout (Appendix D.8)Āℓ = attention matrix of layer ℓ, averaged over all heads (ViT-L/16) R = Ā_L · Ā_(L−1) · … · Ā₁ row of the class token in R → a map over input patches
  • Local heads in the hybrid. The highly local attention seen in early ViT layers is less pronounced in hybrids, because a ResNet stem already did the local mixing. The paper suggests these local heads may serve a similar function to early convolutional layers.
  • Attention rollout. Multiplying the head-averaged attention matrices of all layers accounts for attention mixing across tokens through the whole network. The paper's Figure 6 uses it to show, on representative images, that the class token attends to regions that are semantically relevant for classification. The rollout method as originally proposed also mixes in the identity to account for residual connections. The paper's appendix does not spell this out (general knowledge).
  • Caution. Attention maps are a weak guide to what a model relies on. The caveats are on Attention Cost & Limits (beyond this paper).

13 A preliminary self-supervised result: masked patch prediction

The paper's last experiment borrows masked-token prediction from BERT. With this pre-training, ViT-B/16 reaches 79.9% on ImageNet. That is 2 points better than training from scratch, but still 4 points behind supervised pre-training. The paper calls it preliminary and leaves contrastive pre-training to future work.

the recipe (Appendix B.1.2)corrupt 50 % of patch embeddings: 80 % → a learnable [mask] embedding 10 % → a random other patch embedding 10 % → left unchanged target the 3-bit mean colour of each corrupted patch (3 bits × 3 channels → 512 colours, a 512-way classification) train 1M steps (≈ 14 epochs) on JFT, batch 4096, Adam, base LR 2·10⁻⁴, warmup 10k steps, cosine decay
  • Alternatives the paper tried. It also tried predicting a 4×4 downsized patch, or regressing the full patch with an L2 loss. The mean-colour target gave the best few-shot performance. A 15% corruption rate, as in BERT, did slightly worse than 50%.
  • What came after (beyond this paper). Masked autoencoders later made this idea work at scale. They mask about 75% of patches, run the encoder on the visible ones only, and reconstruct pixels. The hub summarises this in its section on follow-ups (Vision Transformers). The language-side origin of the idea is on BERT.

14 The appendix ablations

Class token vs global average pooling (Appendix D.3)

The paper keeps the extra class token so the model stays as close as possible to the original Transformer. The alternative is to average-pool the patch outputs and feed the result to a linear classifier. At first this pooling approach "performed very poorly". The paper then found the difference is fully explained by the learning rate: each head needs its own, and when both are tuned they work about equally well. The comparison is shown only as a plot (Figure 9), so no accuracies are quoted here.

Positional embeddings (Appendix D.4, Table 8)

ViT-B/16 is evaluated with 5-shot linear accuracy on ImageNet. The four encodings tested are as follows. None means a "bag of patches". 1-D is the default, learned and in raster order. 2-D uses separate learned X and Y tables of width D/2 each, concatenated. Relative uses learned embeddings of the relative distance between patches, added as a bias to the attention logits. The embeddings are added at the input ("stem"), at every layer, or at every layer with one shared table.

Two smaller appendix results

  • Real-world cost (Appendix D.5). Measured inference speed on TPUv3 is comparable to similar ResNets. The theoretical quadratic cost in sequence length, which is quartic in image side, only barely starts to show for the largest models at the largest resolutions. Large ViTs also fit larger per-core batches than ResNets, so they are more memory-efficient.
  • Axial attention (Appendix D.6). Variants that attend along rows and then columns beat their ViT-B counterparts in accuracy at higher compute cost. A naive implementation was too slow on TPUs to use at scale. Window and axis restrictions of this kind are covered on Attention Cost & Limits.

15 Common misconceptions

"ViT has no image prior at all." It has two, used sparingly. The patch cut and the 2-D interpolation of position embeddings at fine-tuning. The per-token MLP is also local and translation-equivariant (§03).
"ViT beats CNNs." Only with enough pre-training data. On ImageNet alone, large-transfer ResNets win, and ViT-L/16 is worse than ViT-B/16 (76.53 against 77.91). On the 9M JFT subset ViT-B/32 is much worse than ResNet50 (§10).
"1-D position embeddings throw away the 2-D layout." The model learns it. The learned table shows distance and row/column structure, and 2-D or relative schemes score within about 0.2 points of it (§12, §14).
"ViT-L/16 beat the ResNet baseline on every benchmark." It tied on two. ReaL 90.54 against 90.54, and VTAB 76.28 against 76.29 with overlapping spreads. ViT-H/14 is the model that wins across the board.
"The compute savings are the TPU-days column." That column mixes recipes. The controlled comparison is the scaling study's exaFLOPs, where the gap is 2–4× (§11).
"The paper introduced masked image pre-training as used today." It tried a small version. It predicted 3-bit mean colours of 50% corrupted patches and stayed 4 points behind supervised pre-training. Masked autoencoders came later (§13).

16 Takeaways

  • Four equations: embed patches plus a class token plus learned 1-D positions, then L pre-norm blocks of MSA and GELU MLP, and read out y = LN(z_L⁰). The head is a tanh MLP for pre-training and a zero-initialised linear layer for fine-tuning.
  • Sizes: Base 12 / 768 / 3072 / 12 heads / 86M, Large 24 / 1024 / 4096 / 16 / 307M, Huge 32 / 1280 / 5120 / 16 / 632M.
  • Recipe: pre-train at 224 with Adam (0.9, 0.999), batch 4096, weight decay 0.1 and 10k-step warmup. Fine-tune with SGD momentum 0.9, batch 512 and no weight decay, at 384 or higher, with 2-D-interpolated position embeddings.
  • Results: ViT-H/14 on JFT-300M reaches 88.55 on ImageNet, 94.55 on CIFAR-100 and 77.63 on VTAB, at about 4× less TPU time than the large-transfer ResNet152x4.
  • Data: CNN priors win at 1.3M or 9M images. ViT overtakes from roughly 90M images up and keeps improving with size.
  • Compute: 2–4× less pre-training compute than ResNets for equal transfer accuracy. Hybrids help only at small budgets. There is no saturation in the range tried.
  • Learned priors: the position table learns 2-D topology, some heads are local from layer 1, and attention distance grows with depth.
